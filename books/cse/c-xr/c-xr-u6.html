6.1 AR technology, features and system architecture

Augmented reality (AR) adds computer-made objects to a live view of the real world and keeps each object fixed to a real place. It exists because information is easier to use when it sits on the thing it describes: a machine on a factory floor, a page in a book, a street ahead. This chapter gives the three tests that separate AR from a simple video overlay, the features and classic methods, and the block architecture every AR system follows.

You need: Unit 1 on what VR, AR, MR and XR are, and the three I's.

The three tests that define AR

Augmented reality (AR) is a live view of the real world with computer-made objects added to it, where each virtual object stays tied to a real place. Virtual reality (VR) replaces the real view with a synthetic one. Mixed reality (MR) is the wider blend of real and virtual; AR is one region of MR.

The reality–virtuality continuum runs from the real environment, through AR, through augmented virtuality (mostly virtual, with real video or objects added), to the virtual environment. AR sits near the real end: the real world is still the main view.

Three tests separate AR from an overlay:

  • It combines real and virtual light in one view.
  • It registers virtual objects in 3D, so they stay fixed to real points as the user moves.
  • It runs in real time, updating the overlay as the user moves.

A phone camera that pastes a sticker at a fixed screen position fails test 2. A face filter that follows the nose passes. Registration needs a pose: the position and orientation of the camera or head in the world. Pose has six degrees of freedom (6DoF): x, y, z for position and yaw, pitch, roll for orientation.

Some syllabi call this set of tests the 'taxonomy of AR' or 'AR systems and functionality'.

Features and the classic AR methods

Features follow from the tests. 3D registration locks virtual objects to world coordinates, not screen coordinates. Real-time response means the pose is estimated and the frame redrawn every 16–33 ms for 30–60 frames per second. Context awareness uses what the system sees or where it is — a printed marker, a detected plane, a GPS point — to choose content. Hands-free use matters when the user is holding a tool or a patient.

The classic AR methods are four:

  • Optical see-through: a half-silvered combiner in front of the eye lets real light pass and reflects a micro-display into the eye. The user sees the real world directly, with virtual light added.
  • Video see-through: a camera captures the real scene, the renderer adds virtual objects to that image, and a screen shows the mix. Phone AR and most headsets with closed displays use this.
  • Monitor-based: a fixed camera looks at the scene and a desktop monitor shows the mixed view, like a window into the world.
  • Projection-based (spatial AR): a projector throws light onto the real surface, so the surface itself carries the virtual content. No display is worn.

Visualization techniques are the drawing choices: text labels pinned to objects, arrows for direction, ghosted or X-ray models, and full 3D models. Wireless displays help education: a tablet shows the overlay while the camera stands on a desk, so students pass it around. See 6.2 for the optics and 6.3 for marker-less tracking.

System architecture: from light to pose to pixels

Every AR system follows the same loop, in this order.

  1. The real world produces light and motion.
  2. Cameras and an IMU (inertial measurement unit: accelerometer and gyroscope) measure the scene and the head.
  3. The tracker estimates the pose.
  4. Registration maps world points into camera coordinates.
  5. The renderer sets its virtual camera to the same pose and draws the virtual scene.
  6. The combiner or display shows real and virtual light together.
  7. The user sees the overlay, moves, and the loop repeats.

X_cam = R X_world + t

X_world is a 3D point in world coordinates; R is a 3×3 rotation matrix; t is a 3-vector translation; X_cam is the same point in camera coordinates.

Inside the AR application, the scene is stored as a scene graph: a tree of transforms. The root is the world. One child is the tracked camera. Other children are virtual objects. Each node's transform places it in its parent. When the tracker updates the camera node, every virtual object keeps its world position and the view moves correctly.

Main challenges:

  • Latency: delay between motion and updated frame. Prediction hides some of it.
  • Registration error: jitter and drift move the overlay off its real anchor.
  • Limited field of view: optical combiners often cover a narrow angle.
  • Changing light: a virtual object must be shaded to match the room.
  • Occlusion: a real hand in front of a virtual object should hide it; virtual objects should also hide real ones.
  • Battery and heat: cameras, tracking and rendering drain a phone.
  • Privacy: an always-on camera in a home or clinic sees more than the app needs.

Worked example

Question. A head tracker updates the head angle every 50 ms. The user turns the head at 120° per second. (a) Find the angle turned during one update and the apparent slip of the overlay at a viewing distance of 2 m. (b) Find the tracker delay that keeps the slip at or below 5 mm at 2 m.

  1. Update interval T = 50 ms = 0.050 s.
  2. Angle turned in T: Δθ = 120°/s × 0.050 s = 6.0°.
  3. Slip at distance d = 2 m: s = d tan Δθ = 2 m × tan 6° = 2 × 0.10510 = 0.2102 m.
  4. Convert: 0.2102 m = 21.0 cm.
  5. For s = 5 mm = 0.005 m, tan θ = s/d = 0.005/2 = 0.0025.
  6. θ = arctan(0.0025) = 0.1432°.
  7. Allowed delay = θ / (120°/s) = 0.1432/120 = 0.001194 s.
  8. Convert: 0.001194 s = 1.19 ms.

Answer. The slip is 6.0° or 21.0 cm at 2 m with a 50 ms delay; keeping it under 5 mm needs a delay of about 1.19 ms, which is shorter than a normal tracker, so the pose must be predicted.

Exam traps

  • Saying AR is a camera picture with a sticker pasted on top. The sticker must be registered in 3D and updated in real time.
  • Treating AR and VR as opposites. AR is part of the reality–virtuality continuum, and MR includes both AR and augmented virtuality.
  • Claiming video see-through is not real AR. A camera between eye and world still gives a combined, registered, real-time view.
  • Believing a higher frame rate always fixes registration. Frame rate cuts latency but cannot fix a wrong pose; drift and jitter remain.
  • Mixing latency and registration error. Latency is a time delay; registration error is a spatial misalignment.

6.2 AR displays: optical and video see-through, projection

An AR display must show the real world and a computer-made object in one view, lined up in 3D and fast enough to follow the head. Three mechanisms do this: optical see-through (real and virtual light enter through a combiner), video see-through (a camera films the world and graphics are mixed into the picture), and projection (virtual light falls on the real surface). Headsets, phones and table-top rigs choose one. The choice fixes delay, brightness, field of view, occlusion, safety and cost, so it is the first hardware decision.

You need: 6.1 for the three AR requirements, and Unit 3 for head-mounted and stereoscopic displays.

Three routes to one view

Every AR display must put two light patterns on the retina: light from the real scene and light from the computer image. Only three mechanisms exist.

Send both as light. The computer image is collimated by the display optics and reflected into the eye by a combiner, while real light passes straight through that same combiner. This is optical see-through, worn on the head.

Send both as pixels. A camera captures the real scene, software draws the virtual object into that picture, and a screen shows the single mixed image. This is video see-through. A phone held up at a sofa is the commonest daily example.

Send the virtual light to the object. A projector paints the image onto the real surface. This is projection AR; the user wears nothing.

A fourth arrangement, monitor-based AR, puts the mixed picture on a desktop screen. Nobody looks through it, so it is not registered to one person's eye; it suits design review, surgical planning and classroom teaching.

Some syllabi call the first two "optical and video see-through displays".

Optical see-through: real light through a combiner

The combiner. A half-silvered mirror, also called a beam splitter, sits at 45° in front of the eye, with the microdisplay to the side. Light from the microdisplay reflects off the mirror into the eye, while room light passes straight through. Both reach the retina along the same line of sight.

The waveguide. A thin flat glass plate carries the image by total internal reflection. Light enters at the edge, bounces inside the glass, and a grating on the surface pushes it out towards the pupil. The plate looks like ordinary spectacle glass.

Real light is never turned into a picture, so the real view has no camera delay, resolution limit or noise. Four costs remain.

  • Brightness: the combiner transmits only part of the real light and reflects only part of the virtual light, so both images arrive dimmer than they left the source.
  • Fixed focus: the virtual image is focused at a fixed distance, often infinity or about 2 m, whatever distance the object claims to be at.
  • Eye offset: the eye sits about 15–25 mm behind the optics (the eye relief) and slightly below the optical axis, so the virtual image is drawn from a point that is not the eye centre, and registration must be calibrated per user, including interpupillary distance.
  • No occlusion: in a plain combiner design a virtual object cannot hide a real one, because real light keeps arriving through the combiner.

Video see-through: one picture from a camera

Two small cameras on the front of the headset capture the scene. The renderer draws the virtual object into each frame using the pose from the tracker (see 6.3), and two screens show the result, one per eye. The eye sees only the screens, so real and virtual parts are formed by one pipeline.

That single pipeline gives video see-through its power. The camera frame can be held back until the render is ready, so both parts carry the same delay and stay aligned; otherwise a fast head turn makes the virtual object slide off the real one. Depth from the two cameras, or from SLAM, lets the renderer cut away hidden parts, so a real cup in front of a virtual machine hides it correctly.

The price is paid in four places. The cameras sit a few centimetres from the eyes, so the camera view is not the eye view (parallax, worst for near objects). The camera and render add delay in series. The world looks like a photograph, limited by screen brightness and resolution. And if the camera or power fails, the wearer is blind.

Phone AR is the same method at hand scale: the rear camera films the room, the screen shows the mix, and your hand supplies the window.

In any head-worn fixed-focus display, including both see-through types, the eyes turn inward (vergence) to meet at the distance of the virtual object, while the optics focus that image at a fixed distance (accommodation). The brain receives two conflicting depth cues, which brings eye strain and double images after some minutes. It is the main reason optical designs are hard. Projection AR does not suffer from it because the image is focused on the real surface.

Projection AR, and choosing between them

Spatial projection AR uses a projector fixed in the room and calibrated to a surface: a workbench, a wall, a machine casing. The image lands on the object, and every worker sees it without holding a device. Handheld and body-worn projection carries the projector in the hand or on the shoulder, so the surface is chosen at the moment; some syllabi list this under mobile projection interfaces.

Projection fails in ways a headset does not. The user's hand, arm or body stops the beam. The surface must be pale and matte, because a dark or shiny object reflects light away. Strong room light washes the image out. Calibration must map projector pixels onto the real surface.

Worked example

Question. A video see-through headset takes 33 ms to capture a frame, 20 ms to compute pose, and 15 ms to display the composited frame. An optical see-through unit needs 20 ms of tracking and display delay. The head turns at 60 degrees per second. Find the misregistration angle and the apparent shift at 1.5 m for each path, and the ratio.

  1. Video total delay = 33 + 20 + 15 = 68 ms = 0.068 s.
  2. Angle = 60 deg/s × 0.068 s = 4.08°.
  3. Shift at 1.5 m = 1.5 × tan(4.08°) = 1.5 × 0.0713 = 0.107 m, about 10.7 cm.
  4. Optical total delay = 20 ms = 0.020 s.
  5. Angle = 60 deg/s × 0.020 s = 1.20°.
  6. Shift at 1.5 m = 1.5 × tan(1.20°) = 1.5 × 0.02095 = 0.0314 m, about 3.1 cm.
  7. Ratio = 68 ms ÷ 20 ms = 3.4.

Answer. The video path misregisters by 4.08° and shifts the object 10.7 cm at 1.5 m; the optical path misregisters by 1.20° and shifts it 3.1 cm, so the video path is 3.4 times worse until the camera frame is delayed to match the render.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the two see-through types: optical uses a combiner and no camera; video uses a camera and a screen.
  • Saying video see-through is simply better. It can draw occlusion and match latency, but adds camera delay, camera–eye parallax, and blindness if the camera fails.
  • Forgetting matched-latency compositing works by delaying the camera frame to the render time. It raises total delay but removes relative slip.
  • Confusing projection shadow, where the user's body blocks the beam, with occlusion, where a real object correctly hides a virtual one.
  • Unit slips: turn rate in degrees per second × delay in seconds gives degrees; multiply the metre shift by 100 before quoting centimetres.

6.3 AR tracking: marker-based, markerless and SLAM

Registration means drawing each virtual object where the real camera sees it. The camera pose has six degrees of freedom: three position values and three orientation values. A tracker must recover that pose in every frame. Three families trade how much world knowledge they assume: a printed marker, natural image features, or SLAM, which builds a map while moving. You meet them in phone AR, museum guides, factory maintenance and surgery overlays.

You need: 6.1 for registration, Unit 2 for trackers and sensors, and Unit 4 for transformation matrices and yaw, pitch, roll.

Camera pose from a printed marker

A fiducial marker is a printed pattern: a black square with a white border and an inner code. The border gives strong edges; the code gives an ID so several markers can coexist.

  1. Convert the frame to grayscale and threshold it; dark marker regions become blobs.
  2. Find contours and fit the four corners of each square candidate.
  3. Warp the square to a front view and check the border and inner code.
  4. Compute the homography H, a 3×3 mapping from the marker plane to the image plane.
  5. Decompose H with camera intrinsics into pose R, t: rotation and translation.
  6. Filter the pose over frames to reduce jitter.

Z = f × W ÷ w

Z is distance in m; f is focal length in pixels; W is marker side in m; w is its width in the image in pixels.

Pose means six degrees of freedom: three position values (x, y, z) and three orientation values (yaw, pitch, roll). Marker size and focal length set the usable range: larger W or longer f gives more pixels w, so pose noise falls. A small, distant marker gives few pixels and noisy depth.

Markerless tracking: features and known models

Markerless tracking does not need a printed target. It uses natural corners and blobs. Detectors such as FAST and ORB find keypoints; descriptors turn the patch around each keypoint into a short code. Matching compares descriptors between frames or against a model. RANSAC (random sample consensus) fits a model from a small random sample, counts inliers, and repeats, so wrong matches are rejected. For a flat surface, a homography is enough. For a known 3D object, Perspective-n-Point (PnP) finds pose from 2D image points matched to 3D model points. Model-based tracking can also follow known edges and contours.

SLAM: map and pose together

SLAM (simultaneous localisation and mapping) estimates camera pose and a feature map together. The front-end tracks features frame to frame. The map stores 3D feature positions. The back-end optimises poses and map points to reduce error. Loop closure recognises a revisited place and corrects accumulated drift. Relocalisation recovers pose after tracking loss. Visual-inertial odometry fuses camera and IMU, which gives scale and short-term robustness. A single camera has scale ambiguity: the map is correct only up to an unknown factor. Drift grows with distance; anchors, such as a known marker or map point, reduce it. Keep the pipeline and ideas, not the optimisation mathematics.

Worked example

Question. An 80 mm square marker appears 160 pixels wide in a camera with focal length 800 pixels. Find its distance. If one corner is located to ±1 pixel, find the depth error. Repeat when the marker appears 320 pixels wide.

  1. Convert W = 80 mm = 0.08 m.
  2. Depth: Z = f × W ÷ w = 800 × 0.08 ÷ 160 = 0.4 m.
  3. Relative width error: 1 ÷ 160 = 0.00625.
  4. Depth error: 0.4 × 0.00625 = 0.0025 m = 2.5 mm.
  5. At w = 320 pixels: Z = 800 × 0.08 ÷ 320 = 0.2 m.
  6. Relative error: 1 ÷ 320 = 0.003125; depth error: 0.2 × 0.003125 = 0.000625 m = 0.625 mm.

Answer. The marker is at 0.4 m with depth error 2.5 mm; at 0.2 m the error is 0.625 mm.

Exam traps

  • SLAM from one camera does not give an absolute metric pose; scale is unknown unless fused with IMU or anchored.
  • Marker pose needs camera calibration: focal length and lens distortion. Clean detection alone is not enough.
  • RANSAC rejects wrong feature matches; it does not smooth jitter. That is the temporal filter.
  • Homography is for a plane; a full 3D object needs PnP or model-based pose.
  • Depth from apparent size uses physical marker size W; if W is wrong, Z scales by the same factor.

6.4 AR tools: Vuforia, ARCore, ARKit and AR Toolkit

Every AR app must answer the same question many times a second: where is the camera, and where should the virtual object sit? An AR toolkit answers it in software. You declare a target, such as a printed pattern or a photo, or an anchor on a detected plane, and the toolkit returns a six-number pose every frame. Your app only draws. Toolkits differ in what they can find and which phones they run on, so the choice is a design decision.

You need: 6.1 for the three requirements of AR, 6.3 for markers and SLAM, and Unit 5 for game engines and scene graphs.

One call: target in, pose out

A pose is six numbers: three for position in metres and three for rotation. The toolkit's contract is to take a camera frame and return the camera's pose relative to something real. Your code places the virtual object at that pose. Tracking becomes a library call, not your own geometry.

Keep two words apart. A target is something recognised in the image: a printed pattern, a photo, a machine part. An anchor is a point in the toolkit's internal map that you attach content to, so it stays still while tracking refines. Targets are found; anchors are remembered.

ARToolKit is the ancestor of marker tracking: an open-source C library that finds square black-bordered patterns printed on paper. It needs a camera calibration file first: focal length in pixels, principal point, and lens distortion. Wrong calibration gives a wrong pose, and the virtual cube slides off the marker.

  1. Load the calibration file for the phone camera.
  2. Threshold each frame to find dark square contours.
  3. Match the square's inner image against stored pattern templates.
  4. Fit a homography from the pattern corners to the image.
  5. Decompose it with the calibration to get the camera pose.

Vuforia: many kinds of target

Vuforia is a commercial SDK with a free tier. It runs on Android, iOS and some smart glasses, and it is valued for the number of target types it accepts.

  • Image target: a flat poster or photo, tracked by natural features.
  • Multi-target: a box built from six image targets.
  • Cylinder target: a can or bottle; the curved surface is unrolled.
  • Model target: a 3D CAD model, found from any side.
  • Ground plane: a flat surface found with no printed marker.
  • VuMark: a custom graphic that also carries data.

Targets sit in a database. A device database is compiled into the app: fast, offline, small. A cloud database is queried over the network: thousands of targets, but each recognition waits for a round trip. The Unity extension wraps the native SDK, so a target becomes a GameObject whose transform is the pose.

ARCore and ARKit, and the pipeline around them

ARCore (Android) and ARKit (iOS and iPadOS) are platform engines, not target libraries first. They fuse camera and inertial measurement unit data: visual-inertial odometry. The IMU gives fast rotation, the camera corrects drift, so the pose survives a few dark frames.

  • Motion tracking: a VIO pose at 30 or 60 frames per second.
  • Planes: horizontal and vertical surfaces in metres, fitted to feature points.
  • Light estimation: ambient intensity and colour for matching shading.
  • Depth: a per-pixel map, from motion on ARCore (plus time-of-flight on some phones) and from the LiDAR scanner on supported ARKit devices, used to hide content behind furniture.
  • Face tracking and people occlusion on ARKit's cameras.
  • Geospatial placement: content fixed to a street address outdoors.

Neither SDK draws. Unity or Unreal renders and calls the SDK once per frame. Models come from Blender, Maya or 3ds Max as FBX or glTF with baked textures; you import, light, and replace the normal camera with the SDK's AR camera. The chain is model, engine, SDK, anchor, phone build.

Worked example

Question. A Unity AR app must hold 60 frames per second on a mid-range phone. The developer sets a frame budget: tracking 6.0 ms, rendering 8.0 ms, and UI plus app logic 1.5 ms. (a) What is the frame budget in milliseconds? (b) What is the sum of the three costs, and the headroom? (c) In a dark corridor the tracker's cost rises to 9.0 ms. What is the new total, the new frame rate, and what render cost would restore 60 frames per second?

  1. Frame budget = 1000 ÷ 60 = 16.67 ms per frame.
  2. Original total = 6.0 + 8.0 + 1.5 = 15.5 ms.
  3. Headroom = 16.67 − 15.5 = 1.17 ms, so the frame fits.
  4. Dark corridor total = 9.0 + 8.0 + 1.5 = 18.5 ms.
  5. New frame rate = 1000 ÷ 18.5 = 54.05 frames per second, below target.
  6. Allowed render cost = 16.67 − 9.0 − 1.5 = 6.17 ms, so rendering must fall by 8.0 − 6.17 = 1.83 ms.

Answer. The budget is 16.67 ms; the original total is 15.5 ms with 1.17 ms headroom; in the dark corridor the total is 18.5 ms giving 54.05 frames per second, and rendering must drop to 6.17 ms to restore 60 frames per second.

Exam traps

  • Vuforia, ARCore and ARKit are not three versions of one product. Vuforia is a target SDK for many devices; ARCore and ARKit are platform engines tied to Android and iOS hardware.
  • ARCore is not a renderer. Unity or Unreal draws the frame; the SDK only supplies the pose.
  • A marker is a physical pattern in the world; an anchor is a software point in the toolkit's map. Calling an anchor a printed image loses marks.
  • Vuforia's device database is offline and small; the cloud database is large and needs a network round trip. Do not swap them.
  • Camera calibration is not optional in ARToolKit. Without it the pose has the wrong scale and the object drifts.

Understand Why does ARCore use the inertial measurement unit as well as the camera? 3 marks

The IMU measures rotation quickly and cheaply between camera frames, while the camera corrects the drift that the IMU accumulates. Together they give a pose that stays stable through motion blur and brief featureless frames.

6.5 Mixed reality interaction and navigation techniques

Mixed reality has no keyboard or mouse. You look, pinch, speak, or press a controller button, and the system must guess your intent and answer in a frame or two. This chapter covers the input set, the four interaction tasks, near and far interaction, gaze-and-commit, gesture design, feedback, surface anchoring, navigation and wayfinding. It ends with the measures used to test an interaction. The same loop appears in a shop-floor repair guide, a surgical rehearsal, and a phone AR game.

You need: Unit 5 for manipulation, travel, wayfinding and system control; Unit 2 for gesture interfaces and hand tracking; and 6.2 for the display.

Inputs and the four tasks

A 6-DoF controller reports six numbers per frame: position (x, y, z) and orientation (yaw, pitch, roll). A hand tracker reports joint poses, the position and orientation of each finger joint. A pinch is thumb tip meeting index tip; a grab is a closed hand around a virtual object. Eye gaze gives a ray from the eye. Voice gives a command word. A mapped table or wall can act as a control surface: tap it, and a menu anchors there.

  • Selection: choose an object or command. Aim with a hand ray or gaze; confirm.
  • Manipulation: move, rotate, or scale an object. Grab it, then move the hand.
  • Travel: move the viewpoint. This is navigation, not object motion.
  • System control: menus, mode changes, save, undo.

Near interaction is within arm's reach: touch or grab a hologram. It is precise and uses real surfaces. Far interaction uses a ray from the hand or eye to a distant object. It gives reach, but aiming error grows with distance and hand tremor. Use near for assembly and far for room-scale menus.

The four tasks are separate. In a repair task, selection picks the bolt, manipulation turns it, travel moves you to the panel, and system control opens the checklist. A single pinch may do selection and manipulation, so the system must know the current mode.

Gaze, gestures and feedback

Gaze alone is noisy: a glance may be accidental. In gaze-and-commit the user looks to aim, then pinches or says 'select' to fire. The commit removes ambiguity. Dwell is a fallback: hold gaze for about 800 ms. Too short causes false clicks; too long feels slow.

  • Use few gestures, perhaps four to six.
  • Make them easy to hold and visually distinct; pinch and grab must not blur.
  • Avoid long holds; they tire the hand and raise recognition errors.
  • Keep the elbow low and the gestures short; raised-arm holds cause gorilla arm.

Every action needs feedback: a hover highlight before selection, a sound on commit, a haptic pulse on the controller, and a visible state change. At 60 frames/s one frame is 16.7 ms. A confirmation after several frames feels like a miss.

To place content, cast a ray to a mapped plane, show a preview, then anchor. The anchor stores a pose relative to the real surface, so the hologram stays put when the user walks. Add an undo gesture.

Multimodal commands pair two channels, such as gaze plus voice or pinch plus voice. Two channels cut errors because a false gaze or a misheard word alone is rejected. The chance that both fail in the same way is smaller. Keep the pair natural and fast.

Navigation, wayfinding and evaluation

Navigation, or travel, moves the viewpoint. Walking is natural but limited by the room. Teleport points to a floor spot and jumps, which avoids sickness. World-in-miniature lets the user move a small avatar on a model. Portals are doorways to another space. Floor arrows guide steps without a jump.

Wayfinding is knowing where to go, separate from travel. Support it with landmarks, a route preview, a minimap, and turn-by-turn arrows on the floor. In a hospital AR guide, a line on the floor plus a minimap beats a compass.

Measure task time in seconds, error rate in percent, selection accuracy in percent, comfort rating, and workload score from a standard questionnaire such as NASA-TLX. Selection accuracy is correct selections divided by attempts. Compare near against far, or gaze against controller, with enough trials. Report the mean and the spread.

Worked example

Question. A gesture recogniser is tested with two gestures, pinch and grab, 50 trials each. Of the 50 pinch trials, 46 are called pinch, 3 are called grab and 1 is missed. Of the 50 grab trials, 45 are called grab, 4 are called pinch and 1 is missed. Find accuracy, and the precision and recall of pinch. State what each mistake means for the user.

  1. 1. Total trials = 50 pinch + 50 grab = 100 trials.
  2. 2. Correct calls = 46 pinch correct + 45 grab correct = 91. The errors are 3 pinch-to-grab, 1 missed pinch, 4 grab-to-pinch and 1 missed grab, for 9 errors.
  3. 3. Accuracy = 91/100 × 100 = 91%.
  4. 4. Pinch precision = true pinch / all called pinch = 46/(46 + 4) × 100 = 92%.
  5. 5. Pinch recall = true pinch / all pinch trials = 46/50 × 100 = 92%.
  6. 6. The 4 grab trials called pinch cost precision; the 1 missed pinch plus 3 pinch trials called grab cost recall. Both give a wrong action, so raise the pinch confidence threshold and add a hover highlight before commit.

Answer. Accuracy is 91%, pinch precision is 92%, and pinch recall is 92%.

Exam traps

  • Travel moves the viewpoint, wayfinding is knowing where to go, manipulation moves the object. Do not mix them.
  • Treating gaze as a click: a glance is noisy; use commit or dwell with a visible hover.
  • Designing many similar gestures or long raised-arm holds; recognisers confuse them and the arm tires (gorilla arm).
  • Copying fixed desktop menus into a head-worn view; place spatial menus within reach or on mapped surfaces.
  • Late feedback: a confirmation after a few frames feels like a miss; keep it within one or two frames.

6.6 Applications in medicine, education, entertainment and industry

AR pays for itself where information belongs to a place or an object: a patient on the table, a page in a book, a machine on the line. The system tracks that place, draws a label or model over it, and shows the result on a display the worker or student already has. The same four questions apply everywhere: what is tracked, what is drawn, which display is used, and how the result was evaluated. This chapter walks through medicine, education, entertainment and industry with those questions fixed in view.

You need: 6.1 for the three AR requirements, 6.2 for display types, 6.3 for tracking families, 6.4 for toolkit calls, and 6.5 for interaction.

Medicine: register to the patient

Surgical navigation is the clearest case. A pre-operative CT or MRI scan is segmented into tumour, vessels and bone. The system registers that scan to the patient on the table. Registration uses fiducial markers stuck on the skin, or a tracked surface probe, or a camera that matches the patient surface. The tracked object is the patient or a marker fixed to the patient; the camera pose is found every frame. The system draws the segmented structures and the planned cut plane over the real patient.

The display can be an optical see-through headset, a video see-through tablet, or a microscope with a combiner. The device is chosen for the task: a headset keeps the surgeon's hands free, a tablet is easier to share with a nurse. Evaluation uses target registration error in millimetres, task time and the number of corrective moves. A small error sounds good but may still be unsafe near a nerve.

Vein finding is projection AR. A near-infrared camera sees veins under the skin; a projector draws the vein map back onto the skin. Nothing is worn. Rehabilitation games track the hand or a marker and draw targets; the patient reaches for them. Anatomy teaching puts a marker on a plastic model and draws labels and cut-away views. Remote mentoring shares the operating field camera; an expert draws an arrow on the remote frame and the local surgeon sees it on a tablet.

  • What is tracked: patient marker, surface, or instrument.
  • What is drawn: tumour, vessels, cut plane, vein map, target.
  • Display: headset, tablet, projector, microscope.
  • Evaluation: registration error, time, safety, clinical score.

Education: page targets and safe practice

An AR book prints a page target. The phone camera finds the page, the app draws a 3D molecule or engine part standing on the page, and the student walks around it. The target is a printed image; the display is a phone or tablet. Chemistry and anatomy use the same pattern: a marker on a model, a label over the real object, a cut-away view that a flat diagram cannot show. Museums and historic sites use a marker, GPS or a visual map; the app draws a rebuilt wall or a person from the period over the ruin.

Laboratory and safety training uses AR to rehearse a procedure before touching the real equipment. A marker on a machine draws the next step, the correct valve, or the safe zone. The student can fail without damage. Evaluation is a checklist score, not a feeling of engagement.

Wireless displays appear in classroom AR: the teacher's tablet casts the camera view and the overlay to a projector. This removes the cable, but adds latency and battery load. The class sees the same target, yet the teacher must keep the tablet pointed at the page.

Evidence for learning gains is real but small. Most studies use one class, a pre-test and a post-test. A difference in normalised gain is a signal. To claim a learning gain you need random assignment, the same teacher for both groups, and marking done without knowing the group. Without those, a keen AR group can beat printed notes for reasons unrelated to AR.

Entertainment: track the street, the face, the field

Location-based games track GPS, the phone camera and inertial sensors. The game draws characters or clues on the street in front of you. The display is the phone. Evaluation is retention, play time and safety reports, because players walk while looking at a screen.

Camera filters track the face with a landmark model. The app draws a mask, glasses or a beard that follows the head. The display is the phone screen. The engineering target is frame rate and low jitter; a filter that slips by a few pixels looks broken. Live sports graphics track camera calibration and the field lines. The broadcaster draws a first-down line, a player name tag or a speed readout over the real field. The display is the television feed. Evaluation is alignment error and how often the graphic hides the play.

Stage and theme-park effects use projection mapping. A projector is calibrated to a physical set; the system draws light and characters onto walls, water or props. The tracked object is the set geometry. The audience sees the result without a device. Distraction and safety matter: a graphic that covers a stair or a moving actor is a hazard.

Industry: guidance, expert help, inspection

Assembly guidance tracks a marker on the bench or a plane found by SLAM. It draws the next bolt, the torque value, or the cable route over the real part. The display is a see-through headset or a tablet. Evaluation is error rate, assembly time and rework. Remote expert help shares the worker's camera view; the expert draws an arrow or circles a valve. The worker sees the mark on the same view. Evaluation is time to fix and the number of callbacks.

Warehouse picking shows the bin location and the count in a headset. The display is see-through, so the picker still sees the shelf. Inspection draws a checklist over the machine and marks the points to photograph. Training uses the same overlay on a real line, so the line does not stop for a dummy. Retail uses a phone to place a chair in a room; defence uses a helmet display for navigation and threat labels; navigation uses a phone camera to draw arrows on the road.

  • Costs: headsets, tablets, projectors, and spare batteries.
  • Content authoring: 3D models, step logic, and safety review take time.
  • Hygiene: shared headsets need cleaning between users.
  • Comfort: weight, heat and vergence strain limit long shifts.
  • Distraction: a label over a moving machine can hide a hazard.

Worked example

Question. Sixty students are split into 30 using an AR anatomy app and 30 using printed notes. Both groups score a pre-test mean of 40 out of 100. The post-test mean is 78 for the AR group and 66 for the control group. Find the normalised gain for each group and compare the raw improvement.

  1. Pre-test mean is 40 out of 100. Maximum possible gain is 100 - 40 = 60 points.
  2. AR raw improvement is 78 - 40 = 38 points.
  3. Control raw improvement is 66 - 40 = 26 points.
  4. AR normalised gain is 38 / 60 = 0.6333..., which rounds to 0.63.
  5. Control normalised gain is 26 / 60 = 0.4333..., which rounds to 0.43.
  6. Compare: 38 points against 26 points raw; 0.63 against 0.43 normalised.

Answer. The AR group has a normalised gain of 0.63 and the control group 0.43; the raw improvement is 38 points against 26 points, but with 30 students per group this is a signal, not proof.

Exam traps

  • Answering with a list of app names. The marks are for what is tracked, what is drawn, which display type is used, and how the system was evaluated.
  • Treating normalised gain as the raw post-minus-pre difference. The denominator is 100 minus the pre-test score.
  • Assuming a marker can always be placed. In a sterile operating field a marker may be impossible, so surface tracking or SLAM may be needed.
  • Claiming a learning gain from a small pre/post study without random assignment, the same teacher and blinded marking.
  • Forgetting the display. A vein finder that projects onto the skin is projection AR, not a headset AR system.

The overlay must sit on the actual tumour, vessel or cut plane. Without a patient-to-scan registration, the drawn model moves with the camera but not with the patient, so it is useless and unsafe.

6.7 Metaverse and future trends in XR

The metaverse is a persistent, shared, synchronous 3D layer over the internet. Persistent means the world keeps its state when you leave. Synchronous means all users see the same moment. Shared means one coordinate space for everyone. XR trends are the hardware and network steps that make this layer wearable and always on: lighter glasses, on-device tracking, edge rendering, and open standards. You meet it in shared design reviews, remote machine support, and training rooms where a phone or headset shows the same anchored object to two people.

You need: 6.1 for the AR requirements, 6.5 for interaction, and Unit 1 for the XR definitions.

What counts as a metaverse

A metaverse is not any VR app. A single-player game has a world, but no shared state with other users. A video call has shared state, but no 3D space. The term needs these properties together.

  • Persistence: the world and its objects keep their state when a user disconnects. A meeting room keeps the notes on the table.
  • Synchrony: every user sees the same event at the same time, within the network delay.
  • Shared space: one coordinate system and one set of anchors, so a virtual chair is in the same place for every device.
  • Avatars and identity: each user has a body or pointer and a stable identity.
  • Economy: optional but common; users can own, buy, or sell items.
  • Interoperability: the same identity, content, and avatar can move between devices and apps.

These properties explain why the metaverse is a layer, not a product. A factory in Pune runs a digital twin of a line; a digital twin is a live model of a real asset. An engineer in Chennai puts an anchor on valve V-12. A second engineer in Delhi opens the same anchor and sees the same label. The world persists after both log out. That is a small metaverse service.

The stack from devices to experience

The working stack reads from the bottom up. Devices and hardware are phones, headsets, and glasses. Network and cloud carry data and store the shared map. Standards and identity let an app run on different devices and keep one account. 3D engine and rendering draws frames; a cloud renderer may do this on a server. Platform and world keeps accounts and shared state. Experience and content is the game, meeting, or training task.

The AR cloud is the shared map in the network layer. A device builds a local map with SLAM (see 6.3). It sends selected feature points to the cloud. The cloud merges them into a world map. A spatial anchor is a named pose in that map: a position and an orientation. Device A creates an anchor at a real valve and stores the anchor ID. Device B localises itself in the same map, reads the ID, and places its label at the same pose. Both devices now agree on the same real point.

WebXR is a browser API for XR. A page asks for an XR session; the browser talks to the device. A link opens AR or VR without an app install. This lowers the cost of a shared session, but browser and device must support the same features.

Cloud rendering and the latency budget

A headset must answer head motion fast. Motion-to-photon latency is the time from a head movement to the display changing. Above about 20 ms, many users feel lag and nausea. The budget splits into rendering, network, and display scan.

t<sub>total</sub> = t<sub>render</sub> + t<sub>network</sub> + t<sub>scan</sub>

ttotal is motion-to-photon latency in ms; trender is frame draw time; tnetwork is round-trip network time; tscan is display scan-out time.

On-device rendering keeps tnetwork small. Cloud rendering moves the GPU to a server, which helps thin glasses, but the network term grows. Light in fibre travels at about 2 × 10⁸ m per second, so distance sets a hard lower bound. A server 1000 km away adds 10 ms of round-trip light time before switching or queueing. Edge computing puts the server in a nearby data centre, often in the same city. 5G and Wi-Fi 6/7 reduce the radio part but cannot beat the speed of light.

Hardware, standards and a realistic view

Glasses trends: waveguides are thin glass plates that guide light from a small projector to the eye, so the display can look like ordinary glasses. Micro-LED and laser scanning are small, bright light engines. Pancake optics fold the light path with polarising films, so a headset becomes thinner. Colour passthrough uses cameras to show the real world in colour on a mixed-reality headset. Eye tracking and hand tracking remove controllers for some tasks, and SLAM on the device removes external cameras.

Standards: OpenXR is an application interface; one app can talk to many headsets and runtimes. glTF and USDZ are 3D content formats; a model in either can be shown by many viewers. These standards reduce porting work, but they do not make every world interoperable.

Challenges are real. Always-on cameras raise privacy problems. Shared spaces need safety rules and moderation. Headsets cost money, and cloud rendering uses energy. Accessibility is incomplete: small text, weight, and battery life exclude users. The digital divide matters when a school cannot buy headsets. Near term, phone AR and light glasses give guidance, translation, and remote help; they will not replace every screen.

Worked example

Question. A cloud-rendered XR system allows 20 ms from head motion to the photon leaving the display. Rendering takes 8 ms and the display scan takes 4 ms. (a) What network round-trip budget is left? (b) Light in fibre travels at about 2 × 10⁸ m per second. What is the maximum one-way distance to the server? (c) A server is 1000 km away. Find the round-trip light time and the total latency. Does it meet the budget?

  1. 1. Network budget = 20 − 8 − 4 = 8 ms.
  2. 2. Round-trip distance in 8 ms: d = c t = (2 × 10⁸ m/s)(8 × 10⁻³ s) = 1.6 × 10⁶ m = 1600 km.
  3. 3. One-way distance = 1600 ÷ 2 = 800 km.
  4. 4. For 1000 km, one-way distance = 10⁶ m; round trip = 2 × 10⁶ m.
  5. 5. Round-trip time = d ÷ c = (2 × 10⁶ m) ÷ (2 × 10⁸ m/s) = 0.01 s = 10 ms.
  6. 6. Total latency = 10 + 8 + 4 = 22 ms. This is 2 ms over the 20 ms budget.

Answer. The network budget is 8 ms, the maximum one-way distance is 800 km, and the 1000 km server gives 22 ms total, so it misses the budget by 2 ms.

Exam traps

  • Calling any VR game 'the metaverse'. A metaverse needs persistence and shared state across users and devices; a single-player game has neither.
  • Using one-way distance as the network time. The latency budget usually uses a round trip; double the one-way light time.
  • Confusing a spatial anchor with a marker. A marker is a printed image tracked by the camera; a spatial anchor is a pose in a shared map.
  • Giving a fixed date for full AR glasses. Near-term products are phone AR and light guidance glasses, not every promised feature.
  • Mixing OpenXR and glTF. OpenXR is the app-to-runtime interface; glTF is a 3D content format.

Takeaways

  1. AR has three hard requirements: combine real and virtual, register in 3D, and run live.
  2. Optical see-through sends real light and virtual light to the eye, while video see-through sends both as pixels to a screen.
  3. Projection AR sends virtual light directly onto a real surface and is useful for hands-free shared views.
  4. Tracking is chosen by how much known structure the scene gives: a printed marker, natural features, or a SLAM map.
  5. An AR toolkit hides tracking behind target or anchor calls that return a pose in every frame.
  6. Mixed reality interaction is multimodal and needs confirmation plus feedback inside a frame or two.
  7. AR earns its cost where information belongs to a place or object: a patient, a machine, a page, or a street.
  8. The metaverse needs persistence, shared synchrony, low motion-to-photon latency, wearable optics and portable APIs such as OpenXR.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to compare display routes and tracking families, explain toolkit pose flow, and connect interaction limits to real applications and XR trends.

Remember and understand

Understand Define augmented reality. Explain the three requirements of an AR system and contrast AR with VR using one example each. 13 marks

Clear definition of AR as live real world plus registered virtual objects; Three requirements: combine real and virtual, register in 3D, run in real time; AR vs VR: real world visible in AR, fully synthetic world in VR; Examples such as phone AR furniture placement vs VR training room.

Understand Explain optical see-through and video see-through AR displays. Compare what reaches the eye, brightness, latency and occlusion, and state where projection AR fits. 13 marks

OST: real light direct, virtual light added by combiner or waveguide; VST: camera captures real scene, virtual pixels mixed on screen; Compare brightness, camera latency, field of view and occlusion handling; Projection AR: virtual light sent onto real surface, no headset.

Understand Explain marker-based, markerless and SLAM tracking. State the assumption each makes and give one use case for each. 13 marks

Marker-based: printed fiducial with known geometry; Markerless: natural features or known images; SLAM: map built while camera moves; pose estimated in that map; Use cases: manual page, product box, room-scale walkthrough.

Understand Describe the pose flow in an AR toolkit: target or anchor, device or cloud database, camera calibration and VIO, and the pose returned each frame. 13 marks

App declares a target image or a real-world anchor; Device database works offline; cloud database needs network; Camera calibration supplies intrinsics for pose computation; VIO fuses camera and IMU; toolkit returns pose every frame.

Apply

Apply Design an AR human anatomy learning app for a phone. Choose the display route, tracking method, toolkit features and interaction, and justify each choice. 13 marks

Handheld video see-through is practical on a phone; Marker or image target for a printed body chart; markerless or SLAM for room-scale model; Toolkit needs target detection, anchors and camera calibration; Interaction: tap, drag, pinch, voice labels; feedback for selection.

Apply Design an AR maintenance guide for a factory machine. Explain how to anchor labels to parts, handle tracking loss, and give feedback for hand and voice commands. 13 marks

Use markerless or SLAM tracking with anchors on machine parts; Store target data on device for weak network areas; Tracking loss: freeze label, show warning, re-localise; Hand and voice commands need confirmation and visible feedback.

Apply Plan an AR walkthrough of a historic place. Compare handheld video see-through with spatial projection, choose marker or SLAM tracking, and describe navigation aids. 13 marks

Handheld VST gives private view; projection gives shared public view; Marker for fixed plaques; SLAM for open outdoor movement; Navigation: map, arrows, audio cues and wayfinding anchors; Evaluate sunlight, surface brightness and tracking drift.

Apply A team wants AR surgery simulation. Select display, tracking and latency targets; say why VR alone is insufficient and where AR helps. 13 marks

OST or VST choice with reasons for surgery training; Tracking must be stable and registered to a phantom or patient model; Low motion-to-photon latency and clear occlusion needs; AR keeps real patient or phantom visible; VR gives full synthetic control.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Analyse the main causes of bad registration in AR: drift, jitter and occlusion. Suggest display and tracking fixes for each. 13 marks

Drift: pose error accumulates; fix with SLAM relocalisation or markers; Jitter: noisy pose; fix with filtering, VIO and stable anchors; Occlusion: virtual objects draw over real ones; fix with depth sensing or VST masking; Display choice affects how easily errors are seen.

Evaluate Evaluate gaze, hand gesture, voice and controller input for mixed reality. Explain confirmation and feedback needs for each. 13 marks

Gaze is fast for pointing but needs dwell or confirmation; Hand gestures are natural but tiring and error-prone; Voice is good for commands but needs privacy and error handling; Controllers are precise; all modes need visible or audio feedback.

Evaluate Evaluate the metaverse as a persistent shared 3D layer. Discuss AR cloud, motion-to-photon latency, wearable optics and OpenXR portability. 13 marks

Persistence and shared synchrony define the metaverse layer; AR cloud stores shared real-world maps; Latency and comfort limit always-on wearables; OpenXR reduces device fragmentation; hardware trends make XR lighter.

Evaluate AR is worth its cost where information belongs to a place or object. Evaluate this claim using medicine, education, entertainment and industry examples. 13 marks

Criterion: information tied to a patient, page, stage or machine; Medicine: overlay on patient or model; education: overlay on book or lab kit; Entertainment: place-based games and try-before-buy; Industry: machine repair and remote guidance; compare where VR is better.

Analyze Compare Vuforia, ARCore and ARKit as AR toolkits: what they track, device bases, databases and calibration. Choose one for a classroom AR app and justify. 13 marks

Vuforia: strong image and object targets, device and cloud databases; ARCore and ARKit: phone platform tracking with anchors and VIO; Compare device coverage, calibration needs and offline support; Classroom choice depends on phones available and target type.

Glossary

Anchor
A real-world point or plane to which virtual content is attached by a toolkit.
AR cloud
A shared cloud map of the real world used to place persistent multi-user AR content.
Augmented reality
A live real-world view with computer-made objects registered to real places.
Camera calibration file
A file of camera intrinsics and distortion values used to compute correct pose.
Cloud database
A remote store of AR targets that the app queries over a network.
Combiner
A semi-transparent optical part that overlays virtual light on the direct real view.
Device database
A local store of AR targets that works without a network connection.
Fiducial marker
A printed pattern with known size and shape used for camera pose estimation.
Gaze
Eye or head direction used as an input signal in mixed reality.
Hand gesture
A hand pose or movement recognised as a command.
Markerless tracking
Tracking from natural features, images or objects instead of a printed marker.
Metaverse
A persistent, shared, synchronous 3D layer over the internet.
Micro-LED
A display technology using tiny light-emitting diodes per pixel for bright, low-power screens.
Mixed reality
A system where real and virtual objects coexist and interact in one registered 3D space.
Motion-to-photon latency
The delay between user motion and the matching updated image on the display.
Multimodal interaction
Interaction that combines inputs such as gaze, voice, gesture and controllers.
OpenXR
An open standard API that lets XR software run on different devices and runtimes.
Optical see-through
A display route where real light reaches the eye directly and virtual light is added.
Pancake optics
A folded optical design using polarisation to make VR and MR headsets thinner.
Registration
Alignment of virtual content with the real world in three dimensions.
SLAM
A method that estimates camera pose while building a map of the unknown environment.
Spatial projection AR
AR made by projecting virtual light onto a real surface rather than wearing a display.
Target
An image or object that an AR toolkit is trained to recognise and track.
Video see-through
A display route where cameras capture the real scene and a screen shows real and virtual pixels together.
Visual-inertial odometry
Camera plus IMU motion estimation used to track a device smoothly.
Waveguide
A thin light-guide that carries virtual display light to the eye in AR glasses.
