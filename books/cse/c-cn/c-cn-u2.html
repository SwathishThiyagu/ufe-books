2.1 Analog and digital signals

The physical layer must turn real world phenomena into bits that computers can process. An analog waveform varies continuously in time and amplitude. A digital signal is a sequence of discrete values taken at specific instants. Converting analog to digital (and back) lets us store voice, video and sensor data on binary media and transmit it over cables or wireless links. This conversion is the basis of PCM telephony, audio sampling for music apps, and sensor interfaces in IoT devices.

You need: basic concepts of signals and systems from Transmission impairments.

Analog and digital signals

An analog signal is a function x(t) that exists for every real time t and can take any value within a range. It is also called a continuous‑time signal. A digital signal is a sequence x[n] defined only at integer indices n and each element belongs to a finite set of symbols. The symbols are often represented by bits (e.g., {0,1}) but may include multiple amplitude levels. A discrete‑time signal is a signal sampled at integer time indices. When the sampled amplitude values are represented by binary symbols, the signal is also a digital signal.

  • Continuous‑time ↔ analog
  • Discrete‑time ↔ sampled signal
  • Pulse‑code modulation (PCM) is the standard method that creates a digital representation of an analog waveform.

When the sampled amplitudes are encoded as binary symbols, the sampled signal becomes a digital signal.

Only a few universities teach the mathematics of this conversion in depth, but the idea appears in every modern communication system.

Sampling and Nyquist rate

Sampling measures the amplitude of an analog signal at regular intervals. If the interval is T seconds, the sampling frequency f_s = 1/T is expressed in samples per second (Hz).

  1. Choose a sampling frequency f_s that is at least twice the highest frequency component f_max of the analog signal.
  2. Take a sample every 1/f_s seconds.

f_s ≥ 2 f_max

f_s is the sampling rate (Hz); f_max is the maximum frequency present in the analog signal (Hz).

If f_s is lower than 2 f_max the reconstructed signal suffers aliasing – high‑frequency components appear as lower frequencies, corrupting the data.

Quantisation and PCM

Quantisation maps each sampled amplitude to the nearest level of a finite set. With B bits per sample there are L = 2^B quantisation levels. The step size Δ (volts) = (V_max – V_min)/L, where V_max and V_min are the allowed voltage range. If V_max and V_min are the extreme quantisation levels, the interval count is L‑1, so Δ = (V_max – V_min)/(L‑1).

L = 2^B

L is the number of quantisation levels; B is the number of bits per sample.

Pulse‑code modulation (PCM) concatenates the binary codes of successive samples into a continuous bit stream. The data rate R (bits/s) is R = f_s × B.

R = f_s × B

R is the PCM bit rate (bits s⁻¹); f_s is the sampling frequency (samples s⁻¹); B is bits per sample.

Python program.

f_s = 8000  # Hz
B = 8        # bits per sample
R = f_s * B
print(R)

Output

64000

Worked example

Question. A 1 kHz sinusoid is sampled at 8 kHz and quantised with 8 bits. How many bits are transmitted per second?

C program.

#include <stdio.h>
int main(){
    int f_s = 8000; // Hz
    int B = 8;      // bits per sample
    int R = f_s * B;
    printf("%d\n", R);
    return 0;
}
  1. Identify the sampling frequency: f_s = 8 kHz = 8000 samples s⁻¹.
  2. Identify bits per sample: B = 8 bits.
  3. Compute the PCM bit rate: R = f_s × B = 8000 × 8 = 64000 bits s⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Using a sampling rate lower than twice the highest frequency produces aliasing, which the examiner may ask you to identify.
  • Confusing the number of quantisation levels L with the number of bits B; remember L = 2^B.
  • Multiplying the sampling frequency by the number of quantisation levels instead of bits per sample gives a result 256 times too large for an 8‑bit case.
  • Assuming PCM adds extra framing bits; the basic PCM rate is only f_s × B.

2.2 Transmission impairments: attenuation, distortion and noise

Signals become weaker and distorted while travelling through any medium. The loss of strength is called attenuation, the change in waveform shape is distortion, and unwanted random variations are noise. These impairments limit how far data can be sent without repeaters or equalisation, and they appear in copper cables, fiber, and wireless links alike.

You need: basic analog and digital signal concepts from Analog and digital signals.

Attenuation and its measurement

Attenuation is the reduction of signal power as it propagates. It is expressed in decibels (dB) per kilometre for long links, or simply dB for a fixed length. The formula is Attenuation(dB) = 10·log₁₀(P₁/P₂) where P₁ is the transmitted power and P₂ is the received power. A positive dB value means loss.

P₂(dBm) = P₁(dBm) – A(dB)

P₂ is received power in dBm; P₁ is transmitted power in dBm; A is attenuation in dB.

  • Copper twisted pair: 0.5–2 dB/km at 100 MHz.
  • Single‑mode fibre: ≤0.35 dB/km at 1550 nm.
  • Free‑space radio: varies with distance and frequency.

Attenuation forces designers to place repeaters or amplifiers after a certain distance, otherwise the signal falls below the receiver sensitivity.

Distortion and noise

Distortion occurs when the channel does not pass all frequency components equally. Band‑limiting removes high‑frequency edges, turning a square wave into a rounded shape. This effect is called inter‑symbol interference (ISI) because the tail of one symbol overlaps the next.

Noise is random energy added by the channel. Thermal noise, also called Johnson‑Nyquist noise, has power N = k·T·B where k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K, T is temperature in kelvin, and B is bandwidth in hertz. Shot noise appears in semiconductor devices and is proportional to the square root of the current.

SNR(dB) = Pₛ(dBm) – Pₙ(dBm)

Pₛ is signal power at the receiver; Pₙ is noise floor power.

A high SNR means the receiver can distinguish the intended signal from random fluctuations. Low SNR leads to bit errors and requires stronger error‑correction codes.

Worked example

Question. A 1 V peak sinusoidal signal is transmitted over a cable that introduces 2 dB attenuation. The receiver has a 50 Ω input impedance. The noise floor of the channel is –90 dBm. Compute the received signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) in dB.

  1. Convert the 1 V peak to RMS: V₍rms₎ = 1 V ÷ √2 = 0.707 V.
  2. Compute transmitted power: P₁ = V₍rms₎² ÷ R = (0.707)² ÷ 50 Ω = 0.01 W = 10 mW = 10 dBm.
  3. Apply attenuation: P₂(dBm) = 10 dBm – 2 dB = 8 dBm.
  4. SNR(dB) = P₂(dBm) – (noise floor) = 8 dBm – (‑90 dBm) = 98 dB.

Answer. The received SNR is 98 dB.

Exam traps

  • Treating the 2 dB attenuation as a linear factor (0.2) instead of using the dB subtraction rule.
  • Using peak voltage directly for power calculation; the RMS value must be used.
  • Ignoring the sign of the noise floor; -90 dBm is much lower than the signal.
  • Adding linear power values and then converting to dB, rather than subtracting dB values.
  • Confusing SNR (signal‑to‑noise ratio) with SINR (signal‑interference‑plus‑noise ratio).

2.3 Data rate limits: Nyquist and Shannon capacity

The maximum data rate of a communication link is limited by the channel bandwidth and the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR). Nyquist gave the limit for a noiseless channel using discrete signal levels. Shannon extended the result to noisy channels and expressed the ultimate capacity in bits per second. These formulas tell designers how fast a link can theoretically run, and they are used when choosing line coding, modulation, or when budgeting for new fiber or wireless links.

You need: basic signal concepts from Analog and digital signals and the idea of noise from Transmission impairments.

Nyquist formula for a noiseless channel

Nyquist considered a channel that does not add any noise. The only limitation is the bandwidth B in hertz and the number of distinct signal levels M that can be transmitted.

C = 2B·log₂M

C is the maximum data rate in bits per second; B is the channel bandwidth in hertz; M is the number of discrete signal levels; log₂ is the base‑2 logarithm.

If the channel can carry two levels (binary), then log₂M = 1 and C = 2B. This is why a binary baseband link can transmit at most twice its bandwidth.

Increasing M raises the data rate without expanding the bandwidth, but it also reduces the distance between signal levels, making the link more vulnerable to noise.

  • B is measured in hertz (Hz).
  • M must be an integer ≥ 2.
  • Nyquist assumes ideal, noise‑free transmission.

Shannon capacity for a noisy channel

Claude Shannon added the effect of additive white Gaussian noise. The result is the channel capacity C in bits per second.

C = B·log₂(1+SNR)

B is the bandwidth in hertz; SNR is the signal‑to‑noise power ratio (linear, not in dB); log₂ is the base‑2 logarithm.

SNR is often given in decibels: SNR(dB) = 10·log₁₀(SNR). To use the formula, convert dB to a linear ratio by SNR = 10^(SNR(dB)/10).

Shannon’s bound is an upper limit. Real systems achieve a fraction of this capacity depending on coding and modulation efficiency.

  • Higher bandwidth directly raises capacity.
  • Higher SNR also raises capacity, but with diminishing returns.
  • The formula applies to any channel that can be modelled as band‑limited with Gaussian noise.

Comparing Nyquist and Shannon limits

Nyquist gives a hard ceiling when noise is absent. In practice, every link has noise, so Shannon’s bound is the realistic ceiling.

If the SNR is very large, the term log₂(1+SNR) approaches log₂SNR, and the Shannon limit becomes close to the Nyquist limit with a large M.

Designers first compute the Shannon capacity to know the theoretical maximum. Then they pick a modulation scheme that offers M levels such that the Nyquist rate is below the Shannon bound.

For a 3 MHz channel with an SNR of 30 dB, the Shannon capacity is about 30 Mbps, while the Nyquist binary limit would be 6 Mbps. This shows why using higher‑order modulation (M>2) is essential in modern cellular and fiber links.

  • Use Nyquist to decide how many signal levels a given bandwidth can support.
  • Use Shannon to assess whether the channel SNR allows the desired data rate.

Worked example

Question. A channel has a bandwidth of 3 MHz and an SNR of 30 dB. What is the theoretical maximum data rate according to Shannon’s capacity formula?

C program.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <math.h>
int main(){
    double B = 3e6; // Hz
    double snr_db = 30.0;
    double snr_lin = pow(10.0, snr_db/10.0);
    double C = B * log2(1.0 + snr_lin);
    printf("%0.1f Mbps\n", C/1e6);
    return 0;
}
  1. Convert SNR from dB to linear: SNR = 10^(30/10) = 10^3 = 1000.
  2. Insert B = 3 MHz = 3×10⁶ Hz into the formula C = B·log₂(1+SNR).
  3. Compute 1+SNR = 1 001.
  4. Find log₂(1 001) ≈ log₁₀(1 001)/log₁₀(2) ≈ 3.0004/0.3010 ≈ 9.97 bits per symbol.
  5. Multiply by bandwidth: C = 3×10⁶ × 9.97 ≈ 2.99×10⁷ bits/s ≈ 29.9 Mbps.
  6. Round to a convenient figure: about 30 Mbps.

Exam traps

  • Putting the SNR value in dB directly into the log₂ term of Shannon’s formula.
  • Using the Nyquist formula with M = 1, which would give log₂1 = 0 and a zero data rate.
  • Confusing bandwidth B (Hz) with data rate C (bits/s) and forgetting to keep units consistent.
  • Assuming the Nyquist limit applies when noise is present, leading to an over‑optimistic rate.
  • Rounding the linear SNR before taking the logarithm, which reduces accuracy.

2.4 Guided and unguided transmission media

Physical links are classified as guided (wired) or unguided (wireless). Guided media keep the signal inside a physical conductor, giving predictable loss and high bandwidth. Unguided media let the signal travel through air or space, offering mobility but higher attenuation. Choosing the right type decides whether a LAN in a Chennai apartment, a campus backbone, or a satellite link for remote villages works reliably.

You need: Analog and digital signals, Transmission impairments and Data rate limits.

Guided transmission media

Guided media use a physical path to confine the electromagnetic wave. The three most common types are twisted‑pair copper, coaxial cable and optical fibre.

  • Twisted‑pair: two insulated copper wires twisted together. Category 5e (Cat5e) supports up to 100 m at 1 Gbps. Attenuation ≈ 22 dB/100 m at 100 MHz.
  • Coaxial: inner conductor, dielectric, metallic shield, outer jacket. Used for cable TV and older Ethernet (10BASE‑2). Bandwidth up to 10 GHz, attenuation ≈ 6 dB/100 m at 1 GHz.
  • Optical fibre: glass core guiding light by total internal reflection. Single‑mode (core ≈ 9 µm) carries long distances with low loss (≈ 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm). Multimode (core ≈ 50 µm) is cheaper for short runs (≤ 2 km) but higher modal dispersion.

A = 10 log₁₀(P₀/P₁)

A is attenuation in dB; P₀ is transmitted power; P₁ is received power.

Attenuation expressed in dB adds linearly with distance for copper cables, but fibre loss is so small that repeaters are needed only after many kilometres.

Unguided transmission media

Unguided media transmit through free space. The main categories are radio, microwave and satellite links; infrared is used for short‑range line‑of‑sight links such as TV remotes.

  • Radio: frequencies from a few kHz up to 3 GHz. Used for Wi‑Fi (2.4 GHz, 5 GHz) and cellular (LTE, 5G). Bandwidth can reach several hundred MHz, but path loss follows the free‑space model.
  • Microwave: 3 GHz–30 GHz, highly directional parabolic antennas. Ideal for point‑to‑point links between towers, e.g., a 10 km backhaul with 1 Gbps capacity.
  • Satellite: 4 GHz–40 GHz bands (C, Ku, Ka). Provides coverage over thousands of kilometres. Latency is high (≈ 500 ms) because of the round‑trip to geostationary orbit.
  • Infrared: 300 THz–400 THz, line‑of‑sight only, typical range < 5 m. Used in remote controls and some indoor data links.

FSPL(d) = 20 log₁₀(d) + 20 log₁₀(f) + 32.44

FSPL is free‑space path loss in dB; d is distance in km; f is frequency in MHz.

FSPL shows why higher frequencies need more power or higher‑gain antennas for the same range.

Choosing guided vs. unguided media

Guided media are preferred when a stable, high‑capacity link is required and a physical path can be laid, such as campus LANs or fiber‑to‑the‑home. Unguided media are chosen for mobility, rapid deployment, or when wiring is impractical, for example, connecting a mobile handset to a 4G base station.

  1. Identify the required data rate.
  2. Check the maximum distance without repeaters.
  3. Consider installation cost and environmental constraints.
  4. Select guided media when a physical path can be laid and the required distance is within the practical reach of the chosen guided medium (e.g., up to 100 m for copper, up to many kilometres for fibre). Otherwise consider unguided media.

2.5 Line coding for digital transmission

Line coding converts binary bits into voltage or current levels that travel on a physical link. The mapping lets the receiver recover the original bits. Different codes balance power, bandwidth, and error detection. They are used in Ethernet, DSL, and serial links such as UARTs. Choosing a code affects the required channel bandwidth and the presence of a DC component.

You need: basic signal concepts from Analog and digital signals and the idea of bandwidth from Data rate limits.

What is line coding?

A line code is a rule that maps each binary digit to a physical level on the transmission medium.

The mapping can be unipolar (0 → 0 V, 1 → +V) or polar (0 → –V, 1 → +V).

Unipolar codes are simple but create a DC component that can saturate transformers.

Polar codes eliminate the DC offset by using both positive and negative voltages.

  • Unipolar: only one polarity of voltage.
  • Polar: two polarities, symmetric around zero.

Common binary line codes

NRZ (Non‑Return‑to‑Zero) keeps the voltage constant for the whole bit interval.

NRZI (NRZ‑Invert) changes the level only when a ‘1’ is transmitted.

Manchester coding embeds a transition in the middle of each bit: 0 → low‑to‑high, 1 → high‑to‑low.

Differential Manchester also forces a transition at the bit centre but the direction of the first half‑bit encodes the data.

Bipolar (AMI – Alternate Mark Inversion) uses zero voltage for ‘0’ and alternating +V/–V for successive ‘1’s.

4B/5B first maps 4 data bits to a 5‑bit code that guarantees at least one transition per 5 bits, then transmits the result with NRZ.

  • NRZ: simple, low bandwidth, possible long runs of same level.
  • Manchester: self‑clocking, doubles bandwidth, no DC.
  • AMI: no DC, limited error detection.
  • 4B/5B: ensures transition density, used in Fast Ethernet.

Effect on bandwidth and DC content

The minimum bandwidth of a line code is roughly half the signalling rate for NRZ.

Manchester forces two level changes per bit, so its bandwidth is about the full bit rate.

Codes that avoid long runs of identical levels (Manchester, Differential Manchester) have no DC component, and 4B/5B limits the DC component by ensuring at least one transition in every 5‑bit block, reducing but not completely eliminating DC bias.

A DC‑free signal can pass through transformer‑coupled links and AC‑coupled repeaters without distortion.

B_min = R / 2 for NRZ,   B_min = R for Manchester

B_min is the minimum required bandwidth (Hz); R is the bit rate (bits s⁻¹).

2.6 Analog transmission and digital modulation techniques

Modulation moves information from a low‑frequency baseband signal onto a higher‑frequency carrier by changing the carrier’s amplitude, frequency or phase. This lets the signal travel long distances over radio, cable or optical links and share the spectrum with other services. Analog modulation (AM, DSB‑SC, FM, PM) is used for broadcast radio and TV. Digital modulation (ASK, FSK, PSK, QPSK) is the basis of Wi‑Fi, cellular and satellite links.

You need: Analog and digital signals, Transmission impairments, and Data rate limits.

Analog modulation techniques

The carrier is a sinusoid with frequency f_c measured in hertz (Hz). Baseband data m(t) varies slowly compared with f_c. In amplitude modulation (AM) the carrier amplitude follows m(t). In double‑sideband suppressed‑carrier (DSB‑SC) the carrier itself is removed, leaving only the two sidebands. Frequency modulation (FM) varies the carrier frequency proportionally to m(t). Phase modulation (PM) varies the carrier phase proportionally to m(t).

  • AM bandwidth = 2 × (highest baseband frequency) (Single‑sideband AM uses only one sideband, so its bandwidth equals the highest baseband frequency).
  • DSB‑SC bandwidth = same as AM but power saved.
  • FM bandwidth ≈ 2(Δf + f_m) where Δf is frequency deviation and f_m is max baseband frequency.
  • PM bandwidth similar to FM because frequency deviation is proportional to the derivative of the message.

B = 2W

B is the required bandwidth in hertz; W is the highest baseband frequency in hertz.

  1. Choose carrier frequency f_c well above the baseband highest frequency.
  2. Select modulation type based on bandwidth and power constraints.
  3. Generate the modulated waveform using a mixer (for AM/DSB‑SC) or a voltage‑controlled oscillator (for FM/PM).
  4. Transmit the waveform through the channel.

Digital modulation (shift keying)

Digital data are binary symbols 0 and 1. Shift keying changes a carrier property for each symbol. Amplitude‑Shift Keying (ASK) uses two amplitudes. Frequency‑Shift Keying (FSK) uses two frequencies f_0 and f_1. Phase‑Shift Keying (PSK) uses two phases, usually 0° and 180°. Quadrature‑Phase‑Shift Keying (QPSK) uses four phases, encoding two bits per symbol.

  • ASK bandwidth ≈ B (the bit rate) for rectangular NRZ pulses; with pulse shaping the bandwidth may be slightly larger, e.g., ≈ 1.2 B for a raised‑cosine filter with roll‑off 0.2.
  • FSK bandwidth ≈ 2(Δf + 1/(2T)) where T is the bit duration (so 1/(2T) = B/2). In terms of bit rate B, this becomes B_FSK ≈ 2Δf + B.
  • BPSK bandwidth ≈ B (the bit rate).
  • QPSK bandwidth ≈ B/2 because two bits are sent per symbol.

C = B log₂(1+SNR)

C is channel capacity in bits per second; B is channel bandwidth in hertz; SNR is signal‑to‑noise ratio (linear).

  1. Map each bit (or group of bits) to a carrier property.
  2. Generate the corresponding waveform.
  3. Transmit over the channel.
  4. At the receiver, detect the property and map back to bits.

Carrier, baseband, and bandwidth relationships

The carrier frequency f_c does not determine the required channel bandwidth. Bandwidth depends on how fast the baseband changes and on the modulation rule. Over‑modulating (changing amplitude beyond 100 % in AM) creates distortion and extra sidebands, wasting spectrum.

  • Baseband: original low‑frequency signal that carries the information.
  • Carrier: high‑frequency sinusoid that transports the baseband.
  • Bandwidth: range of frequencies occupied after modulation.

The required channel bandwidth depends on the modulation rule. For AM, B_total = 2·B_baseband; for FM, B_total ≈ 2(Δf + B_baseband); for digital schemes, B_total is given by the respective bandwidth formulas.

Worked example

Question. A system uses Binary Phase‑Shift Keying (BPSK) to transmit data at 10 kbps over a channel of 1 MHz bandwidth. Using the Shannon capacity formula, compute the minimum required signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) in linear scale and in decibels.

C program.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <math.h>
int main(){
    double C = 10000.0; // bits per second
    double B = 1000000.0; // hertz
    double log_val = C / B; // 0.01
    double SNR = pow(2.0, log_val) - 1.0;
    double SNR_trunc = floor(SNR * 1e5) / 1e5; // truncate to 5 decimal places
    double SNR_dB = 10.0 * log10(SNR_trunc);
    printf("SNR (linear) = %.5f\n", SNR_trunc);
    printf("SNR (dB) = %.1f\n", SNR_dB);
    return 0;
}
  1. Write Shannon formula: C = B·log₂(1+SNR).
  2. Insert C = 10 000 bps and B = 1 000 000 Hz: 10 000 = 1 000 000·log₂(1+SNR).
  3. Solve for log₂(1+SNR): log₂(1+SNR) = 0.01.
  4. Convert to linear: 1+SNR = 2^{0.01}. Compute 2^{0.01} ≈ 1.00695555.
  5. Subtract 1: SNR ≈ 0.00695555.
  6. Truncate to five decimal places as required: SNR_linear = 0.00695.
  7. Convert to decibels: SNR_dB = 10·log₁₀(0.00695) ≈ -21.58 dB, round to one decimal → -21.6 dB.

2.7 Multiplexing: FDM, TDM and WDM

Multiplexing lets many users share one physical link. It saves copper, fibre or spectrum and reduces cost. The technique is used in telephone trunks, cable TV, mobile back‑haul and long‑haul optical networks.

You need: Analog and digital signals, Transmission impairments, and Data rate limits.

Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM)

FDM splits the available spectrum into separate frequency slots. Each slot carries one channel. The slots are placed side by side.

A guard band separates adjacent slots. The guard band prevents interference caused by imperfect filters.

B_total = n × B_chan + (n‑1) × B_guard

B_total is the required bandwidth; n is the number of channels; B_chan is the bandwidth of one channel; B_guard is the width of one guard band.

FDM is common in radio broadcasting, cable TV and old telephone systems where each voice call occupies a 4 kHz band.

  • Simple hardware: band‑pass filters
  • Continuous transmission, no time slots
  • Limited by total spectrum available

Time Division Multiplexing (TDM)

TDM shares the link in the time dimension. The link is divided into repeating frames.

In synchronous TDM each frame has a fixed number of slots. Every source gets one slot in every frame.

T_slot = T_frame ÷ N

T_slot is the duration of one time slot; T_frame is the duration of a whole frame; N is the number of slots per frame.

Statistical TDM allocates slots only when a source has data. It improves efficiency when traffic is bursty.

  1. Collect bits from each active source.
  2. Place them in the next free slot of the current frame.
  3. Transmit the frame.
  4. Repeat for the next frame.

Wavelength Division Multiplexing (WDM)

WDM works like FDM but on an optical fibre. Each channel uses a different light wavelength (colour).

A multiplexer (called a MUX) combines the light from several lasers. A demultiplexer (DEMUX) separates them at the far end.

  • Dense WDM (DWDM) packs channels 0.8 nm apart.
  • Coarse WDM (CWDM) uses 20 nm spacing.
  • Enables terabit per second links for backbone traffic.

WDM is the backbone of modern internet traffic. It carries data for banks, video streaming and cloud services across continents.

Worked example

Question. Three voice channels each require 4 kHz of bandwidth. The system uses FDM with a guard band of 0.5 kHz between adjacent channels. What total bandwidth must the shared link provide?

C program.

#include <stdio.h>
int main(){
    int n = 3;
    double B_chan = 4.0; // kHz
    double B_guard = 0.5; // kHz
    double B_total = n * B_chan + (n-1) * B_guard;
    printf("%g\n", B_total);
    return 0;
}
  1. Identify the number of channels: n = 3.
  2. Identify the bandwidth per channel: B_chan = 4 kHz.
  3. Identify the guard band width: B_guard = 0.5 kHz.
  4. Compute total channel bandwidth: n × B_chan = 3 × 4 kHz = 12 kHz.
  5. Compute total guard bandwidth: (n‑1) × B_guard = 2 × 0.5 kHz = 1 kHz.
  6. Add the two amounts: B_total = 12 kHz + 1 kHz = 13 kHz.

2.8 Spread spectrum

Spread spectrum widens a narrow‑band signal over a much larger frequency band. The technique hides the signal, makes it tolerant to narrowband interference, and allows many users to share the same spectrum with low cross‑talk. It is used in Wi‑Fi, Bluetooth, GPS, and some cellular systems where security and robustness are required.

You need: basic signal concepts from Analog and digital signals and the idea of bandwidth from Transmission impairments.

Why spread spectrum?

A narrow‑band carrier can be jammed by a single interferer that occupies the same small slice of spectrum. By spreading the energy over a wide band, the power density of the signal becomes low, so a jammer would need to spread its power over the same wide band to be effective. This also gives the receiver processing gain, which improves the signal‑to‑noise ratio after despreading.

Security is another benefit. The spreading code looks random to anyone who does not know it, so an eavesdropper cannot recover the original bits without the correct code. Bluetooth devices use FHSS/AFH to reduce the risk of casual sniffing.

  • Resists narrowband interference
  • Provides processing gain
  • Enables multiple users (CDMA)
  • Adds a layer of security

Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum (DSSS)

In DSSS each data bit is multiplied by a fast binary sequence called the chip sequence. The chip rate (chips per second) is much higher than the data rate. If the data rate is Rₙ bits/s and the chip rate is R_c chips/s, the spreading factor is S = R_c / Rₙ. The product is a pseudo‑random waveform that occupies a bandwidth roughly equal to R_c.

  1. Generate a pseudo‑random binary sequence whose length per data bit equals the spreading factor.
  2. Map data bit ‘1’ to the chip sequence, data bit ‘0’ to the inverted sequence (inversion means multiplying each chip by –1).
  3. Transmit the resulting high‑rate chip stream.
  4. At the receiver, generate the same chip sequence using the shared seed.
  5. Multiply received chips with the local sequence to recover the original bits.

Processing gain (dB) = 10·log₁₀(R_c ÷ Rₙ)

R_c is chip rate in chips/s; Rₙ is data rate in bits/s.

A common spreading code is the Gold code, used in GPS L1 C/A. In a campus Wi‑Fi network, a 2 Mbps data stream may be spread with a 20 Mbps chip rate, giving a processing gain of 10 dB (10·log₁₀(20/2) = 10 dB).

Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS)

FHSS changes the carrier frequency at regular intervals called hops. The hop rate is the number of frequency changes per second. A pseudo‑random hopping pattern, known to both transmitter and receiver, determines which channel is used for each hop.

  • Hop set: the list of allowed carrier frequencies (e.g., 79 channels in the 2.4 GHz ISM band).
  • Hop duration: time spent on one frequency before moving to the next.
  • Processing gain ≈ 10·log₁₀(bandwidth of hop set ÷ occupied bandwidth per hop).

The term “occupied bandwidth per hop” means the channel bandwidth used for each hop, for example 1 MHz for Bluetooth.

Bluetooth Classic uses FHSS with 1 ms hop interval across 79 channels, each 1 MHz wide. The technique allows many devices to coexist because the probability of two devices colliding on the same channel at the same time is low.

In India, railway signalling sometimes employs FHSS for short‑range telemetry, because the hopping makes the link less vulnerable to narrowband interference from nearby electrical equipment.

Worked example

Question. A 1 Mbps BPSK data stream is spread using a chip sequence of 10 Mbps. Compute the processing gain in decibels.

  1. Data rate Rₙ = 1 Mbps.
  2. Chip rate R_c = 10 Mbps.
  3. Spreading factor S = R_c ÷ Rₙ = 10.
  4. Processing gain = 10·log₁₀(S) = 10·log₁₀(10).
  5. log₁₀(10) = 1, so gain = 10·1 = 10 dB.

Answer. The processing gain is 10 dB.

Exam traps

  • Treating the spreading factor as a reduction in net data rate; it only reduces the power spectral density, not the useful bit rate.
  • Using log₂ instead of log₁₀ in the processing‑gain formula, which yields a value 3 dB lower.
  • Confusing chip rate with occupied bandwidth; the bandwidth is roughly equal to the chip rate only for binary phase‑shift keying.
  • Assuming any pseudo‑random sequence provides security; weak sequences can be guessed and the signal recovered.

Apply For a TDM system with 8 users sharing a 1 Gbps link, calculate the slot duration for each user assuming equal bandwidth allocation. 9 marks

Total bit period = 1 / 1 Gbps = 1 ns; Slot per user = 8 ns (since 8 users); Explain that each user gets 125 Mbps effective rate.

Analyze Compare Frequency Division Multiplexing (FDM) and Time Division Multiplexing (TDM) for a satellite communication system. Discuss bandwidth efficiency, latency, and implementation complexity. 15 marks

Define FDM and TDM; Calculate bandwidth usage for given number of channels; Explain latency impact of TDM slot waiting vs. FDM propagation; Discuss hardware complexity: filters for FDM, timing circuits for TDM; Conclude which is preferable under typical satellite constraints.

Evaluate Assess the advantages and disadvantages of using spread‑spectrum techniques (e.g., DSSS) in a Wi‑Fi (802.11) network operating in a crowded urban environment. 14 marks

Explain DSSS principle and processing gain; List advantages: interference resistance, security, multipath mitigation; List disadvantages: lower spectral efficiency, higher power consumption; Relate to urban spectrum congestion and regulatory limits; Provide overall evaluation for Wi‑Fi deployment.

Analyze Explain how attenuation and dispersion affect signal integrity in an optical fiber link of 50 km. Suggest two mitigation techniques. 13 marks

Define attenuation (loss per km) and dispersion (pulse spreading); Show impact on eye diagram and BER; Mitigation 1: Use optical amplifiers (EDFA) to compensate loss; Mitigation 2: Employ dispersion‑shifted fiber or dispersion compensation modules.

Evaluate Discuss the trade‑offs between using NRZ and Manchester line coding in a high‑speed Ethernet (10 GbE) link. 14 marks

NRZ: lower bandwidth, no inherent clock, risk of baseline wander; Manchester: double bandwidth, built‑in clock, better synchronization; Impact on transceiver design, power consumption, and error performance; Conclusion on suitability for 10 GbE.

Glossary

Analog signal
Continuous waveform representing physical quantities
ASK
Amplitude Shift Keying, varies carrier amplitude
Attenuation
Reduction of signal amplitude with distance
Bandwidth
Range of frequencies a channel can carry
Baseline wander
Low‑frequency drift in signal level caused by long runs of identical bits
Bit rate
Number of bits transmitted per second
Chip rate
Rate of pseudo‑random code symbols in spread spectrum
Coaxial cable
Shielded cable with a central conductor, used for broadband
Digital signal
Discrete sequence of binary symbols
Dispersion
Pulse broadening due to different propagation speeds of light components
Distortion
Alteration of signal shape during transmission
DSSS
Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum, a common spread‑spectrum method
EDFA
Erbium‑Doped Fiber Amplifier, boosts optical signals
FDM
Frequency Division Multiplexing, separates channels by frequency
FSK
Frequency Shift Keying, varies carrier frequency
Guided media
Physical cables that confine signal propagation
Line coding
Technique to represent bits as voltage or current levels
Manchester coding
Encoding with a transition in each bit period
Noise
Random electrical fluctuations that interfere with the signal
NRZ
Non‑Return‑to‑Zero coding without a built‑in clock
Nyquist rate
Minimum sampling rate to avoid aliasing
Optical fiber
Guided medium that carries light pulses for high‑speed links
Processing gain
Ratio of chip rate to data rate, indicating spreading factor
PSK
Phase Shift Keying, varies carrier phase
Quantization
Mapping of sampled values to a finite set
Sampling
Periodic measurement of an analog signal
Shannon capacity
Maximum achievable data rate for a noisy channel
SNR
Signal‑to‑Noise Ratio, a measure of signal quality
Spread spectrum
Technique that spreads signal over wide bandwidth
TDM
Time Division Multiplexing, separates channels by time slots
Twisted pair
Two insulated wires twisted together, common in LANs
Unguided media
Wireless transmission through free space
WDM
Wavelength Division Multiplexing for optical fibers
