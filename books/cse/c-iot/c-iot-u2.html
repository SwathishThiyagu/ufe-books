2.1 Sensors and transducers: types and characteristics

A sensor responds to a physical quantity — heat, light, pressure, motion — and a transducer inside it converts that response into an electrical signal. That signal is usually weak, noisy and not linear, so a signal conditioner shapes it before an ADC reads it. An IoT device lives or dies by this chain: a badly chosen sensor sends confident but wrong data to the cloud. This chapter names the parts, classifies the sensor families, and gives the characteristics — range, accuracy, resolution, hysteresis, drift, time constant — that tell you how far the output can be trusted.

You need: Unit 1, for where a sensor sits inside an IoT device, and the device block picture in 2.3.

Sensor, transducer, signal conditioner

A sensor responds to a physical quantity. A transducer converts energy from one form to another — here from the physical form to electrical. A thermistor is both: it turns temperature into resistance. A signal conditioner makes the raw output usable by amplifying, filtering, linearising, and converting resistance or capacitance into a voltage or a 4–20 mA current. Without it, the ADC in 2.4 reads noise.

Three classifications matter.

  • Active or passive: an active sensor needs an external supply (a strain gauge in a bridge); a passive one generates its own signal (a piezoelectric crystal, a thermocouple).
  • Primary or secondary: a primary sensor reacts directly to the quantity (a mercury thermometer); a secondary sensor uses a primary to give an electrical output.
  • Analog or digital: analog is a continuous voltage; digital is pulses or a serial word, as in a DS18B20 temperature chip.

Mechatronics draws the device as modules: sensing, processing, actuation and drive, joined by signal flow in one direction and power flow in the other. The sensor starts the signal chain; the actuator (2.2) ends it.

Static characteristics

Static characteristics describe the sensor when the input is steady or changing slowly. They are what a datasheet quotes.

  • Range: the lowest and highest input it measures, say 0–100 °C, so the input span is xmax − xmin = 100 °C. The output span is ymax − ymin, for example 4–20 mA giving 16 mA.
  • Accuracy: closeness of the reading to the true value, quoted as ±0.5 °C or as a percentage of span.
  • Precision: how closely repeated readings agree with each other, not with the truth.
  • Resolution: the smallest input change that produces a detectable output change.
  • Linearity: the ideal output is a straight line; linearity error is the largest departure from it, as a percentage of span.
  • Hysteresis: the output at one input differs on the rising path and the falling path.
  • Repeatability: the spread of readings over repeated identical runs in the same direction.
  • Drift: slow change of output with time or ambient temperature when the input is fixed.
  • Dead band and threshold: an input band that gives no output change, and the smallest input that gives any output at all.
  • Loading effect: the measuring circuit draws current and pulls the reading down.

S = Δy / Δx

S is the sensitivity, in output units per input unit (e.g. mA/°C); Δy is the change in output; Δx is the change in input.

Dynamic characteristics

Static data never says how fast a sensor follows a change. Dynamic characteristics do.

  • Zero order: the output follows instantly, with no delay. A potentiometer is close to this.
  • First order: one energy-storing element, usually thermal mass, described by a time constant τ in seconds.
  • Second order: mass and spring, as in an accelerometer, described by the undamped natural frequency ωₙ in rad/s and the damping ratio ζ, which has no unit. ζ < 1 gives overshoot and ringing, ζ = 1 is critical damping (fastest settle without overshoot), and ζ > 1 is sluggish (overdamped).
  • Rise time: time to go from 10% to 90% of the final value. Settling time: time to stay inside a band, often ±2%, of the final value. Bandwidth: the highest frequency the sensor still follows at 70.7% (−3 dB) of its low-frequency output.

y(t) = y_final (1 − e^(−t/τ))

y(t) is the output at time t in seconds after a step; y_final is the steady value it settles to; τ is the time constant in seconds. At t = τ the output is 63.2% of y_final; at t = 5τ it is within 1%.

Circuit families

  • Resistive: a potentiometer for displacement; a strain gauge, where the gauge factor GF = (ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L) is about 2 for metal foil; an RTD of platinum, nearly linear, with a temperature coefficient of about 0.00385 Ω per Ω per °C (that is 0.385% per °C, so a 100 Ω Pt100 element changes by about 0.385 Ω per °C); a thermistor, large but non-linear.
  • Capacitive: C = εA/d, where ε is permittivity, A is plate area and d is the gap. Change A, d or ε to measure displacement, pressure or level.
  • Inductive: the LVDT slides a core between one primary and two secondary coils and gives an AC output proportional to displacement; an eddy-current sensor sees a metal target without touching it.
  • Resonant: a quartz crystal vibrates at a very stable frequency that shifts with temperature or pressure.
  • Optical: an LDR drops in resistance with light; a photodiode gives a current with a responsivity in A/W; a phototransistor gives a larger current. Vision, laser and fibre-optic sensing extend this — only a few universities go past the principle.
  • Solid state: a Hall sensor gives a voltage proportional to magnetic field; MEMS etches tiny beams and masses on silicon for accelerometers and gyroscopes.
  • Piezoelectric: charge under stress, used for vibration, knock and impact. Ultrasonic: send a pulse, time the echo, and distance d = v t / 2, with v the speed of sound in m/s and t the round-trip time in s.

Worked example

Question. A transmitter gives 4–20 mA for 0–100 °C. (a) Find the span and the sensitivity in mA/°C. (b) What temperature does 12.00 mA show? (c) At 60 °C the rising reading is 13.60 mA and the falling reading is 13.68 mA; find the hysteresis in mA and in °C.

  1. Output range: 4 mA at 0 °C and 20 mA at 100 °C.
  2. Span = 20 − 4 = 16 mA over an input span of 100 − 0 = 100 °C.
  3. Sensitivity S = 16 mA / 100 °C = 0.16 mA/°C.
  4. For 12.00 mA, the output above the live zero is 12.00 − 4 = 8.00 mA.
  5. Temperature = 8.00 / 0.16 = 50.0 °C.
  6. Hysteresis in mA = falling − rising = 13.68 − 13.60 = 0.08 mA.
  7. Hysteresis in °C = 0.08 mA / 0.16 mA/°C = 0.5 °C.

Answer. Span 16 mA, sensitivity 0.16 mA/°C, 12.00 mA corresponds to 50.0 °C, and the hysteresis is 0.08 mA = 0.5 °C.

Exam traps

  • Accuracy, precision and resolution are three different things. A reading can be precise (repeats well) and still inaccurate (far from truth), and a high sensitivity does not by itself improve resolution.
  • A transducer is the converter inside a measuring system. The instrument adds the conditioner, the ADC and the display; do not call the whole box a transducer.
  • Active and passive reverse meaning between books: in IoT texts an active sensor needs external power, while in instrumentation an active transducer generates its own signal. State which convention you use.
  • Hysteresis quoted in mA must be divided by the sensitivity to become °C. Mixing the two units is the commonest lost mark in this question.
  • A first-order sensor reaches 63.2% of the final value in one time constant — not 50%, and not 100%.
  • The 4 mA live zero sits at the bottom of the range; forgetting to subtract it shifts every temperature by 25 °C.

2.2 Actuators: types and uses

An actuator turns an electrical command into motion or force. A sensor listens to the world; an actuator changes it. The command may be a simple on or off, as with a relay, or a precise position, as with a motor. The motor type decides whether the motion is precise, fast or cheap. Actuators open water valves in a smart home, move robot arms, position printer heads and drive the wheels of a delivery robot. This chapter groups actuators by energy source and then explains the motors that most syllabi name.

You need 2.1 for the sensor side of the feedback loop.

Actuator, sensor and energy source

An actuator is a device that converts an input signal, usually electrical, into controlled motion or force. It is the output side of an IoT node. A sensor measures temperature, light or position and produces a signal; an actuator takes a signal and produces a physical change. In a feedback loop the sensor checks what the actuator did (see 2.1).

Group actuators by energy source. Electrical actuators use a motor, solenoid or heater. Pneumatic actuators use compressed air; hydraulic actuators use oil pressure. Thermal actuators use expansion, as in a bimetallic strip. Magnetic actuators use a coil and a magnet. Electrical actuators dominate IoT because they run from low voltage and are easy to switch.

A solenoid is a coil that pulls a plunger when current flows. It gives short linear motion, on or off. A relay is a solenoid that moves contacts: a small coil current controls a larger load circuit. Both are on-off actuators, not positioners. Pneumatic and hydraulic valves need sizing for flow and pressure; only a few universities examine that, so this chapter stays with electrical motors.

BLDC motor: electronic commutation

A brushless DC (BLDC) motor has a permanent-magnet rotor and fixed stator windings. There are no brushes. The stator current must change direction as the rotor turns, or the torque falls to zero. Hall sensors or a back-EMF estimate tell the driver the rotor position. The driver switches the three stator phases in sequence; this is electronic commutation.

Six-step drive turns two phases on at a time and gives six current states per electrical cycle. It is simple and common. Sinusoidal drive gives smoothly varying phase currents and produces less torque ripple and less noise. Either way the motor needs a driver with power switches, not just a DC voltage. A BLDC is efficient, has a high torque-to-weight ratio and is used in drones, fans, e-bikes and pumps.

f = p × n ÷ 120

f is the electrical frequency in hertz, p is the number of magnetic poles, and n is the rotor speed in rpm.

Stepper motor: steps and lost steps

A stepper motor moves in fixed angular steps. A variable-reluctance stepper has a soft-iron rotor with teeth and a wound stator. A hybrid stepper has a permanent-magnet rotor with two cups and teeth; it is the common 1.8° motor. The step angle is set by rotor teeth and stator phases.

θ_step = (360° ÷ N_r) ÷ s

θ_step is the full-step angle in degrees, N_r is the number of rotor teeth, and s is the number of steps per electrical cycle.

A 1.8° hybrid has 50 rotor teeth, a tooth pitch of 360/50 = 7.2°, and 4 steps per electrical cycle; 7.2/4 = 1.8°. Holding torque is the torque needed to turn the shaft while one phase is energised. Detent torque is the small torque from the permanent magnet with no current. Microstepping splits each full step into smaller current levels; 1/8 microstepping gives 0.225° per microstep for a 1.8° motor. A stepper is often run open loop: the controller counts pulses and assumes the shaft followed. If the load torque is too high or acceleration too fast, it loses steps without reporting an error. Use a servo or add an encoder when position must be guaranteed.

Servo motors and selection

A servo motor is a motor with a closed loop. A DC servo has a brushed or brushless DC motor plus an encoder. An AC servo uses a synchronous AC motor plus an encoder or resolver. The drive compares commanded position with measured position and adjusts current. It can hold position under load and produces torque in all four quadrants of the torque-speed plane: forward motoring, reverse motoring, forward braking and reverse braking.

Selection in words: torque at the needed speed, load inertia, duty cycle, accuracy, cost and controller. A stepper is cheap and holds position without feedback at low speed. A BLDC is efficient and fast but needs commutation. A servo is accurate and can correct errors but costs more. A relay is best for on-off loads such as a lamp or a valve. Driving these motors from a board is covered in Unit 4, Interfacing sensors and actuators with boards.

Worked example

Question. A hybrid stepper has a step angle of 1.8° and drives a lead screw of pitch 2 mm. (a) How many steps per revolution? (b) How much linear travel per step? (c) With 1/8 microstepping, what is the resolution in degrees and in micrometres?

  1. Full-step angle α = 1.8°.
  2. Steps per revolution N = 360° ÷ 1.8° = 200 steps/rev.
  3. Lead-screw pitch p = 2 mm per revolution.
  4. Linear travel per full step = p ÷ N = 2 mm ÷ 200 = 0.01 mm.
  5. Convert to micrometres: 0.01 mm × 1000 = 10 µm.
  6. Microstep angle = 1.8° ÷ 8 = 0.225°.
  7. Microsteps per revolution = 200 × 8 = 1600. Travel per microstep = 2000 µm ÷ 1600 = 1.25 µm.

Answer. 200 steps per revolution, 0.01 mm (10 µm) per full step, 0.225° per microstep, and 1.25 µm per microstep at 1/8 microstepping.

Exam traps

  • A stepper can lose steps with no error; a servo uses feedback and reports following error. Do not call an open-loop stepper a servo.
  • A 1.8° step angle is the full-step angle; microstepping divides it. It is not the resolution by itself.
  • A BLDC cannot run from a fixed DC voltage. The driver must commutate the phases.
  • Holding torque is with current on; detent torque is with current off. Do not swap them.
  • A relay coil and its contact are separate circuits; the coil is the low-power input, the contact switches the load.

2.3 Smart objects and sensor nodes

A smart object is a small package of five blocks: it senses, computes, communicates, carries its own power and knows its identity. Pack those blocks into one node and the object can report on itself and act on a command. A sensor node, also called a mote, is the hardware core of this idea: it supplies sensing, computing, communication and power. Add actuation and identity and the node becomes a full smart object. You meet smart objects in home meters, factory vibration tags, smart parking sensors and crop-field nodes. The power budget, not the radio peak, decides how long a battery lasts.

You need: 2.1 and 2.2 for the sensing and actuation blocks.

Five blocks of a smart object

Five blocks make a smart object: processing, sensing and actuation, communication, power, and identification. The processing unit runs local logic. The sensing unit measures the world; the actuation unit changes it. The communication unit sends data and receives commands. The power unit supplies every block. The identification unit gives the object a name or address.

Three terms look alike. A sensor node (mote) is a small board with sensor, microcontroller, radio and battery; it usually senses and forwards data. A smart object adds actuation and identity, so it can act as well as report. An IoT device is the wider product, often one or more smart objects plus a network service. A sensor is not a smart object; it is one block inside it.

The awareness–representation–interaction view: the object is aware of its state, represents it in data, and interacts with users or other objects. That is a useful design lens, but the five blocks are the engineering content.

Inside a sensor node

A sensor node has a sensing unit, a processing unit, a transceiver and a power unit. The sensing unit holds the sensor and an analog-to-digital converter (ADC). A 0–5 V signal into a 10-bit ADC gives 2¹⁰ = 1024 codes. The step is 5 V ÷ 1024 ≈ 4.9 mV, so smaller changes may not change the code.

The processing unit is a microcontroller with memory. It filters samples and decides when to wake the radio. The transceiver is often a 2.4 GHz low-power radio; it carries short packets. The power unit is a battery, a harvester or both. Some nodes add a location unit (GPS) or a mobility unit (motor, drone mount). The radio sets the peak current. The sleep current is set mainly by the microcontroller when it is not put into its deep-sleep mode, plus any leakage in the regulator and the sensor left powered.

Choosing the link and paying for it

Pick a link on nine criteria: range (distance), data rate (bits/s), duty cycle (fraction of time on), latency (delay), reliability (packets delivered), energy per useful bit (joules per delivered bit), topology (star, tree, mesh), cost (per node and gateway), and licence band (2.4 GHz is licence-free in most countries; cellular bands need an operator).

I<sub>avg</sub> = I<sub>awake</sub> × D + I<sub>sleep</sub> × (1 − D)

Iavg is the average current in mA; D is the awake fraction (0 to 1); Iawake and Isleep are the currents in mA during the awake and sleep states.

A node at 30 mA for 1 ms every 10 s has D = 0.0001 and adds only 3 µA to the average. A sleep leak of 50 µA costs more. Sleep current and duty cycle decide the battery, not the transmit peak. The worked example does the full sum.

Node software cycle

The node program is a loop. Each pass does five jobs.

  1. Sense: power the sensor and wait its warm-up time.
  2. Convert: read the ADC and turn the code into a physical value.
  3. Compute: filter, average or compare with a threshold.
  4. Transmit: send only the result or a short packet.
  5. Sleep: turn off sensor and radio, put the microcontroller into its lowest power mode, wake on a timer or interrupt.

Order matters. Sending every raw sample wastes energy and clogs the network. A mean of 100 samples sent once is often better. Sleep is a block, not an afterthought; forget it and the average current becomes the awake value. For actuation, add a command step before sleep: receive a setpoint, drive the actuator, then sleep.

Worked example

Question. A sensor node runs on a 1000 mAh battery of which 80% is usable. It draws 8 mA for 200 ms every 60 s and 3 µA otherwise. Find the average current and the battery life in days and years.

  1. Usable charge = 1000 mAh × 0.80 = 800 mAh.
  2. Awake fraction D = 200 ms / 60 s = 0.2 s / 60 s = 0.003333.
  3. Awake current = 8 mA × 0.003333 = 0.02667 mA.
  4. Sleep current = 3 µA = 0.003 mA. Sleep fraction = 1 − 0.003333 = 0.99667. Sleep average = 0.003 mA × 0.99667 = 0.00299 mA.
  5. Average current = 0.02667 + 0.00299 = 0.02966 mA ≈ 0.0297 mA.
  6. Life = 800 mAh / 0.02966 mA = 26 975 h.
  7. Days = 26 975 / 24 = 1124 days.
  8. Years = 1124 / 365 = 3.1 years.

Answer. Average current 0.0297 mA; battery life 26 975 h ≈ 1124 days ≈ 3.1 years.

Exam traps

  • A sensor is one block of a smart object, not a smart object. The object also needs processing, radio, power and identity.
  • Battery life follows average current. A high transmit peak for a short duty cycle may cost less than a constant sleep leak.
  • Convert µA to mA before adding: 3 µA = 0.003 mA, not 3 mA.
  • Duty cycle D is a fraction between 0 and 1. A 1% duty cycle is D = 0.01, not 1.
  • mAh is charge, mA is current. Divide usable mAh by average mA to get hours.

2.4 IoT components: control units, communication modules, power sources

Every IoT device is three buyable parts around its sensors: a control unit that computes, a communication module that carries bits, and a power source that feeds both. Choose one badly and the others suffer: a fast radio empties a coin cell in hours; a slow processor cannot encrypt in time. This chapter names each part and its options, then gives a selection procedure.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for the five-block picture of a sensor node.

Control unit: the part that computes

The control unit runs the program, reads sensors and decides what to send. A microcontroller (MCU) puts CPU, flash, RAM and peripherals on one chip and runs bare-metal code or a small real-time OS, not Linux. An applications processor or system-on-chip (SoC) runs Linux and draws far more current. Chapter 2.5 compares them at board level; here you only size one.

A node reading a sensor once a minute and sending 20 bytes needs about 32–64 kB flash, 8 kB RAM and a 16 MHz clock. More is paid for in current every second.

The blocks built around the CPU:

  • Clock: crystal or internal oscillator sets instruction speed and wake accuracy.
  • Memory: flash holds code across power cuts, SRAM variables, EEPROM or FRAM calibration.
  • GPIO: general-purpose input/output pins read or drive one logic level, 0 V or 3.3 V.
  • ADC: analog-to-digital converter turns a voltage into a number, 10 or 12 bits against a reference.
  • PWM: pulse-width modulation, a square wave whose duty cycle sets average voltage; used for dimming and motor speed.
  • Timers: counters that trigger a sample or wake the chip at a fixed interval.

Three serial buses link CPU to outside. UART: two wires, one device per port, e.g. 9600 baud to GPS. SPI: four wires, fastest, for flash and displays. I²C: two wires shared by up to 127 addressed devices, usual for sensors.

Communication module: the part that talks

A radio is a chain, not a chip. The transceiver generates and detects the modulated signal. A matching network of inductors and capacitors transforms its impedance to 50 Ω. The antenna radiates. A matching error costs range and can push emissions past the legal limit.

Most products use a pre-certified module: transceiver, matching network, antenna and shield in one metal can, approved for sale, with protocol firmware (the stack) inside. A bare chip plus your own antenna means months of testing.

Power source: the part that pays

Options, simplest to hardest:

  • Mains adaptor: unlimited energy, but a wire and fixed position.
  • Primary cell: alkaline or lithium AA/AAA, or coin cell such as CR2032 at roughly 220 mAh; not rechargeable.
  • Rechargeable Li-ion or LiPo: 3.7 V nominal, high current, needs protection.
  • Energy harvesting: solar, piezoelectric strip for vibration, thermoelectric generator for heat; tiny and intermittent.

Between cell and chip sits a regulator. An LDO (low-dropout linear regulator) is cheap and quiet but burns voltage drop as heat, so efficiency is roughly Vout ÷ Vin. A switching regulator (buck or boost) reaches 80–95% and adds switching noise. Check quiescent current (the regulator's own no-load draw). A 5 µA quiescent current against a 10 µA sleep load raises the sleep drain to 15 µA and cuts battery life to two-thirds; sleep life scales as I_sleep ÷ (I_sleep + I_q), where I_sleep is the sleep load and I_q is quiescent current. Halving needs I_q equal to the sleep load. Add undervoltage cutoff, reverse-polarity protection and a fuse, plus a fuel gauge (coulomb counter or voltage lookup) if the user must see charge.

t = (V_bat × Q_bat × η) ÷ (V_rail × I_avg)

t is battery life in hours; V_bat is cell voltage in V; Q_bat is cell capacity in Ah; η is regulator efficiency as a fraction; V_rail is the regulated supply in V; I_avg is the average current drawn by the load in A.

Optional blocks and a selection procedure

A security element stores keys and runs AES or elliptic-curve signing in hardware, so a stolen node cannot be cloned and its key never leaves the chip. A real-time clock (RTC) is a 32.768 kHz crystal and counter, often coin-cell backed, that keeps wall-clock time and wakes the control unit.

Selection runs in one direction, because each choice constrains the next:

  1. Fix application: what is measured, how often, and how far data travels.
  2. Set required range and data rate; pick radio from table.
  3. Compute energy per day: sum V × I × t over active, radio-on and sleep time.
  4. Divide cell energy by daily energy for life in days; if it fails, cut duty cycle before changing cell.
  5. Pick control unit: enough ADC channels, buses, flash for crypto stack, solderable package.
  6. Check peak: a Wi-Fi burst of about 300 mA cannot come from a CR2032; add a supercapacitor or choose a smaller radio.

Worked example

Question. A node runs from a 3.7 V, 1200 mAh Li-ion cell. The 3.3 V rail draws 30 mA on average; the regulator is 85% efficient. Find battery life. What is the life if sleep scheduling lowers the average to 3 mA?

  1. E = V_bat × Q_bat = 3.7 × 1.2 = 4.44 Wh.
  2. At 30 mA: P_load = 3.3 × 0.030 = 99 mW.
  3. P_in = 99 ÷ 0.85 = 116.5 mW.
  4. t = 4.44 ÷ 0.1165 = 38.1 h.
  5. At 3 mA: P_load = 3.3 × 0.003 = 9.9 mW; P_in = 9.9 ÷ 0.85 = 11.65 mW.
  6. t = 4.44 ÷ 0.01165 = 381.2 h ≈ 15.9 days.
  7. Check ratio: current fell ten times and life rose ten times, because life is inversely proportional to average current (t ∝ 1 ÷ I_avg).

Answer. About 38.1 h at 30 mA, rising to 381.2 h ≈ 15.9 days at 3 mA.

Exam traps

  • Sizing a battery from mAh alone: mAh is charge, mA is current, and regulator efficiency sits between them. Convert to Wh and W first.
  • Picking a radio by data rate only. A 100 Mbps Wi-Fi link on a coin cell lasts hours; a 250 kbps ZigBee link lasts months.
  • Forgetting regulator quiescent current at microamp sleep: 5 µA against a 10 µA sleep load cuts sleep life to two-thirds; doubling needs I_q equal to the sleep load.
  • Assuming a small cell can deliver any peak current. The radio's transmit burst, not the average, decides brownout.
  • Quoting open-air range as real range; walls and interference cut it, and examiners ask why.

2.5 Embedded systems: microcontrollers and single-board computers

A washing machine, a fitness band and a highway toll tag each hold a small computer that runs one program for years. That computer is an embedded system: built into the product, hidden from the user, sized to the job. Two families do this work. A microcontroller is one chip holding a CPU core, memory, timers and pins; it starts in microseconds and sips power. A single-board computer is a small PC with an operating system, RAM and networking. Choosing the wrong family is the most common hardware mistake in an IoT project.

You need: the control unit block from 2.4 and the sensor signal chain from 2.1.

What makes a computer embedded

An embedded system is a computer whose program is fixed at design time and whose purpose is to serve the product around it. A laptop is general purpose: you install new software on it. An embedded board ships with one firmware image and runs it until the product is thrown away. Five properties separate the two families from a PC.

  • Fixed function: one program, no user installing apps, no keyboard or screen.
  • Real-time response: an input must be answered inside a deadline. Real-time means the worst case is known in advance, not that the average is fast.
  • Small memory: kilobytes, not gigabytes, so the program must fit by design.
  • Low power: a battery node in a field may have to run for years.
  • Long life in harsh conditions: the board may sit in a street cabinet for ten years, in heat and dust.

A missed deadline is a failure even if every other answer was quick. That is why the software level matters as much as the chip.

Inside a microcontroller

Every microcontroller repeats the same block diagram. The CPU core fetches and executes instructions. Flash holds the program and keeps it when power is removed. SRAM holds variables and loses them at power-off. EEPROM keeps a few settings across a battery change. Timers count clock ticks and produce exact delays and PWM waveforms for actuators. The interrupt controller lets a pin or a timer stop the main program and run a short handler at once. GPIO pins are the general-purpose digital inputs and outputs. The ADC turns a voltage into a number. Serial buses — UART, I²C, SPI — talk to sensors and radio modules.

Two memory layouts exist. In a von Neumann machine one memory holds both program and data, so one bus carries both. In a Harvard machine they sit in separate memories with separate buses, so an instruction fetch and a data read happen in the same cycle. Most microcontrollers are Harvard and most PCs are von Neumann. Harvard gives fixed, predictable instruction timing, which control code needs.

The width — 8, 16 or 32 bit — is the size of the arithmetic registers. A wider core handles bigger numbers in one instruction. Clock speed is not throughput: a 16 MHz core averaging one instruction per four cycles runs about 4 million instructions per second, while a 1.5 GHz core does far more but with cache jitter.

Microcontroller, SoC and single-board computer

  • Microprocessor: the CPU alone, needing external RAM, flash, clock and support chips.
  • Microcontroller (MCU): CPU, flash, SRAM, timers, ADC, serial buses and GPIO on one chip, running without an operating system.
  • System on chip (SoC): a die that adds radio, GPU or DSP blocks to a CPU, often running Linux.
  • Single-board computer (SBC): an SoC with RAM, storage, USB, video and a pin header on one board, running Linux.

Arduino Uno. The board carries an ATmega328P: an 8-bit core at 16 MHz, 32 KB flash, 2 KB SRAM, 1 KB EEPROM, 14 digital I/O pins, 6 analog inputs behind a 10-bit ADC, and 5 V logic. It has no operating system; the sketch starts within milliseconds of power-on.

Raspberry Pi 4. A Broadcom SoC with a 64-bit ARM Cortex-A72 quad core, 1 to 8 GB of RAM, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Ethernet, USB and HDMI, booting Linux from a microSD card in tens of seconds. Its 40-pin header works at 3.3 V and has no analog input at all.

ESP8266 and ESP32 sit in between: microcontrollers with a Wi-Fi radio on the same chip. The ESP32 adds a second core and Bluetooth Low Energy, and still runs no operating system unless you load one.

Three software levels fix the response time. Bare metal is one loop plus interrupt handlers, with response in microseconds and no scheduler. An RTOS adds tasks, priorities and a bounded context switch, with response in tens of microseconds. Linux gives files, processes and a network stack, but a GPIO edge reaches your program in milliseconds because the kernel schedules other work first. Choose the software level before the board.

A rule for choosing

  1. List every input and output with its deadline. A motor current loop at 10 kHz needs a microcontroller; a temperature upload every minute does not.
  2. Add up flash and SRAM for firmware and buffers. If it fits in tens of kilobytes, an MCU is enough.
  3. Check the analog inputs. The Pi has none, so an external ADC over I²C is needed.
  4. Check voltage. Arduino is 5 V logic and Pi GPIO is 3.3 V, so a 5 V sensor output needs a level shifter or a divider before it touches a Pi pin.
  5. Check power. A node that must last a year on a cell wants an MCU that sleeps at microamps, not a Linux board drawing hundreds of milliamps.
  6. Take the SBC only for a camera, a database, TLS-secured Wi-Fi, or Python libraries you cannot do without.

One line to remember: MCU for control, SBC for thinking. If you need both, run the fast loop on an MCU and let it talk over UART to the SBC.

Worked example

Question. An Arduino Uno uses a 10-bit ADC with V_ref = 5.00 V. Find the resolution in mV, the code produced by an input of 1.20 V, and the input voltage that produces code 512.

  1. A 10-bit ADC divides the reference range into 2¹⁰ = 1024 steps, giving output codes 0 to 1023.
  2. Resolution (one LSB) = V_ref / 2ⁿ = 5.00 V / 1024 = 0.0048828 V = 4.88 mV per step.
  3. Code for 1.20 V = 1.20 V / 0.0048828 V = 245.76; the converter truncates, so the code is 245.
  4. Check: 245 × 4.88 mV = 1196 mV, close to 1.20 V (the missing 4 mV is less than one step).
  5. Input for code 512 = 512 × 0.0048828 V = 2.500 V = 2500 mV.
  6. Check: 512/1024 = 0.5, and half of 5.00 V is 2.50 V.

Answer. Resolution = 4.88 mV per step, an input of 1.20 V gives code 245, and code 512 corresponds to 2.50 V.

Worked example 2

Question. A 12-bit ADC has V_ref = 3.30 V. Find the resolution in mV and the code for an input of 1.00 V.

  1. Steps = 2¹² = 4096.
  2. Resolution = 3.30 V / 4096 = 0.0008057 V = 0.806 mV per step.

Exam traps

  • Raspberry Pi GPIO has no analog input. A sensor giving 0-5 V needs an external ADC, usually over I²C; connecting it straight to a pin does nothing useful.
  • Arduino drives 5 V logic and Pi GPIO is 3.3 V, and Pi pins are not 5 V tolerant. A divider or level shifter is mandatory, not optional.
  • Resolution is not accuracy. The 10-bit ADC gives 4.88 mV steps, but reference drift and sensor error can be tens of millivolts.
  • Clock speed is not instruction rate. 16 MHz does not mean 16 million instructions per second; the average is set by cycles per instruction.
  • On the Uno, variables live in 2 KB SRAM, not the 32 KB flash. Three float arrays of 200 elements need about 2400 bytes of RAM and overflow silently.

2.6 Wireless sensor networks

A wireless sensor network (WSN) is a group of small battery-powered nodes. Each senses the world, processes the reading, and passes it hop by hop to one collector called the sink. No node needs a cable or long radio range. WSNs suit crop fields, pipelines, factory machines, forest-fire watch and bridge strain. This chapter gives the elements, topologies, protocol layers, energy arithmetic and open challenges.

You need 2.3 for the five parts of a sensor node and 2.5 for the microcontroller that runs the stack.

Nodes, sinks and topologies

A WSN has three elements. A sensor node is a smart object (see 2.3) with a sensing unit, processing unit, radio transceiver and power unit. A sink, or base station, collects data from the field and usually has mains power. A gateway links the sink to the outside world, for example over 4G.

Nodes differ from ordinary computers in three ways: they run on a battery or harvester, so energy is the first constraint; their radio is short range; and they are many—hundreds or thousands. The network moves a few bytes a long distance, not many bytes a short distance.

Three topologies cover most designs. In a star every node talks directly to the sink: simple, one hop, but the sink must be in range of every node. In a tree each node sends to a parent that forwards upward; reach grows, but a dead parent cuts off its children. In a mesh any node may forward for any neighbour, giving many paths and fault tolerance at the cost of more routing work.

A path with more than one hop is multi-hop, the normal case, because radio energy grows with distance.

The stack and the listening problem

A node runs a small stack. The physical layer turns bits into modulation and sets transmit power. The MAC layer decides when a node may use the shared channel. The routing layer picks the next hop towards the sink. The transport layer keeps end-to-end reliability where needed. The application layer runs the sensing task and aggregation.

The radio is the biggest energy consumer. A node spends energy transmitting, receiving, and—worst—listening for a packet that may never arrive. Idle listening can draw nearly as much power as receiving. So the MAC layer uses duty cycling: the radio sleeps most of the time and wakes on a schedule.

A contention-based MAC lets a node transmit when it has data and back off after a collision; simple and adaptive, but collisions waste energy. A schedule-based MAC gives each node a fixed slot; no collisions, but slots are wasted when a node has nothing to send. Most designs mix both.

A sleeping node cannot hear. Sender and receiver must share wake times, so the MAC also handles time synchronisation and the cost of a long preamble that holds the receiver awake.

Routing, energy and the limits

Three routing ideas appear in every course. Flooding: each node rebroadcasts every packet once; it finds a path if one exists, but wastes energy on duplicates. Gossiping: a node forwards to one random neighbour, which cuts duplicates but may never deliver. LEACH: nodes form clusters, each elects a head that aggregates readings into one packet for the sink, and the head role rotates so no single node drains first.

Directed diffusion is data-centric: the sink broadcasts an interest, matching nodes send data back along the path the interest came from, and the sink reinforces the best path. Only a few universities examine it; remember the idea—name the data, not the node.

E_tx(k, d) = k × E_elec + k × ε × d²

E_tx is transmit energy in joules; k is bits; E_elec is electronics energy per bit, typically 50 nJ/bit; ε is amplifier energy per bit per square metre (100 pJ/bit/m² in this book's examples); d is distance in metres. Receiving k bits costs k × E_elec.

Splitting d into n equal hops changes the amplifier cost from εd² to n × ε(d/n)² = εd²/n, but the electronics cost becomes n × E_elec. So transmit energy per bit is n × E_elec + εd²/n: only the amplifier part falls, by the factor n.

  • Coverage: every point of the field lies within sensing range of at least one node.
  • Connectivity: every node has a path to the sink, and above a density threshold it holds almost surely.
  • Localisation: each node finds its own position, from GPS, anchor nodes, or ranging.
  • Time synchronisation: nodes agree on a clock so wake schedules and timestamps line up; drift makes this a repeating task.

Network lifetime has at least three definitions: time until the first node dies, time until a stated fraction die, and time until coverage or connectivity breaks so the network stops delivering usable data. The first is strictest and commonest in exams. The challenges follow: limited energy, changing topology as nodes die, fault tolerance, clock drift and security.

Exam traps

  • A WSN is not Wi-Fi. Nodes are battery-limited and multi-hop; no access point reaches every node in one hop.
  • Multi-hop is not free. It saves transmit energy but adds latency, routing overhead and a receive cost at every relay; at high hop counts total energy can rise.

  • Sink, gateway and node are different. The node senses, the sink collects, the gateway leaves the network.
  • Flooding and gossiping are not table-driven routing protocols. Flooding guarantees delivery and wastes energy; gossiping saves energy and may never deliver.

2.7 RFID and identification of things

RFID is a way to give a thing an electronic identity that a reader can capture without contact and without line of sight. A tag holds a code; a reader radios the code out; middleware passes it to a database. The code is the key, not the whole record. Used on toll tags, library books, access cards, shipping pallets and animal chips. This chapter explains the parts, the radio bands, passive and active tags, the EPC code, singulation and the privacy cost of hidden tags.

You need 2.4 for radio modules and power ideas.

Identity, parts and why a reader is needed

Identification in IoT means giving a thing a name or number that a computer can use as a database key. A key alone does nothing; the system must capture it. RFID has three parts. A tag is a chip plus an antenna, attached to the thing. A reader has a transmitter, a receiver and an antenna; it powers or wakes tags and receives their codes. Middleware cleans the stream: it removes duplicate reads, filters false reads and maps each code to a record in the server. The EPC is the code; the database holds price, batch, owner or location.

A barcode and a QR code are printed patterns read by light. They need line of sight, one label at a time, and they cannot be rewritten. An RFID tag is read by radio through cardboard or plastic, many tags can answer in one pass, and a read-write tag can be updated. The trade is cost: a printed label is cheap; a tag costs more.

Bands, coupling and tag classes

The band decides range and behaviour. LF and HF use near-field inductive coupling: the reader coil makes a magnetic field, the tag coil takes power from it, and the tag changes its load to send bits. UHF and microwave use far-field backscatter: the reader radiates a wave, the tag harvests a little power, and it reflects the wave by switching its antenna load. Backscatter is why a passive UHF tag can be read several metres away without a battery.

EPC Gen 2, singulation, other IDs, privacy

EPC Gen 2 is a UHF air-interface standard for passive tags. Its common EPC is 96 bits: an 8-bit header, a 28-bit manager number, a 24-bit object class and a 36-bit serial number. The reader must read one tag at a time even when many reply. EPC Gen 2 uses frame-slotted ALOHA with the Q parameter: the reader sets a frame of slots, and each tag picks a random slot so that most tags answer alone. Binary-tree splitting is a different anti-collision method used by some other UHF standards; it divides colliding tags into smaller groups until each answers alone. Both methods solve the same problem: many tags, one reader.

Other identifiers used with things: an IPv6 address names a network interface and works over the Internet; 6LoWPAN compresses IPv6 for IEEE 802.15.4 links; a BLE beacon broadcasts a short ID that a phone can hear. These are not RFID: they need a radio network or a phone, not a dedicated tag reader.

NFC is HF RFID at 13.56 MHz built into a phone. It works at a few centimetres: the phone can read a tag, act as a tag, or talk to another phone. Metro cards, door cards and tap-to-pair use it.

A hidden tag can be read after the buyer leaves the shop. A card in a pocket can be a people-tracking token. A kill command can disable a tag permanently; a blocker tag or shielded sleeve can stop reads. Privacy law is in Unit 5.

  2. With 3.28 W EIRP, at d = 3 m the power density is S = 3.28/(4π×3²) = 0.0290 W/m².
  3. Wavelength: λ = c/f = 3×10⁸/(866×10⁶) = 0.346 m.
  4. Tag effective aperture: A_e = Gλ²/(4π) = 2×0.346²/(4π) = 0.0191 m²; G = 2 is the tag antenna gain.
  5. Power at chip: P = S×A_e = 0.0290×0.0191 = 5.54×10⁻⁴ W = 554 µW.
  6. The tag needs P_min = 30 µW. In free space P falls as 1/d², so d_max/d = √(P/P_min) = √(554/30) = 4.30.
  7. d_max = 3×4.30 = 12.9 m.

Exam traps

  • A passive tag has no battery, so its read range is set by the forward power delivered to its chip, not by the backscatter return link.
  • NFC is HF RFID at 13.56 MHz, not UHF 860–960 MHz; do not put NFC in the toll-tag band.
  • EPC is an identifier code; EPC Gen 2 is a UHF air-interface standard. Do not call EPC a tag type.
  • A semi-passive tag has a battery but still backscatters; an active tag has its own transmitter and can start a message.
  • Keep units straight: ERP is not EIRP, W is not dBm, and cm is not m in range formulas.

A chip and an antenna; it has no battery.

Power falls as 1/d². Distance doubles, so power becomes one quarter: 30 µW.

Analyze Why is the read range of a passive tag set by the forward link, not by the backscatter link? 5 marks

The chip needs a minimum wake-up power from the reader. Once awake, the reflected signal usually has enough margin for the reader to decode.

QR: printed labels are very cheap. RFID: reads many tags without line of sight and can rewrite the tag.

Takeaways

  1. Accuracy, drift, hysteresis and dead band decide whether a sensor reading can be trusted.
  2. Active sensors need a power source; passive sensors convert energy from the measured quantity.
  3. Analog sensors suit simple continuous measurement; digital sensors suit buses, noise immunity and microcontrollers.
  4. An actuator type is chosen for position accuracy, speed, cost and feedback needs.
  5. A servo closes the loop; a stepper counts steps; a BLDC gives efficient continuous motion.
  6. A smart object is a sensor node plus local compute, identity and sometimes an actuator.
  7. Control unit, communication module and power source must be selected together for an IoT device.
  8. A microcontroller gives hard real-time control at low power; an SBC gives an OS and rich connectivity.
  9. A WSN buys coverage with relay energy, so duty cycling and clustering decide network lifetime.
  10. RFID supplies contactless identity, while sensors supply measurement; do not confuse the two.

Glossary

Accuracy
How close a reading is to the true value.
Actuator
Device that turns an electrical command into motion or force.
Bandwidth
Frequency range over which a sensor responds usefully.
BLDC motor
Permanent-magnet motor with electronic commutation.
Communication module
Radio or wired interface that carries data to and from the device.
Control unit
Processor that runs the device logic and interfaces sensors and actuators.
Dead band
Input interval with no output change.
Drift
Slow unwanted change in output at constant input.
Duty cycling
Switching a node between sleep and active states to save power.
Hysteresis
Difference in output for the same input when approached from opposite directions.
LEACH
Clustering protocol that rotates cluster heads to save WSN energy.
Microcontroller
Single-chip computer with CPU, memory and input/output for control.
Power source
Battery, mains supply or energy harvester that runs the device.
Precision
How close repeated readings are to each other.
Resolution
Smallest input change that gives a detectable output change.
RFID
Radio-frequency identification; contactless reading of a tag identity.
Sensitivity
Change in output divided by change in input.
Sensor
Device that converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal.
Sensor node
WSN node with sensor, processor, radio and battery.
Servo motor
Motor plus feedback and controller for commanded position or speed.
Single-board computer
Full computer on one board with operating system and ports.
Sink
Node that collects data from a WSN and connects it to a network.
Smart object
Small device with sensing, compute, communication, power and identity.
Stepper motor
Motor that moves in fixed steps for input pulses.
Transducer
Element that converts one form of energy into another; a sensor is a transducer used for measurement.
WSN
Network of small sensing nodes that relay data to a sink.
