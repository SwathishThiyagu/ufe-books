3.1 IoT protocols and their standardization

A protocol is the rulebook for messages; a standard is an agreed written version of that rulebook. IoT devices from different vendors need both. Without shared rules they cannot exchange even a temperature reading. Standards appear in homes, factories, smart cities and farms.

You need: Unit 1, logical design and communication models.

Why IoT needs standards

A protocol is a set of rules for messages: which side sends, in what order, what fields appear, and what a reply means. A standard is a written agreement that many vendors follow. They are not the same: a standard can leave many features optional, and a de facto protocol can dominate the market with no formal standard. IoT needs standards because a sensor, a phone and a cloud service often come from different vendors. Interoperability means they exchange a reading without a custom adapter for every pair. Metcalfe's law says the value of a network grows with the square of its users, so each compatible device helps every existing user.

De jure standards come from a recognized body. De facto standards come from market use first, with formal text later or never. A buyer should ask which is which, because a de facto protocol may have no conformance test. A profile is a smaller list of mandatory options chosen from a broad standard. Profiles matter in IoT because a full standard may not fit a small device.

The layered view

Figure 3.1 shows a five-layer IoT stack. The application layer holds MQTT and CoAP; these are message styles, not radios. The transport layer uses TCP or UDP. The network layer uses IPv6, often compressed by 6LoWPAN. The MAC layer uses IEEE 802.15.4 or Bluetooth Low Energy. The PHY layer is the radio itself. When a message is sent, each layer adds its own header to the payload. The receiver removes the headers in reverse order. This is encapsulation. Links to the detailed standards are in IEEE 802.15.4, 6LoWPAN and Bluetooth Low Energy.

Layering lets one layer change without rewriting the others. A new radio can carry the same MQTT message. A gateway can translate between two stacks: it terminates one stack and opens another. The cost is header bytes. Constrained devices have small frames, so every byte matters. The frame formats are in later chapters.

S = P + Σ hᵢ

S is the final packet size in bytes; P is the application payload in bytes; hᵢ is the header added by layer i in bytes; the sum runs over all layers that add a header.

Standardization in practice, and its pain points

Standardization has practical problems. Too many options: a standard may define ten ways to do one job, and two compliant devices may still not talk. Profiles fix this by making one choice mandatory. IPR is another problem: a standard may include patented technology, and a small vendor must know the licensing terms before shipping. Security is often optional in older IoT standards, so a product can be compliant and still unprotected. Constrained devices cannot carry full web stacks, so compression and small buffers are needed. Gateways translate between incompatible stacks, but they add cost and a single point of failure. Some syllabi call this protocol standardization for IoT.

  • M2M protocols are examples of device-to-server messaging.
  • WSN protocols are examples of sensor-network routing.
  • SCADA protocols are examples of industrial supervision; see industrial protocols.
  • RFID protocols are examples of tag reading; see Unit 2.

A common exam question asks why standards do not remove gateways. Installed devices follow different generations and profiles; a gateway is cheaper than replacing them.

Worked example

Question. A 4-layer IoT protocol stack adds 2 bytes at layer 1, 4 bytes at layer 2, 8 bytes at layer 3 and 20 bytes at layer 4. The application payload is 100 bytes. What is the total header overhead in bytes and as a percentage of the final packet?

  1. Header bytes: 2 + 4 + 8 + 20 = 34 bytes.
  2. Application payload: 100 bytes.
  3. Final packet: 100 + 34 = 134 bytes.
  4. Overhead fraction: 34 / 134 = 0.25373...
  5. Overhead percentage: 0.25373... × 100 = 25.373...%.
  6. Round to one decimal place: 25.4%.

Answer. The total header overhead is 34 bytes; the final packet is 134 bytes; overhead is 25.4% of the final packet.

Exam traps

  • A protocol is not automatically a standard. A de facto protocol can have no formal standard, and a standard can contain many optional features.
  • Do not call a market leader a de jure standard. De jure means a recognized standards body produced the text.
  • More layers add header bytes. A 100-byte payload does not stay 100 bytes on the wire.
  • Overhead percentage uses the final packet as the denominator, not the application payload.
  • A compliant device is not always a secure device. Older IoT standards often make security optional.

3.2 IEEE 802.15.4 low-power wireless standard

IEEE 802.15.4 is a low-rate, low-power wireless standard for the physical and MAC layers of small sensor and control networks. It exists because Wi-Fi and Bluetooth cost too much power for battery nodes that send few bytes. It gives a shared radio foundation under ZigBee, 6LoWPAN and WirelessHART, so home sensors, smart meters and factory monitors can interoperate. Some syllabi call it LR-WPAN, for low-rate wireless personal area network. It is the shared radio layer under ZigBee and 6LoWPAN; the next chapters build the upper layers on it.

You need: Unit 2 wireless sensor networks and Chapter 3.1 on layered protocol models.

Physical layer: bands, rates and channels

IEEE 802.15.4 defines the physical layer, or PHY, and the medium access control layer, or MAC. The PHY turns bits into radio signals. The MAC decides when a node may transmit. The standard is low rate and low power: a node can sleep most of the time and wake to send a few bytes.

The PHY uses three licence-free bands. At 868 MHz it has one channel, binary phase-shift keying (BPSK) and 20 kbps. At 915 MHz it has ten channels, BPSK and 40 kbps. At 2.4 GHz it has sixteen channels numbered 11 to 26, offset quadrature phase-shift keying (OQPSK) and 250 kbps. The 2.4 GHz band is worldwide and gives the highest rate, so most consumer nodes use it. A PHY frame has a 4-byte preamble, a 1-byte start-of-frame delimiter (SFD), a 1-byte length field and a PSDU, the physical service data unit. The PSDU is the MAC frame and can be up to 127 bytes. Its last 2 bytes are the frame check sequence (FCS), a CRC that detects bit errors. The PHY adds 6 bytes before the PSDU: a 4-byte preamble, a 1-byte SFD and a 1-byte length field.

MAC layer: CSMA/CA, beacons and time slots

The MAC uses carrier sense multiple access with collision avoidance (CSMA/CA). A node that has a frame first listens to the channel. If the channel is busy, it waits. If free, it waits a random backoff and listens again. It sends only when the channel is still free. The receiver returns an acknowledgement (ACK) frame. If the ACK does not arrive, the sender retries.

In beacon-enabled mode a coordinator sends a beacon at fixed intervals. The time between beacons is the beacon interval, BI. The active part is the superframe duration, SD. The rest is inactive, when nodes may sleep. The active superframe has 16 equal slots. The contention access period (CAP) uses CSMA/CA. The contention-free period (CFP) has guaranteed time slots (GTS), up to seven, for urgent or periodic data.

BI = aBaseSuperframeDuration × 2<sup>BO</sup>, SD = aBaseSuperframeDuration × 2<sup>SO</sup>

BI is the beacon interval; SD is the superframe duration; BO is the beacon order; SO is the superframe order; aBaseSuperframeDuration is the smallest superframe length in symbols; SO ≤ BO.

The duty cycle is SD / BI = 2SO−BO (two raised to the power SO − BO). When SO = 3 and BO = 6 this gives 2−3 = 1/8 = 12.5 %. A small duty cycle saves battery but gives fewer active slots. In non-beacon mode there is no superframe. Nodes use unslotted CSMA/CA and may sleep on their own schedule. This is simpler but has no guaranteed slots. Beacons also carry network information and wake sleeping nodes.

Topologies, addresses and frame types

An 802.15.4 network has three topologies. In a star, every device talks only to one coordinator. It is simple and predictable. In peer-to-peer, any device can talk to any other device in range; this allows mesh routing above 802.15.4. In a cluster tree, coordinators form a tree, and devices join the nearest parent. ZigBee uses these topologies on top of 802.15.4; see Chapter 3.3.

Each device has a 64-bit extended address, a unique hardware address. A coordinator can also give a 16-bit short address, which saves frame space. A frame carries source and destination address fields, so short addresses reduce overhead.

There are four MAC frame types. A beacon frame starts a superframe and advertises the network. A data frame carries payload. An ACK frame confirms receipt. A MAC command frame carries control messages such as association requests. Each frame has a frame control field, sequence number, addressing fields, payload and FCS. The maximum PSDU is 127 bytes, so payload is small; 6LoWPAN compresses IPv6 headers to fit; see Chapter 3.5.

Worked example

Question. An 802.15.4 data frame has a 127-byte PSDU. It is sent at 250 kbps. What is the total time on air including the 4-byte preamble, 1-byte SFD and 1-byte length? Ignore inter-frame spacing.

  1. PSDU length = 127 B.
  2. PHY overhead = 4 B preamble + 1 B SFD + 1 B length = 6 B.
  3. Total bytes on air = 127 + 6 = 133 B.
  4. Total bits = 133 × 8 = 1064 bits.
  5. Time = 1064 / 250000 = 0.004256 s = 4.256 ms.

Answer. The total time on air is 4.256 ms for 133 bytes.

Exam traps

  • Do not add the 4-byte preamble, 1-byte SFD and 1-byte length to the 127-byte PSDU and call the result the MAC frame; 127 B is the PSDU, and the PHY adds 6 B.
  • Do not mix beacon-enabled and non-beacon CSMA/CA: beacon-enabled networks can use CAP and CFP with GTS; non-beacon networks have no superframe.
  • Do not treat the 16-bit short address as globally unique; it is assigned by a coordinator, while the 64-bit extended address is unique.
  • Do not use the 2.4 GHz rate of 250 kbps for the 868 MHz band; that band runs at 20 kbps.
  • Do not say 802.15.4 is the whole IoT stack; it is only PHY and MAC, and ZigBee or 6LoWPAN adds upper layers.

An IPv6 header is 40 B. Inside a 127-B PSDU the MAC header and 2-B FCS already take up to about 27 B, so well under 87 B (roughly 60 B) remains for transport and application data. That tight budget is why 6LoWPAN compresses headers and fragments.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. IEEE 802.15.4 is the base of Thread, a newer IPv6 mesh for homes, and of ZigBee PRO networks. Chip vendors sell 802.15.4 radios inside systems-on-chip with ARM cores, so a sensor node can run the MAC in hardware and sleep at microamp levels. Research extends it with energy harvesting, channel hopping and deterministic scheduling for industrial loops. Search for "IEEE 802.15.4e TSCH" and "Thread protocol" to go further.

3.3 ZigBee and Z-Wave

ZigBee and Z-Wave are two low-power wireless mesh protocols built for control, not for streaming. A light switch sends a few bytes; it must run for years on a coin cell and still reach a lamp three rooms away. Both solve this by forming a mesh: every mains-powered node forwards packets for its neighbours. ZigBee rides on IEEE 802.15.4 at 2.4 GHz and appears in smart homes, smart meters and street lighting. Z-Wave uses its own sub-GHz radio and dominates consumer home automation. This chapter covers both stacks and where each one wins.

You need Chapter 3.2 on IEEE 802.15.4 for the PHY and MAC layers that ZigBee reuses.

The ZigBee stack and device roles

ZigBee reuses IEEE 802.15.4 for its bottom two layers and adds the rest. The 802.15.4 PHY gives 16 channels at 2.4 GHz with 250 kbit/s, or regional 868/915 MHz channels at 20 and 40 kbit/s. The MAC gives CSMA/CA, a 64-bit IEEE address per device, and a 16-bit short address handed out by a parent at join time.

The NWK (network) layer builds the mesh. A coordinator starts the network: it picks a channel, a PAN ID (network identifier) and its own short address, usually 0x0000. Three topologies are allowed — star, tree and mesh. Tree routing is arithmetic: each parent owns a block of addresses, so a node can test whether the destination lies in its own subtree and pass the packet down, or send it up to its parent. Mesh routing is on-demand, a simplified AODV: a node with no route broadcasts a route request, the destination replies, and routers keep a route entry for a while. On-demand routing costs a burst of broadcasts but needs no periodic table exchange.

Device roles follow from this. One coordinator per network forms and manages it. Routers are mains-powered nodes that forward packets and accept children. End devices do not forward; a sleepy end device switches its radio off and its parent buffers frames until it polls. In 802.15.4 terms the coordinator and routers are full-function devices and the end device is a reduced-function device.

APS, profiles and security

Above NWK sits the APS (Application Support Sublayer). APS gives a device up to 240 endpoints, numbered 1 to 240, which work like ports: endpoint 1 might be a light, endpoint 2 a temperature sensor. Inside an endpoint sit clusters, each a numbered group of attributes and commands. The On/Off cluster has ID 0x0006 and an attribute onOff. A profile ID ties a set of clusters into one application: Home Automation (0x0104), Smart Energy (0x0109) for metering and load control, Light Link (0xC05E) for touch-to-pair lamps, and Commercial Building Automation. Some syllabi call these application profiles, others the ZigBee application layer. Endpoint 0 is reserved for the ZDO (ZigBee Device Object), which handles joining, discovery and binding. A binding is a stored rule that sends frames from one endpoint and cluster to another, so a switch reaches a lamp without asking each time.

Security has two layers. The network key is shared by all nodes and protects each hop: a router decrypts a frame, checks it and re-encrypts it for the next hop, so every router can read network-protected payloads. The APS key is end-to-end between two devices and hides application data from routers. The trust center, normally the coordinator, decides who may join and hands out the network key. A device can be pre-loaded with a link key or an install code so the key exchange never travels in the clear.

Z-Wave and the comparison

Z-Wave is a competing mesh for home automation, defined by ITU-T G.9959. It does not use 802.15.4. It transmits in sub-GHz ISM bands, about 868 MHz in Europe and 908 MHz in North America, using GFSK at 9.6, 40 or 100 kbit/s. A lower frequency means a lower data rate but better penetration through walls and no competition from Wi-Fi. Each network has a 32-bit home ID and 8-bit node IDs, so one controller manages up to 232 nodes. A controller, primary or secondary, owns the routing table and the inclusion process; slaves only follow commands. Z-Wave uses source routing — the controller writes the whole route into the frame header — so slaves need almost no routing logic, which keeps them cheap. Z-Wave Plus added network-wide inclusion, explorer frames and the 100 kbit/s rate.

The trade-off is clear. ZigBee runs at 2.4 GHz, 250 kbit/s, 16 channels, sharing the band with Wi-Fi and microwave ovens; many vendors make chips; the standard is open but certified. Z-Wave runs sub-GHz at up to 100 kbit/s with one channel per region, meets less interference, comes from a single chip vendor (Silicon Labs) and requires certification, which keeps its ecosystem tighter. For a battery switch in a concrete house, Z-Wave's penetration wins. For a large smart-meter rollout that needs many vendors, ZigBee wins.

Exam traps

  • Classic ZigBee is not IP-based. ZigBee IP is a separate profile; if you need IPv6 over 802.15.4, that is 6LoWPAN and RPL (Chapter 3.5).
  • Z-Wave is not built on 802.15.4 and is not at 2.4 GHz. It is ITU-T G.9959 in sub-GHz bands.
  • Network security is hop-by-hop and APS security is end-to-end: a router can read a network-protected frame but not an APS-protected one.
  • An end device never routes, and a sleepy end device's parent buffers its downlink frames. Exactly one coordinator exists per ZigBee network.
  • 250 kbit/s belongs to the 2.4 GHz ZigBee PHY; 20 and 40 kbit/s belong to the 868/915 MHz PHYs. Do not mix them in a calculation.

3.4 Bluetooth and Bluetooth Low Energy

Bluetooth is a short-range radio that replaces cables. Classic Bluetooth streams audio and moves files between a phone and a headset or car kit. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) sends small data now and then from a coin cell, which suits IoT sensors. This chapter covers the classic piconet, then BLE: channels, GAP roles, advertising, connection interval, GATT, BLE 5 PHYs, and a comparison.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for the idea of a layered protocol and Chapter 3.2 for the low-power radio comparison.

Classic Bluetooth: one master, seven slaves

Classic Bluetooth uses the 2.4 GHz ISM band, the same unlicensed band as Wi-Fi. It divides the band into 79 channels of 1 MHz and hops 1600 times per second. Hopping defends against interference: a microwave or Wi-Fi access point spoils only the hops that land on its frequencies. Basic Rate carries 1 Mbps; Enhanced Data Rate (EDR) raises this to 2 or 3 Mbps.

The network is a piconet: one master and up to seven active slaves. Seven is fixed by the 3-bit address the master uses to tag each slave's packets. The master owns the clock and hopping sequence; each slave synchronises to it. Time is cut into 625 µs slots, and the master polls one slave per slot. Slaves never talk directly; every packet passes through the master.

Two piconets sharing one device form a scatternet. The shared device time-shares its radio, so throughput falls. The standard allows it; real deployments are rare.

A profile fixes what a device implements for one use case: SPP for serial cable, A2DP for stereo audio, HFP for hands-free calls, HID for a keyboard. Devices interoperate only if both support the same profile. Classic Bluetooth carries audio, file transfer and car kits. A sensor reporting every few seconds needs BLE, which builds links differently.

Bluetooth Low Energy: advertise, scan, connect

BLE is a separate radio design, not classic Bluetooth running slowly. It uses 40 channels of 2 MHz. Three are advertising channels at 2402, 2426 and 2480 MHz, placed between common Wi-Fi channels so advertisements survive; the other 37 carry data.

The Generic Access Profile (GAP) defines four roles.

  • Broadcaster: sends advertisements only, never connects. A battery beacon is one.
  • Observer: only listens to advertisements.
  • Peripheral: advertises, then accepts a connection. A temperature sensor is one.
  • Central: scans and starts connections. A phone or gateway is one.

The peripheral repeats an ADV_IND packet every advertising interval, from 20 ms to 10.24 s. A legacy advertisement holds up to 31 bytes, enough for a sensor reading or short name. A central wanting more sends SCAN_REQ and gets SCAN_RSP.

To open a link, the central sends CONNECT_REQ. It carries the connection parameters: connection interval, slave latency, channel map, hop increment and CRC initial value. The two ends then meet in a connection event once per connection interval. The interval runs from 7.5 ms to 4 s in steps of 1.25 ms. The central sends the first packet; the two alternate until neither has data or the event's time is used. Slave latency lets the peripheral skip up to 499 events when it has nothing to send, so a sensor reporting once a minute sleeps through hundreds of chances to talk.

The data unit is small. The ATT MTU defaults to 23 bytes; 3 are header, leaving 20 bytes of application data per packet. Either side can negotiate a larger MTU.

GATT, BLE 5 and how to choose

GATT (Generic Attribute Profile) says how data is arranged. A service groups related values; a characteristic is one value with a UUID and properties: read, write, notify, indicate. A heart-rate sensor exposes the Heart Rate Service (UUID 0x180D) with a Heart Rate Measurement characteristic that supports notify. The peripheral pushes a value only when the reading changes, so the central never polls. Phone BLE APIs and Matter use this model.

  • LE 2M PHY: 2 Mbps instead of 1 Mbps. Same data takes half the airtime, so less energy per bit.
  • LE Coded PHY: forward error correction. S=2 roughly doubles range at 500 kbps; S=8 roughly quadruples range at 125 kbps.
  • Extended advertising: payloads up to 255 bytes per packet, chained for more, with packets on data channels.

3.5 6LoWPAN and IoT routing protocols

6LoWPAN lets IPv6 run on tiny IEEE 802.15.4 frames. 802.15.4 gives about 127 bytes per frame, too small for a normal IPv6 packet. 6LoWPAN sits between IPv6 and 802.15.4, compresses headers and splits big packets into fragments. RPL then chooses paths through the same low-power, lossy mesh. Together they let thousands of small sensors join an IP network without a gateway that translates every message. You meet them in smart meters, building sensors and industrial monitoring.

You need Chapter 3.2 for 802.15.4 frames and Chapter 3.1 for the network layer idea.

Constrained links and the two jobs

An IoT sensor node often has a small battery, a weak radio and links that drop packets. IEEE 802.15.4 gives it a frame with a 127-byte PHY payload, far below the 1280-byte minimum MTU that IPv6 expects. MTU means maximum transmission unit, the largest packet a link can carry. Two jobs follow. The first is adaptation: make IPv6 fit the small frame. 6LoWPAN does that. The second is routing: find a path from a node to a border router through a lossy mesh. RPL does that. A border router connects the mesh to the Internet. The figure shows RPL's tree view: a root at rank 0, routers at rank 1, and end devices deeper. Rank is a node's position in the tree as measured by the objective function, often hop count. 6LoWPAN stands for IPv6 over Low-Power Wireless Personal Area Networks. It is an adaptation layer, not a routing protocol. RPL is a routing protocol, not an adaptation layer. Keep the two separate in an answer.

6LoWPAN: compression, fragmentation, mesh

6LoWPAN sits between IPv6 and IEEE 802.15.4. Its main jobs are header compression, fragmentation and local mesh forwarding. Header compression uses IPHC, IPv6 Header Compression. A full IPv6 header is 40 bytes and a UDP header is 8 bytes. Many fields repeat or can be inferred: the version is 6, the prefix is known, and link-local addresses share a prefix. IPHC sends one dispatch byte and a set of bit flags saying which fields are omitted or compressed, plus the few fields that actually change; the receiver rebuilds the full 40-byte IPv6 header from those flags. This cuts overhead from dozens of bytes to a few bytes.

Fragmentation handles a packet larger than one frame. An IPv6 host may send up to 1280 bytes, but one 802.15.4 frame carries about 100 usable bytes. 6LoWPAN splits the IPv6 datagram. The first fragment carries a dispatch value, the datagram size and a tag. Later fragments carry the same tag and an offset. The destination reassembles them. More fragments mean more chances to lose one, and one lost fragment loses the whole datagram.

N_frag = ceil(L_packet / L_usable)

N_frag is the number of 6LoWPAN fragments; L_packet is the IPv6 datagram size in bytes; L_usable is the usable bytes per 802.15.4 frame after MAC and 6LoWPAN overhead; ceil rounds up to the next whole number.

Mesh-under and route-over are two ways to forward. In mesh-under, 802.15.4 MAC forwarding carries frames through a mesh; all nodes appear on one IP link. In route-over, every hop is an IP router; each 802.15.4 node forwards IPv6 packets. Route-over fits IP tools better; mesh-under saves IP header processing. Neighbor discovery is also adapted: IPv6 ND uses multicast and large options, so 6LoWPAN-ND compresses messages and registers addresses with a border router.

RPL: DODAG, rank, trickle, alternatives

RPL is the IPv6 Routing Protocol for Low-Power and Lossy Networks. It builds a DODAG: Destination Oriented Directed Acyclic Graph. The DODAG has one root, often the border router, at rank 0. Each node picks a parent that is closer to the root, so rank increases as you move away. The objective function is the rule for choosing a parent and a path. It may use hop count, link quality or energy. RPL control messages are DIO (DODAG Information Object), DAO (Destination Advertisement Object) and DIS (DODAG Information Solicitation). The trickle timer controls DIO sending. When the network is stable, DIOs are rare. When a node hears inconsistent information, trickle speeds up. Upward routes go from a node to the root. Downward routes go from the root to nodes; nodes send DAO messages to advertise their prefixes. RPL suits many-to-one traffic, such as meters sending readings to a collector. Alternatives exist. LOADng is a lightweight on-demand protocol. AODV is a reactive protocol from mobile ad hoc networks. Use RPL when you need a stable tree and low control overhead.

Worked example

Question. A 1280-byte IPv6 packet is sent over IEEE 802.15.4 frames. Each frame has a 127-byte PHY payload and 25 bytes of 6LoWPAN and MAC overhead. How many 6LoWPAN fragments are needed?

  1. List the frame payload: L_PHY = 127 bytes.
  2. Subtract the overhead: L_usable = 127 − 25 = 102 bytes per frame.
  3. Divide the IPv6 packet by usable bytes: 1280 ÷ 102 = 12.549... (12.55 when rounded to two decimals).
  4. Round up because a partial fragment still needs a frame: ceil(12.549...) = 13 fragments.

Answer. 13 fragments are needed.

Exam traps

  • 6LoWPAN is an adaptation layer, not a routing protocol. RPL routes; it does not compress IPv6 headers.
  • Fragmentation is not free: each extra fragment adds header bytes and increases the chance that the whole IPv6 packet is lost.
  • Rank is not an IP address or a hardware priority. It is the node's position in the DODAG under the objective function.
  • Mesh-under forwards at the MAC or adaptation layer on one IP link; route-over forwards IPv6 at every hop. Do not mix the two.
  • RPL is designed for low-power and lossy links, not for high-speed data centres.

3.6 MQTT publish-subscribe messaging protocol

MQTT is a publish-subscribe messaging protocol for IoT. A publisher client sends a message to a broker, not to a subscriber directly. The broker routes the message by topic to every client that subscribed to that topic. This decouples senders and receivers in time, address and network. A small temperature sensor can publish once, and a phone, a dashboard and a controller can all receive the value. MQTT runs over TCP, usually on port 1883, or port 8883 with TLS. Some syllabi call MQTT Message Queuing Telemetry Transport.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for application-layer protocols and Chapter 3.5 for constrained IPv6 transport.

Broker, clients and topics

MQTT has clients and one broker. A client can publish, subscribe, or both. The broker is the server: it accepts connections, stores subscriptions and forwards messages. A topic is a UTF-8 string with levels separated by /. For example, home/room1/temp has three levels. A publisher does not know which subscribers exist. It only names a topic, and the broker finds matching subscriptions.

A subscription can use wildcards. The plus sign + matches exactly one topic level. The hash sign # matches all remaining levels and must be the last character. home/+/temp matches home/room1/temp and home/kitchen/temp, but not home/room1/floor1/temp. home/# matches home/room1/temp and home/room1/floor1/temp. home/room1/+ matches home/room1/temp, but not home/room1. Topic names are case-sensitive.

The broker decouples publisher and subscriber. Many publishers can write to one topic, and the broker sends each message to every matching subscriber. One subscriber can hold many subscriptions. A clean session flag controls stored state. Clean session true discards subscriptions and queued messages at disconnect; false keeps them for the next connection. Plain MQTT uses TCP port 1883; MQTT over TLS uses port 8883.

QoS levels and message flow

QoS is the delivery guarantee for a PUBLISH between one client and the broker. The publisher requests a QoS, and the broker may lower it to the subscription's QoS. The packet names are: PUBACK is publish acknowledgement, PUBREC is publish received, PUBREL is publish release, and PUBCOMP is publish complete.

QoSNamePacketsResult
0At most oncePUBLISH onlyMay be lost; no duplicate
1At least oncePUBLISH, PUBACKArrives; duplicate possible
2Exactly oncePUBLISH, PUBREC, PUBREL, PUBCOMPNo duplicate between that client and broker

In QoS 1, the publisher sends PUBLISH with a packet identifier. The broker replies PUBACK. The broker then sends PUBLISH to each subscriber and each subscriber replies PUBACK. If a PUBACK is lost, the sender retransmits with the DUP flag, so a duplicate can arrive. The broker may deliver to a subscriber at a lower QoS.

In QoS 2, four packets complete the handshake: PUBLISH, PUBREC, PUBREL and PUBCOMP. PUBREC means the broker received the message; PUBREL releases it; PUBCOMP completes it. This gives exactly once between the publisher and the broker. The guarantee is not automatically end-to-end to every subscriber.

Retained messages, last will, keepalive and MQTT-SN

A retained message is stored by the broker as the last known value on a topic. When a new client subscribes, the broker sends that stored message immediately. The broker keeps one retained message per topic, not a history of all messages. Sending a retained message with an empty payload deletes the stored value.

The last will is a message a client registers at connect time. If the connection closes abnormally, the broker publishes the will. A clean disconnect does not publish it. Keepalive is a timer. If the client has nothing to send, it sends PINGREQ; the broker replies PINGRESP. If no traffic arrives within about 1.5 times the keepalive period, the broker closes the connection. Keepalive detects dead links; it is not a QoS.

Clean session true starts fresh and discards subscriptions and queued state. Clean session false resumes stored state. Port 1883 carries plain MQTT; port 8883 carries MQTT inside TLS, which encrypts and authenticates the connection. Security is covered in Unit 5, IoT security and privacy.

MQTT-SN is MQTT for Sensor Networks. It runs over UDP, not TCP. It uses short two-byte topic IDs, smaller messages, sleeping nodes and a gateway that translates to a normal MQTT broker. This suits constrained nodes on 6LoWPAN links.

Worked example

Question. A sensor publishes a 20-byte payload to topic home/room1/temp every 10 s with QoS 1. The MQTT fixed header is 2 bytes. The broker forwards each message to 3 subscribers. How many bytes per hour does the broker send to all subscribers? Ignore acknowledgements and ignore topic-name bytes.

  1. One PUBLISH to one subscriber carries the 20-byte payload plus the 2-byte fixed header: 20 + 2 = 22 B.
  2. The broker sends the same message to 3 subscribers: 3 × 22 = 66 B per message.
  3. The sensor sends one message every 10 s. Messages per hour = 3600 s ÷ 10 s = 360 messages.
  4. Bytes per hour to all subscribers = 360 × 66 = 23760 B.
  5. Convert to kilobytes using 1 kB = 1000 B: 23760 ÷ 1000 = 23.76 kB per hour.

Answer. The broker sends 23760 B per hour, which is 23.76 kB per hour, to all 3 subscribers.

Exam traps

  • QoS 2 is exactly once between one client and the broker, not automatically end-to-end to every subscriber. The broker may deliver to a subscriber at a lower QoS.
  • A retained message is stored by the broker as the last value on a topic. It is not a queue of every past message sent to every future client forever.
  • Wildcard rules: + matches exactly one level, # matches all remaining levels and must be last. Topic names are case-sensitive, and a leading / adds an empty first level.
  • Clean session controls stored subscriptions and queued state. Keepalive detects a dead connection. Last will is published only on an abnormal disconnect, not a clean one.
  • Plain MQTT uses TCP port 1883; MQTT over TLS uses port 8883. MQTT-SN is the UDP variant for constrained nodes, not MQTT over TCP.

3.7 LPWAN technologies: LoRaWAN and NB-IoT

LPWAN stands for low-power wide-area network. It trades data rate for range and battery life: a small packet every few minutes can travel kilometres and run for years on one cell. Two common families are LoRaWAN, which uses unlicensed chirp radio, and NB-IoT, which uses licensed LTE spectrum. They serve water meters, gas meters, street lights, parking sensors and soil probes where Wi-Fi and BLE are too short and ordinary cellular is too costly.

You need: Chapter 3.2 on low-power radio and Chapter 3.4 on BLE for the short-range contrast.

LPWAN criteria and LoRa radio

LPWAN means low-power wide-area network. It is built for many devices that send tiny messages over kilometres and live on a battery for years. It accepts a low data rate, often 0.3 to 50 kbit/s, and a delay of seconds. The design goals are long range, low power, low cost per device and high capacity per gateway or cell. A water meter that reports one reading a day fits. A video camera does not.

  • Long range: several kilometres in a city and more in open land.
  • Low rate: a few bytes to a few hundred bytes per message.
  • Low power: years on one battery, so the radio sleeps most of the time.
  • High capacity: one gateway or cell serves thousands of nodes.

LoRa is the radio layer under LoRaWAN. It uses chirp spread spectrum: each symbol is a frequency sweep, called a chirp, across the channel. A receiver can decode chirps below the noise floor, so the link reaches far. The spreading factor SF sets how many chips one symbol uses. SF ranges from 7 to 12. Common bandwidths BW are 125, 250 and 500 kHz.

Ts = 2SF / BW

Ts is symbol duration in seconds; SF is spreading factor (7 to 12); BW is bandwidth in hertz.

A higher SF gives a longer symbol. That adds range and lowers the data rate. It also increases time on air, so the node spends more energy and occupies the channel longer. The network chooses SF and power to balance these effects.

LoRaWAN network and classes

LoRaWAN is the network protocol above LoRa. An end device is the sensor node. A gateway listens on LoRa channels and forwards frames over IP. A network server handles join requests, removes duplicate frames from several gateways, runs adaptive data rate and routes data. An application server reads the sensor payload. Figure 3.7.1 shows the chain.

  • Class A: the default. After each uplink the device opens two short receive windows. It sleeps otherwise, so it uses the least power.
  • Class B: adds scheduled receive windows driven by a gateway beacon. It gives bounded downlink delay at extra power.
  • Class C: keeps the receiver open except while transmitting. It is for mains-powered nodes, not battery nodes.

ADR, adaptive data rate, lets the network server set the spreading factor and transmit power. A node near a gateway can use SF7. A distant node can use SF12. ADR saves battery and airtime.

The unlicensed band has a duty cycle limit, for example 1% in some sub-bands. After a transmission the node must wait. This limits how often it can send.

Join has two forms. OTAA, over-the-air activation, sends a join request with DevEUI, AppEUI and AppKey; the server returns session keys. ABP, activation by personalization, preloads the keys and skips the join exchange. OTAA is preferred for large deployments. ABP is simpler for a fixed test.

NB-IoT on licensed LTE

NB-IoT is a narrowband cellular standard from 3GPP Release 13. It uses licensed LTE spectrum, so the operator controls interference and can charge a subscription. The channel is 180 kHz wide, made of 12 subcarriers spaced 15 kHz. That is one LTE resource block. Some syllabi call this LTE Cat NB1.

  • Standalone: uses refarmed GSM carriers.
  • Guard-band: uses unused edges of an LTE band.
  • In-band: uses resource blocks inside an LTE carrier.

Coverage enhancement, CE, repeats a message many times. A meter in a basement can reach a base station at high path loss. The cost is lower data rate and more energy per useful bit.

PSM, power saving mode, lets the device sleep deeply after a short active timer. It keeps its registration but cannot be paged. eDRX, extended discontinuous reception, stretches the paging cycle to minutes or hours. The device wakes less often and stays reachable. PSM saves more power; eDRX gives lower downlink delay.

The stack is cut down from LTE. PHY handles the 180 kHz radio. MAC schedules the shared channel. RLC and PDCP carry data with retransmission and ciphering. RRC controls the connection. NAS carries signalling between the device and the core network.

NB-IoT suits utility meters, smoke alarms, tracking and agricultural sensors where licensed spectrum and operator management matter.

  2. Bandwidth BW = 125 kHz = 125000 Hz.

3.8 Industrial protocols: SCADA, BACnet, WirelessHART

A refinery control room, a shopping mall's air-conditioning, and a battery-powered pressure transmitter on a pipeline all need to send measurements and receive commands, but each works under different rules. SCADA is the architecture that supervises a whole plant from one screen. BACnet is the standard that lets one vendor's chiller talk to another vendor's controller inside a building. WirelessHART puts a HART field instrument on a radio mesh that never misses its scheduled timeslot. This chapter covers what each one is made of, how it moves data, and where it stops being the right tool.

You need Chapter 3.2 for the 802.15.4 radio that WirelessHART builds on, and Chapter 3.1 for what a protocol standard is.

SCADA: supervision across a wide area

SCADA means Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition. It is a system architecture, not one protocol. A water board with pumping stations spread across a district runs one SCADA system; the link from each pump house to the control room might be Modbus, DNP3 or a radio modem, and the architecture is still SCADA.

  • Field device: a sensor or actuator in the plant.
  • RTU (Remote Terminal Unit): a rugged box that reads inputs, drives outputs and talks to the master. A PLC (Programmable Logic Controller) does the same with a local control program for fast logic.
  • MTU (Master Terminal Unit) or SCADA server: polls the RTUs and holds the tag database.
  • HMI (Human Machine Interface): the operator's screen of mimics, trends and alarms.
  • Historian: a database that stores every value with a timestamp for trends and audits.

Data acquisition runs in cycles. The RTU scans its inputs, converts raw counts to engineering units (4 mA reads as 0 %, 20 mA as 100 %), stamps the value with time and stores it. The MTU polls each RTU on a schedule: a slow poll saves bandwidth, a fast poll catches fast trips.

Control runs the other way. The operator types a setpoint on the HMI, the MTU writes it into an RTU register, and the RTU drives the output. Safety interlocks stay inside the PLC, so a lost communication link cannot create an unsafe state.

Modbus is the usual example. Modbus RTU runs on RS-485 as a master-slave bus with up to 247 slave addresses and 16-bit registers. Function code 03 reads holding registers; 06 writes one register. Classic Modbus has no timestamp and no security, so the RTU or gateway adds both.

BACnet: objects and services for buildings

BACnet (Building Automation and Control Networks, ASHRAE 135) is the vendor-neutral protocol for heating, ventilation, lighting and access control. Before it, each vendor's controller spoke only its own language, and a building was a set of islands.

Everything in BACnet is an object with properties. An Analog Input object for a room sensor has Present_Value (23.5 °C), Units (°C), Object_Name and COV_Increment. Other types include Analog Output, Binary Input, Schedule and Device.

Services are the verbs. ReadProperty and WriteProperty are confirmed: a reply is required. Who-Is and I-Am are unconfirmed broadcasts used for discovery. SubscribeCOV asks a device to report a value only when it moves by more than COV_Increment; the server then sends Change-of-Value notifications. This removes the polling traffic that would otherwise flood a slow bus.

BACnet runs over several data links. BACnet/IP puts the same messages in UDP datagrams on port 47808, so it rides ordinary Ethernet and Wi-Fi. MS/TP (Master-Slave/Token-Passing) runs on RS-485 at 9.6 kbps to 76.8 kbps; a token passes around the masters and only the token holder transmits, which keeps the bus orderly.

WirelessHART: scheduled radio in the field

WirelessHART is the wireless form of HART, the Highway Addressable Remote Transducer protocol. Wired HART superimposes a digital signal on the 4-20 mA current loop. WirelessHART keeps the HART commands and device descriptions and replaces the cable with a radio mesh. Its standard number is IEC 62591.

The radio is IEEE 802.15.4 at 2.4 GHz: 250 kbps, O-QPSK, 16 channels numbered 11 to 26.

Above the radio, WirelessHART replaces 802.15.4's CSMA/CA MAC with a TDMA data link. Time is cut into 10 ms timeslots. A superframe is a repeating pattern of timeslots. A device gets dedicated slots inside it; leftover slots are shared and used with contention. A dedicated slot is deterministic: the transmission happens at a known instant, so a control loop can rely on it.

Each timeslot also hops to a different channel. A channel blacklist removes channels busy with Wi-Fi. Hopping gives frequency diversity; the mesh gives path diversity. A packet that fails on one channel or one neighbour is retried elsewhere.

Devices form a mesh. A central Network Manager installs routes as graph routes or source routes, and a gateway bridges the mesh to the plant network. The Security Manager distributes keys; every frame is protected with AES-128 in CCM* mode and carries a message integrity code, so a forged frame is dropped at each hop.

Worked example

Question. A WirelessHART network uses a 10 ms timeslot. One superframe contains 100 timeslots. A particular field device is given 3 dedicated timeslots per superframe, and each timeslot carries 80 bytes of payload. Find the superframe duration and the device's effective data rate.

  1. Superframe duration: T_sf = N × 10 ms = 100 × 10 ms = 1000 ms = 1 s.
  2. Payload per superframe: k × B = 3 × 80 = 240 bytes.
  3. Convert to bits: 240 × 8 = 1920 bits.
  4. Effective rate: R = 1920 bits ÷ 1 s = 1920 bps.
  5. The 802.15.4 radio itself runs at 250 kbps; this device owns only 3 of 100 slots, so its share is 1.92 kbps.

Answer. The superframe lasts 1000 ms (1 s) and the device's effective rate is 1920 bps, that is 1.92 kbps.

Worked example 2

Question. A second device on the same 10 ms timeslot network gets 2 slots per superframe, each carrying 60 bytes, and the superframe has 50 timeslots. Find its effective rate.

  1. T_sf = 50 × 10 ms = 500 ms = 0.5 s.
  2. Payload = 2 × 60 = 120 bytes = 960 bits.
  3. R = 960 ÷ 0.5 = 1920 bps.

Answer. The device's effective rate is 1920 bps.

Analyze Explain how channel hopping and mesh routing give WirelessHART two independent kinds of diversity. 5 marks

3.9 Service discovery: mDNS, DNS-SD, UPnP

Plug a laptop and a printer into the same Wi-Fi router. Nobody typed the printer's IP address anywhere, yet the laptop lists the printer and printing works. Service discovery is the set of protocols that makes this happen: a device announces, or answers questions about, the services it offers on the local network, and clients find those services by name instead of by address. This chapter covers three ways to do it — mDNS, DNS-SD built on mDNS, and UPnP — used in home routers, printers, media streamers and industrial gateways.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for what a protocol is, plus the idea of IP addresses, ports and multicast from a networking course; IPv6 addressing is at Chapter 3.5.

mDNS: the same DNS packets, sent to a group

Normal DNS assumes a server address is configured, usually handed out by DHCP. A home LAN or a small factory network may have no DNS server at all, yet its devices still need to find each other. mDNS (multicast DNS) removes the server. The DNS question and answer messages keep their standard format, but hosts send them to a multicast group that every device on the link has joined. Names in this space end in .local.

  • IPv4 group 224.0.0.251; IPv6 group ff02::fb.
  • UDP port 5353 for queries and responses.
  • Domain suffix .local, for example printer.local.
  • No server address to configure: every host answers for its own names.

A client multicasts a query holding one question, say the address of printer.local. The host that owns the name answers with a response holding the matching record. The responder usually multicasts the answer so every listener can cache it, and may answer by unicast when asked to. Each record carries a TTL (time to live) in seconds; 120 s is typical for records that carry host addresses, and 4500 s (75 minutes) for other records. When the TTL runs out the entry is deleted and the name is queried again.

Two hosts can pick the same name. Before using a name, a host sends probe queries for it. If another host answers, the name is taken and the newcomer picks another, often by appending -2. After a short run of unanswered probes the name is treated as free and announced.

DNS-SD: PTR, SRV and TXT

DNS-SD (DNS-Based Service Discovery) adds no new packets. It defines how service information is written as ordinary DNS records, so a client can list services and choose one by name instead of by address. mDNS usually carries these records, but a normal unicast DNS server can hold them too. Instances are named Instance.Service.Domain, such as MyPrinter._ipp._tcp.local: _ipp is Internet Printing Protocol, _tcp is the transport (the alternative is _udp), and local is the mDNS domain.

  2. For the chosen instance, query SRV for the port and target host, and TXT for metadata.
  3. Resolve the target host to an address with an A or AAAA query.
  4. Open a TCP connection to that address and the SRV port, then speak the service protocol.

A TXT record must exist even when it is empty. Browsing means asking PTR questions; you only ask SRV and TXT once you care about a particular instance.

UPnP: SSDP discovery, then description, control and events

UPnP is a bigger stack than mDNS: it covers addressing, discovery, description, control, eventing and presentation. Discovery uses SSDP (Simple Service Discovery Protocol), which sends HTTP-style messages over UDP to the multicast group 239.255.255.250, port 1900 (IPv6 group ff02::c). A device announces itself with a NOTIFY ssdp:alive message when it joins, and ssdp:byebye when it leaves. A client, called a control point, multicasts an M-SEARCH with a search target header; the device replies by unicast with 200 OK, carrying a LOCATION: URL and a unique service name.

The client then fetches that URL over HTTP. The description XML gives the device type, a friendly name, and a list of services. Each service has a service type such as urn:schemas-upnp-org:service:WANIPConnection:1, plus a controlURL and an eventSubURL. Control is SOAP over HTTP POST to the controlURL, with an action name and arguments; the reply is a SOAP envelope. Eventing uses GENA: the client SUBSCRIBEs to the eventSubURL, gets a subscription ID and a timeout, and the device then sends NOTIFY messages when state changes.

Home routers use UPnP for port forwarding, media servers and smart TVs use it for streaming, and NAS boxes expose services with it. Exposing a control URL beyond the LAN is a known security risk, because many control actions need no authentication.

Implementations you will meet

  • Avahi: the mDNS/DNS-SD daemon on most Linux systems; avahi-browse -a lists services on the link.
  • Bonjour: Apple's implementation of mDNS and DNS-SD; some syllabi call the whole idea Bonjour.
  • Android's NSD API and Apple's network frameworks expose discovery to apps.
  • miniupnpd and libupnp are common UPnP stacks in routers and NAS boxes.
  • Windows uses WS-Discovery for many printers, which is a different protocol.

Worked example

Question. A laptop browses for printers with an mDNS query for _ipp._tcp.local. At time t = 0 the printer answers with three records: a PTR record naming MyPrinter._ipp._tcp.local with TTL 4500 s, an SRV record giving port 631 and target printer.local with TTL 120 s, and a TXT record with txtvers=1 and TTL 4500 s. (a) State what each record gives the client. (b) At t = 200 s the laptop still holds all three in cache. Which are valid, and how many seconds remain on each?

  1. PTR record: maps the service type to the instance name MyPrinter._ipp._tcp.local. This is what browsing returns.
  2. SRV record: gives port 631 and target host printer.local (with priority and weight for choosing among copies).
  3. TXT record: gives metadata as key=value pairs, here txtvers=1.
  4. Remaining lifetime = TTL − elapsed time, in seconds. PTR: 4500 s − 200 s = 4300 s left.
  5. SRV: 120 s − 200 s = −80 s, so it expired 80 s ago. TXT: 4500 s − 200 s = 4300 s left.
  6. The client may use the cached instance name and metadata, but must re-query SRV before connecting.
  7. Connect to printer.local on port 631 once the SRV and the host address are resolved.

Answer. PTR gives the instance name, SRV gives port 631 and target printer.local, TXT gives metadata; after 200 s the PTR and TXT have 4300 s left and the SRV expired 80 s ago.

Exam traps

  • mDNS is only a transport for DNS-format messages; the service listing comes from the DNS-SD record convention. A host that answers mDNS queries is not automatically browseable as a service.
  • UPnP discovery uses SSDP over UDP port 1900 to 239.255.255.250, not mDNS on port 5353. Writing 'UPnP uses mDNS' loses marks.
  • Multicast stays on the link. The group 224.0.0.251 is not forwarded by routers unless configured, so devices on two subnets cannot discover each other.
  • SRV gives a port and a target host name, not an IP address; the A or AAAA record is still needed for the target.
  • TTL is a countdown in seconds, not a timestamp. A record cached with TTL 120 s at t = 0 is stale at t = 120 s, and a negative remaining time means expired.

Takeaways

  1. Standard protocols let devices from different vendors work together; a vendor-only protocol creates isolated islands.
  2. IEEE 802.15.4 provides the low-power radio and MAC foundation for ZigBee, 6LoWPAN and WirelessHART.
  3. ZigBee and Z-Wave suit home and industrial control because mesh routing extends coverage at low power.
  4. BLE is not just Bluetooth with less power; it uses advertisements, connection events and GATT characteristics for small data.
  5. 6LoWPAN plus RPL lets IPv6 run over tiny IEEE 802.15.4 frames and routes packets through lossy mesh networks.
  6. MQTT's broker and topic model decouples many sensors from many applications and gives selectable delivery guarantees.
  7. LPWAN chooses range and battery life over data rate; LoRaWAN uses unlicensed chirp radio, NB-IoT uses licensed LTE.
  8. Industrial IoT uses different protocols by job: SCADA supervises, BACnet controls buildings, WirelessHART handles field instruments.
  9. Service discovery removes manual address entry by letting devices find services through mDNS, DNS-SD or UPnP.

WirelessHART is an industrial wireless field-network standard using IEEE 802.15.4, mesh routing and scheduled channel hopping.
Z-Wave
Z-Wave is a sub-GHz home-automation mesh protocol used mainly for lighting, locks and sensors.
ZigBee
ZigBee is a low-power mesh protocol built on IEEE 802.15.4 for control and sensor applications.
ZigBee coordinator
A ZigBee coordinator is the node that forms a ZigBee network, assigns addresses and often acts as trust center.
