1.1 Definition, characteristics and evolution of IoT

IoT, the Internet of Things, is a network of identifiable physical things that sense, act and exchange data over networks to deliver services with little or no direct human control. The idea exists because cheap chips, wireless links and cloud services let ordinary objects report facts and receive commands. A smart water meter in a city, a home thermostat and a crop sensor in a field are common uses. This chapter fixes the definition, names the characteristics that separate IoT from a simple connected gadget, and traces how machine links, tags, sensor networks, IP, cloud platforms, edge computing and AI built today's systems.

Definition: thing, internet, service

IoT is a network of identifiable physical things that sense, act and exchange data over networks to deliver services with little or no direct human control. Three words carry the definition.

A thing is a physical object with an identity, such as a meter, lamp, lock or pump. The identity lets the network name that object and its data. The physical design of things and their parts is in 1.2.

The internet is a network of networks that moves data using Internet Protocol (IP) addresses and routing. In IoT the internet may end at a gateway; the last link to a thing can be short-range radio. Data can still travel beyond one cable or room.

A service is the useful result: a bill, a warning, a closed valve, a cooled room. A sensor alone is not IoT. The system must use the sensed data and often act on the physical world.

Little or no direct human control means rules and software run the loop. A person may set a threshold once; after that the system reports, decides and acts. This separates IoT from a person reading a meter and typing the value. Some syllabi call this definition and characteristics of IoT, foundations, or evolution. The logical functions behind the service are in 1.3.

Characteristics that define IoT

Eight characteristics appear in most definitions. They create design trade-offs.

  • Connectivity: a thing must have a communication path, wired or wireless, to send data and receive commands.
  • Identity: each thing has a unique name or address, so data can be tied to the right object. A reading without identity is only a number.
  • Sensing: the thing measures a property of the world, such as flow, temperature, light or motion.
  • Actuation: the thing changes the world, such as closing a valve, switching a lamp or locking a door.
  • Intelligence: some processing decides what to send or do. It can be a simple threshold; it need not be machine learning.
  • Energy limit: many things run on batteries or harvested power. Radio and sensing use energy, so designers use sleep schedules and send summaries, not every raw sample.
  • Heterogeneity: devices differ in processors, radios, power and data formats. Gateways and common data models make them work together.
  • Dynamic operation: things join, leave, move and fail. The network and software must handle changes without a person reconfiguring every device.

These characteristics explain why IoT is more than a sensor with a Wi-Fi chip. Sensor and actuator details are in Unit 2; enabling technologies are in 1.6.

Evolution from M2M to edge AI

Evolution is a chain of borrowings, not one invention.

Early machine-to-machine (M2M) links connected machines directly for remote monitoring and control, often in factories and utilities. They worked without internet-scale addressing.

Radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags identified objects by radio. Supply chains and toll plazas used them, but a tag mainly carried identity, not a full sensing network.

Wireless sensor networks (WSN) put small sensing nodes into multi-hop radio networks. Battery life limited them, and they often used custom protocols.

IP and cloud changed scale. Embedded devices could use IP or reach an IP gateway, and cloud servers stored and analysed data from many sites. This is the internet-scale step.

IoT platforms made device management practical: registration, dashboards, rules, APIs and billing. A platform is the highlighted stage in the figure.

Edge and AI moved some decisions near the thing. Local processing cuts delay, saves bandwidth and can keep raw data private. Small machine-learning models can classify vibration or images on the device.

M2M versus IoT is compared in 1.7. Impact is summarised only in one line here; details are in 1.8.

Worked example

Question. A city installs 2,000 smart water meters. Each meter sends 24 readings per day. Each reading is 40 bytes. Find the raw data per day in kB. Take 1 kB = 1000 bytes. Ignore packet headers and retransmissions.

  1. Readings per day = 2,000 meters × 24 readings per meter per day = 48,000 readings per day.
  2. Bytes per day = 48,000 readings per day × 40 bytes per reading = 1,920,000 bytes per day.
  3. Data in kB = 1,920,000 bytes per day ÷ 1000 bytes per kB = 1,920 kB per day.

Answer. The raw data is 1,920 kB per day.

Exam traps

  • Do not define IoT as only internet-connected sensors. The service and the action on the physical world are part of the definition.
  • A sensor alone is not an IoT system. It needs identity, a communication path and a use of its data; an actuator alone is also not enough.
  • M2M is not a synonym for IoT. M2M often uses direct links; IoT adds internet-scale addressing, cloud services and human-facing applications.
  • Use 1 kB = 1000 bytes in this chapter's data examples. Mixing in 1024 changes the answer.
  • RFID proves identity, not necessarily sensing. A tag can say which object it is without measuring flow, temperature or light.

1.2 Physical design of IoT: things and devices

Physical design names the actual things and devices in an IoT system and the parts inside them. It answers the hardware question: what is this made of? A thing is a physical object with an identity. A device is the engineered package that adds sensing, computation, communication and action. Every deployment uses this layer: a soil-moisture node, a smart meter, an RFID-tagged parcel. The choices made here fix cost, size and battery life before any software is written.

You need Chapter 1.1 for what IoT is and why identifiable things matter.

Thing, device and physical design

Physical design is the hardware answer to a simple question: what is this IoT thing made of? It lists the parts, their package, their power supply and their connections. It does not describe what functions the system performs; that is the logical design.

A thing is a physical object with an identity. The identity may be an RFID tag on a parcel, a serial number in a chip, or a network address. A device is the engineering package that gives a thing sensing, computation, communication and action. One thing may contain several devices. A smart water meter is a thing; inside it, the electronics box with a flow sensor, a microcontroller, a radio module and a battery is the device.

A gateway is a device that collects data from many things and forwards it to the internet. It is the bridge between short-range radios and the wide-area network.

The physical design fixes the bill of materials: which sensor, which control unit, which radio, which battery. It also fixes the enclosure and the duty cycle. Those choices decide cost, size and battery life.

Five parts of an IoT device

Every active IoT device has five jobs, and usually five parts.

The control unit runs the logic. It reads sensors, decides, drives actuators and formats data for the radio. A microcontroller is a small chip with memory and input/output pins; an Arduino board and an ESP8266 module are common microcontroller examples. A single-board computer such as Raspberry Pi runs an operating system and is used when more computing is needed. Programming these boards is covered in Unit 4.

Sensors convert a physical quantity into an electrical signal. A soil-moisture sensor gives a voltage that depends on water content. Actuators convert an electrical signal into action: a relay switches a pump, a motor opens a valve.

The communication module sends data to a gateway or a cloud. It may use Wi-Fi, Bluetooth Low Energy, LoRa or a cellular link. Radio standards are in Unit 3.

The power source supplies energy. Mains power is used where wiring exists. Batteries are used where it does not. Energy harvesting takes small amounts of power from light, vibration or heat.

A passive RFID tag is a special case: it has an antenna and a chip but no battery. The reader's radio wave powers it. It is a thing with identity, but it is not a full device with sensing and actuation.

Power budget and battery life

The first calculation in physical design is the power budget. A device rarely runs at full current all the time. It wakes, measures, transmits and sleeps. The duty cycle is the fraction of time spent active.

I<sub>avg</sub> = (I<sub>active</sub> × t<sub>active</sub> + I<sub>sleep</sub> × t<sub>sleep</sub>) ÷ T

Iavg is the average current in mA; Iactive is the active current in mA; tactive is the active time in seconds; Isleep is the sleep current in mA; tsleep is the sleep time in seconds; T = tactive + tsleep is the period in seconds.

Life = Capacity ÷ Iavg

Life is the battery life in hours; Capacity is the battery capacity in mAh; Iavg is the average current in mA.

Use the same unit for every current. 20 µA = 0.02 mA. Ignore conversion loss unless the question gives an efficiency.

Worked example

Question. A soil-moisture node uses a 3.7 V, 2,000 mAh battery. Sleep current is 20 µA. Active current is 30 mA for 2 s every 1 hour. Estimate the average current and the battery life. Ignore conversion loss.

  1. Write the period T = 1 hour = 3600 s. Active time tactive = 2 s. Sleep time tsleep = 3600 − 2 = 3598 s.
  2. Convert sleep current to mA: 20 µA = 0.02 mA.
  3. Active charge per period = 30 mA × 2 s = 60 mA·s. Sleep charge per period = 0.02 mA × 3598 s = 71.96 mA·s.
  4. Total charge per period = 60 + 71.96 = 131.96 mA·s. Average current = 131.96 mA·s ÷ 3600 s = 0.03666 mA ≈ 0.0367 mA.
  5. Battery life = 2000 mAh ÷ 0.0367 mA = 54495.9 h ≈ 54,500 h.
  6. In years: 54495.9 h ÷ 8760 h/year = 6.22 years ≈ 6.2 years.

Answer. Average current ≈ 0.0367 mA; battery life ≈ 54,500 h ≈ 6.2 years.

Exam traps

  • Do not confuse physical design with logical design. Physical design is the hardware parts; logical design is the functions the system performs.
  • A sensor alone is not an IoT system. It needs a control unit, a communication module and a power source to become a device.
  • Mix mA and µA at your peril. 20 µA is 0.02 mA, not 20 mA. Convert every current to the same unit before averaging.
  • Average current is not active current. A device that wakes for 2 s in an hour spends almost all its time asleep, so the sleep current can dominate the average.
  • A passive RFID tag has no battery. The reader supplies its power. Do not call it a full device with sensing and actuation.

1.3 Logical design of IoT: functional blocks

The logical design of an IoT system lists the jobs it must do — identify, sense, act, process, communicate, serve, manage and secure — without saying which chip, board or radio does them. It exists so a design can be argued, costed and compared before any hardware is bought, and so a system built in 2015 can be checked against one built today. Engineers use it as a checklist, buyers use it to compare two products that look identical on the shelf.

You need: 1.1 for what makes a thing an IoT thing, and 1.2 for the physical parts that carry out these functions.

Two designs of the same system

The physical design names the parts you can hold: sensor, control unit, radio module, battery. The logical design names the jobs the system must do. The same jobs appear whether the radio is Wi-Fi, LoRa or a wire, and whether the processor is a low-cost microcontroller or a cloud server.

This separation exists because hardware ages fast and functions do not. A cold-chain monitoring system built years ago and one built today share almost no parts. Both identify their boxes, sense temperature, decide whether it is too warm, move the reading to a server and raise an alert. Designing at the level of functions lets a review ask whether every necessary job is covered, before anyone buys a board.

Some syllabi call this the logical design, some the functional view, and some simply IoT functional blocks. All three mean the same thing.

A functional block is a job, not a part. One device often does several blocks at once: a sensor node identifies itself, senses, filters and transmits. One block often spans many devices: the service block may run on three servers. Treat each block as a question the system must answer, and the hardware as one possible answer. The layers that organise where these blocks sit are covered in 1.5.

Two blocks are not in the main line of flow: management and security act on the others. Keep them in the list. Dropping them is the most common design error.

The blocks, one by one

  • Identification gives every thing a name and an address. The name (a serial number, an EPC, a UUID) says which thing it is; the address (an IP address or a URI) says where to reach it.
  • Sensing collects the state of the thing or its surroundings: temperature in °C, a GPS fix, a door open or closed.
  • Actuation changes the physical world: a relay starts a compressor, a valve opens, a buzzer sounds. It is sensing in reverse.
  • Processing turns raw readings into meaning: average ten samples, drop a spike, compare with a limit of 8 °C, decide "excursion".
  • Communication moves data from the place it is produced to the place it is used.
  • Services present useful output: an alert on a phone, a dashboard, a reply from an API, a line in a billing system.
  • Management keeps the system running: set the sampling interval, push new firmware, detect a sensor that has gone silent, log battery level.
  • Security protects all of the above: prove a device is genuine, encrypt the link, decide who may read the data.

Processing is not tied to one place. A first pass often runs on the device, close to the sensor, to cut noise and traffic. A second pass runs on a server, where data from many devices is combined. Same block, two locations. That choice is an engineering trade-off, not a change of design.

Protocols, boards and radios are deliberately left out of this list. They are the implementation. The protocols that carry the communication block are in Unit 3, and actuator hardware is in Unit 2.

The path from thing to service

The blocks are not a random list. They describe one path that data follows, and that path is what the figure below draws.

  1. Identify — the device presents its name and address when it joins the network.
  2. Sense — the sensor produces a value with a unit and a timestamp.
  3. Process (first pass) — filter, average, convert units, discard readings that have not changed.
  4. Communicate — move the payload over the network.
  5. Process (second pass) — combine with other devices, compare against a model, decide.
  6. Serve — send an alert, update a dashboard, write a row to a database.

Actuation is not a step after services. It is a return path from the decision back to the physical device: when processing decides, the act block drives an actuator at the device end, for example the cooling unit restarts. The service block can inform a person at the same time.

D = N × 86400 ÷ T

D is the number of data items produced per day; N is the number of data-producing sources; T is the sampling interval in seconds; 86400 is the number of seconds in one day. This formula assumes every source produces exactly one data item every T seconds and that all sources share the same interval T.

Management sets T and then checks that D items actually arrive; a device whose count falls to zero has failed. Security applies to every block, with particular attention to identification, communication, services and management, because those are places where a stranger can interfere. Both act across the path rather than inside it.

Worked example

Question. A cold-chain van carries 6 temperature sensors, 1 GPS unit and 2 door sensors. Each sensor produces one data item every 5 minutes. Find the total number of data items the van produces per day, and name the functional block that decides a reading of 9 °C needs an alert.

  1. Count the data-producing sources: N = 6 temperature + 1 GPS + 2 door = 9 sources.
  2. Convert the sampling interval to seconds: T = 5 minutes = 5 × 60 = 300 s.
  3. Samples per source per day = 86400 ÷ T = 86400 ÷ 300 = 288 samples.
  4. Data items per day D = N × 288 = 9 × 288 = 2592 data items.
  5. The processing block compares 9 °C with the 8 °C limit and decides on the alert. The services block then presents it to a person.

Answer. The van produces 2,592 data items per day, and the processing block makes the alert decision.

Worked example 2

Question. A shop installs 3 door sensors and 2 temperature sensors. Every sensor sends one reading every 30 seconds. How many data items reach the server in one day?

  1. Data-producing sources: N = 3 + 2 = 5.
  2. Sampling interval: T = 30 s.
  3. Samples per source per day = 86400 ÷ 30 = 2880.
  4. Data items per day D = 5 × 2880 = 14400.

Answer. 14,400 data items per day.

Exam traps

  • Asked for functional blocks, do not answer Arduino, Raspberry Pi, Wi-Fi or MQTT. Functions are verbs: identify, sense, act, process, communicate, serve, manage, secure.
  • Identification is not only addressing. A name says which thing it is; an address says where to reach it. The one block covers both, and neither replaces the other.
  • Do not merge processing with services. Deciding that 9 °C is too warm is processing; showing that decision on a phone is a service.
  • Management and security are blocks, not afterthoughts. A question asking which block is missing usually wants one of these two.
  • Unit slip: 5 minutes is 300 s, not 50 s. Convert the interval to seconds before dividing into 86400.

1.4 IoT communication models and APIs

An IoT system rarely acts alone. Devices, gateways and cloud services must exchange readings and commands in a repeatable way. A communication model is the pattern of that exchange, such as one device asking and another answering, or many devices publishing to a shared topic. An API, short for application programming interface, is the contract a program uses to reach such a service. This chapter names the common models and shows how REST and WebSocket APIs make them usable by ordinary software. It is the bridge between the functional blocks of an IoT system and the protocols studied later.

You need 1.3 for the functional blocks; a basic client-server idea helps.

Four communication models

A communication model is the rule for who sends, who receives and how the exchange ends. Four patterns cover most IoT systems.

  • Request-response: a client sends a request to a server and waits for one reply. The server keeps no session state between calls. A sensor reading lookup is the common case. CoAP is an example of this model.
  • Publish-subscribe: a publisher sends a message to a broker under a topic, a short name such as room1/temperature. The broker forwards it to every subscriber of that topic. Publisher and subscriber do not know each other's addresses. This decoupling lets a new dashboard subscribe without changing the device. MQTT is an example of this model.
  • Push-pull: a producer pushes data into a buffer or queue; a consumer pulls it when ready. The queue absorbs bursts. A camera may push frames to storage while an analytics job pulls them later.
  • Exclusive pair: two parties keep one dedicated connection for the whole session. Each side can send while the connection is open, as in a phone call. A control link between a robot arm and its controller may use this pattern.

The model is not the radio. The same model can run over Wi-Fi, Ethernet or a mobile network.

APIs expose the models

An API (application programming interface) is a named set of operations a program may call, with agreed inputs and outputs. A device API might offer readTemperature, setSwitch and listDevices. The API hides whether the server talks to the device over Wi-Fi or a serial cable.

A REST-style API (representational state transfer) uses HTTP and treats data as resources with URLs. The common methods are:

  • GET: read a resource. GET /devices/17 returns device 17's data.
  • PUT: replace or update a resource at a known URL.
  • POST: create a new resource, or send a command for processing.
  • DELETE: remove a resource.

A successful GET returns status 200 OK with a body such as {"value": 31.5, "unit": "°C"}. The figure shows this path.

WebSocket is a different API style. The client opens one connection and both sides can send messages at any time. This full-duplex link suits live dashboards and fast control loops better than repeated polling.

REST usually carries request-response. Publish-subscribe APIs expose publish(topic, message) and subscribe(topic). MQTT is the common IoT protocol for this model; CoAP is used for constrained request-response work. See 1.5 for where APIs sit in a layered design.

Choosing a model

Choose the model from the job, not from fashion. Request-response fits a client that needs one fresh answer now, such as a mobile app checking a door lock. Publish-subscribe fits one reading that many consumers need, such as a temperature sent to a dashboard, a logger and an alarm. Push-pull fits a fast producer and a slower consumer, such as camera frames and an analytics job. Exclusive pair fits continuous two-way control, such as a robot arm and its controller.

The choice changes traffic. Polling means asking again at fixed intervals. Fifty clients polling every ten seconds create steady load even when nothing changes. A publish-subscribe broker forwards every message it is given, so the publisher decides how often to publish — often only when a value changes. If the broker keeps the last message (the retained message in MQTT), a new subscriber gets that value at once. The cost is a broker to run and secure. Record the request and response sizes before you promise a data plan. Also record how many clients subscribe or poll. These two numbers decide whether the link is cheap or congested. The API style fixes what the client sees, not what sits behind it. A REST endpoint backed by a broker is still request-response for the client, unless the client itself subscribes to a topic.

Worked example

Question. A REST API is polled by 50 clients every 10 s. Each request is 300 bytes and each response is 500 bytes. Find traffic per hour in MB, taking 1 MB = 10^6 bytes.

  1. One poll exchanges one request and one response: 300 bytes + 500 bytes = 800 bytes per poll.
  2. Polls per hour per client: 3600 s ÷ 10 s = 360 polls.
  3. Traffic for one client per hour: 800 bytes × 360 = 288,000 bytes.
  4. Traffic for 50 clients per hour: 50 × 288,000 bytes = 14,400,000 bytes.
  5. Convert to MB: 14,400,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 14.4 MB.

Answer. The traffic is 14.4 MB per hour.

Exam traps

  • Do not call MQTT request-response. MQTT is publish-subscribe through a broker.
  • An API is an interface, not a radio protocol. The same API can sit above Wi-Fi, Ethernet or a mobile link.
  • POST and PUT both write data, but POST creates or processes while PUT replaces at a known URL.
  • Polling traffic counts both request and response bytes. Forgetting the response under-counts badly. In worked example 1 it gives 5.4 MB instead of 14.4 MB per hour.
  • Publish-subscribe decoupling does not remove the broker. Every message still passes through it.

The publisher sends to a broker under a topic and does not store subscriber addresses. The broker forwards each message to current subscribers. A new subscriber joins the topic without changing the publisher.

Understand Give one difference between REST GET and REST POST. 3 marks

GET reads a resource and should not change server state. POST creates a resource or sends a command for processing, so it may change state.

Apply A REST API is polled by 20 clients every 5 s. Each request is 200 bytes and each response is 400 bytes. Find traffic per hour in MB, with 1 MB = 10^6 bytes. 4 marks

Bytes per poll = 200 + 400 = 600 bytes. Polls per hour = 3600 ÷ 5 = 720. One client per hour = 600 × 720 = 432,000 bytes. Twenty clients = 20 × 432,000 = 8,640,000 bytes = 8.64 MB per hour.

Analyze A dashboard with 500 viewers polls one sensor every second. Suggest a better communication model and give the reason. 5 marks

Use publish-subscribe. The sensor publishes once per update to a broker, and the 500 viewers subscribe to the topic. This removes 500 separate polling requests per second: the sensor publishes once per update instead of answering 500 queries. The saving is on the upstream side — one publish instead of 500 requests. The broker still sends one copy to each of the 500 subscribers per update, so downstream traffic per viewer stays roughly the same. The cost is running a broker.

Analyze Why is WebSocket often better than REST polling for a live control loop? 4 marks

WebSocket keeps one open, full-duplex connection, so either side can send immediately. REST polling repeats a request and response cycle, adding delay and overhead when values change quickly.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In industry, API gateways add authentication, rate limits and logging in front of device APIs. Device shadows store the last known state so a client can read it when a device is offline. Newer designs combine REST for setup with WebSocket or publish-subscribe for live data. Useful search terms are REST resource modelling, WebSocket, MQTT topic design, API gateway and device shadow.

1.5 IoT architectures and layered reference models

An IoT architecture is a plan for where data is sensed, moved, stored, processed and used. It breaks a large system into layers with clear jobs, so teams can build and test each part without breaking the rest. The same system can be drawn as three layers, five layers, or a seven-level reference model. This chapter shows those models, places edge, fog and cloud correctly, and gives design principles. It is used when you plan a real deployment, such as thousands of Chennai water meters or a factory sensor network.

You need: 1.2, 1.3 and 1.4.

Three layers and five layers

An IoT system has many small devices, several networks and many users. Architecture divides the work into layers so each part has one job. A change in a dashboard then does not force a change in a sensor. The three-layer model is the simplest.

  • Perception layer: sensors and actuators touch the physical world. It collects readings and can act.
  • Network layer: communication modules, gateways and routers move data.
  • Application layer: software gives reports, alarms and controls to users.

The five-layer model keeps perception at the bottom and application near the top. It calls the second layer transport; this does the work of the network layer. It adds processing in the middle for storage and analysis. It adds business at the top for rules, decisions and value. Business is the top layer only in the five-layer model. Do not mix the layer names of the two models.

The logical design describes functions; a layered architecture arranges those functions into levels. Use the three-layer model for a quick sketch and the five-layer model when storage, analysis or business rules need their own place.

Seven-level reference model

Some syllabi call this an architectural overview; others call it a layered reference model. The seven-level model, often called the IoT World Forum model, gives more detail. Read it from bottom to top.

  • Physical devices and controllers: sensors, actuators and machines that produce data or act.
  • Connectivity: links, gateways and protocols that carry data.
  • Edge computing: local processing near devices for filtering, fast alarms and control.
  • Data accumulation: storage of current and historical data.
  • Data abstraction: schemas and views that make stored data usable.
  • Application: dashboards, reports and apps.
  • Collaboration: people and processes that share results across teams or organisations.

Management and security cut across every level. Management covers device setup, monitoring and updates. Security covers identity, encryption and access control. They are not extra layers. A weak sensor and a weak cloud account are both entry points. Only a few universities teach the seven levels in detail; know the order and the two cross-cutting concerns. The levels are not a protocol stack; they divide responsibilities.

Placement and design principles

Where processing sits matters. Edge is on or near the device: quick decisions and less traffic. Fog is a local server or gateway cluster: regional storage and analysis. Cloud is central: large storage, long history and many users. A good design pushes only needed data upward. Keep control loops at the edge when delay matters. Cloud and fog implementation is in Unit 5.

Design principles guide the layout.

  • Scalability: adding devices must not need a redesign.
  • Modularity: replace one sensor or gateway without rebuilding all.
  • Interoperability: different vendors' devices exchange data through standard formats.
  • Security and privacy: protect data and personal information at every layer.
  • Reliability: keep working when a link fails; use retries and local buffers.
  • Energy efficiency: send less and sleep more for battery devices.
  • Open standards: avoid lock-in and make testing easier.

Use these principles to judge an architecture, not just count layers. A design that sends every raw reading to the cloud may be simple but costly at scale.

Worked example

Question. A three-layer design has 4,000 sensors. Each sensor sends 1 packet per minute. The network layer aggregates 20 packets into one uplink. Find the uplinks per minute.

  1. Packets from one sensor in one minute = 1 packet per minute.
  2. Total packets per minute = 4,000 sensors × 1 packet per minute = 4,000 packets per minute.
  3. Aggregation ratio = 20 packets per uplink.
  4. Uplinks per minute = 4,000 packets per minute ÷ 20 packets per uplink = 200 uplinks per minute.

Answer. The network layer sends 200 uplinks per minute.

Exam traps

  • Business is the top layer only in the five-layer model. In the three-layer model the top layer is application.
  • The second layer is called network in the three-layer model and transport in the five-layer model; they refer to the same data-movement job.
  • Management and security are cross-cutting concerns in the seven-level model, not extra layers on top.
  • More layers do not automatically mean a better design. Match the model to the system's real needs.
  • Aggregating 20 packets into 1 uplink reduces uplinks to 1/20; it does not reduce the packets sent by sensors.

1.6 IoT enabling technologies

A single clever idea is not enough to build IoT. It works because several mature technologies arrived at low cost at the same time: cheap sensors and RFID tags to sense and identify, microcontrollers to compute locally, radio links to carry data, cellular networks to reach anywhere, cloud servers to store it, and analytics to make sense of it. Each technology was built for another purpose and then reused. A Bengaluru fleet tracker combines all of them: a GPS chip, a microcontroller, a SIM, a cloud dashboard and a fuel-wastage report. This chapter takes the technologies one at a time and shows the job each one does.

You need: 1.2 for the parts inside a device, and 1.5 for where each part sits in a layered architecture.

Sensing, identity and local computing

A sensor converts a physical quantity — temperature, motion, light — into an electrical signal. Cheap micro-machined sensors are the first reason IoT became practical: measurement turned into a component costing a few rupees instead of a laboratory instrument.

RFID supplies identity. A reader radiates a radio field; a passive tag harvests that energy, wakes up and returns the ID stored in its chip. No battery, no contact, no line of sight. The FASTag on an Indian car windscreen is an RFID tag read at a toll lane. RFID answers “which thing is this”, not “how hot is it”.

A wireless sensor network (WSN) is many small nodes, each with a sensor, a radio, a microcontroller and a battery. Radio range is short, so nodes forward each other's packets hop by hop until they reach a gateway. The hardware side is in Unit 2.

Embedded computing is the third piece. A microcontroller is one chip holding a CPU, flash program memory, RAM and input/output pins; it runs a single program forever, draws milliamps and costs tens of rupees. A single-board computer runs Linux when the job needs a camera, a screen or a full network stack. Local computing matters because a decision may be needed in milliseconds, the link may be down, and shipping every raw sample wastes radio energy.

The radio links that carry IoT data

A radio link is chosen on three numbers: range, data rate and energy per bit. No standard is best on all three, so IoT uses a family of them.

  • IEEE 802.15.4: 2.4 GHz, up to 250 kbit/s, short range, years on two AA cells. It defines only the physical and MAC layers.
  • ZigBee adds a mesh network layer and application profiles on top of 802.15.4; used in smart meters and home lighting.
  • Bluetooth Classic carries a continuous stream such as a headset. Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) sends small bursts and runs a year on a coin cell — fitness bands, shop beacons.
  • Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) gives megabits per second but draws too much current for a battery node, so it suits mains-powered devices.
  • LoRaWAN: unlicensed sub-GHz band, a few kilometres in a city, a few kbit/s, years of battery life.
  • NB-IoT: narrowband radio inside licensed cellular spectrum, run by the mobile operator. Built for massive numbers of low-throughput devices, long battery life, deep coverage into basements, and low device cost. It needs a SIM and a data plan.
  • Mobile internet: LTE is the 4G packet-data standard from 3GPP that made cellular data cheap enough for machines. 5G adds three service classes — enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB, fast downloads), massive machine-type communication (mMTC, very many low-rate devices) and ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC, factory control).

Protocol stacks and frame formats are in Unit 3.

Cloud, big data, edge and fog

Cloud computing rents compute and storage by the hour. A device usually never contacts a database directly; it publishes to a broker, and the cloud platform authenticates the device, stores the readings and serves them to phone apps. See Unit 5.

Big data analytics handles streams too large or too fast for one machine. Stream processing keeps a moving average over the last ten minutes; batch processing runs a nightly report over months of history.

Edge computing moves computation to the gateway or to the device itself, and fog computing is the older name for the same idea placed at intermediate nodes between device and cloud. Three reasons: latency, bandwidth cost, and continued operation when the link drops — a Chennai flat keeps its last reading on the local gateway during a power cut and uploads when the network returns.

D = N × S × (86,400 ÷ T)

D is bytes uploaded per day; N is the number of devices; S is bytes per sample; T is seconds between two samples from one device; 86,400 is the number of seconds in a day.

Worked example

Question. A narrowband sensor sends one sample of 512 bytes every 30 minutes. There are 10,000 such devices. Find the total uplink data per day in MB, taking 1 MB = 10^6 bytes.

  1. Samples per device per day = 24 × 60 ÷ 30 = 48 samples.
  2. Bytes per device per day = 512 bytes × 48 = 24,576 bytes.
  3. Total bytes per day = 10,000 × 24,576 = 245,760,000 bytes.
  4. Convert to MB: 245,760,000 ÷ 10^6 = 245.76 MB per day.

Answer. The 10,000 devices upload 245.76 MB per day in total.

Exam traps

  • NB-IoT is not fast 5G. A question about video, megabits per second or millisecond latency is not an NB-IoT question — NB-IoT is for very many slow devices with years of battery life.
  • IEEE 802.15.4 and ZigBee are not the same standard. 802.15.4 covers the physical and MAC layers only; ZigBee adds network and application layers on top.
  • 1 MB means 10^6 bytes in these sums. If the question says MiB, divide by 2^20 = 1,048,576 instead, and the answer changes.
  • RFID identifies a thing; a sensor measures a quantity. Writing that an RFID tag “senses temperature” loses the mark.
  • LoRaWAN works in unlicensed spectrum with no SIM; NB-IoT needs licensed spectrum, an operator, a SIM and a data plan. The examiner checks which constraint you noticed.

1.7 IoT versus M2M communication

Two machines can exchange data with no person in the loop. That is machine-to-machine, or M2M, communication. Internet of Things (IoT) does that too, but adds internet-scale addressing, cloud platforms, analytics and applications that people use. The two are related, not identical. This chapter defines each, compares the communication path, scale, standards, protocols, data use and ownership, and shows how far the data volumes differ. Fleet tracking, smart meters and factory control all sit somewhere on this spectrum.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for what makes a thing an IoT thing, and Chapter 1.4 for the interaction patterns that both M2M and IoT links use.

M2M: two machines, one link

M2M communication is the exchange of data between machines, with no human deciding each message. A sensor node reports to a controller. A controller trips a relay. A vending machine sends a stock-out alert. In each case the message serves one function, and the network exists only for that function.

The classic M2M path is point-to-point. Two endpoints, or a small set of endpoints and one collector, are joined by a serial cable, a private radio link, a GSM or GPRS modem, or a short-range link. Addressing is local: an equipment ID, a Modbus register number, the phone number of a SIM. The endpoint usually has no globally routable IP address.

Protocols are often proprietary or drawn from industrial automation, such as Modbus, DNP3 and the SCADA family you meet in Unit 3. Payloads are small and fixed-format: one reading, one alarm, one status word. The data is used at once for control and then dropped, or kept in a local log. Nobody builds a dashboard on it.

Ownership is single. One firm owns the machines, the link and the data. Scale runs from a few tens to a few thousand endpoints inside one estate.

Example: a diesel generator in an apartment block has a GSM modem that sends a 140-byte fault SMS to the facility manager when oil pressure drops. One device, one SIM, one message, one action. That is a complete M2M system.

What IoT adds

IoT keeps the machine-to-machine core and changes four things around it.

  • Addressing: each thing is identifiable and reachable through IP, or through a gateway that speaks IP upstream, such as 6LoWPAN, NB-IoT or LoRaWAN.
  • Reach: data lands on a cloud platform, not only on a local controller. Many devices feed one platform and many applications read from it. That is many-to-many, not point-to-point.
  • Data use: readings are stored, joined with other data, analysed, and turned into alerts, dashboards, predictions and billing. Chapter 1.6 lists the enabling technologies; Unit 5 covers the platforms.
  • Participants: the device owner, the network operator, the platform provider and the app developer can all be different firms. Chapter 1.9 follows value along that chain.

Standards matter more here. Open protocols such as MQTT, CoAP and HTTP with JSON payloads let a device from one vendor feed an app from another. Reporting gets more frequent, payloads grow, and the link usually becomes a subscription rather than a wire.

So IoT is not M2M with a new name, and it is not simply a larger M2M. It is M2M plus internet-scale addressing, cloud services, analytics and human-facing applications. A system can carry M2M links inside it and still be called IoT at the top.

Worked example

Question. A logistics firm runs 800 trucks. In its present M2M setup, each truck's telematics unit sends one SMS of 140 bytes every hour to a control room. The firm moves to an IoT platform where each truck uploads a 20 kB record every hour. Compare the data sent per truck per day and for the whole fleet per day in the two designs, and give the ratio of IoT traffic to M2M traffic. Use 1 kB = 1000 bytes and 1 MB = 1000 kB.

  1. Messages per truck per day = one per hour × 24 hours = 24.
  2. M2M bytes per truck per day = 140 bytes × 24 = 3,360 bytes = 3,360 ÷ 1000 = 3.36 kB.
  3. M2M fleet per day = 800 trucks × 3.36 kB = 2,688 kB = 2,688 ÷ 1000 = 2.688 MB.
  4. IoT per truck per day = 20 kB × 24 = 480 kB.
  5. IoT fleet per day = 800 × 480 kB = 384,000 kB = 384,000 ÷ 1000 = 384 MB.
  6. Ratio = 384 MB ÷ 2.688 MB = 142.86, about 143 times.

Answer. M2M sends 3.36 kB per truck and 2.688 MB for the fleet each day; the IoT design sends 480 kB per truck and 384 MB for the fleet, about 143 times more data.

Exam traps

  • Do not say M2M is simply a subset of IoT. A serial-cable M2M link is not part of any IoT system until something connects it to the internet; the two overlap in devices and sensors, they are not nested.
  • Do not say IoT is exactly M2M plus the internet. The change also brings cloud storage, analytics, applications and multi-party ownership.
  • Unit slips: 140 bytes is 0.14 kB, not 140 kB, and 1 kB here is 1000 bytes, not 1024. Also 140 bytes is 1,120 bits, so never compare a byte figure with a bit rate without converting.
  • M2M does not mean there is no network, and IoT does not mean every link is new. A valid IoT system often has M2M links at the bottom and an IP link at the gateway.
  • Do not confuse M2M with machine learning, and do not read it as machine-to-human. The second M is a machine.

1.8 IoT challenges, issues and impact

IoT challenges are the practical limits that appear when many identifiable things must sense, act and exchange data for a service. The limits are technical (scale, heterogeneity, energy, connectivity, latency, reliability, data volume, addressing), security and privacy, business (cost, return on investment, skills) and social (surveillance, e-waste, digital divide, safety). The impact of IoT is therefore mixed: it can raise efficiency, safety and new services, but it can also create dependence and change jobs. Designers, buyers and policy makers use this view before a deployment.

You need 1.1 for what IoT is, 1.2 for devices and power, and 1.5 for where data is sensed and processed.

Technical challenges: scale to addressing

Scale means many devices, messages and firmware copies. A parking system may hold thousands of sensors; each needs an identity, an address, a software version and an owner record. Heterogeneity means unlike hardware, operating systems and protocols. Interoperability is the ability of those unlike parts to exchange useful data. Variety is not itself a fault; failure to agree on data format, units or timing is.

Energy and connectivity limit placement. A battery node may sleep, wake, sense and send; a mains node can stream. Latency is the delay from sensing to action. Reliability is the chance that a message or action completes correctly. A factory loop may need milliseconds; a monthly water reading can wait hours. Data volume grows with frequent samples and many nodes. Identification and addressing decide how each thing is named and found.

Tbatch = (n × S) ÷ R

n is the number of devices updated together (no unit); S is firmware size per device in kB; R is gateway rate in kB/s; Tbatch is time for one batch in seconds. In data-rate problems use 1 kB = 1000 bytes.

Security, privacy and trust

Security has several separate jobs. Authentication proves that a device or user is genuine. Authorization decides what that identity may do. Confidentiality keeps data secret, integrity stops undetected change, and availability keeps the service working. A cheap sensor may ship with a default password; an attacker who finds it can send false readings. Identity must be unique and hard to copy. Physical access matters: a person who can open a meter or hold a reset button can change behaviour.

Firmware updates are a security issue, not only a maintenance task. A device without a safe update path keeps old flaws for years. An update needs signed code, a rollback plan and enough power to finish; a half-written firmware can brick a device. Privacy asks who may collect, store and link data about people. A smart meter can reveal meal times and sleep patterns. Trust is the user's belief that the system will behave, report honestly and not surprise them. Encryption alone does not prove identity, fix updates or stop physical tampering.

Business and social impact

Business issues begin with cost: device, installation, connectivity, cloud, integration and maintenance. Return on investment (ROI) compares the gain with the total cost over a period. A factory may save energy by sensing idle machines, but the saving must repay sensors, gateways and skilled staff. Skills are a real constraint: embedded programming, networking, data analysis and security are different skills, and a small firm may not hold all of them. A pilot reduces risk before a large order.

Social issues include surveillance, e-waste, digital divide and safety. Always-on cameras and wearables can watch workers or family members without clear consent. E-waste is discarded electronic equipment; it grows when cheap devices are sealed, unrepairable or short-lived. The digital divide appears when useful services need a smartphone, data plan or reliable power that some households lack. Safety matters when IoT controls a lock, a pump, a vehicle or a medical device: failure can hurt people, not just lose data.

The impact is balanced. Efficiency improves through remote monitoring, predictive maintenance and automatic billing. Safety improves through fault alerts and faster response. New services appear, such as pay-by-use insurance or smart parking. The same systems create dependence on networks and vendors, and they change jobs: some manual checks reduce, while installation, data and security roles grow. A fair judgement compares gains and losses for users, workers and the environment.

Worked example

Question. A building has 5,000 sensors. Each firmware update is 250 kB. The gateway updates 50 devices at a time at 2 Mbps. Find the time for one batch and for all batches, ignoring overhead. Use 1 kB = 1000 bytes.

  1. Gateway rate: 2 Mbps = 2,000,000 bits/s. Divide by 8 bits per byte: 2,000,000 ÷ 8 = 250,000 bytes/s = 250 kB/s.
  2. Data in one batch: 50 × 250 kB = 12,500 kB.
  3. Time for one batch: 12,500 kB ÷ 250 kB/s = 50.0 s.
  4. Number of batches: 5,000 ÷ 50 = 100.
  5. Total time: 100 × 50.0 s = 5,000.0 s.
  6. Convert: 5,000.0 ÷ 60 = 83.33 min; 83.33 ÷ 60 = 1.39 h (or 5,000.0 ÷ 3,600 = 1.39 h).

Answer. One batch takes 50.0 s, there are 100 batches, and all batches take 5,000.0 s, which is 83.33 min or 1.39 h.

Exam traps

  • Do not reduce IoT security to encryption only. Identity, firmware updates, physical access and privacy are also part of the issue.
  • 2 Mbps is 2,000,000 bits/s, not 2,000,000 bytes/s. Divide by 8 before comparing with kB/s.
  • Heterogeneity is variety; interoperability is successful exchange. The examiner often asks for the difference.
  • A positive simple ROI does not prove a project is safe: maintenance, skills, downtime and security costs may be excluded.
  • A social benefit can hide a cost. Smart cameras may improve safety and also create surveillance; mention both sides.

Scale, heterogeneity or interoperability, energy or connectivity, latency or reliability, data volume, identification or addressing. Any four.

Understand Why is heterogeneity not the same as interoperability? 3 marks

Heterogeneity is the presence of different devices and protocols. Interoperability is whether they can exchange and use data correctly. Different parts can interoperate through standards or gateways; identical parts can fail if data meanings differ.

Apply A site has 2,000 sensors. Each firmware image is 100 kB. A gateway updates 40 devices at a time at 4 Mbps. Use 1 kB = 1000 bytes. Find one batch time, the number of batches and the total time. 4 marks

4 Mbps = 4,000,000 bits/s = 500,000 bytes/s = 500 kB/s. Batch data = 40 × 100 = 4,000 kB. Batch time = 4,000 ÷ 500 = 8 s. Batches = 2,000 ÷ 40 = 50. Total = 50 × 8 = 400 s = 6.67 min.

Analyze Give two reasons why firmware updates are harder for battery-powered IoT devices than for phones. 4 marks

Limited energy may not last through download and flash write. An interrupted or failed update can brick a device with no easy physical recovery. Constrained links make large images slow, and many devices need scheduled batches.

Understand Distinguish authentication from authorization in an IoT door lock. 3 marks

Authentication proves that the phone or user is genuine. Authorization decides whether that identity may unlock this door at this time. A valid user may be authenticated but not authorized for another flat.

Analyze Explain one positive and one negative social impact of always-on smart cameras in a housing society. 5 marks

Positive: faster response to theft or fire and evidence for safety. Negative: surveillance of residents and visitors, loss of privacy, risk of misuse, and pressure to consent. A fair policy limits retention and access.

1.9 IoT value chains and market perspective

An IoT service is built by many firms, not one. Chips, devices, connectivity, platforms, apps and integration each add a piece of value. The IoT value chain maps these roles in order. The market perspective asks a second question: which role earns the money, and which business model charges the customer? This matters when a smart-meter project, a fleet tracker or a factory sensor system is priced, bought and scaled. A firm that only counts hardware misses the recurring revenue in connectivity, data and services.

You need: 1.1 for what IoT is, 1.7 for the M2M comparison, and 1.8 for business issues.

M2M value chains and IoT value chains

An M2M value chain links a device maker, a connectivity supplier, an integrator and an end user. The flow is mostly one-way: a machine sends readings to a monitoring centre. The chain is vertical and hardware-led; value is counted in devices, SIM cards and integration labour. Chapter 1.7 contrasts this with IoT.

An IoT value chain is longer and more layered. Component and chip makers supply sensors, microcontrollers and radios. Device makers turn them into meters, tags, cameras and boards. Network operators supply SIMs, NB-IoT, LoRaWAN or Wi-Fi backhaul. Platform providers run device registries, message brokers, storage and dashboards. System integrators assemble these parts for a customer. Application and service providers build the user-facing service: a phone app, an alert, a billing rule. End users receive the value: a lower bill, a safe plant, a timely repair.

A firm may occupy one role or several. A large company can make chips, devices and a platform. What matters is that each role adds a distinct capability, and no single role owns the whole service. Some syllabi call this layered arrangement the emerging industrial structure.

Market perspective and business models

Market analysis asks which customer segment buys, what it pays for, and who captures the margin. Common segments are consumer (smart home), enterprise (fleet, retail), industrial (factory, energy) and public (water, street lighting). A hospital, a city and a household buy differently: one wants compliance, one wants uptime, one wants low price.

Business models include one-time sale of a device, subscription (₹ per device per month), pay-per-use (₹ per reading or per litre), and outcome-based pricing (a share of savings). XaaS, everything as a service, sells the whole outcome: monitoring, analytics, maintenance and reports as a monthly service, not a box. Subscription gives predictable revenue; pay-per-use matches cost to usage; outcome-based pricing needs trusted measurement.

revenue = N × P;  cost = fixed cost + N × c;  margin = revenue − cost;  margin % = (margin ÷ revenue) × 100

N is the number of paying units; P is price per unit per month in ₹; c is variable cost per unit per month in ₹; fixed cost is the monthly platform or operations cost in ₹; margin is monthly profit in ₹; margin % is margin as a percentage of revenue.

Business processes change too. A meter reading that was a monthly visit becomes a continuous data stream. Analytics turns that stream into theft detection, demand forecasts and fault alerts. Knowledge management stores what operators learn: which fault codes repeat, which sites fail in monsoon, which thresholds cause false alarms. Value therefore sits in data and knowledge, not only in hardware.

Partnerships, standards and industrial structure

The IoT industry is layered but not cleanly separated. A network operator may host a platform; a platform may sell an app; an integrator may own the customer relationship. Partnerships matter because no firm owns every layer. A device maker needs a certified radio; a platform needs standard APIs; an app provider needs open data models.

Standards reduce integration cost: MQTT for messaging, JSON for payloads, oneM2M for service layers, and open APIs. See Unit 3 for protocols. When standards are weak, each project is custom, margins leak into integration labour, and scaling stops at a few sites. When standards are strong, components plug in, platforms compete on analytics, and apps can move between clouds. The market shifts from selling boxes to selling uptime, insight and outcomes.

Worked example

Question. A smart-meter service charges ₹80 per meter per month for 25,000 meters. Platform cost is ₹5,00,000 per month, and SIM and connectivity cost is ₹15 per meter per month. Find monthly revenue, total cost and margin. Also find margin as a percentage of revenue.

  1. Revenue = N × P = 25,000 × ₹80 = ₹20,00,000 per month.
  2. Connectivity cost = N × c = 25,000 × ₹15 = ₹3,75,000 per month.
  3. Total cost = connectivity cost + platform cost = ₹3,75,000 + ₹5,00,000 = ₹8,75,000 per month.
  4. Margin = revenue − total cost = ₹20,00,000 − ₹8,75,000 = ₹11,25,000 per month.
  5. Margin % = (₹11,25,000 ÷ ₹20,00,000) × 100 = 56.25%.

Answer. Monthly revenue is ₹20,00,000, total cost is ₹8,75,000, margin is ₹11,25,000, and margin is 56.25% of revenue.

Exam traps

  • Do not equate the IoT value chain with hardware manufacturing only. Connectivity, platforms, data and services also carry value.
  • Do not confuse a network operator with a platform provider. One sells connectivity; the other runs device data services.
  • Do not compute margin percentage on cost. Use revenue as the base unless the question says otherwise.
  • Do not treat a one-time device sale as recurring service revenue. Subscription and pay-per-use are different models.
  • In M2M questions, remember the usual chain is shorter and lacks the internet-scale platform and app layers.

Understand Why can a platform provider capture more value than a device maker? 3 marks

The platform holds device data, APIs and analytics that many apps reuse. It can earn recurring subscription revenue and scale across many device brands, while a device maker often earns a one-time sale.

Apply A service has 10,000 meters at ₹100 per meter per month. Platform cost is ₹4,00,000 per month and SIM cost is ₹10 per meter per month. Find revenue, total cost, margin and margin percentage. 4 marks

Revenue = 10,000 × ₹100 = ₹10,00,000. Connectivity = 10,000 × ₹10 = ₹1,00,000. Total cost = ₹1,00,000 + ₹4,00,000 = ₹5,00,000. Margin = ₹10,00,000 − ₹5,00,000 = ₹5,00,000. Margin % = 50%.

Analyze Why do partnerships and standards matter in a layered IoT industry? 5 marks

No firm owns every layer. Standards such as MQTT and open APIs let devices, platforms and apps connect without custom work. Partnerships combine certified radios, connectivity, cloud and integration. Weak standards raise integration cost and block scale.

Understand Distinguish subscription and pay-per-use pricing with one IoT example each. 3 marks

Subscription charges a fixed ₹ per device per month, for example ₹80 per smart meter per month. Pay-per-use charges for actual usage, for example ₹2 per sensor reading or ₹5 per litre of water monitored.

Analyze A firm says it sells smart meters, so it belongs only to the device layer. Critique this view. 4 marks

The statement confuses a product with a value chain. The meter needs connectivity, a platform and an app. Revenue may come from subscription or analytics. The firm may also act as integrator or service provider, so one label is incomplete.

Analyze Which roles are usually absent or weak in a simple M2M chain compared with an IoT chain? 3 marks

M2M often lacks an internet-scale platform provider, an independent app provider, and analytics or knowledge-management services. It is more vertical and hardware-led, with direct monitoring and control.

Takeaways

  1. IoT is defined by three abilities together: identify each thing, sense or act on the physical world, and exchange data over a network with little human effort.
  2. A physical device is only five parts — sensor, actuator, control unit, communication module and power source — and most design problems sit in balancing them.
  3. The logical design names what the system must do; the physical design names what it is built from; exam answers need both.
  4. Four communication patterns cover almost every IoT exchange: request-response, publish-subscribe, push-pull and the exclusive pair.
  5. A layered architecture separates sensing, network, processing and application, so each layer can be replaced without rewriting the others.
  6. IoT exists because cheap sensors, low-power radios, cloud storage and analytics matured at the same time and became affordable.
  7. M2M is point-to-point control; IoT adds internet-scale addressing, cloud platforms, analytics and apps that people actually use.
  8. Security, privacy, power, cost and device management are the recurring issues that decide whether a deployment survives.
  9. Value in IoT is spread across a chain, and platform providers and service providers earn continuing revenue while device makers earn mostly once.

Apply

Apply A housing society wants an overhead tank controller that measures water level and switches the pump, and reports to the cloud every five minutes. Select sensors, actuator, control unit, power source and communication model, and justify each choice. 12 marks

Sensor choice: ultrasonic or float switch for level, with reason; Actuator: relay or contactor driving the pump, with isolation note; Control unit: microcontroller with enough GPIO and a timer; Power source: mains with a small backup, or battery with duty-cycled radio; Communication: Wi-Fi through the society router, or NB-IoT if range is poor; Communication model: publish-subscribe to a broker topic for periodic reports; Local safety: dry-run cutoff and overflow cutoff must not depend on the cloud.

Apply Design a REST API for a home automation system that can read and set the state of room lights. List the resources and show the GET, POST, PUT and DELETE calls. 10 marks

Resource naming: /lights and /lights/{id}; GET /lights returns the list with states; GET /lights/3 returns one light; PUT /lights/3 with a body of state replaces the state; safe for repeated calls; POST /lights creates a new light entry; DELETE /lights/3 removes it; Status codes 200, 201, 404 and their meaning; Note that REST here is stateless request-response over HTTP.

Apply A battery-powered soil moisture sensor wakes for 2 s every 10 minutes, drawing 20 mA while awake and 0.02 mA while asleep. Compute the duty cycle and the average current, and explain two ways to extend battery life. 10 marks

Duty cycle = 2 s ÷ 600 s = 1/300 ≈ 0.0033, that is 0.33 percent; Average current = I_active × duty cycle + I_sleep × (1 − duty cycle); = 20 × (1/300) + 0.02 ≈ 0.067 + 0.02 ≈ 0.087 mA; Battery life ≈ capacity in mAh ÷ 0.087 mA, in hours, then divide by 24 for days; Extend by: fewer wake-ups, shorter active time, sleep the radio, send only on change, use a low-power link; Mention that waking the radio often costs more than the sensor itself.

Analyze Explain the three-layer and five-layer IoT reference models. Compare them and state where fog and edge computing fit. 13 marks

Three-layer model: perception, network, application, with the job of each; Limitation: no place for processing, management or security in the middle; Five-layer model: perception, transport, processing, application, business; Business layer: reports, billing, policy, model building; Edge computing sits at or near the device; fog sits between edge and cloud; Comparison table on number of layers, where processing happens and latency; Example: streetlight data processed at the gateway for switching, sent to cloud for billing.

Evaluate A city plans 50,000 connected streetlights with fault reporting and dimming. Propose a communication model and an architecture, and justify the choices against cost, latency and power. 13 marks

Identify the traffic: small periodic status messages plus commands; Choose publish-subscribe over a broker, because many readers and independent topics; Architecture: perception at the pole, gateway or cellular link, processing in fog near the zone, application in cloud; Scale argument: 50,000 devices make per-client polling and request-response wasteful; Latency: dimming commands need a fast local loop, not a round trip to cloud; Cost: one gateway per group of poles versus one SIM per pole; Power: mains-powered poles relax battery limits but not radio congestion; Security: device identity, key provisioning and firmware update path.

Evaluate Evaluate the claim that IoT is simply M2M communication with internet connectivity. 10 marks

What is true: both connect machines and carry sensor data; What IoT adds: unique IP-scale addressing, cloud platforms, analytics, human-facing apps; Scale difference: M2M is usually a closed point-to-point link; Data use: M2M acts on a reading, IoT stores and learns from many readings; Business difference: M2M is a one-time equipment sale, IoT is a subscription service; Verdict: M2M is a subset of patterns inside IoT, not a synonym.

Glossary

Actuator
A transducer that converts an electrical command into physical motion or switching.
Broker
The server in a publish-subscribe system that receives messages and forwards them to matching subscribers.
Communication module
The radio or wired interface that carries device data to other nodes or the internet.
Control unit
The processor, memory and I/O that run a device's logic.
Device
The hardware assembly inside a thing that performs sensing, computation, communication and actuation.
Device management
Provisioning, configuring, monitoring and updating devices after they are deployed.
Duty cycle
The fraction of time a device is active; it drives average current and hence battery life.
Edge computing
Processing data at or very near the device, before it travels to the cloud.
Exclusive pair
A full-duplex connection kept open between two parties for the duration of a session.
Fog computing
Processing in nodes between the edge and the cloud, such as a local gateway or zone server.
Functional block
A named function of an IoT system, independent of the hardware that implements it.
Gateway
A node that aggregates traffic from short-range devices and forwards it over a wider-area link.
Identification
Assigning each thing a unique name, address or tag so its data can be attributed to it.
IoT
A network of identifiable physical objects that sense, act and exchange data over networks to deliver services with little human control.
Layered architecture
A reference model that splits an IoT system into ordered layers from sensing to application.
M2M
Direct machine-to-machine communication for monitoring and control, typically without cloud or human interaction.
Power source
The battery, mains connection or harvester that supplies energy to a device.
Publish-subscribe
An interaction where publishers send messages to topics and subscribers receive matching topics through a broker.
Push-pull
An interaction where the sender pushes messages into a queue and the receiver pulls them when ready.
Request-response
An interaction where a client sends a request and waits for the server's reply.
REST
An API style that treats data as resources named by URLs and acts on them with GET, POST, PUT and DELETE.
Sensor
A transducer that converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal.
Thing
Any physical object that can carry a unique identity and be connected to a network.
Transducer
A general element that converts one form of energy to another; sensors and actuators are both transducers.
Value chain
The ordered set of players whose work together turns a connected thing into a delivered service.
XaaS
Everything as a service: computing, platforms or full applications delivered as metered services.
