2.1 Postulates of quantum mechanics

Quantum mechanics is written as four postulates. They say what a state is, how a closed system changes, what a measurement returns, and how two systems combine. Some syllabi call this list 'basic postulates' or 'postulates and operator algebra'. The postulates turn superposition into calculation: unit vectors, unitary matrices, probabilities and tensor products. Every qubit experiment, gate circuit and key-distribution protocol follows them. The four rules are stated at exam depth, with one numerical example.

You need: Unit 1 on Hilbert spaces, Dirac notation and unitary matrices; for qubit vocabulary see 2.2.

Postulate 1: the state is a unit vector

A quantum system at a fixed time is described by a state vector |ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space H. H is a vector space with an inner product; Dirac notation writes it as ⟨φ|ψ⟩. Normalisation requires ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1, so total probability is one. A global phase e^{iα} changes no measurement result: |ψ⟩ and e^{iα}|ψ⟩ are the same state.

⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1

|ψ⟩ is the state vector; ⟨ψ| is its conjugate transpose; the inner product must equal one after normalisation.

For one qubit the computational basis is |0⟩=(1,0)ᵀ and |1⟩=(0,1)ᵀ. A pure state is |ψ⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ with complex amplitudes a and b and |a|²+|b|²=1. Amplitudes are not probabilities; their modulus squared are. In the worked example |ψ⟩=(√3/2)|0⟩+(1/2)|1⟩, so |a|²=3/4 and |b|²=1/4. Superposition and relative phase are in 2.3.

Postulates 2 and 3: evolution and measurement

Postulate 2. A closed system evolves by a unitary operator U: |ψ'⟩ = U|ψ⟩. Unitary means U†U=I, where U† is the conjugate transpose and I is identity. This preserves the norm: ⟨ψ'|ψ'⟩=⟨ψ|U†U|ψ⟩=⟨ψ|ψ⟩=1. For a Hamiltonian H, a Hermitian energy operator, U(t)=e^{-iHt/ħ}; ħ is the reduced Planck constant. No measurement happens during unitary evolution.

U†U = I, |ψ'⟩ = U|ψ⟩

U is the evolution operator; U† is its conjugate transpose; I is the identity matrix; |ψ'⟩ is the state after evolution.

Postulate 3. A projective measurement uses Hermitian operators. An observable A has orthonormal eigenvectors |m⟩, outcomes a_m and projectors P_m=|m⟩⟨m|. The Born rule gives p(m)=⟨ψ|P_m|ψ⟩=|⟨m|ψ⟩|². After outcome m the state is P_m|ψ⟩/√p(m), so it is renormalised. For |ψ⟩=Σ c_m|m⟩, p(m)=|c_m|². In the worked example p(0)=|√3/2|²=0.75 and p(1)=|1/2|²=0.25. Details are in 2.5.

Postulate 4: composite systems

Postulate 4. If system A has Hilbert space H_A and B has H_B, the joint system has H_A⊗H_B, the tensor product. If A is in |α⟩ and B in |β⟩, the product state is |α⟩⊗|β⟩, written |α⟩|β⟩. A general joint state is Σ c_{ij}|i⟩⊗|j⟩. States that cannot be written as one product are entangled; see 2.7. Two qubits use |00⟩,|01⟩,|10⟩,|11⟩, a four-dimensional space. X⊗I flips only the first qubit.

Density-operator pointer. The same postulates can use a density operator ρ. For a pure state ρ=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|. For a mixture ρ=Σ p_i|ψ_i⟩⟨ψ_i| with p_i≥0 and Σp_i=1. Then Tr ρ=1, evolution is ρ→UρU†, and outcome probability is p(m)=Tr(P_mρ). A pure state has ρ²=ρ; a mixed state does not. Reduced states and mixtures are in 2.6.

Worked example

Question. A qubit is in |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. (a) Check normalisation. (b) Find the probability of outcome 0 and outcome 1 in the computational basis. (c) Apply X = [[0,1],[1,0]] and write the new state.

  1. Write a=√3/2 and b=1/2. Norm: |a|²+|b|²=(√3/2)²+(1/2)²=3/4+1/4=1.0. The state is normalised.
  2. Born rule: p(0)=|a|²=3/4=0.75; p(1)=|b|²=1/4=0.25. These add to 1.0.
  3. X(a,b)ᵀ=(b,a)ᵀ. New state: X|ψ⟩=(1/2)|0⟩+(√3/2)|1⟩=0.5|0⟩+0.866|1⟩. Norm check: 0.5²+0.866²≈0.25+0.75=1.0.

Answer. Normalisation is 1.0; p(0)=0.75 and p(1)=0.25; after X the state is 0.5|0⟩+0.866|1⟩, exactly (1/2)|0⟩+(√3/2)|1⟩.

Exam traps

  • Squaring the amplitude instead of taking its modulus squared: for a=1/2+i/2, |a|²=1/2, not a².
  • Forgetting to renormalise after measurement: the state is P_m|ψ⟩/√p(m), not P_m|ψ⟩.
  • Calling any matrix unitary: unitary needs U†U=I; Hermitian needs H†=H, and a Hermitian matrix is unitary only in special cases.
  • Treating a tensor product as a Cartesian product: dimensions multiply, so two qubits give a 4-dimensional space, not a pair of 2-dimensional spaces.
  • Thinking global phase changes probabilities: e^{iα}|ψ⟩ gives the same statistics as |ψ⟩.

2.2 Classical bits versus quantum bits (qubits)

A classical bit holds one of two values, 0 or 1. A qubit holds a two-component vector of complex numbers, and hands back only 0 or 1 when you measure it. That gap — a rich state, a one-bit readout — is where every quantum algorithm lives, from factoring to search to chemistry simulation. This chapter defines both objects precisely, gives the normalisation rule, and separates what a qubit stores from what you can actually read out of it.

You need Chapter 2.1 for the postulates, and Unit 1 for complex numbers, modulus, vector spaces and Dirac notation.

The classical bit: one choice, one value

A classical bit is the smallest unit of ordinary information. It holds exactly one of two symbols, 0 or 1. A transistor is on or off, a capacitor holds charge or does not, a spot on a disk is magnetised one way or the other. The physics changes; the logic never does.

Write one bit as x, with x ∈ {0, 1}. Two bits give the four strings 00, 01, 10, 11. In general n bits give 2ⁿ strings, and naming one of them takes n binary digits. That count is why classical memory scales the way it does: double the bits, square the number of distinguishable states.

Reading a classical bit is free and harmless. Copying it is also free: measure the voltage, write the same voltage elsewhere, and the original is untouched. A bank database stores your rupee balance as a string of bits, and you can read it a thousand times with identical results. A qubit gives up exactly this freedom.

The qubit: a unit vector of complex amplitudes

A qubit is a state vector in a two-dimensional complex vector space, written as a ket |ψ⟩. With the two computational basis kets |0⟩ and |1⟩, every pure qubit state has the form below.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, with |α|² + |β|² = 1

α and β are complex numbers called probability amplitudes; |0⟩ and |1⟩ are the two basis states, playing the role of 0 and 1 for a bit; |α|² is the modulus squared, so α = a + ib gives |α|² = a² + b².

So amplitudes may be negative, imaginary or complex, but their squared moduli must add to exactly 1. A classical bit picks one of two points. A qubit picks one point on the surface of a sphere — far more choices. Four real numbers describe α and β; normalisation removes one, and an overall phase removes another, leaving two real parameters. That is why one qubit fits on a sphere, drawn in Chapter 2.4.

Measurement returns one classical bit

However much the state holds, one measurement gives one bit. Measuring in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis returns outcome 0 with probability p(0) = |α|² and outcome 1 with p(1) = |β|², and p(0) + p(1) = 1 by normalisation. This is the Born rule; the general projective case is in Chapter 2.5.

The state changes at the moment of measurement. Outcome 0 leaves |0⟩; outcome 1 leaves |1⟩. The old amplitudes are gone, so a second measurement in the same basis repeats the first result. The randomness is real in the sense that no hidden list exists to be peeked at: identical preparations give different outcomes across runs, and only p(0) and p(1) are predictable.

Global phase does nothing. Multiply the whole state by e^{iγ} for any real γ: α → e^{iγ}α and β → e^{iγ}β. Every probability |e^{iγ}α|² = |α|² is unchanged, so |ψ⟩ and e^{iγ}|ψ⟩ are the same physical state. Changing one amplitude only, say β → −β, is a relative phase, and that does change later interference, as Chapter 2.3 shows.

Where the advantage comes from: interference

The qubit's advantage is not carrying many classical bits at once. It is interference. Before measurement, amplitudes for different paths to the same outcome add as complex numbers. Two paths of amplitude 1/2 each add to 1 (probability |1|² = 1) or cancel to 0 (probability 0), depending on relative phase. Adding the two path probabilities classically would give 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2, which can never cancel.

A quantum algorithm engineers this: wrong answers' amplitudes cancel, the right answer's amplitudes reinforce, then you measure. That needs careful unitary gates and repetition — the subject of Unit 4, with the gate building blocks in Unit 3. The encoding is also fragile. A qubit cannot be copied (no-cloning, Unit 3), so long computations need quantum error correction, Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A qubit is prepared in the state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with α = 1/√2 and β = i/√2. (a) Verify that the state is normalised. (b) Find the probability of outcome 0 and of outcome 1 for a measurement in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis. (c) Write the state immediately after a measurement that returns 0.

  1. Compute |α|². Here α = 1/√2 is real, so |α|² = (1/√2)² = 1/2 = 0.5.
  2. Compute |β|². Here β = i/√2, so |β|² = |i|²/2 = 1/2 = 0.5, because |i| = 1.
  3. Add them: |α|² + |β|² = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0, so the state is normalised.
  4. Apply the Born rule: p(0) = |α|² = 0.5 and p(1) = |β|² = 0.5. The two probabilities add to 1.0, as they must.
  5. Outcome 0 collapses the state onto |0⟩. The amplitude β, and with it the factor i, disappears; only the basis state of the observed outcome remains.

Answer. The state is normalised with |α|² + |β|² = 1.0; the outcome probabilities are p(0) = 0.5 and p(1) = 0.5; after outcome 0 the state is |0⟩.

Exam traps

  • Saying a qubit is "both 0 and 1 at the same time". It is one vector; a measurement gives one bit.
  • Treating α and β as probabilities. They are amplitudes; probabilities are |α|² and |β|².
  • Thinking global phase is observable. e^{iγ}|ψ⟩ is the same state as |ψ⟩; only relative phase matters, and only through interference.
  • Forgetting the modulus when β is complex: with β = i/√2, |β|² = 1/2, not i/2 and not 1/√2.
  • Writing the post-measurement state as α|0⟩ instead of |0⟩. Collapse discards the amplitude.

2.3 Quantum states and superposition

A qubit state is a unit vector, and writing that vector as a weighted sum of basis states is called superposition. The weights are complex amplitudes, and their modulus squared, |α|² and |β|², give the measurement probabilities. The relative phase between amplitudes does not change those probabilities, but it decides how the state interferes with later gates. Superposition is the raw material of every quantum algorithm: search, factoring and simulation all build it and then interfere it. This chapter shows how to write, normalise and combine such states, and how the Hadamard gate turns phase into a visible outcome.

You need: the postulates and the qubit-versus-bit picture from Chapter 2.1 and Chapter 2.2, plus matrices and column vectors from Unit 1.

A state written as a sum

A single qubit lives in a two-dimensional complex vector space with an inner product. Fix the computational basis: |0⟩ = (1, 0)ᵀ and |1⟩ = (0, 1)ᵀ. Every state is a unit vector, so it can be written as a combination of these two basis vectors.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

α and β are complex amplitudes for |0⟩ and |1⟩; the state is normalised when |α|² + |β|² = 1.

When both α and β are non-zero, the state is a superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩. It is one definite vector, not a hidden mixture. The basis is your choice: the same vector can be expanded in the |+⟩, |−⟩ basis, and then different amplitudes appear. Superposition always means with respect to the basis you have chosen.

  • |0⟩ and |1⟩ are valid states: take α = 1, β = 0, or the reverse.
  • Always check |α|² + |β|² = 1 after a hand calculation. A real gate preserves this.
  • Amplitudes are complex numbers. Only their magnitudes squared are probabilities.
  • Some syllabi call this idea the superposition principle.

Amplitudes and relative phase

Write each amplitude in polar form: α = a e^{iφ₀} and β = b e^{iφ₁}, with a and b real and non-negative. The factor e^{iφ₀} is a global phase. Multiplying the whole state by it gives the same physical state, so drop it. What is left is the relative phase δ = φ₁ − φ₀.

Two standard states show the point. |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 has δ = 0, while |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 has δ = π. Both give p(0) = p(1) = 1/2, yet they are different states.

Interference with the Hadamard gate

H = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]],   Z = [[1, 0], [0, −1]]

H is the Hadamard gate and Z is the Pauli-Z gate; both are unitary 2×2 matrices in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis.

H maps |0⟩ to |+⟩ and |1⟩ to |−⟩. Apply H a second time to |+⟩: replace H|0⟩ by |+⟩ and H|1⟩ by |−⟩, then expand. The |1⟩ terms carry opposite signs and cancel, and only |0⟩ survives. That cancellation is interference.

  1. Write the gate as a matrix in the same basis as the state.
  2. Multiply the matrix into the amplitude column vector.
  3. Group the |0⟩ and |1⟩ terms and simplify.
  4. Take magnitude squared of each amplitude to get probabilities.

Z changes the sign of the |1⟩ amplitude, so Z|+⟩ = |−⟩. The probabilities stay at 1/2 and 1/2, and a careless check sees nothing happen. But a later H now returns |1⟩, whereas without Z it would return |0⟩. The relative phase, invisible in the probabilities, has controlled the final outcome.

Worked example

Question. A qubit starts in |0⟩. (a) Apply H = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]] and write the state. (b) Find p(0) and p(1). (c) Apply Z = diag(1,−1) to the result, write the new state and show that the probabilities are unchanged.

  1. Write the input as the column vector (1, 0)ᵀ.
  2. (a) H|0⟩ is the first column of H: (1/√2, 1/√2)ᵀ. So the state is (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩. Each amplitude is 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071.
  3. (b) p(0) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 = 0.5 and p(1) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 = 0.5. The two probabilities sum to 1.
  4. (c) Z multiplies the second entry by −1: (1/√2, −1/√2)ᵀ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 = |−⟩.
  5. Recalculate: p(0) = |1/√2|² = 0.5 and p(1) = |−1/√2|² = 0.5. The probabilities are unchanged.

Answer. (a) |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2; (b) p(0) = p(1) = 0.5; (c) |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2, still with p(0) = p(1) = 0.5.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the relative phase: |+⟩ and |−⟩ give the same p(0) and p(1), so students treat them as the same state. A later H sends them to |0⟩ and |1⟩, so the phase decides the answer.
  • Adding probabilities instead of amplitudes: a superposition is not 50% of |0⟩ plus 50% of |1⟩ with real weights. Amplitudes add with signs and can cancel to zero.
  • Forgetting normalisation: after writing α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, check |α|² + |β|² = 1. A gate preserves this, but hand algebra often does not.
  • Applying a gate to only one term of the superposition: the matrix multiplies the whole state, and each output amplitude is built from both input amplitudes; a gate may change only one of them (Z on |+⟩ changes only β) or both (H changes both).
  • Mixing global and relative phase: multiplying the entire state by e^{iθ} changes nothing observable; only the ratio β/α carries physics.

2.4 Bloch sphere representation of a qubit

A pure qubit state has two complex amplitudes but only two real settings survive normalization (probabilities add to 1) and global phase, an overall complex factor that cancels in every probability. The Bloch sphere turns those settings into a point: polar angle θ from the north pole and azimuth φ around the equator. The north pole is |0⟩, the south pole is |1⟩, and the equator holds equal superpositions whose relative phase is φ. Engineers use this picture to read single-qubit gates, calibrate hardware and see measurement probabilities at a glance.

You need: complex amplitudes and superposition from Chapter 2.3, and unit vectors from Chapter 2.2.

From amplitudes to two angles

A qubit state is |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, where α and β are complex numbers and |α|² + |β|² = 1. Write α = r₀eiγ₀ and β = r₁eiγ₁. Multiplying the whole state by eiδ is a global phase and leaves every measurement probability unchanged, so one overall phase is redundant. Remove it and two real numbers remain. Choose θ and φ so α = cos(θ/2) and β = eiφsin(θ/2). Some syllabi call this the Bloch sphere representation of a qubit.

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩

θ is the polar angle measured from the +z axis (north pole), 0 ≤ θ ≤ π; φ is the azimuth measured from the +x axis in the xy plane, 0 ≤ φ < 2π; i is the imaginary unit, i² = −1; e is Euler's number.

Why θ/2? Orthogonal states sit at antipodal points, so an angle of π/2 between state vectors becomes an angle of π on the sphere. That doubling is why the amplitudes carry θ/2: at θ = π the |1⟩ amplitude is sin(π/2) = 1, whereas cos θ and sin θ would put |1⟩ at θ = π/2 and break the poles.

Poles, equator and the six named states

The north pole is θ = 0: |ψ⟩ = |0⟩. The south pole is θ = π: |ψ⟩ = eiφ|1⟩, which is |1⟩ up to global phase. On the equator θ = π/2, so cos(θ/2) = sin(θ/2) = 1/√2. The state is (|0⟩ + eiφ|1⟩)/√2. Four equator points get names: φ = 0 gives |+⟩, φ = π gives |−⟩, φ = π/2 gives |+i⟩, φ = 3π/2 gives |−i⟩.

StateθφAmplitude form

Measurement in the Z basis gives 0 with probability cos²(θ/2) and 1 with probability sin²(θ/2). The angle θ therefore controls the bias; φ controls interference between the two amplitudes when a later gate mixes them. Changing φ alone does not change Z-basis probabilities, but it changes results of measurements along x or y. Chapter 2.5 covers the measurement rule.

Pauli gates rotate, mixed states shrink

The Pauli gates act as half-turns about the sphere's axes. X maps θ→π−θ and φ→−φ; on the sphere it is a π rotation about the x axis. Z leaves θ fixed and sends φ→φ+π, a π rotation about the z axis. Y is a π rotation about the y axis. Hadamard is not a Pauli gate; it swaps the x and z axes, so it maps |0⟩ to |+⟩ and |+⟩ to |0⟩.

r = (sinθ cosφ, sinθ sinφ, cosθ) = (⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩, ⟨Z⟩)

r is the Bloch vector; ⟨X⟩, ⟨Y⟩ and ⟨Z⟩ are expectation values of the Pauli operators in the state; for a pure state |r| = 1, for a mixed state |r| < 1.

A pure state has |r| = 1 and sits on the surface of the sphere. A mixed state has |r| < 1 and sits inside the sphere; it is described by a density matrix rather than a single ket, and Chapter 2.6 covers that. The centre, |r| = 0, is the maximally mixed state, equally likely to give 0 or 1 in any basis.

Worked example

Question. A pure qubit state is |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. (a) Find θ in radians and φ in radians. (b) Write the Bloch vector.

  1. Match to |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩. The |0⟩ coefficient gives cos(θ/2) = √3/2.

  3. The |1⟩ coefficient is 1/2. Also sin(θ/2)=sin(π/6)=1/2, so the magnitude matches. The phase factor eiφ multiplies 1/2 and the result is positive real, so eiφ=1 and φ=0 rad = 0.0 rad.
  4. The Bloch vector is (sinθ cosφ, sinθ sinφ, cosθ). Substitute: sin(π/3)=√3/2 and cos(π/3)=1/2.

Exam traps

  • Writing α = cosθ and β = eiφsinθ instead of half-angles gives the wrong poles: |1⟩ needs θ = π, so the amplitude must be sin(θ/2).
  • A global phase eiα|ψ⟩ gives the same Bloch point; only the relative phase φ between the two amplitudes moves the point around the equator.
  • θ is measured from +z (north pole), not from the xy plane. The equator is θ = π/2, not θ = 0.
  • A point inside the sphere is a mixed state, not a pure state with a rounding error. Pure states have Bloch vector length 1.
  • The equator state |+⟩ has φ=0, while |−⟩ has φ=π; they are orthogonal even though both have θ=π/2.

2.5 Quantum measurement

Measurement is not a passive look at a qubit. It is an interaction that forces one outcome from a set, with probabilities fixed by the state. For projective measurement the Born rule gives p(m)=⟨ψ|P_m|ψ⟩, and the state becomes P_m|ψ⟩/√p_m. This rule is the bridge from amplitudes to observed bits. It is used whenever a quantum algorithm reads a result, when a detector clicks in a lab, and when an error-correction circuit measures a stabiliser.

You need: Chapter 2.1 and Chapter 2.3; Unit 1 inner products.

Projectors and the Born rule

A measurement basis is a complete set of orthonormal states {|m⟩}. For each outcome m, the projector is P_m=|m⟩⟨m|. A projector is Hermitian and idempotent: P_m†=P_m and P_m2=P_m. Different outcomes have orthogonal projectors, P_mP_n=0 for m≠n, and they sum to identity, Σ_m P_m=I.

For a unit state |ψ⟩, the probability of outcome m is p(m)=⟨ψ|P_m|ψ⟩=|⟨m|ψ⟩|2. This is the Born rule. The number ⟨m|ψ⟩ is the amplitude for m; square its modulus to get probability. Probabilities are real, lie between 0 and 1, and sum to 1.

p(m) = ⟨ψ|P_m|ψ⟩ = |⟨m|ψ⟩|2

p(m) is the probability of outcome m; |ψ⟩ is the unit state; P_m=|m⟩⟨m| is the projector onto basis state |m⟩; ⟨m|ψ⟩ is the amplitude.

If the outcome is m, the state after measurement is |ψ_m⟩=P_m|ψ⟩/√p_m. For a basis projector this equals |m⟩ up to a phase. The division by √p_m renormalises the state to unit length. If p_m=0, outcome m cannot occur and the update is undefined.

Measuring in a rotated basis

You may measure in any orthonormal basis. In the computational basis {|0⟩,|1⟩}, projectors are P_0=|0⟩⟨0| and P_1=|1⟩⟨1|. In the X basis {|+⟩,|−⟩}, with |+⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩=(|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2, projectors are P_+=|+⟩⟨+| and P_−=|−⟩⟨−|.

To find probabilities, compute inner products with the new basis. For |ψ⟩=a|0⟩+b|1⟩, p(+)=|(a+b)/√2|2 and p(−)=|(a−b)/√2|2. The state after outcome + is |+⟩; after − is |−⟩. A measurement in the X basis is also called an X measurement; on the Bloch sphere it asks for the spin along the x-axis.

BasisProjectorsOutcome states

Basis choice matters. Measuring |0⟩ in the Z basis gives 0 with probability 1. Measuring the same |0⟩ in the X basis gives + or − with probability 1/2 each. The state is not changed by a vague look; it is projected onto the chosen basis.

POVMs in one paragraph

Projective measurement is the standard postulate, but a more general measurement is a positive operator-valued measure (POVM). It is a set of positive operators {E_m} with Σ_m E_m=I. The probability of outcome m is p(m)=⟨ψ|E_m|ψ⟩. Projective measurement is the special case E_m=P_m with P_m2=P_m and P_mP_n=0. General POVM elements may be non-orthogonal and need not be projectors.

POVMs describe noisy detectors and measurements where the system is not fully known. They do not give a simple collapse to E_m|ψ⟩ in the same way. A POVM is a bookkeeping device for probabilities; it may not specify the post-measurement state. POVM details are rarely examined in this unit; most exam questions use projective measurements. Open systems and measurement disturbance are left to advanced courses and to Chapter 2.9.

p(m)=⟨ψ|E_m|ψ⟩, Σ_m E_m=I

E_m is a positive operator for outcome m; I is the identity operator; p(m) is the outcome probability.

Worked example

Question. A qubit is in state |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. It is measured in the {|+⟩,|−⟩} basis, with |+⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩=(|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2. (a) Find p(+) and p(−). (b) Give the state after each outcome.

  1. Write the amplitudes: a=√3/2 for |0⟩ and b=1/2 for |1⟩.
  2. Inner product with |+⟩: ⟨+|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(a+b) = (√3+1)/(2√2). Its squared modulus is p(+)=((√3+1)²)/(8) = (2+√3)/4 ≈ 0.933.
  3. Inner product with |−⟩: ⟨−|ψ⟩ = (1/√2)(a−b) = (√3−1)/(2√2). Its squared modulus is p(−)=((√3−1)²)/(8) = (2−√3)/4 ≈ 0.067.
  4. Check the probabilities sum to one: (2+√3)/4 + (2−√3)/4 = 4/4 = 1.
  5. After outcome +: P_+|ψ⟩/√p(+) = |+⟩ because the amplitude is positive. After outcome −: P_−|ψ⟩/√p(−) = |−⟩.

Answer. p(+)=(2+√3)/4≈0.933 and p(−)=(2−√3)/4≈0.067. The states after the outcomes are |+⟩ and |−⟩ respectively.

  • Assuming measurement leaves |ψ⟩ unchanged; projective measurement changes it unless |ψ⟩ is already an eigenstate.
  • Mixing bases: computing p(+) with Z-basis projectors or using p(+) for the Z outcome.
  • Writing a probability greater than 1 after squaring the wrong amplitude or dropping the modulus.

2.6 Composite systems, reduced and mixed states

A composite system is two or more qubits described together. Its state lives in a tensor-product space. Looking at only one part gives a reduced density matrix. That matrix can be mixed even when the whole system is pure. This idea is used in quantum communication, error correction and algorithms that measure one qubit while ignoring others. Density operators describe both pure and mixed states.

You need: 2.1, 2.3, 2.5, and tensor products from Unit 1.

Density operators: pure and mixed

A state vector |ψ⟩ works for a system known exactly. A mixed state is a classical probability distribution over pure states. You may know a qubit is |0⟩ with probability p and |1⟩ with probability 1-p, but not which. The density operator records that knowledge.

ρ = Σ_i p_i |ψ_i⟩⟨ψ_i|

p_i ≥ 0 is the probability of pure state |ψ_i⟩; Σ_i p_i = 1; each |ψ_i⟩ is a unit vector; ρ is the density operator.

The matrix ρ is Hermitian, positive semidefinite, and has trace 1. The trace tr ρ is the sum of diagonal entries. For a measurement with projector P, the probability is tr(ρ P), not |amplitude|² alone.

Pure case: ρ=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|. Then ρ²=ρ and tr ρ²=1. Mixed case: tr ρ²<1. The number tr ρ² is the purity. It lies between 1/d and 1 for a d-level system. A pure state has one nonzero eigenvalue, equal to 1. A mixed state has at least two nonzero eigenvalues. Some syllabi call this topic density operators. Density matrices are needed when the preparation is uncertain or when part of a larger system is ignored.

Composite systems and tensor products

When A and B are separate, their joint Hilbert space is H_A ⊗ H_B. Here H_A is the state space of A and H_B is the state space of B. Basis vectors are |i⟩_A ⊗ |j⟩_B, written |ij⟩. For two qubits the basis is 00, 01, 10, 11, so the dimension is 2×2=4. A pure joint state is |ψ⟩_AB = Σ_{i,j} c_{ij} |ij⟩. Here c_{ij} is a complex amplitude and Σ_{i,j} |c_{ij}|² = 1.

If |ψ⟩_AB = |φ⟩_A ⊗ |χ⟩_B, the state is a product. If no such factorization exists, it is entangled; see 2.7. The density matrix of a pure joint state is ρ_AB=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|. A local operator on A alone is O_A⊗I_B, where O_A acts on A and I_B is the identity on B. Its expected value is tr(ρ_AB (O_A⊗I_B)).

To predict statistics on A alone, you do not need the full joint state. You need its reduced state. The reduced state is the only part of ρ_AB that affects measurements made on A.

Partial trace and reduced state

Choose an orthonormal basis {|j⟩_B} for B. The reduced state of A is the partial trace over B.

ρ_A = tr_B ρ_AB = Σ_j (I_A ⊗ ⟨j|_B) ρ_AB (I_A ⊗ |j⟩_B)

I_A is the identity on A; the sum runs over the B basis; ρ_A acts only on H_A; tr_B means trace over B.

In block-matrix form, write ρ_AB with blocks labelled by B. The partial trace keeps the diagonal blocks and sums them. It removes B. It is linear. It is not the full trace; a full trace would remove both systems and give a number.

The reduced state gives every measurement statistic on A: tr(ρ_A O_A)=tr(ρ_AB (O_A⊗I_B)). If ρ_AB is a product state, ρ_A is pure. If ρ_AB is pure and entangled, ρ_A can be mixed. For an entangled pair, no ket describes A alone. You must use the density matrix. The Bell pair example below gives ρ_A=I/2. That matrix has two equal diagonal entries and no off-diagonal terms.

Worked example

Question. For |Φ+⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 in the basis {|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩}: (a) write ρ_AB; (b) trace out B and find ρ_A; (c) show that ρ_A is mixed.

  1. Normalise: the coefficient is 1/√2. The pure density matrix is ρ_AB = |Φ+⟩⟨Φ+| = 1/2(|00⟩⟨00| + |00⟩⟨11| + |11⟩⟨00| + |11⟩⟨11|).
  2. In the given basis the nonzero entries are ρ_{00,00}=0.5, ρ_{00,11}=0.5, ρ_{11,00}=0.5, ρ_{11,11}=0.5. All other entries are 0. The 4×4 matrix is [[0.5,0,0,0.5],[0,0,0,0],[0,0,0,0],[0.5,0,0,0.5]].
  3. Trace over B with basis j=0,1: ρ_A = (I_A⊗⟨0|_B)ρ_AB(I_A⊗|0⟩_B) + (I_A⊗⟨1|_B)ρ_AB(I_A⊗|1⟩_B).
  4. For j=0 the surviving diagonal block is [[0.5,0],[0,0]]. For j=1 the surviving diagonal block is [[0,0],[0,0.5]]. Their sum is ρ_A = [[0.5,0],[0,0.5]] = I/2, where I is the 2×2 identity.
  5. Check the trace: tr ρ_A = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0.
  6. Check purity: tr ρ_A² = 0.5² + 0.5² = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5. Since 0.5 < 1, ρ_A is mixed, not a pure ket.

Exam traps

  • Writing a ket for the reduced state of an entangled pair. A reduced state can be mixed and needs a density matrix.
  • Confusing ρ_AB with ρ_A⊗ρ_B. For an entangled state the joint density matrix is not a product of the two reduced states.
  • Using the full trace instead of the partial trace. Trace out only B to get ρ_A.
  • Saying ρ²=ρ always. Only pure density matrices are projectors; mixed states have tr ρ²<1.
  • Mixing probabilities p_i with amplitudes c_{ij}. Probabilities are nonnegative and sum to 1; amplitudes are complex and their moduli squared sum to 1.

2.7 Entanglement, Bell states and the EPR paradox

Two qubits can be so strongly correlated that neither has a definite state of its own. The joint state is entangled: it cannot be written as one state for qubit A times one state for qubit B. Bell states are the four standard examples. They power quantum key distribution, teleportation and Bell tests. This chapter defines entanglement by non-factorability, lists the Bell states, and explains the EPR argument and why entanglement cannot send a signal.

You need: tensor products and reduced states from Chapter 2.6, and measurement from Chapter 2.5.

Entanglement means non-factorability

A product state of two qubits is |ψ⟩ = |α⟩A ⊗ |β⟩B. Write |α⟩ = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩ and |β⟩ = c|0⟩ + d|1⟩. The amplitudes a, b, c, d are complex numbers. For each qubit, |a|²+|b|²=1 and |c|²+|d|²=1. A joint state is entangled when no such product can reproduce it.

|ψ⟩ = (a|0⟩ + b|1⟩) ⊗ (c|0⟩ + d|1⟩) = ac|00⟩ + ad|01⟩ + bc|10⟩ + bd|11⟩

a, b, c, d are complex amplitudes; |a|²+|b|²=1 and |c|²+|d|²=1.

Non-factorability is the definition. The whole has definite joint properties, but neither part has a pure state of its own. For the Bell states below the reduced state is I/2, the maximally mixed state, and that is why Alice's local measurement looks perfectly random. A general entangled state gives a reduced state that is mixed but not necessarily I/2; only maximally entangled states give I/2.

  1. Write the target state in the basis |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩.
  2. Expand the product (a|0⟩+b|1⟩)⊗(c|0⟩+d|1⟩) and collect the four coefficients.
  3. Match the four coefficients to the target.
  4. Solve the four equations. A contradiction means the state is entangled.

Use this test on |Φ+⟩ = (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. Matching gives ac=1/√2, ad=0, bc=0, bd=1/√2. The next section solves it fully.

The four Bell states

The four Bell states are an orthonormal basis for two qubits. Each has two nonzero amplitudes of magnitude 1/√2. The square of each nonzero amplitude is 1/2. A minus sign is a relative phase; it changes interference, not the measurement probabilities in the Z basis.

StateExpressionZ outcomes
|Φ+⟩(|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2same bits
|Φ−⟩(|00⟩−|11⟩)/√2same bits
|Ψ+⟩(|01⟩+|10⟩)/√2different bits
|Ψ−⟩(|01⟩−|10⟩)/√2different bits

“Same bits” means 00 or 11. “Different bits” means 01 or 10. Normalisation holds because each state has Σ|amplitude|² = 1. Orthogonality holds because the inner product of any two different Bell states is 0. Measuring one qubit in Z gives 0 or 1 with probability 1/2, always. The other qubit's result is fixed by the first result and by which Bell state the pair is in.

Alice's marginal is I/2 no matter what Bob does locally. Bob cannot change Alice's outcome statistics by choosing a gate or a measurement on his qubit. Entanglement gives correlation, not communication. An unknown quantum state cannot be copied, and this includes a qubit that is part of an entangled pair; see the No-cloning theorem chapter.

The EPR argument and no-signalling

The EPR argument uses an entangled pair. Alice measures her qubit and instantly knows what Bob would get for the same measurement. EPR assumed local realism: no influence travels faster than light, and physical properties exist before measurement. Then Bob's bit must have been fixed all along. Quantum mechanics says Bob's bit is not fixed until measured. EPR concluded that quantum mechanics is incomplete.

Quantum mechanics escapes because the local outcomes are random. The correlation appears only when Alice and Bob compare classical records. Choosing a measurement on one side does not change the other side's marginal. This is no-signalling. A Bell test turns the disagreement into an experiment; local hidden variables obey CHSH ≤ 2, while quantum mechanics reaches 2√2, as Chapter 2.8 shows. Some syllabi call this topic “Bell states” and the “EPR paradox”.

Worked example

Question. Show that |Φ+⟩=(|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 is entangled. Try to write it as (a|0⟩+b|1⟩)⊗(c|0⟩+d|1⟩). Then Alice measures her qubit in the computational basis. Give the joint probabilities and Bob's state after each Alice outcome.

  1. Expand the product: (a|0⟩+b|1⟩)⊗(c|0⟩+d|1⟩) = ac|00⟩ + ad|01⟩ + bc|10⟩ + bd|11⟩.
  2. Match coefficients with |Φ+⟩: ac = 1/√2, ad = 0, bc = 0, bd = 1/√2.
  3. From ad = 0, either a = 0 or d = 0. If a = 0 then ac = 0, contradicting ac = 1/√2. If d = 0 then bd = 0, contradicting bd = 1/√2. So ad = 0 is impossible.
  4. The same contradiction follows from bc = 0. No complex numbers a, b, c, d satisfy all four equations, so |Φ+⟩ is entangled.
  5. Write the state as (1/√2)|0⟩A|0⟩B + (1/√2)|1⟩A|1⟩B. By the Born rule, p(00) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 and p(11) = |1/√2|² = 1/2. Also p(01) = 0 and p(10) = 0.
  6. If Alice gets 0, the state collapses to |0⟩A|0⟩B, so Bob has |0⟩. If Alice gets 1, Bob has |1⟩. Each Alice outcome has probability 1/2.

Answer. The amplitudes must satisfy ac=bd=1/√2≈0.7071 and ad=bc=0, which is impossible, so |Φ+⟩ is entangled; p(00)=p(11)=0.5 and Bob's state is |0⟩ or |1⟩ matching Alice's bit.

Exam traps

  • Thinking entanglement sends a signal. Local outcomes are random; the correlation shows only after Alice and Bob compare classical records.
  • Forgetting the normalisation 1/√2 in a Bell state. Each nonzero amplitude has magnitude 1/√2, so each outcome pair has probability 1/2.
  • Calling any correlated pair entangled. Classical correlation can exist without entanglement; the test is non-factorability of the joint state.
  • Confusing the Bell states with the basis states |00⟩ and |11⟩. A Bell state is a superposition, not a single computational-basis state.
  • Saying measurement on Alice changes Bob's state faster than light. The update is a bookkeeping rule for the joint state, not a physical message.

2.8 Bell's inequality and the CHSH game

Bell's inequality turns a philosophical question into a number. If each particle carried a pre-set answer, a local hidden variable, certain correlation sums cannot exceed 2. Quantum mechanics predicts up to 2√2. The CHSH game makes the same gap visible as a win probability: 3/4 classically, about 0.8536 with entanglement. Labs use these ideas in Bell tests, and the game is a standard way to see why entanglement is a resource.

You need: quantum measurement and the Born rule, plus Bell states and entanglement.

The CHSH game

A referee sends one bit x to Alice and one bit y to Bob. Each bit is 0 or 1, chosen uniformly and independently. Alice returns a bit a and Bob returns a bit b. They may agree on a strategy before the game, but after receiving x and y they cannot communicate. They win when a ⊕ b = x · y. Here ⊕ is XOR (addition modulo 2), and x · y is AND, which is 1 only when x = y = 1.

xyx·yWin when
000a = b
010a = b
100a = b
111a ≠ b

For three input pairs the answers must match; for (1,1) they must differ. A classical local strategy gives Alice and Bob shared random data λ, and each answer depends only on the player's own input and λ. Any fixed λ gives a deterministic strategy. No deterministic strategy can satisfy all four rows: making a = b always fails at (1,1), and making a ≠ b always fails at the other three. So at most three rows win. Shared randomness is an average of deterministic strategies, so the classical win probability is at most 3/4. The strategy 'both always answer 0' reaches 3/4.

Correlations and the CHSH value

Use ±1 outcomes. Write A_x = (−1)^a for Alice's outcome when her input is x, and B_y = (−1)^b for Bob. Both A_x and B_y are observables with eigenvalues +1 and −1; the bit 0 maps to +1 and the bit 1 maps to −1. Their product is AB = +1 when a = b and −1 when a ≠ b. Define the correlation E(x,y) = ⟨A_x B_y⟩. For a fixed input pair, E = P(a = b) − P(a ≠ b), so P(a = b) = (1 + E)/2 and P(a ≠ b) = (1 − E)/2.

S = E(0,0) + E(0,1) + E(1,0) − E(1,1)

E(x,y) is the correlation for Alice input x and Bob input y; S is the CHSH value.

Local hidden variables obey |S| ≤ 2. This is the CHSH inequality, one form of Bell's inequality. Quantum mechanics obeys a different bound, |S| ≤ 2√2. The number 2√2 ≈ 2.828 is Tsirelson's bound. A measured S above 2 cannot be explained by any local hidden-variable model of that experiment. Some syllabi call these Bell inequality violation experiments. The CHSH game converts S into a win probability. For the three inputs with x·y = 0, Pwin = (1 + E)/2. For (1,1), Pwin = (1 − E)/2. A strategy with E(0,0) = E(0,1) = E(1,0) = 1/√2 and E(1,1) = −1/√2 has S = 2√2. Then every input pair has Pwin = (1 + 1/√2)/2 = (2 + √2)/4 ≈ 0.8536.

Quantum strategy with |Φ+⟩

Take the Bell state |Φ+⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2. Alice and Bob each hold one qubit. Use the Pauli operators Z = diag(1,−1) and X = [[0,1],[1,0]]. A measurement of Z or X returns +1 or −1. For input x = 0 Alice measures Z; for x = 1 she measures X. For input y = 0 Bob measures B0 = (Z + X)/√2; for y = 1 he measures B1 = (Z − X)/√2. These Bob observables are Hermitian and square to the identity, so their outcomes are also ±1.

Substituting in S gives 2√2. The winning probability is the same for all four inputs: (2 + √2)/4 ≈ 0.8536. This beats 3/4. Exam traps

  • Do not mix the CHSH value with the win probability: local hidden variables give |S| ≤ 2 and win chance ≤ 3/4; quantum mechanics gives |S| ≤ 2√2 and win chance ≈ 0.8536. The numbers 2 and 2√2 are not probabilities.
  • Sign slip: S has a minus before E(1,1). For |Φ+⟩, E(1,1) = −1/√2, so the last term contributes +1/√2, not −1/√2.
  • For input (1,1), the winning condition is a ≠ b. The formula is Pwin = (1 − E)/2. Using (1 + E)/2 for all four rows gives the wrong input rule, even if this state hides the error.
  • The quantum strategy does not signal. Alice's marginal is uniform and unchanged by Bob's input; the correlation appears only when both lists of outcomes are compared later.
  • S > 2 rules out local hidden variables under the experiment's assumptions; it does not remove experimental loopholes. Loophole-free tests are a separate subject.

2.9 Stern-Gerlach experiment and the uncertainty principle

The Stern-Gerlach experiment fires silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field. Two spots appear, not a continuous smear: spin along that axis is quantised. Rotate the magnet and the beam splits again, while the earlier result is wiped out. That wiping out is measurement disturbance, the physical face of the uncertainty principle. The Robertson relation turns it into an inequality about spreads. Spin measurement, NMR and spin qubits rest on this.

You need: Chapter 2.5 on projective measurement and the Born rule, plus commutators from Unit 1.

Silver atoms in a non-uniform field

An oven heats silver until atoms evaporate. A slit collimates them into a beam that passes between magnet poles and lands on a screen. The magnet is shaped so the field is stronger near one pole: the field is non-uniform along z.

A silver atom has 47 electrons. Forty-six fill closed shells with no net angular momentum. The forty-seventh sits in a 5s orbital with zero orbital angular momentum. So the atom's magnetic moment comes almost entirely from that electron's spin. Write μ for magnetic moment and S for spin. Then μ = -g(e/2m)S, where e is the electron charge magnitude in coulombs, m is the electron mass in kilograms, and g ≈ 2 (more precisely 2.0023) is the electron spin g-factor, a dimensionless number.

A uniform field twists the magnetic moment but exerts zero net force. A non-uniform field also pushes. The force along z is proportional to μ_z, and μ_z is proportional to S_z. Classically S_z could take any value, so the beam would spread into a continuous band. Quantum mechanics allows only S_z = +ħ/2 or S_z = -ħ/2, where ħ = h/2π ≈ 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J·s is the reduced Planck constant. So the band becomes two spots.

F_z = μ_z (dB/dz)

F_z is the force on one atom along z in newtons; μ_z is the z-component of its magnetic moment in A·m² (or J/T); dB/dz is the field gradient along z in tesla per metre.

Take z toward the upper spot and the field stronger at the lower pole (dB/dz < 0). Then S_z = +ħ/2 gives μ_z < 0 and a force toward +z, so the upper spot is spin-up along z.

  1. Heat silver in an oven; atoms escape through a hole.
  2. Collimate the beam with slits.
  3. Pass it through a magnet whose field points along z and changes strength along z.
  4. Record the landing positions: two spots, symmetric about the straight-through line.

Sequential magnets and measurement disturbance

First run: one magnet along z. Two spots. Block the lower spot and send only the upper beam into a second magnet along z. Only one spot appears, in the upper position. A second measurement of S_z gives the same answer. After the first measurement the atom is in the spin-up state along z.

Second run: keep the z-up beam but rotate the second magnet along x. The single beam splits into two equal beams. An atom with definite S_z has no definite S_x. Each x outcome has probability 1/2, and the state after the second magnet is spin-up or spin-down along x. The old z information is gone.

Third run: take one x beam and pass it through a magnet along z. Two equal beams appear again. Nothing of the earlier z measurement survives. Each magnet measures one axis and randomises the others. This is projective measurement, described in Chapter 2.5.

|↑_z⟩ = (|↑_x⟩ + |↓_x⟩)/√2

|↑_z⟩ is the state with S_z = +ħ/2; |↑_x⟩ and |↓_x⟩ are the states with S_x = +ħ/2 and -ħ/2; the factor 1/√2 makes the total probability equal to 1.

  • A z magnet on a fresh beam: half exits each port.
  • The upper z beam into an x magnet: half exits each x port.
  • As fractions of the original beam: 1/2 exits each z port, then 1/4 exits each x port.
  • The relative phase between the two terms does not change these probabilities, but decides interference if the beams are recombined.

The Robertson uncertainty relation

Let A and B be two observables, Hermitian operators, and let the system be in any state. The spread of A is ΔA = √(⟨A²⟩ − ⟨A⟩²), the standard deviation of many measurements on identical copies. Robertson's inequality relates the two spreads.

ΔA ΔB ≥ ½ |⟨[A,B]⟩|

ΔA and ΔB are the standard deviations of A and B in the given state; [A,B] = AB − BA is the commutator; ⟨·⟩ is the expectation value in that state. If [A,B] = 0, the bound is zero and both observables can be sharp together.

For spin, [S_x,S_y] = iħS_z and cyclic partners. Substitute: ΔS_x ΔS_y ≥ (ħ/2)|⟨S_z⟩|. A state with sharp S_z cannot have sharp S_x or S_y. For the spin-up state along z, ΔS_x = ħ/2 and ΔS_y = ħ/2, so the product is ħ²/4. The bound is (ħ/2)(ħ/2) = ħ²/4, so the inequality is exact there, not slack.

The same logic with [x,p] = iħ gives Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, where x is position in metres and p is momentum in kg·m/s. The spread is a property of the state, not of a clumsy apparatus. A perfect instrument still cannot prepare a state with both values sharp. This matters for entanglement: in an entangled pair the outcomes are correlated along whichever axis is measured. A measurement on one part tells you what a measurement on the other part along that same axis will give; it does not change the other part, and it says nothing about other axes.

Worked example

Question. A silver atom beam passes a Stern-Gerlach magnet along z. (a) What fraction exits each z port? (b) The upper z beam enters an x magnet. What fraction of the original beam exits each x port? (c) For a state with ΔS_x = ħ/2 and ⟨S_z⟩ = ħ/2, find the minimum ΔS_y.

  1. (a) No preferred spin axis, so equal z outcomes: 1/2 up, 1/2 down.
  2. (b) Upper z beam is |↑_z⟩ = (|↑_x⟩ + |↓_x⟩)/√2, so each x outcome has probability 1/2. It is 1/2 of original, so each x port carries (1/2)(1/2)=1/4.
  3. (c) Use [S_x,S_y]=iħS_z: ΔS_x ΔS_y ≥ (ħ/2)|⟨S_z⟩|. Put in values: (ħ/2)ΔS_y ≥ ħ²/4, so ΔS_y ≥ ħ/2 ≈ 5.27×10⁻³⁵ J·s.

Answer. Each z port gets 1/2, each x port 1/4 of the original beam, and minimum ΔS_y = ħ/2.

Exam traps

  • Adding probabilities across axes. Each x port holds 1/4 of the original, not 1/2.
  • Expecting the second z magnet to split the upper beam. Undisturbed, it gives one spot.
  • Treating the uncertainty relation as instrument noise. ΔA belongs to the state.
  • Dropping ½ or ħ in ΔS_x ΔS_y ≥ (ħ/2)|⟨S_z⟩|.
  • Confusing ΔA with the eigenvalue gap. For spin-½ the gap is ħ but ΔS_x ≤ ħ/2.

Takeaways

  1. Four postulates — state vector, unitary evolution, measurement rule and tensor products — are the whole grammar of quantum computing.
  2. A classical bit holds 0 or 1; a qubit holds two complex amplitudes and still hands back only one bit when measured.
  3. Superposition is linear addition of amplitudes, and relative phase decides whether amplitudes add or cancel.
  4. Every pure qubit state is a point on the Bloch sphere, so single-qubit gates are rotations of that sphere.
  5. Measurement is probabilistic (Born rule) and disturbs the state; the outcome statistics come from the amplitudes, not from ignorance.
  6. A pure state of a joint system can look mixed on one side once you trace out the other side.
  7. Entanglement means the whole has definite properties that the parts separately do not have.
  8. Bell's inequality and the CHSH game turn that statement into a number: local realism stops at 2, quantum mechanics reaches 2√2.
  9. Stern–Gerlach showed that spin is quantised and that measuring one component destroys sharpness in another.

Long answers here are usually built around stating a postulate and then using it: syllabi stress the postulates, the Bloch sphere, the Born rule, density matrices and the Bell/CHSH violation, with Stern–Gerlach used as the physical illustration.

Remember and understand

Understand State the four postulates of quantum mechanics and explain what each one says about state, evolution, measurement and composite systems. 13 marks

Postulate 1: state is a unit vector |ψ⟩ in a complex Hilbert space; global phase is unobservable; Postulate 2: closed-system evolution is unitary, |ψ(t)⟩ = U|ψ(0)⟩, U†U = I; it is reversible; Postulate 3: measurement is a set {Mₘ} with Σ Mₘ†Mₘ = I; probability p(m) = ⟨ψ|Mₘ†Mₘ|ψ⟩, post-measurement state Mₘ|ψ⟩/√p(m); Postulate 4: composite space is the tensor product of the component spaces; One line on why the postulates matter: every circuit, gate and algorithm is written using only these four.

Understand Compare a classical bit with a quantum bit. What information does a qubit carry before and after measurement? 12 marks

Classical bit: one of two values, 0 or 1; can be copied freely; reading does not change it; Qubit: unit vector α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with α, β complex and |α|² + |β|² = 1; Measurement in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis returns 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|²; Before measurement the amplitudes can carry more descriptive content; after measurement only one classical bit remains; Contrast: n bits hold one of 2ⁿ strings; n qubits hold 2ⁿ amplitudes but yield n bits per measurement.

Understand Explain superposition and relative phase in quantum states, and show how phase controls interference. 10 marks

Define a basis, then a superposition Σ αᵢ|i⟩ with Σ|αᵢ|² = 1; Example: |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 differ only in relative phase; Global phase e^{iγ}|ψ⟩ gives identical statistics; relative phase between two amplitudes is observable; Interference: amplitudes add before squaring, so a relative phase of π cancels a path; Hadamard on |0⟩ and on |1⟩ as a concrete demonstration.

Understand Describe the Stern–Gerlach experiment and explain what it shows about quantised spin and about measurement. 13 marks

Silver atoms in a non-uniform magnetic field deflect by a force proportional to the spin component along the field; Classically a continuous smear is expected; two spots are seen, so the component takes only two values ±ħ/2; A second analyser along the same axis repeats the first result; a second analyser along a perpendicular axis splits the beam again; Reversing the order destroys the earlier sharpness: the measurement disturbs the state; Link to Pauli matrices, to incompatible observables and to the uncertainty principle.

Apply

Apply Starting from |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, derive the Bloch sphere coordinates and place |0⟩, |1⟩, |+⟩ and |+i⟩ on the sphere. 12 marks

Remove the global phase: write α = cos(θ/2)e^{−iφ/2}, β = sin(θ/2)e^{+iφ/2}; State |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^{iφ}sin(θ/2)|1⟩; Map to the Bloch vector (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ), with 0 ≤ θ ≤ π and 0 ≤ φ < 2π; Placement: |0⟩ at the north pole, |1⟩ at the south pole, |+⟩ on +x, |+i⟩ on +y; Note that orthogonal states are antipodal and that global phase does not move the point.

Apply A qubit is in the state |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. Compute the probabilities of the outcomes of a measurement in the Z basis, then in the X basis. 12 marks

Z basis: p(0) = |√3/2|² = 3/4, p(1) = |1/2|² = 1/4; probabilities sum to one; X basis states |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2; Amplitudes ⟨+|ψ⟩ = (√3+1)/(2√2), ⟨−|ψ⟩ = (√3−1)/(2√2); p(+) = (2+√3)/4 ≈ 0.933, p(−) = (2−√3)/4 ≈ 0.067; State the post-measurement state in each case and note the Bloch vector component along the measured axis.

Apply For the Bell state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, write the density matrix and take the partial trace over one qubit. Interpret the result. 14 marks

ρ = |Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺| is a 4×4 matrix with entries ½ at (00,00), (00,11), (11,00), (11,11); Trace out qubit B: ρ_A = Tr_B ρ, computed by summing the diagonal blocks over B's index; Result ρ_A = ½|0⟩⟨0| + ½|1⟩⟨1| = I/2, the maximally mixed one-qubit state; ρ_A is mixed although ρ is pure, so mixedness is not the same as classical randomness; Tr(ρ_A²) = ½ < 1 confirms mixedness; no local measurement can recover the correlations.

Apply Show that the four Bell states form an orthonormal basis of the two-qubit space, and prove that each is entangled. 12 marks

List |Φ±⟩ = (|00⟩ ± |11⟩)/√2 and |Ψ±⟩ = (|01⟩ ± |10⟩)/√2; Compute inner products to show orthonormality; count four states in a four-dimensional space; Proof of entanglement: assume (a|0⟩+b|1⟩)⊗(c|0⟩+d|1⟩) and compare coefficients; For |Φ⁺⟩ the conditions ac ≠ 0, bd ≠ 0 and ad = bc = 0 contradict each other; Note that Bell states are produced by H on one qubit followed by CNOT.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Derive the CHSH inequality |S| ≤ 2 for a local hidden-variable model and show that quantum mechanics gives S = 2√2. 16 marks

Define E(a,b) = P(same) − P(different) for measurement settings a, a′ on one side and b, b′ on the other; Local hidden variables: each outcome is a function of the setting and the hidden variable λ, with a shared distribution; Use the algebraic identity showing S = ±2 at worst for each λ, hence |⟨S⟩| ≤ 2; Quantum case: choose observables A₀ = Z, A₁ = X, B₀ = (Z+X)/√2, B₁ = (Z−X)/√2 on |Φ⁺⟩; Each E evaluates to ±1/√2, giving S = 2√2, above the classical bound and at the Tsirelson limit.

Evaluate Explain the CHSH game. Compute the best classical winning probability and the best quantum winning probability, and discuss what the gap proves. 14 marks

Rules: referee gives bits x, y; players reply a, b; they win when a ⊕ b = x·y; Classical: a deterministic strategy wins on 3 of 4 input pairs, so p ≤ 0.75; Quantum: share |Φ⁺⟩, measure at angles depending on the received bit; The quantum strategy wins with p = cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.854; Conclusion: no local hidden-variable model reproduces the statistics; the gap is a device-independent test of entanglement.

Analyze Using the postulates, show step by step that measurement on one half of an entangled pair instantly fixes the state of the other half, and explain why no signal travels. 16 marks

Start from |Φ⁺⟩ and apply the measurement postulate with projectors P₀ = |0⟩⟨0|⊗I and P₁ = |1⟩⟨1|⊗I; Outcome probabilities are ½ each; the post-measurement states are |00⟩ and |11⟩; The remote qubit becomes |0⟩ or |1⟩ correlated with the outcome, so the joint state is a product afterwards; The reduced state of the remote qubit is I/2 before and after, so its local statistics do not change; No-signalling: the sender cannot choose the outcome, so no message is transmitted; explaining the correlation needs a classical channel.

Evaluate Compare the state-vector and density-operator descriptions of a qubit. When is a density operator necessary, and how do you test purity? 12 marks

State vector describes only pure states; density operator describes pure and mixed states; Properties of ρ: Hermitian, positive semi-definite, Tr ρ = 1; pure iff Tr(ρ²) = 1; Bloch picture: ρ = (I + r·σ)/2 with |r| ≤ 1; |r| = 1 pure, |r| < 1 mixed, r = 0 maximally mixed; Mixed states arise from entanglement with an unobserved system, from noise, or from ignorance in an ensemble; Measurement statistics p(m) = Tr(Pₘρ) reduce to the Born rule for pure states.

Evaluate Discuss the EPR argument and Bell's reply. What assumptions does Bell's theorem rule out, and what loopholes remain in experiments? 15 marks

EPR: perfect correlations plus locality plus no disturbance seem to force pre-existing values (elements of reality); Bell: any model with local hidden variables must obey an inequality; quantum mechanics predicts a violation; Assumptions tested: locality, realism (pre-existing definite values), and freedom of setting choice; Experiments with entangled photons see the violation, closing the locality loophole with fast switching; Remaining loopholes and interpretations: detection efficiency, superdeterminism, many-worlds versus collapse readings.

Analyze Explain the uncertainty principle for two observables, derive ΔA·ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A,B]⟩|, and connect it to non-commuting Pauli matrices. 13 marks

Define variance ΔA² = ⟨A²⟩ − ⟨A⟩² for a state |ψ⟩; Use the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the vectors (A − ⟨A⟩)|ψ⟩ and (B − ⟨B⟩)|ψ⟩; Insert the commutator and anticommutator to obtain ΔA·ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A,B]⟩|; For Pauli matrices [σx, σy] = 2iσz, so Δσx·Δσy ≥ |⟨σz⟩|; Implication: a state with sharp x-spin has completely uncertain z-spin, exactly as sequential Stern–Gerlach shows.

Glossary

Amplitude
A complex coefficient in a superposition; its squared modulus gives a measurement probability.
Basis
A set of linearly independent vectors that spans the space, so every state is a unique combination of them.
Bell state
Any of the four maximally entangled two-qubit states built from |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩ with ± signs.
Bloch sphere
The unit sphere whose points label all pure single-qubit states by angles θ and φ.
Born rule
The rule p(m) = ⟨ψ|Pₘ|ψ⟩ giving the probability of each measurement outcome.
CHSH inequality
The bound |S| ≤ 2 that every local hidden-variable model obeys for the four-setting correlation combination S.
Collapse
The change of the state to Pₘ|ψ⟩/√p(m) immediately after an outcome m is recorded.
Density operator
A positive Hermitian operator with trace one that describes pure and mixed states and yields p(m) = Tr(Pₘρ).
Dirac notation
The bra-ket notation in which |ψ⟩ is a vector and ⟨ψ| is its conjugate transpose.
Entanglement
A joint state that cannot be factored into a product of states of its parts.
Global phase
A factor e^{iγ} multiplying the whole state vector; it changes nothing observable.
Hermitian operator
An operator with A† = A; it has real eigenvalues and represents an observable.
Hilbert space
A complete complex vector space with an inner product; quantum state vectors live in it.
Local hidden variable
A pre-existing value carried by each particle that fixes its outcome for any setting, shared through a common λ.
Mixed state
A statistical mixture of states, with Tr(ρ²) < 1; it has no single state vector.
No-signalling
The principle that local measurement statistics on one side of an entangled pair do not depend on what is done on the other side.
Observable
A Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are the possible measurement outcomes.
Orthonormal basis
A basis whose vectors have unit norm and zero inner product with each other.
Partial trace
The operation that removes one subsystem from a joint density operator, giving the reduced state of the rest.
Projector
An operator P with P² = P and P† = P; it picks out a subspace, and a projective measurement uses orthogonal projectors.
Pure state
A state that can be written as a single vector, equivalently one with Tr(ρ²) = 1.
Qubit
A two-level quantum system whose pure states are unit vectors in a two-dimensional complex space.
Relative phase
The phase difference between two amplitudes in a superposition; it is observable through interference.
Tensor product
The ⊗ construction that combines two state spaces into a joint space of multiplied dimension.
Unitary operator
An operator U with U†U = I; it preserves inner products and describes closed-system evolution.
