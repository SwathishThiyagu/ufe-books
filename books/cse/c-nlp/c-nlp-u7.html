7.1 Human speech production and its discrete-time model

Speech starts as air pushed out of the lungs. The vocal folds chop that air into pulses, and the throat and mouth shape the pulses into sounds. Engineers model this as a source signal driving a slowly changing filter. Sampled at 8 kHz or 16 kHz and cut into 20–30 ms frames, speech becomes a stream of numbers. Every speech recogniser, synthesiser and codec in this unit starts from this model.

You need: You only need to know that a signal sampled at regular instants can be stored as a list of numbers.

Air, folds and cavities: how the body makes speech

Speech begins with breathing out. The lungs push air up the trachea, the windpipe. At the top sits the larynx, or voice box, holding the vocal folds — two small folds of tissue. When the folds are pulled together, air pressure builds until they are forced apart; the pressure drops, they close, and the cycle repeats. Each opening releases a small puff of air. This periodic pulsing is voiced excitation.

The pulsing rate is the fundamental frequency F0, in hertz (cycles per second). A typical adult male voice has F0 near 100–150 Hz; a typical adult female voice near 180–250 Hz. A pulse train repeating F0 times per second carries energy at F0 and at the whole-number multiples 2F0, 3F0 and so on. These multiples are the harmonics. When the folds are held apart the air stream is not pulsed, and the sound is unvoiced. The noise comes from turbulent air forced through a narrow constriction: at the glottis for /h/, and in the mouth for /s/ and /f/. In the source-filter model this unvoiced source is still fed into the same tract filter.

Above the larynx the air passes the pharynx (throat) and the oral cavity (mouth); when the soft palate is lowered it also enters the nasal cavity (nose). It leaves through the lips for oral sounds, and through the nose as well when the soft palate is lowered, as in /m/, /n/ and /ng/. These cavities act as resonators — they boost some frequencies and damp others. Move the tongue or the lips and the shape changes, so a different set of frequencies is boosted. That is why one pitch can carry many different vowels.

The source-filter model

The source-filter model splits the work in two. The source e[n] is the excitation from the vocal folds: a pulse train for voiced sounds, noise for unvoiced ones. The filter h[n] is the vocal tract's impulse response, the output it would give for one puff of air. Real speech is the source shaped by the filter, so the sampled signal is their convolution:

x[n] = Σₖ e[k] · h[n − k]

x[n] is sample n of the recorded speech; e[k] is the k-th excitation sample; h[n − k] is the filter response to a puff k samples earlier; the sum runs over all past samples.

Source and filter are roughly independent. Hum a vowel low and then high: the pitch changes, the vowel does not, because the source changed while the filter stayed. The lips radiate the result into the air and add a mild high-frequency boost, which most implementations fold into the source. Some syllabi call e[n] the glottal source and H(z) the vocal tract transfer function.

The tract behaves like a tube, and a tube is well modelled by an all-pole filter:

H(z) = 1 / (1 − Σₖ aₖ z⁻ᵏ), k = 1 to p

H(z) is the filter transfer function; aₖ are the filter coefficients; z⁻ᵏ is a delay of k samples; p is the filter order.

The poles of H(z) are the tract resonances, called formants. The usual order is p ≈ 10 at a sampling rate of 8 kHz and p ≈ 18 at 16 kHz, roughly one pole per kilohertz plus a few. Linear predictive coding (LPC) estimates the aₖ from one frame of speech. Section 7.3 turns these coefficients into a visible spectrum.

Sampling and frames: from air to numbers

A microphone converts air pressure into a voltage, and an analog-to-digital converter samples that voltage fs times per second. Speech is normally stored as 8 kHz or 16 kHz, 16-bit PCM. At 8 kHz the highest frequency that can be represented is 4 kHz, enough for telephone speech; 16 kHz keeps a wider band and is common in modern systems. In 16-bit PCM each sample is an integer from −32768 to 32767, so 65536 levels exist.

The tract cannot change shape instantly: the tongue and jaw have mass. Over about 20–30 ms the filter is nearly constant. This property is quasi-stationarity, and it licenses framing — cut the signal into short overlapping blocks and treat each block as if a fixed filter produced it. A 25 ms frame taken every 10 ms is a common choice, so neighbouring frames overlap by 15 ms.

With N samples in total, frame length L samples and hop S samples, frames start at 0, S, 2S and so on, and each must fit inside the signal:

N_frames = floor((N − L)/S) + 1

N is the total number of samples; L is the frame length in samples; S is the frame shift (hop) in samples; floor rounds down to a whole number.

Frame rate is fs/S frames per second, set by the hop and not by the frame length. Many toolkits pad the tail instead of dropping it and report one extra frame.

Worked example

Question. A 2-second mono recording is made at a sampling rate of 16 kHz with 16-bit PCM samples. (a) How many samples does it contain? (b) How many bits and how many bytes of raw storage does it need? (c) The signal is cut into frames of 25 ms with a hop of 10 ms. Give the frame length and hop in samples, and the number of frames.

  1. Number of samples = fs × duration = 16000 samples/s × 2 s = 32000 samples.
  2. Number of bits = 32000 samples × 16 bits/sample = 512000 bits.
  3. Number of bytes = 512000 ÷ 8 = 64000 bytes, that is 64 kB.
  4. Frame length = 0.025 s × 16000 samples/s = 400 samples.
  5. Hop = 0.010 s × 16000 samples/s = 160 samples.
  6. Number of frames = floor((N − L)/S) + 1 = floor((32000 − 400)/160) + 1 = floor(31600/160) + 1 = floor(197.5) + 1 = 197 + 1 = 198 frames.

Answer. 32000 samples, 512000 bits = 64000 bytes (64 kB) of storage, a 400-sample frame with a 160-sample hop, and 198 frames.

Exam traps

  • Mixing up sampling rate and frame rate. 16 kHz is samples per second; the frame rate is fs/hop = 16000/160 = 100 frames per second.
  • Thinking F0 is the sampling rate. A male F0 is 100–150 Hz and a female F0 is 180–250 Hz, while fs is 8000 or 16000 Hz.
  • Using the frame length as the step. A 25 ms frame with a 10 ms hop overlaps by 15 ms; the hop fixes the frame rate.
  • Dropping the +1 in the frame count, or using ceil((N − L)/S) instead of floor((N − L)/S) + 1.
  • Swapping source and filter. F0 and its harmonics belong to the source; formants belong to the filter.

7.2 Phonetics: categories of speech sounds and articulation

Every speech sound belongs to a small class. The class is fixed by two things: whether the vocal folds vibrate, and where and how the vocal tract narrows. Phonetics gives each class a name. Three labels — voicing, place, manner — name any consonant; three more — height, backness, rounding — name any vowel. Speech recognisers and synthesisers care because the phone set of a language is the unit they model. Language teachers and speech therapists use the same labels. This chapter builds the labels and reads them off English, Hindi and Tamil sounds.

You need: 7.1 for the lungs, the vocal folds and the vocal tract as a filter.

Voicing and place of articulation

Air from the lungs rises through the larynx. The vocal folds there either vibrate or stay apart. Vibrating folds make a voiced sound; folds held apart make a voiceless sound. Say /s/ and then /z/ with a finger on your throat. /z/ buzzes and /s/ does not, and that buzz is their only difference.

Place of articulation is the point where the vocal tract narrows most. These nine places cover English and most Indian languages.

  • Bilabial: both lips — /p/, /b/, /m/
  • Labiodental: lower lip on upper teeth — /f/, /v/
  • Dental: tongue tip at the upper teeth — /θ/ in 'think', /ð/ in 'this'
  • Alveolar: tongue tip at the ridge behind the teeth — /t/, /d/, /s/, /z/, /n/, /l/
  • Retroflex: the tongue tip is curled back so that it touches (or the underside faces) the alveolar ridge and the region just behind it — Hindi ट /ʈ/, Tamil ட
  • Palato-alveolar: the tongue blade makes the narrowing just behind the alveolar ridge, on the way to the hard palate — /ʃ/ in 'ship', /tʃ/ in 'church'
  • Palatal: the front of the tongue body rises to the hard palate itself — /j/ in 'yes'
  • Velar: back of tongue at the soft palate — /k/, /g/, /ŋ/ in 'sing'
  • Glottal: narrowing at the vocal folds themselves — /h/

Ask 'where is the air squeezed?' and the answer is the place. Ask 'are the folds vibrating?' and the answer is the voicing. Two labels are done. The third is manner.

Manner of articulation

Manner is how the narrowing is made. Some syllabi call it the constriction type. Narrowing comes in three broad kinds: a full closure, a narrow gap that makes turbulence, and a free approach that makes none.

  • Stop (plosive): full closure, then a release — /p/, /t/, /k/, /b/, /d/, /g/
  • Nasal: full closure in the mouth, air escapes through the nose — /m/, /n/, /ŋ/
  • Fricative: narrow gap, air hisses — /f/, /v/, /s/, /z/, /ʃ/, /h/
  • Affricate: a stop released slowly into a fricative — /tʃ/ in 'church', /dʒ/ in 'judge'
  • Lateral: air flows around the sides of the tongue — /l/
  • Approximant: articulators approach, no turbulence — /w/, /j/
  • Tap or flap: one quick touch of the tongue — Hindi र /ɾ/
  • Trill: repeated vibration — Tamil ற, the rolled /r/ of many Indian languages
  1. Decide voicing: is the buzz there or not?
  2. Decide place: which two articulators are closest?
  3. Decide manner: closure, hiss, or free flow?
  4. Say the three labels in that order and you have named the consonant.

A complete label reads as voicing, then place, then manner. /b/ is a voiced bilabial stop. /s/ is a voiceless alveolar fricative.

Vowels and the vowel quadrilateral

In a vowel the mouth stays open and nothing closes. The tongue body moves up or down and forward or back, and the lips may round. Each of these changes the shape of the tube above the larynx, and so changes the sound.

  • Tongue height: close (high) to open (low)
  • Tongue backness: front to back
  • Lip rounding: rounded or unrounded

The vowel quadrilateral is a trapezoid standing for the mouth: front on the left, back on the right, close at the top, open at the bottom. Plot a vowel inside it and you have its height and backness.

  • /iː/ in 'beat': close front unrounded
  • /uː/ in 'boot': close back rounded
  • /æ/ in 'cat': open front unrounded
  • /ɑː/ in 'calm': open back unrounded
  • /ə/ in 'about': mid central unrounded

Phone, phoneme, allophone, syllable

A phone is one actual sound, written in square brackets: [p]. A phoneme is a sound category that changes meaning, written in slashes: /p/. 'pat' versus 'bat' shows that English /p/ and /b/ are separate phonemes. An allophone is a variant of one phoneme. English /p/ is aspirated [pʰ] in 'pin' and plain [p] in 'spin'; the two never separate words, so both are allophones of /p/.

A syllable is onset + nucleus + coda. The nucleus is usually a vowel. In 'cat', /k/ is the onset, /æ/ the nucleus, /t/ the coda. In 'speaks' /spiːks/, /s p/ is the onset, /iː/ the nucleus, /k s/ the coda.

  • Hindi stops come in fours at each place: voiceless /k/, voiceless aspirated /kʰ/ (ख), voiced /g/, voiced aspirated /gʰ/ (घ). Aspiration alone is a phoneme difference.
  • Hindi त /t̪/ and ट /ʈ/, and Tamil த and ட, keep dental and retroflex apart. English has only alveolar /t/, so many Indian speakers use the retroflex for it.

Worked example

Question. Classify the first sound of each of these five words: pat, bat, mat, fat, sat. For each, give voicing, place and manner. Then say how many of the five are voiceless and how many are voiced.

  1. 'pat' begins with /p/: folds apart, so voiceless; both lips, so bilabial; full closure released, so stop. Voiceless bilabial stop.
  2. 'bat' begins with /b/: same lips and same closure, but the folds vibrate. Voiced bilabial stop. So /p/ and /b/ differ only in voicing.
  3. 'mat' begins with /m/: voiced, bilabial, and air leaves through the nose, so nasal. Voiced bilabial nasal.
  4. 'fat' begins with /f/: voiceless, lower lip on upper teeth, so labiodental, with a hiss, so fricative. Voiceless labiodental fricative.
  5. 'sat' begins with /s/: voiceless, tongue tip at the ridge, so alveolar, with a hiss, so fricative. Voiceless alveolar fricative.
  6. Count voiceless: /p/, /f/, /s/ give 3. Count voiced: /b/, /m/ give 2. Total 3 + 2 = 5 words, which matches.
  7. Note the pairs. 'pat' and 'bat' differ only in voicing; 'pat' and 'fat' differ in place and manner together.

Answer. The five first sounds are: /p/ voiceless bilabial stop, /b/ voiced bilabial stop, /m/ voiced bilabial nasal, /f/ voiceless labiodental fricative, /s/ voiceless alveolar fricative; 3 of the five are voiceless and 2 are voiced.

Exam traps

  • Calling /m/ a bilabial stop. The mouth is fully closed, but air still flows out through the nose, so the manner is nasal.
  • Swapping place and manner: /s/ and /t/ are both alveolar, and they differ in manner (fricative versus stop), not in place.
  • Trusting English spelling. 'ship' starts with one fricative /ʃ/, not /s/ + /h/; 'think' starts with dental /θ/, not /t/ + /h/; 'church' starts with the single affricate /tʃ/.
  • Mixing the brackets. [p] is one phone, /p/ is a phoneme, and [pʰ] and [p] are two phones of one English phoneme.
  • Reading Hindi /kʰ/ as 'k plus h'. It is one aspirated stop, released with a puff of air, and क versus ख is a meaning difference.

7.3 Time-dependent and short-time Fourier analysis of speech

Speech changes every few milliseconds. A plain Fourier transform over a whole recording tells us which frequencies exist but not when they occur. The short-time Fourier transform cuts speech into short overlapping frames, multiplies each frame by a window, and takes a DFT. Stacking the magnitudes of those DFTs over time gives a spectrogram: time on one axis, frequency on the other, darkness or colour for energy. This view is used in speech recognition front ends, pitch tracking, finding speech versus silence, and reading formants and harmonics by eye. Some syllabi call it the STFT.

You need 7.1 for sampling and framing and 7.2 for voiced and unvoiced sounds.

Frames and windows

Speech is not stationary. The vocal tract shape changes as the tongue and lips move, so one Fourier transform over a whole sentence smears every sound into a single spectrum. The fix is to assume the signal is stationary only for a short slice. Cut the signal into frames of N samples, often 20 to 30 ms. At 16 kHz, 25 ms is 400 samples. Consecutive frames start a hop H samples apart, often 10 ms = 160 samples, so frames overlap. Overlap stops a sound falling at a frame edge from being lost.

w[n] = 0.54 − 0.46 cos(2πn/(N−1)),  n = 0, 1, …, N−1

w[n] is the window weight at sample n; N is the frame length in samples; n is the sample index inside the frame.

Before the transform, multiply the frame by a window w[n]. A rectangular window has abrupt edges and causes spectral leakage: energy from one frequency leaks into neighbouring bins. The Hamming window tapers the edges. Its value is about 0.08 at the frame edges and 1.0 at the centre. The windowed frame is xw[n] = x[n]·w[n].

  1. Choose sampling rate fs, frame length in samples N, and hop H in samples.
  2. Take frame m as x[n] for n = mH to mH + N − 1.
  3. Multiply the frame by the Hamming window to get xw[n].
  4. Take the DFT of xw[n] and keep the magnitude for this time position.

One DFT per frame and the spectrogram

X<sub>m</sub>[k] = Σ<sub>n=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> x<sub>w</sub>[n] e<sup>−j2πkn/N</sup>

Xm[k] is the DFT of frame m at bin k; xw[n] is the windowed frame; N is the DFT length; k is the bin index from 0 to N−1; j is √−1.

Bin k represents a frequency near k·fs/N. The bin spacing is fs/N hertz. With fs = 16 kHz and N = 512, one bin is 31.25 Hz wide. For a real signal, bins k = 0 to N/2 are non-redundant; the rest are mirror images. Zero-padding a 400-sample frame to 512 points gives a finer display grid but does not improve true frequency resolution. True resolution is set by the window length, not by the DFT length.

A spectrogram is made by placing the magnitude |Xm[k]| of each frame as one vertical column and moving to the next frame in time. This is the short-time Fourier transform. The frame length controls a trade-off. A wide-band spectrogram uses a short window, about 5 ms. It has good time resolution and poor frequency resolution, so it shows pitch pulses and formants as broad bands. A narrow-band spectrogram uses a long window, about 45 ms. It has good frequency resolution and poor time resolution, so it shows individual harmonics as horizontal lines.

Short-time energy, zero-crossing rate, pitch and boundaries

Two cheap frame-level measures separate speech from silence and voiced from unvoiced. Short-time energy is E = (1/N) Σ x²[n]. It is large in vowels and weak in silence. Zero-crossing rate is ZCR = (1/(N−1)) Σ |sgn(x[n]) − sgn(x[n−1])|/2. It is high for unvoiced sounds such as /s/ and low for voiced sounds and silence.

R(τ) = Σ<sub>n</sub> x[n]·x[n+τ],   τ* = argmax R(τ),   F0 = fs/τ*

R(τ) is the autocorrelation at lag τ samples; τ* is the lag with the largest autocorrelation in the allowed pitch range; F0 is the fundamental frequency in hertz; fs is the sampling rate in hertz.

For a voiced frame, the waveform repeats after one pitch period. Autocorrelation measures that repetition. Search τ over the plausible pitch range, take the lag with the largest R(τ), and convert it to F0. Energy dips between words give candidate word boundaries, but a real boundary needs the lexicon and language model of 7.6. Energy alone also drops inside weak sounds, so it over-segments.

Worked example

Question. Speech is sampled at fs = 16000 Hz. A frame is 25 ms long and the hop is 10 ms. The DFT length is 512. For 1.00 s of speech: (a) find the frame length and hop in samples, (b) find the bin spacing, (c) find the number of frames, (d) find the number of non-redundant DFT bins and the number of positive-frequency bins after dropping DC, (e) list the first three harmonic frequencies for a voiced frame with F0 = 125 Hz and the DFT bin nearest to each.

  1. Frame length in samples = fs × 25 ms = 16000 × 0.025 = 400 samples.
  2. Hop in samples = fs × 10 ms = 16000 × 0.010 = 160 samples.
  3. Bin spacing = fs/N = 16000/512 = 31.25 Hz per bin.
  4. Number of frames = floor((16000 − 400)/160) + 1 = floor(15600/160) + 1 = floor(97.5) + 1 = 97 + 1 = 98 frames.
  5. Non-redundant bins for a real signal = N/2 + 1 = 512/2 + 1 = 257 bins, k = 0 to 256.
  6. Positive-frequency bins after dropping DC = 257 − 1 = 256 bins.
  7. Harmonics: 1×125 = 125 Hz, 2×125 = 250 Hz, 3×125 = 375 Hz.
  8. Nearest bins: 125/31.25 = 4, 250/31.25 = 8, 375/31.25 = 12.

Answer. The frame is 400 samples, the hop is 160 samples, the bin spacing is 31.25 Hz, there are 98 frames, 257 non-redundant bins (256 after dropping DC), and harmonics at 125 Hz, 250 Hz and 375 Hz, nearest bins 4, 8 and 12.

Exam traps

  • Confusing window length with hop length: a 25 ms window and 10 ms hop give overlapping frames, not separate blocks.
  • Believing zero-padding improves frequency resolution. Going from 400 to 512 points only interpolates the spectrum; resolution is set by the 25 ms window.
  • Reversing the spectrogram trade-off: wide-band uses a short window, has good time resolution and poor frequency resolution; narrow-band uses a long window and shows harmonics.
  • Forgetting that a real DFT of length N has N/2 + 1 non-redundant bins, not N/2, when DC and Nyquist are included.
  • Treating every energy dip as a word boundary. Weak sounds also have low energy; the lexicon and language model decide real word boundaries.

7.4 Speech perception and critical bands

Speech reaches the brain through a mechanical spectrum analyser. The ear splits sound into overlapping frequency bands, and a different set of hair cells serves each band. Two tones in the same band interfere; two tones in different bands do not. This is why speech systems space their filters by perception and not by hertz. The mel and Bark scales put that spacing into a formula, and mel-spaced filter banks are the front end that produces MFCCs, the standard feature vector for speech recognition.

You need 7.3 for the magnitude spectrum of a frame and 7.2 for where speech energy sits.

The ear as a bank of filters

Sound first meets the outer ear: the pinna and a canal about 2.5 cm long. The canal behaves like a tube closed at one end and resonates near 3 kHz, adding a few decibels in the 2–5 kHz range. That is exactly where the weak consonants of speech carry much of their information.

The middle ear, behind the eardrum, is three small bones: malleus, incus and stapes. They match the low acoustic impedance of air to the much higher impedance of the fluid in the inner ear. Without that matching, most of the sound would reflect off the fluid surface. A small muscle reflex stiffens the chain for very loud sounds.

The inner ear holds the cochlea, a coiled, fluid-filled tube. Along its length runs the basilar membrane, narrow and stiff at the base and wide and slack at the apex. A pure tone makes the membrane vibrate most strongly at one place: high frequencies near the base, low frequencies near the apex. Hair cells at that place bend and fire the auditory nerve.

This is the place theory of pitch: the brain reads frequency mainly from which place is active, with firing rate helping at low frequencies. The practical point for us: each place answers a band of frequencies, and neighbouring places answer overlapping bands. The ear is a bank of filters, not one Fourier transform of the whole signal.

Critical bands and the Bark scale

Play a tone together with a narrow band of noise. As the noise bandwidth grows, the tone becomes harder to hear, but only up to a limit. Past that bandwidth, extra noise changes nothing. That limit is the critical band around the tone's frequency.

So a critical band is the range of frequencies within which a second sound interferes with the first. Two components inside one critical band mask each other; two components in different bands are heard separately.

About 24 critical bands cover 20 Hz to 16 kHz. The width is nearly constant at roughly 100 Hz between 100 Hz and 500 Hz, then grows with the centre frequency, reaching a few kilohertz at the top. The Bark scale numbers these bands: one Bark is one critical band, so the scale runs from 0 to about 24. Below 500 Hz, z ≈ f/100, with f in hertz and z in Bark, so 300 Hz sits at about z = 3 Bark, at the edge between the third and fourth critical band.

Some syllabi call the critical band the Bark band. Filter banks in coders and recognisers place one filter per band, so a wide high-frequency band gets one filter where a linear scale would give twenty.

The mel scale

mel = 2595 × log₁₀(1 + f/700)

f is frequency in hertz; mel is the perceived pitch in mel. The constants are chosen so that 1000 Hz maps to exactly 1000 mel, which anchors the scale.

Below about 700 Hz the curve is still steep: near 0 Hz its slope is roughly 1.6 mel per hertz, and 100 Hz is about 150 mel. Averaged over 0 to 1000 Hz the slope is 1 mel per hertz, because 1000 Hz is the anchor at 1000 mel. Above 1 kHz the curve flattens into a logarithm. Each doubling of frequency then adds about 500 to 780 mel, approaching 781 mel (2595 × log₁₀ 2) at high frequencies. 1000 Hz is 1000 mel, 2000 Hz is about 1521 mel, and 4000 Hz is about 2146 mel. The figure plots the curve against a line that treats hertz directly as mel.

Why this suits speech: the first formant of most vowels lies below 1 kHz and the second below about 3 kHz, while energy above 4 kHz is weak. A linear filter bank would spend half its filters on the empty top of the range and would merge two neighbouring formants below 1 kHz into one band. Mel spacing gives more bands where the energy is concentrated.

Masking, just-noticeable difference and loudness

Masking is the reason bands matter. A loud low tone hides a quiet higher tone far more than the reverse; this is the upward spread of masking. Masking also works in time: forward masking lasts up to about 100–200 ms after the masker, backward masking a few milliseconds before it.

The just-noticeable difference (JND) is the smallest frequency change a listener can detect. Near 100 Hz it is a few hertz; near 10 kHz it is a few tens of hertz. In relative terms it is roughly 0.3 % over most of the range, which is another reason a perceptual scale must be logarithmic at high frequencies.

Loudness has its own scales. The loudness level in phon of a sound is the sound pressure level in decibels of a 1 kHz tone that sounds equally loud. The sone is a ratio scale: 1 sone is defined at 40 phon, and each rise of 10 phon doubles the sone value. Equal-loudness contours, measured by Fletcher and Munson and standardised later, show that the ear is most sensitive around 2–5 kHz, where the ear canal resonates.

Worked example

Question. Using mel(f) = 2595 × log₁₀(1 + f/700), convert 300 Hz, 1000 Hz and 8000 Hz to mel. Then find what percentage of the mel range from 0 Hz to 8000 Hz is used by the first 1000 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Mel is perceptual, not physical. Do not subtract two mel values and call the result hertz; convert back with f = 700 × (10^(mel/2595) − 1).
  • Mixing units in a filter-bank question: a filter centre given in mel compared with a bandwidth given in hertz. Convert both to one unit first.
  • Critical bands are equal in width on the Bark scale, not in hertz. One band near 300 Hz is about 100 Hz wide; one band near 6 kHz is about a kilohertz wide.
  • The mel formula uses log base 10. If you switch to the natural log, the constant must change from 2595 to 1127; keeping 2595 with ln inflates the answer by a factor of about 2.3.
  • 1000 Hz is not the top of the mel scale. It is the anchor point; 8000 Hz is about 2840 mel and higher frequencies go further.

7.5 Speech features and speech coding

Speech recognition does not need every sample of the waveform. It needs a short vector every 10 ms that says what the vocal tract is doing. The MFCC pipeline turns each frame into 13 cepstral coefficients: pre-emphasis, framing, windowing, DFT, a mel filterbank, log, and DCT. For storage or transmission, speech coding compresses the waveform itself: PCM at 64 kbps, LPC vocoders near 2.4 kbps, and hybrid CELP coders such as GSM, G.729 and Opus. Features feed recognisers; coders save bits.

You need: 7.3 for the DFT and windows, and 7.4 for the mel scale and why bands are spaced that way.

MFCC: from samples to 13 numbers

A feature vector is a small set of numbers that summarises one frame of speech. The most common set is the mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). The aim is to keep the shape of the vocal-tract spectrum and throw away pitch detail and channel effects.

  1. Pre-emphasis: replace each sample by y[n] = x[n] − 0.97x[n−1]. This lifts the high frequencies, which speech loses naturally.
  2. Frame the signal into 25 ms windows with a 10 ms hop. At 16 kHz, one frame holds 400 samples and the hop is 160 samples.
  3. Multiply each frame by a window, usually Hamming, so the edges taper to zero and the DFT does not see a sudden cut.
  4. Compute a 512-point DFT of the windowed frame. Keep the magnitude spectrum.
  5. Pass the spectrum through a mel filterbank: 26 triangular filters spaced by the mel scale, dense at low frequencies.
  6. Take the log of each filter energy. The log turns multiplication by a channel gain into addition.
  7. Apply the DCT. The low-order cepstral coefficients describe the smooth spectral envelope.
  8. Keep c1 to c12 or c1 to c13. c0 carries frame energy. The rest are discarded.

y[n] = x[n] − 0.97x[n−1]

x[n] is the input sample at time index n; y[n] is the pre-emphasised sample; 0.97 is a typical pre-emphasis coefficient.

Motion, channel and other feature sets

A single MFCC frame is a snapshot. Speech is motion, so we add deltas and delta-deltas. A delta is the slope of a coefficient over nearby frames; a delta-delta is the slope of the delta. With 13 MFCCs, 13 deltas and 13 delta-deltas, the vector has 39 dimensions. At 100 frames per second this gives 3900 feature numbers per second.

ĉt = ct − μ

ct is the coefficient vector at frame t; μ is the mean vector over the utterance; ĉt is the cepstral-mean-normalised vector. This removes a fixed channel effect such as a microphone colour.

  • LPC (linear predictive coding) models each sample as a weighted sum of past samples. A common order rule is order ≈ fs(kHz) + 2, so 10 at 8 kHz and 18 at 16 kHz.
  • PLP (perceptual linear prediction) uses auditory smoothing before LPC; it is an alternative front end to MFCC.
  • CMN is cepstral mean normalisation. It is cheap and improves robustness to a fixed channel.

Speech coding: waveform, source and hybrid

Speech coding reduces the bit rate needed to send or store speech. Bit rate is bits per second, not samples per second. A waveform coder sends samples or a close approximation. PCM at 8 kHz sampling and 8 bits per sample gives 8000 × 8 = 64 kbps. Companding (μ-law or A-law) first compresses the amplitude scale, so quiet sounds get more codes than loud ones; this improves perceived quality at the same bit count.

7.6 Speech recognition with hidden Markov models

A speech recogniser does not hear words. It sees a stream of feature frames and must guess the sentence behind them. The hidden Markov model turns that guess into arithmetic: each word is a small machine of hidden states, and the recogniser picks the word sequence that best explains the frames while also being likely English. This is the classic route behind dictation, voice search and telephone menus, and it still explains the scoring inside newer systems.

You need: hidden Markov models, Viterbi and n-gram language models from Unit 2, feature vectors from 7.5, and phoneme classes from 7.2.

The Bayes decision rule for a whole sentence

Let X = o1, o2, ..., oT be the sequence of feature frames, one every 10 ms. Let W = w1, ..., wM be a candidate word sequence. Bayes' rule says P(W|X) = P(X|W)P(W) / P(X). The denominator P(X) is the same for every candidate W, so it cannot change the ranking.

Ŵ = argmaxW P(X|W) · P(W)

Ŵ is the recognised word sequence; X is the observed frame sequence; W ranges over all word sequences; P(X|W) is the acoustic likelihood of the frames given W; P(W) is the language model prior.

P(X|W) comes from the HMM acoustic model: it is high when the frames look like the sounds of W. P(W) comes from an n-gram language model: it is high for phrases that occur in English. Multiplying them is the whole idea — acoustics alone cannot separate 'recognise speech' from 'wreck a nice beach', but the prior can. In practice we add logs, log P(X|W) + log P(W), because a product of thousands of small probabilities underflows.

The HMM that scores one word

A word HMM has N hidden states. From state i at time t it moves to state j at t+1 with transition probability aij. While in state j it emits the frame vector ot with density bj(ot), usually a Gaussian mixture over the 39-dimensional vector from 7.5. The initial distribution πi gives the probability of starting in state i.

A phoneme uses a left-to-right three-state model: state 1 goes to 1 or 2, state 2 to 2 or 3, state 3 to 3 only. The self-loops absorb variation in duration. A word is a chain of such phoneme models read from the lexicon.

α<sub>t</sub>(j) = [ Σ<sub>i</sub> α<sub>t−1</sub>(i) a<sub>ij</sub> ] · b<sub>j</sub>(o<sub>t</sub>)

αt(j) is the total probability of the first t frames ending in state j; the sum runs over all states i at t−1; aij is the transition probability; bj(ot) is the emission density of frame ot in state j.

The forward algorithm applies this recursion and sums over every state path, giving P(X|W). Viterbi replaces the sum by a maximum and remembers the best predecessor, giving the single most likely path. Both are needed: forward for likelihoods during training, Viterbi for decoding, where the recogniser must output one path and one sentence.

Training, context and the final word string

Training starts from a flat start: transition probabilities uniform, emission means and variances copied from all speech. Baum-Welch, which is expectation-maximisation, alternates two steps.

  1. E-step: run forward-backward to get the posterior probability of every state and every transition at every frame.
  2. M-step: re-estimate aij from expected transition counts and the Gaussian means and variances from expected frame occupancies.
  3. Repeat until the total log likelihood stops rising.

Embedded training strings all words together through the lexicon, so each phoneme model learns from every occurrence in every word. A triphone model replaces a phoneme by (left context, phoneme, right context), because the vowel in 'cat' and 'cab' differs; decision trees tie the many triphones that behave alike.

Word boundaries do not come from silence. They come from paths through the lexicon, scored by the language model; a pause inside a word is possible and a boundary without a pause is common. The decoder searches a lexicon tree or a weighted finite-state transducer that composes HMMs, lexicon and n-gram. In a DNN-HMM hybrid, a neural network supplies P(state|frame), and the emission is bj(ot) ∝ P(j|ot)/P(j).

Accuracy is not reported. Report word error rate, WER = (S + D + I)/N, where S is substitutions, D deletions, I insertions and N is the number of reference words. WER can exceed 100 % when a system inserts many words. All-neural systems replace this pipeline with CTC or attention encoder-decoder models (see Unit 6, Neural NLP).

Worked example

Question. A recogniser compares three candidate sentences for the same frame sequence X. W1: P(X|W1) = 0.02, P(W1) = 0.001. W2: P(X|W2) = 0.01, P(W2) = 0.0005. W3: P(X|W3) = 0.008, P(W3) = 0.002. (a) Which sentence does the Bayes rule choose? (b) A 10-word reference transcript gives 2 substitutions, 1 deletion and 1 insertion. Compute the word error rate.

  1. For W1: P(X|W1) · P(W1) = 0.02 × 0.001 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵.
  2. For W2: P(X|W2) · P(W2) = 0.01 × 0.0005 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶.
  3. For W3: P(X|W3) · P(W3) = 0.008 × 0.002 = 1.6 × 10⁻⁵.
  4. Compare the three products: 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ > 1.6 × 10⁻⁵ > 5.0 × 10⁻⁶, so the largest is W1.
  5. WER = (S + D + I)/N = (2 + 1 + 1)/10 = 4/10 = 0.40.

Answer. The Bayes rule chooses Ŵ = W1, because 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ is the largest product; the word error rate is 0.40, that is 40 %.

Exam traps

  • Maximising P(X|W) alone and ignoring P(W). That makes homophone errors such as 'recognise speech' versus 'wreck a nice beach' impossible to resolve.
  • Confusing the forward algorithm with Viterbi. Forward sums over all state paths and gives P(X|W); Viterbi takes the maximum and gives one best path.
  • Reporting recognition accuracy instead of WER. WER can exceed 100 % when there are many insertions, so an 'accuracy' figure is misleading.
  • Assuming silence marks word boundaries. Boundaries come from the lexicon and the language model; pauses can occur inside words and boundaries can occur without pauses.
  • Mixing up the transition probability aij with the emission density bj(ot). One moves between hidden states, the other scores the observed frame.

7.7 Text-to-speech synthesis

Text-to-speech synthesis, or TTS, turns written text into spoken audio. It runs the recognition path backwards: normalise the text, convert it to phonemes, plan prosody, and generate a waveform. The output is used in screen readers, railway and metro announcements, IVR menus, and UPI payment confirmations. Two classic families are rule-based formant synthesis, which builds sound from a source and filters, and concatenative synthesis, which joins recorded units. Both need text normalisation and a prosody plan; they differ in how the waveform is made.

You need: phonemes and syllables from <a href='#phonetics-categories-of-speech-sounds-and-articulation'>7.2, frames, F0 and coding from <a href='#speech-features-and-speech-coding'>7.5, and tokenisation and normalisation from Unit 1.

From text to a phoneme string

The first stage is text normalisation. It rewrites anything that is not an ordinary word into the words a speaker would say. '₹250' becomes 'two hundred fifty rupees'. A date such as '12/08/2025' becomes 'twelfth August twenty twenty-five' in one locale. 'Dr.' becomes 'Doctor'. An abbreviation such as 'IRCTC' may be spelled out or read as a word.

  1. Normalise: expand numbers, money, dates, times and abbreviations.
  2. Tokenise into sentences, words and punctuation, using Unit 1 ideas.
  3. Look up each word in a pronunciation dictionary to get phonemes and stress.
  4. For unknown words, apply letter-to-sound rules.
  5. Join the phonemes into a string and keep syllable and word boundaries.

A phoneme is the smallest sound unit that changes meaning: /k/ in 'cat' versus /b/ in 'bat'. Grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, G2P, is the name for the step that turns a written word into its phoneme string. A dictionary is preferred for English because spelling is irregular. Letter-to-sound rules handle names and new words. Languages with regular spelling, such as Hindi or Telugu, can rely more on rules. Punctuation is kept because it later marks pauses and phrase boundaries.

Prosody: duration, pitch and pauses

Prosody is the rhythm and melody of speech. A prosody model assigns every phoneme a duration, gives the phrase an F0 contour, and places pauses. Duration depends on the phoneme (vowels are longer than stops), its position in the syllable, stress, and position in the phrase. A phrase-final syllable is lengthened. Pauses go at strong punctuation, not at every comma.

F0(t) = F0(0) − k × t

F0(t) is the fundamental frequency in hertz at time t seconds; F0(0) is the starting pitch in hertz; k is the fall rate in hertz per second. This is a simple declination line for a statement.

Stress raises F0 and lengthens the vowel. A question may add a final rise instead of falling. These rules are enough for an intelligible robotic voice, but not for natural emotion. More on emotion is in <a href='#emotion-recognition-from-speech'>7.8.

Waveform generation: rules or recorded units

Rule-based formant synthesis uses the source-filter model from <a href='#human-speech-production-and-its-discrete-time-model'>7.1. A voiced source is a pulse train at F0; fricatives use noise; resonators set the formants for each phoneme. The Klatt formant synthesiser is the classic example. It needs little memory and can vary pitch freely, but it sounds buzzy.

Concatenative synthesis joins recorded speech. A diphone database stores one recording for every pair of phonemes, cut from the centre of one phoneme to the centre of the next. Unit selection uses a larger database and chooses longer chunks. Pitch marking marks glottal cycles. PSOLA, pitch-synchronous overlap-add, shifts and repeats or drops pitch-synchronous grains. It changes duration and F0 without changing the short-time spectrum.

C = C_target + C_join; C_target = w_F0 × |F0_c − F0_t|/100 + w_d × |d_c − d_t|/100

C is total cost; C_target is target cost; C_join is join cost, which measures spectral mismatch and discontinuity at the splice; F0_c and d_c are the candidate unit's fundamental frequency in hertz and duration in milliseconds; F0_t and d_t are the target values; w_F0 and w_d are weights, 0.6 and 0.4 in the worked example; the divisor 100 rescales the F0 penalty (hertz) and the duration penalty (milliseconds) into units of 100 Hz and 100 ms, so both terms are of similar size and can be added after weighting; it puts them on a comparable scale, it does not remove the units.

The total cost is target cost plus join cost. Target cost measures how close a candidate is to the wanted F0 and duration. Join cost measures how smoothly two neighbouring units splice. The synthesiser picks the unit with minimum total. Evaluation uses mean opinion score (MOS), a 1–5 listener rating, and intelligibility tests that count words listeners understand. Neural TTS replaces these stages with a trained model; see Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A unit-selection synthesiser must choose a recorded unit for the target diphone /a-t/. The target F0 is 180 Hz and the target duration is 90 ms. Use weight 0.6 for the F0 term and 0.4 for the duration term, with both penalties divided by 100. Candidate 1 has F0 = 170 Hz, duration = 100 ms, join cost = 0.20. Candidate 2 has F0 = 200 Hz, duration = 80 ms, join cost = 0.50. Compute each total cost and pick the better unit.

  1. Target values: F0_t = 180 Hz and d_t = 90 ms.
  2. Candidate 1 F0 penalty: |170 − 180|/100 = 10/100 = 0.10. Weighted term: 0.6 × 0.10 = 0.06.
  3. Candidate 1 duration penalty: |100 − 90|/100 = 10/100 = 0.10. Weighted term: 0.4 × 0.10 = 0.04.
  4. Candidate 1 total: 0.06 + 0.04 + 0.20 = 0.30.
  5. Candidate 2 F0 penalty: |200 − 180|/100 = 20/100 = 0.20. Weighted term: 0.6 × 0.20 = 0.12.
  6. Candidate 2 duration penalty: |80 − 90|/100 = 10/100 = 0.10. Weighted term: 0.4 × 0.10 = 0.04.
  7. Candidate 2 total: 0.12 + 0.04 + 0.50 = 0.66.
  8. Compare: 0.30 < 0.66, so candidate 1 has the minimum total cost.

Answer. Candidate 1 is chosen because its total cost is 0.30, which is less than candidate 2's total cost of 0.66.

Exam traps

  • Treating synthesis as plain unit concatenation with no prosody. The result sounds robotic and is often marked wrong; durations, F0 and pauses must be planned.
  • Swapping target cost and join cost. Target cost measures how close a unit is to the wanted F0 and duration; join cost measures how smoothly two neighbouring units splice.
  • Leaving text normalisation as an afterthought. '₹250' must be spoken as words; it is a graded pipeline step, not a display problem.
  • Thinking PSOLA selects units. PSOLA joins and stretches already chosen grains; unit selection is the separate minimum-cost search.
  • Confusing MOS with intelligibility. MOS is a 1–5 listener opinion score; intelligibility tests count how many words listeners correctly understand.

7.8 Emotion recognition from speech

Emotion is carried less by the words and more by how they are said: pitch rises in anger and happiness, energy drops in sadness, pauses lengthen in fear. So we describe each utterance by summary statistics of pitch, energy and spectral features over its frames, and classify that vector into a label. The labels are usually anger, happiness, sadness, fear, disgust, surprise and neutral. The idea is used to spot upset customers in a call-centre queue. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: 7.5 for MFCC and delta features, 7.3 for spectrograms, and Unit 5 for SVM and classification metrics.

Cues, labels and corpora

Emotion labels are the six basics — anger, happiness, sadness, fear, disgust, surprise — plus neutral. Words matter little; prosody and voice quality carry most of the signal.

  • F0 (fundamental frequency, the vocal-fold vibration rate in Hz): mean and range rise in anger and happiness, fall in sadness.
  • Energy (frame loudness): high in anger and happiness, low in sadness.
  • Speaking rate in syllables per second: faster in anger and happiness, slower in sadness.
  • Pause length: longer in sadness and fear.

Voice quality adds jitter (cycle-to-cycle variation of the F0 period) and shimmer (cycle-to-cycle variation of amplitude); both rise in tense voices. Spectral shape comes from MFCCs and their deltas (see 7.5).

eGeMAPS is a standard fixed bundle of such low-level descriptors. Corpora are acted (actors perform the emotion) or natural (real recordings). Acted speech is easier; scores drop on natural speech. Always state whether testing is speaker-dependent or speaker-independent.

From frames to an utterance vector

  1. Frame the signal: 25 ms frames with a 10 ms hop.
  2. Compute frame features: F0, energy, 13 MFCCs and their deltas.
  3. Summarise each feature over the utterance's voiced frames by mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum.
  4. Classify the summarised vector: an SVM with an RBF kernel is the baseline; a CNN on the spectrogram (see 7.3) or an LSTM on the frame sequence is the neural alternative.

Evaluation uses a confusion matrix C, where C[i][j] counts utterances of true class i predicted as class j.

accuracy = (1/N) Σ C[i][i]     recall(i) = C[i][i] / Σ C[i][j]     UAR = (1/K) Σ recall(i)

N is the number of test utterances, K the number of emotion classes, C the confusion matrix with C[i][j] = utterances of true class i predicted as class j; sums run over i or j as shown. UAR (unweighted average recall) gives every class equal weight, whatever its size.

Worked example

Question. A test set has 100 utterances: 40 angry, 35 happy, 25 neutral. The classifier gets angry 32 right (5 as happy, 3 as neutral), happy 28 right (4 as angry, 3 as neutral) and neutral 20 right (2 as angry, 3 as happy). Find accuracy, per-class recall and unweighted average recall.

  1. Row sums check: 32+5+3 = 40 angry; 4+28+3 = 35 happy; 2+3+20 = 25 neutral; total = 100.
  2. Accuracy = (32+28+20)/100 = 80/100 = 0.80.
  3. Recall(angry) = 32/40 = 0.80.
  4. Recall(happy) = 28/35 = 0.80.
  5. Recall(neutral) = 20/25 = 0.80.
  6. UAR = (0.80+0.80+0.80)/3 = 0.80.

Answer. Accuracy = 0.80 (80 %) and unweighted average recall = 0.80.

Exam traps

  • Reporting accuracy with imbalanced classes: always answering 'angry' scores 0.40 here with no skill. Report UAR.
  • Splitting by utterance instead of by speaker leaks the speaker's voice into test data and inflates the score.
  • Acted-corpus scores do not transfer to natural calls; state the corpus and the split.
  • F0 in Hz depends on speaker sex and age, so absolute values are not comparable across speakers.
  • High energy alone does not mean anger — happiness raises it too. Arousal is easier than valence.

Takeaways

  1. Speech production is a source-filter system: lungs supply air, vocal folds switch voicing and the vocal tract filters the sound.
  2. Sampling turns the source and the slowly changing vocal-tract filter into discrete-time signals.
  3. Every speech sound belongs to a place and manner class, with voicing as a third major contrast.
  4. Speech is nonstationary, so analysis uses short frames and the short-time Fourier transform.
  5. A spectrogram shows how the frequency content of speech changes over time.
  6. The ear uses overlapping critical bands, so Mel or Bark scales suit speech features better than a linear frequency axis.
  7. MFCCs and linear prediction compress a frame, while speech codecs compress the waveform by discarding what the ear may not miss.
  8. HMM recognition chooses W = argmax P(X|W)·P(W), combining acoustic evidence with a language prior.
  9. Text-to-speech reverses recognition: text to phonemes, a prosody plan, then a waveform by rules or recorded units.
  10. Emotion recognition uses utterance-level pitch, energy, pause, speaking-rate, jitter and shimmer statistics.

Glossary

Acoustic model
A model that gives P(X|W), the likelihood of acoustic feature sequence X for a word or phone sequence W.
Argmax
The argument that maximises a function, written argmax, as in choosing W that maximises P(X|W)·P(W).
Bark scale
A perceptual frequency scale based on critical bands.
Beam search
A search that keeps only the few best partial hypotheses at each step.
Cepstrum
The inverse Fourier transform of the log magnitude spectrum, used to separate source and filter effects.
Codec
A coder-decoder pair that compresses speech for storage or transmission.
Delta features
Frame-to-frame differences that add local dynamics to a static feature vector.
Discrete cosine transform
A transform that converts log mel spectrum values into decorrelated cepstral coefficients.
Emission probability
The probability of an observed acoustic frame given a hidden HMM state.
Energy
A measure of signal strength, often the sum of squared sample values in a frame.
Formant
A resonance peak of the vocal tract visible as a dark band in a spectrogram.
Framing
Dividing a speech signal into short overlapping segments for analysis.
Fundamental frequency
The repetition rate of voiced speech, perceived as pitch and measured in hertz.
Mel filterbank
A set of triangular filters spaced on the Mel scale.
Parametric synthesis
Text-to-speech that generates speech from a compact model rather than joining recorded units.
Pause length
The duration of silence or near-silence between speech segments.
Phonetics
The study of speech sounds, their production, transmission and perception.
Pitch
The perceived highness of a voice, related to fundamental frequency.
Pre-emphasis
A first-order high-pass filter that boosts high frequencies before speech analysis.
Speaking rate
The number of syllables, words or phones spoken per unit time.
Unit selection
Concatenative synthesis that chooses recorded units from a database to match a target context.
Vocoder
A system that analyses speech into parameters and resynthesises it from those parameters.
Window function
A taper such as Hamming applied to a frame to reduce spectral leakage.
Word boundary detection
Finding where one word ends and the next begins in a continuous speech signal.
