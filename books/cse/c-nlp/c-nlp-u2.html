2.1 N-gram language models

An n-gram language model gives a probability to a word sequence. It reads that probability from counts in a corpus: the chance of each word depends only on the n−1 words before it. A bigram model looks one word back; a trigram looks two back. The model powers spell correction, speech recognition, predictive keyboards and machine translation scoring, where a system must choose between word sequences that all look possible.

You need Unit 1 on tokenization and normalisation, so that words are already defined, plus basic conditional probability.

From a sentence to a product of small probabilities

A word n-gram is a block of n consecutive tokens. The order n is the block length: a unigram has n=1, a bigram n=2, a trigram n=3. A language model assigns a probability to a whole sentence, not to one word alone.

P(w₁ … w_m) = Π_{i=1}^{m} P(w_i | w₁ … w_{i−1})

P(w₁ … w_m) is the probability of the sentence; w_i is the i-th word; m is the number of words; P(w_i | w₁ … w_{i−1}) is the chance of word i after all earlier words.

The full history w₁ … w_{i−1} is too long to count. The Markov assumption keeps only the last n−1 words. A bigram uses one word of history: P(w_i | w₁ … w_{i−1}) ≈ P(w_i | w_{i−1}). A trigram uses two: P(w_i | w_{i−2} w_{i−1}).

This cut is the whole trick. With boundaries added, the sentence “the cat sat” is scored as P(the | ⟨s⟩) × P(cat | the) × P(sat | cat) × P(⟨/s⟩ | sat); the middle factors show the Markov cut, and each context is short enough to count. A bigram model is a first-order Markov chain: the next word depends on the present word only.

Maximum likelihood counts and boundary symbols

P(w_i | w_{i−n+1} … w_{i−1}) = c(w_{i−n+1} … w_i) ÷ c(w_{i−n+1} … w_{i−1})

c(x) is the number of times the token sequence x appears in the corpus; the numerator counts the full n-gram, and the denominator counts its (n−1)-word history. For a bigram: P(w_i | w_{i−1}) = c(w_{i−1} w_i) ÷ c(w_{i−1}).

The denominator is c(context), not c(w_i). For P(sat | cat), divide c(cat sat) by c(cat).

Add ⟨s⟩ before the first word and ⟨/s⟩ after the last word. Both are tokens and take part in counts. ⟨s⟩ makes P(first word | ⟨s⟩) defined. ⟨/s⟩ gives the sentence a stopping probability, so P(⟨/s⟩ | last word) is in the product; without it, a model cannot compare sentences of different lengths fairly.

For the sample corpus, the bigram count matrix has a row for each context and a column for each next word. The table lists non-zero cells and the row probability P(next | context).

Smoothing moves probability mass to unseen events. Some syllabi call this topic “unsmoothed n-grams”.

Worked example

Question. Corpus: “the cat sat on the mat the cat sat” (9 words). Add ⟨s⟩ at the front and ⟨/s⟩ at the end. Using an unsmoothed bigram model, find P(sat | cat) and P(on | cat). Show the bigram counts.

  1. Tokenise and add boundaries: ⟨s⟩ the cat sat on the mat the cat sat ⟨/s⟩. This is 11 tokens.
  2. List the 10 bigrams: ⟨s⟩ the, the cat, cat sat, sat on, on the, the mat, mat the, the cat, cat sat, sat ⟨/s⟩.
  3. Count: ⟨s⟩ the = 1, the cat = 2, cat sat = 2, sat on = 1, on the = 1, the mat = 1, mat the = 1, sat ⟨/s⟩ = 1. Total bigrams = 10.
  4. The context cat occurs 2 times, so c(cat) = 2.
  5. P(sat | cat) = c(cat sat) ÷ c(cat) = 2 ÷ 2 = 1.00.
  6. P(on | cat) = c(cat on) ÷ c(cat) = 0 ÷ 2 = 0.00.

Answer. P(sat | cat) = 1.00 and P(on | cat) = 0.00; the zero is exactly what smoothing in 2.2 repairs.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting ⟨s⟩ and ⟨/s⟩ changes the token count, the bigram count and the denominator for the last word.
  • Reading a cell as P(context | word) when the table stores P(word | context); a row fixes the context.
  • Using c(word) in the denominator instead of c(context). For P(sat | cat), divide by c(cat), not c(sat).
  • Treating a zero count as proof that a word string is impossible; it means unseen in this corpus, which smoothing repairs.
  • Examiner favourite: ask for P(on | cat) after giving P(sat | cat); the answer is 0, not 1/2.

2.2 Smoothing, interpolation and backoff

A count-based language model gives an n-gram probability zero if that n-gram never appeared in training. One zero kills a sentence probability, because sentence probability is a product of terms. Smoothing fixes this by moving a small amount of probability mass from seen n-grams to unseen ones. Interpolation and backoff combine a detailed high-order model with a safer low-order model. Classic n-gram systems in phone keyboards, speech recognition and statistical MT use some form of this.

You need: 2.1, especially the count matrix and the maximum likelihood estimate.

Why a zero count is fatal

In 2.1 the probability of a sentence w1…wm is the product of P(wi | context) over all words. If any factor is 0, the whole product is 0. A trigram model has many possible trigrams, and most never appear in a small corpus. A test sentence then gets probability 0. The log probability becomes −∞ and perplexity becomes infinite; see 2.3.

PMLE(w) = c(w) / N

c(w) is the number of times word w occurs; N is the total number of word tokens. For a conditional n-gram, PMLE(wi | h) = c(h wi) / c(h), where h is the history, that is, the previous n−1 words.

Smoothing assigns nonzero probability to unseen events. It does not invent data. It reserves part of the total probability mass for events not seen in training. The same idea applies to unigrams and to n-grams with a history.

Add-one and add-k smoothing

Add-one smoothing adds 1 to every count. For unigrams, Padd1(w) = (c(w)+1)/(N+V). Here V is the number of word types in the vocabulary. The denominator is N+V, not N. For a conditional n-gram, Padd1(wi | h) = (c(h wi)+1)/(c(h)+V). If c(h wi) is 0, the numerator is 1, so the probability is nonzero.

Paddk(w) = (c(w)+k) / (N + kV)

k is the smoothing amount, a small positive number. k=1 gives add-one. k<1 gives less mass to unseen words. k is chosen on held-out data. V is the vocabulary size, including words with count 0.

Discounting is a different route. Take a little probability from each seen count and give it to unseen events. Good-Turing gives one line of intuition: the total mass for unseen events is estimated from the number of n-grams seen exactly once, called singletons. Add-one is simple but gives too much mass to unseen words when V is large.

Interpolation and backoff

Linear interpolation mixes models of different orders with weights λ. A common trigram form is P(wi | h) = λ1Ptrigram + λ2Pbigram + λ3Punigram, with λ1+λ2+λ3=1 and every λ≥0. If the trigram count is zero, its term is zero, but the bigram and unigram terms still give a nonzero result. The λ are not set on training data. Choose them on held-out data to maximise held-out likelihood or to minimise perplexity. A two-model form is P = λPbigram + (1−λ)Punigram.

Katz backoff works differently. If c(h wi) is greater than 0, use the discounted conditional count. If it is 0, back off to a shorter history. The form is Pkatz(wi | h) = c*(h wi)/c(h) when c(h wi)>0, and α(h)Pkatz(wi | h′) otherwise. Here h′ drops the oldest word from h, c* is the discounted count (c*<c for discounted counts; high counts may keep c*=c), and α(h) is a normalising factor that makes probabilities sum to 1 over all words. Backoff does not mix all orders at once; it uses the longer context only when that context was seen.

Worked example

Question. A corpus has N = 10 word tokens. The unigram counts are the 4, cat 2, sat 2, on 1, mat 1, dog 0. The vocabulary has V = 6 words, because dog is a known word that never appeared. Find the maximum likelihood and add-one estimates for the and dog. Then find the interpolated bigram P(dog | the) using λbigram = 0.7 and λunigram = 0.3. The bigram count c(the dog) is 0. Use the maximum likelihood estimate for the bigram term and the add-one estimate for the unigram term.

  1. N = 10 tokens and V = 6 word types.
  2. c(the) = 4, so PMLE(the) = 4/10 = 0.40.
  3. Add-one for the: (4+1)/(10+6) = 5/16 = 0.3125.
  4. c(dog) = 0, so PMLE(dog) = 0/10 = 0.
  5. Add-one for dog: (0+1)/(10+6) = 1/16 = 0.0625.
  6. c(the dog) = 0, so the MLE bigram Pbigram(dog | the) = 0.
  7. Interpolated value using the MLE bigram and add-one unigram: 0.7×0 + 0.3×0.0625 = 0.01875.

Answer. The maximum likelihood estimates are 0.40 for the and 0 for dog; the add-one estimates are 0.3125 for the and 0.0625 for dog; using the MLE bigram and add-one unigram, the interpolated bigram probability P(dog | the) is 0.01875, about 0.019.

Exam traps

  • Using N instead of N+V in the add-one denominator. The probabilities then sum to more than 1.
  • Choosing λ values that do not sum to 1, or tuning λ on training data instead of held-out data.
  • Confusing interpolation with backoff. Interpolation mixes all orders; backoff switches to a shorter context only when the longer count is zero.
  • Reporting a maximum likelihood zero as a smoothed probability without saying which smoothing method was used.

2.3 Evaluating language models: perplexity

A language model returns a probability for a whole sentence. That number is tiny and it shrinks as the sentence grows, so it cannot be compared across test sets. Perplexity removes both problems: it turns the sentence probability into an average surprise per predicted word, so it has no units and lower is better. Speech recognisers, machine translation systems and spelling correctors all choose between candidate language models by comparing perplexity on text the models have never seen.

You need: 2.1 for the chain rule that turns a sentence probability into a product of conditional probabilities, and 2.2 for smoothing, because a single unseen n-gram gives probability zero and makes perplexity infinite.

Definition and the branching-factor reading

Take a test text W made of m predicted tokens. The first token is predicted after the sentence-start marker ⟨s⟩, and the last token is the sentence-end marker ⟨/s⟩. So m equals the number of ordinary words plus one. The model gives the whole sequence a probability, and perplexity is that probability raised to the power −1/m.

PP(W) = P(W)^(−1/m)

PP(W) is the perplexity of the test text W, a positive number with no units; P(W) is the probability the model gives the whole token sequence; m is the number of predicted tokens, which counts ⟨/s⟩ and does not count ⟨s⟩.

PP(W) = exp( −(1/m) Σ ln P(wᵢ | w₁…wᵢ₋₁) )

exp is the exponential function eˣ; ln is the natural logarithm; the sum runs over i = 1 to m; P(wᵢ | w₁…wᵢ₋₁) is the model's probability for the i-th predicted token given every token before it.

The second form is the working one. Multiplying many small probabilities underflows to zero on a computer, so implementations add logs, negate and divide. The quantity H = −(1/m) Σ log₂ P(wᵢ | history) is the cross-entropy in bits per token, and PP = 2^H. Perplexity and cross-entropy always move together: the model with lower cross-entropy also has lower perplexity.

The branching-factor reading makes the number meaningful. Suppose a model is completely unsure among k equally likely continuations at every step. Each conditional probability is then 1/k, the product is k^(−m), and PP = k. A model with perplexity 20 behaves, on average, like a reader choosing uniformly among 20 possible next words. Lower is better.

Splits, unseen words and fair comparisons

  • Training text: estimate the n-gram counts here.
  • Dev text, also called validation: choose the order n, the smoothing method and its constants here.
  • Test text: score once, at the very end, and report here. Never tune on it.
  • Test text must be unseen. A model that has memorised the sentences will look far better than it is.
  • Replace every test word missing from the training vocabulary by one token ⟨unk⟩. Add ⟨unk⟩ to the vocabulary and give it a real count, usually by mapping rare training words to it too.
  • Compare perplexities only when both models use the same vocabulary and the same tokenisation.

The last rule is easy to forget. A model with a 50,000-word vocabulary faces a different set of choices at every step from one with a 5,000-word vocabulary, so the two perplexities are not on the same scale and cannot be ranked. Moving from whole words to subword units changes m as well, because one word becomes several tokens. Write the vocabulary size and the token definition next to every perplexity figure you report.

The ranking survives any increasing monotone transform. Since PP = 2^H, comparing perplexities is exactly comparing average log-loss. One bit per token less halves the perplexity, so a gap of 2 or 3 points means little while a factor of two means a lot.

Comparing orders on one test set

Fix the test set, the vocabulary and the smoother, and change only the order n. The usual picture is a U shape. The unigram model ignores context and is worst on test at 130. The bigram improves to 70. The trigram is best at 55. Orders 4 and 5 get worse, 60 and then 75, because most 4-grams and 5-grams in the test text never appeared in training, so the smoothed estimates are noisy.

Training perplexity tells a different story: 120, 60, 40, 30, 25, falling at every step. Higher orders memorise the training text. The widening gap between a falling training curve and a rising test curve is overfitting, and it is why n is picked on dev data. Choose the n with the lowest dev perplexity and report that model's test perplexity once.

Worked example

Question. A smoothed bigram model gives P(the | ⟨s⟩) = 0.5, P(cat | the) = 0.4 and P(⟨/s⟩ | cat) = 0.5. Find the perplexity of the test sentence "the cat". The vocabulary is {the, cat, dog, ⟨/s⟩}. Compare with a uniform model over the same vocabulary.

  1. Write the sentence with its markers: ⟨s⟩ the cat ⟨/s⟩. The model predicts three tokens: the, cat and ⟨/s⟩. So m = 3. The marker ⟨s⟩ is conditioning context, not a prediction.
  2. Multiply the conditional probabilities: 0.5 × 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.1.
  3. Apply the definition: PP = 0.1^(−1/3). Take logs: ln PP = −(1/3) ln 0.1 = −(1/3)(−2.302585) = +0.767528. Exponentiate: PP = e^0.767528 = 2.1544.
  4. Uniform baseline over 4 words: each token has probability 1/4 = 0.25. Product = 0.25³ = 0.015625. PP = 0.015625^(−1/3) = 4.00.
  5. Compare: 2.15 is smaller than 4.00, so the bigram model is less surprised by this sentence than the uniform model.

Output

0.1
2.15
4.0

Answer. The bigram model's perplexity is 2.15 and the uniform model's is 4.00, so the bigram is better.

Exam traps

  • Off-by-one in m. m counts predicted tokens: every ordinary word plus ⟨/s⟩. The ⟨s⟩ marker is context, not a prediction. Counting ⟨s⟩ or dropping ⟨/s⟩ shifts the root and gives a wrong answer.
  • Comparing perplexities of models with different vocabularies or tokenisations. A larger vocabulary gives the model more choices at each step, and a subword model predicts more, shorter tokens, so the numbers are on different scales.
  • Thinking a larger perplexity is better. Lower perplexity means the model gives the test text higher probability.
  • Tuning the order n, the smoothing constant or the vocabulary on the test set. All of that belongs on dev data; test is scored once.
  • Quoting a finite perplexity from an unsmoothed model. One zero probability makes the product zero and the perplexity infinite.

Not for the exam. 2.4 Part-of-speech tagging: rule-based, stochastic, transformation-based

Part-of-speech tagging assigns every token in a sentence its grammatical class: noun, verb, determiner and so on. The tagger's knowledge can come from hand-written rules, from counts in a tagged corpus, or from rules learned by correcting a baseline. Tagging is used before parsing, named entity recognition and speech synthesis, and in Indian-language pipelines where case and gender marks change the tag.

You need tokenization and word classes from Unit 1, and the probability notation from 2.1 helps for the stochastic case.

Tags, open classes and closed classes

A part of speech is a word's grammatical class in a sentence. Open classes take new words: noun, verb, adjective, adverb. Closed classes are small and fixed: determiner, preposition, pronoun, conjunction. A tagset is the fixed label list. The Penn Treebank tagset for English has about 36 tags, for example NN noun, VB verb, JJ adjective, RB adverb, DT determiner, IN preposition, PRP pronoun, CC conjunction.

Indian-language tagsets often add case, gender and number, so one label may be 'noun, dative, feminine, singular'. Ambiguity is normal: book is NN in 'a book' and VB in 'to book'; that is DT in 'that car', PRP in 'I know that', and IN in 'so that we leave'. The average English word has more than one possible tag, so context must decide.

Rule-based tagging

A rule-based tagger works in two stages. Stage 1 is a lexicon: a dictionary mapping each word to its possible tags. fly gets NN and VB; that gets DT, PRP, IN. Stage 2 is hand-written context rules that delete candidates. A rule may test the previous tag, the next word, or a suffix. Example: 'after the infinitive marker TO, a verb is VB' removes NN from fly. Rules run in a fixed order and the surviving tag is output.

ENGTWOL is the classic English design: a morphological lexicon followed by about a thousand constraints, each removing a reading. The strength is precision on known patterns with no training corpus. The weakness is cost: linguists write and order every rule, and a new domain needs new rules.

Stochastic and transformation-based tagging

A stochastic tagger estimates P(t|w), the probability of tag t given word w, from a tagged corpus.

P(t | w) = count(w, t) / count(w)

count(w, t) is how often word w carries tag t; count(w) is the total occurrences of w.

The most-frequent-tag baseline picks argmaxt P(t|w). If fly is NN 7 times and VB 3 times, baseline outputs NN everywhere. It is simple and roughly 90% accurate on English but ignores context.

A transformation-based (Brill) tagger starts from that baseline and compares it with the correct tags. It finds errors and proposes rules from templates: 'change X to Y when the previous tag is Z', or 'when the next word is W'. Each candidate is scored by errors fixed minus errors broken. The best rule is kept and applied to the corpus. The loop repeats until no rule helps. At test time the ordered rules are applied one after another. Later rules can override earlier ones.

Issues in POS tagging

  • Unknown words: names, typos and new words have no lexicon entry or counts, so P(t|w) is undefined. Suffix rules or an NN default are guesses.
  • Ambiguity: many words have several tags; wrong context rules and sparse counts both cause errors.
  • Tagset size and granularity: fine tags such as NNP versus NNPS are harder and need more data; coarse tags merge useful distinctions.
  • Annotation cost: a tagged corpus needs trained annotators. Inter-annotator agreement on English is often above 95%, so human labels set a ceiling.
  • Domain shift and tokenization errors change the tag distribution.

2.5 Hidden Markov models and the Viterbi algorithm

An HMM tags a sentence by treating tags as hidden states and words as observed output. You cannot see the tag path, only the words, so the model assigns probabilities to state paths and Viterbi picks the single most probable one. POS tagging, named-entity chunking and sequence labelling use this idea. The model is small: start probabilities, transition probabilities and emission probabilities. Viterbi fills a table of best scores and backpointers. It returns a tag for every word, not just a score.

You need 2.1 for the Markov assumption and 2.4 for what a tag is.

The HMM triple and two independence assumptions

An HMM is a model λ=(π,A,B). Let S be the number of tags. Let T be the number of tokens. Let qt be the tag at position t and wt the word. The three parts are π(j)=P(q1=j), the start probability; a(i,j)=P(qt=j|qt−1=i), the transition probability; b(j,o)=P(wt=o|qt=j), the emission probability. Row sums are 1: Σja(i,j)=1 and Σob(i,o)=1. Two independence assumptions make this small. The Markov assumption says the current tag depends only on the previous tag: P(qt|q1…qt−1)=a(qt−1,qt). The emission assumption says the current word depends only on its own tag: P(wt|q1…qT,w1…wt−1)=b(qt,wt). With a dummy start state q0, where a(q0,q1)=π(q1), the joint probability is P(Q,W)=∏t=1Ta(qt−1,qt)b(qt,wt). Here Q is the tag sequence and W is the word sequence.

P(Q,W)=∏<sub>t=1</sub><sup>T</sup> a(q<sub>t−1</sub>,q<sub>t</sub>) b(q<sub>t</sub>,w<sub>t</sub>)

Q is the full tag sequence q1…qT; W is the full word sequence w1…wT; q0 is a dummy start state; a(q0,q1)=π(q1); a(i,j) is the transition probability; b(j,o) is the emission probability; T is the number of tokens.

Three classic problems and the forward sum

Three classic problems are asked of an HMM. First, the likelihood P(W|λ): the total probability of the words under all tag paths. The forward algorithm sums: αt(j)=b(j,wt)Σiαt−1(i)a(i,j), with α1(j)=π(j)b(j,w1) and P(W|λ)=ΣjαT(j). Here αt(j) is the summed probability of all paths ending in tag j after t words; i runs over tags. Second, the best state sequence: which single tag path is most probable? Viterbi takes maxima instead of sums. Third, parameter learning: count transitions and emissions from tagged text, or use Baum-Welch (EM) on raw text. Keep the answers separate: forward gives a likelihood, Viterbi gives one path, Baum-Welch gives parameters.

α<sub>t</sub>(j)=b(j,w<sub>t</sub>) Σ<sub>i</sub> α<sub>t−1</sub>(i) a(i,j)

αt(j) is the summed probability of all tag paths ending in tag j after t words; b(j,wt) is the emission of word wt from tag j; a(i,j) is the transition from tag i to tag j; the sum is over all previous tags i; base case α1(j)=π(j)b(j,w1); P(W|λ)=Σj αT(j).

Viterbi: best path, not sum

Viterbi fills a trellis with one node for each tag at each word position. δt(j) is the probability of the single best tag path ending in tag j after t words. ψt(j) is the previous tag on that path; it is the backpointer. For t=1, δ1(j)=π(j)b(j,w1) and ψ1(j)=start. For t=2…T, δt(j)=maxi[δt−1(i)a(i,j)]b(j,wt) and ψt(j)=argmaxi[δt−1(i)a(i,j)]. The max is over previous tag i; δt−1(i) already stores the best path to i. After the last word, pick qT=argmaxjδT(j). Backtrace: for t=T…2, set qt−1=ψt(qt). Reverse the list to get q1…qT. Cost is O(T·S²) time for T tokens and S tags: each target tag compares S previous tags. Memory is O(T·S) with all backpointers, or O(S) scores if only the previous row is kept. The figure shows the two-word example: the winning end node has δ2(V)=0.108, with backpointer N.

δ<sub>t</sub>(j)=max<sub>i</sub> [δ<sub>t−1</sub>(i) a(i,j)] b(j,w<sub>t</sub>)

δt(j) is the probability of the single best tag path ending in tag j after t words; i runs over previous tags; a(i,j) is the transition probability; b(j,wt) is the emission probability of the current word wt from tag j; ψt(j) records the argmax i as the backpointer.

2.6 Maximum entropy Markov models

A maximum entropy Markov model tags a sequence with a log-linear classifier at every position. Instead of one emission table, many overlapping features can fire: the word, its suffix, capitalisation, the previous tag, the next word. Each feature has a weight, and the weighted sum scores a candidate tag. The scores become local probabilities that sum to one at that position. Because the model is still a chain, Viterbi decodes the whole sentence. Taggers use it for part-of-speech tagging and named entity recognition. Only a few universities teach this model, but it explains why CRFs exist. Some syllabi call it a conditional Markov model.

You need: 2.5 for the HMM chain and Viterbi, and the logistic regression and maximum entropy classifier from Unit 5.

Why the HMM emission table is too narrow

An HMM scores a tag sequence with two tables: transition P(ti | ti−1) and emission P(wi | ti). The emission sees one word only. It cannot ask whether the word ends in -ing, starts with a capital letter, contains a digit, or sits next to the word to. A tagger needs those clues together. For example, Chennai is a place because it is capitalised and appears after in; the single word identity may be unseen in training.

A maximum entropy Markov model (MEMM) replaces the emission table with a classifier at each position i. Let x = x1 ... xn be the whole sentence, ti the tag at position i, and ti−1 the previous tag. A feature function fj(i, ti−1, ti, x) returns 1 when its condition holds, else 0. Each feature has a weight λj. The score of tag ti is the sum over all features that fire.

score(i, t<sub>i−1</sub>, t<sub>i</sub>, x) = Σ over j of λ<sub>j</sub> f<sub>j</sub>(i, t<sub>i−1</sub>, t<sub>i</sub>, x)

i is the position; ti is the candidate tag; ti−1 is the previous tag; x is the whole sentence; fj is a feature function returning 0 or 1; λj is a real-valued weight learned from data.

The local probability is a softmax over tags.

P(t<sub>i</sub> | t<sub>i−1</sub>, x) = exp(score(i, t<sub>i−1</sub>, t<sub>i</sub>, x)) ÷ Σ over t′ of exp(score(i, t<sub>i−1</sub>, t′, x))

exp(·) is the exponential function; t′ ranges over every possible tag at position i; the denominator normalises over tags at that position only, with the previous tag fixed.

Features, weights and Viterbi

Features can look anywhere in x. Typical ones are: current word equals flies; current word ends in ies; current word is capitalised; previous tag is N; next word is like; current word is in a small gazetteer of verbs. All fire for a candidate tag, so the classifier can combine spelling, context and tag history. The maximum entropy principle picks weights that make the model's feature counts on training data match the observed counts, while keeping the distribution as uniform as possible otherwise. We state that objective only; training uses iterative numerical methods.

The model is still a chain. The probability of a tag sequence t1 ... tn given x is P(t | x) = Π over i from 1 to n of P(ti | ti−1, x), with a fixed start tag t0. Π means multiply over positions i. Because each factor depends only on ti−1 and ti, Viterbi works exactly as in 2.5: keep the best score for each tag at each position, and backpointers recover the path. The only change is that the local score is a log-linear sum, not a table lookup.

Label bias and the HMM comparison

Per-position normalisation has a cost. Suppose a state offers only one outgoing tag. Its local probability is 1, so the model is barely penalised for taking that path, even when the observation is a poor match. A state with many outgoing choices spreads probability mass and is penalised. The path can therefore be chosen for its low branching rather than for the words. This is the label bias problem. It is the reason the next chapter, 2.7, normalises once over whole sequences.

Exam traps

  • The normaliser is per position over tags only. Dividing by a sum over all positions and histories gives a CRF, not a MEMM.
  • A MEMM is discriminative: it models P(t | x), not P(x, t). It is not an HMM with extra feature columns.
  • Label bias comes from per-state normalisation. Do not blame Viterbi or the feature set.
  • The previous tag is part of the feature vector, so the model is a first-order chain. If you drop ti−1, Viterbi no longer applies as a chain decoder.
  • Do not call one local probability a sentence probability. Multiply the local factors along a path to get the sequence probability.

2.7 Conditional random fields

A conditional random field (CRF) is a sequence model that scores a whole label sequence y for a sentence x with one log-linear function and normalises once over every possible sequence. It exists because per-position classifiers in a maximum entropy Markov model normalise each step alone, so a confident early step can hide later evidence. A linear-chain CRF fixes that by making complete sequences compete. Taggers use it for part-of-speech, chunking, named entities and any task where neighbouring labels depend on each other.

You need 2.6 for log-linear scoring and label bias, and 2.5 for Viterbi decoding.

One score for the whole label sequence

A conditional random field (CRF) models P(y | x) directly. Here x = (x₁, ..., xₙ) is the observed sentence and y = (y₁, ..., yₙ) is a label sequence such as tags. A linear-chain CRF is an undirected graphical model: the labels form a chain, and each label may look at the whole observation x. The chain keeps inference tractable while still allowing overlapping features.

P(y | x) = (1 / Z(x)) exp( Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> Σ<sub>j=1</sub><sup>m</sup> λ<sub>j</sub> f<sub>j</sub>(y<sub>i−1</sub>, y<sub>i</sub>, x, i) )

y is the label sequence; x is the sentence; n is the number of tokens; m is the number of feature functions; fj is feature function j; λj is its weight; y0 = START; Z(x) is the partition function.

The exponent is the sequence score S(y, x) = Σi Σj λj fj(yi−1, yi, x, i). A feature function returns a number, often 0 or 1, from the previous label, the current label, the whole sentence and the position. The weight says how useful that feature is.

Z(x) = Σy′ ∈ Y(x) exp( S(y′, x) )

Y(x) is the set of all possible label sequences for x; with two labels and n tokens, |Y(x)| = 2ⁿ.

No position is normalised alone. A high score at token 1 is weighed against every complete sequence, including sequences with different later labels. This is the difference from the per-position normaliser in 2.6.

Decoding, the partition function and training

Z(x) sums over |Y|ⁿ label sequences, where |Y| is the number of labels — too many to list. The forward algorithm computes it in O(n |Y|²) by passing a vector of partial sums along the chain. Viterbi uses the same chain but replaces sum with max, returning argmaxy P(y | x) in O(n |Y|²).

Training maximises conditional log-likelihood on tagged pairs (x, y): L = Σ log P(y | x) = Σ [S(y, x) − log Z(x)]. The gradient for λj is the observed count of feature fj on the training sequence minus its expected count under the model, P(y′ | x). Forward-backward computes those expected counts. There is no closed form, so an iterative optimiser is used.

The price is slower training: every gradient step touches all labels through Z, and every new sentence needs its own Z. Training is heavier than an HMM or a per-position classifier. Decoding remains linear in sentence length for a fixed label set.

HMM, MEMM and CRF side by side

The three sequence models answer different questions. An HMM is generative: it models P(x, y) = P(y)P(x | y) and assumes observations are independent given the state. A maximum entropy Markov model (MEMM) is discriminative but normalises at each position, so a locally confident transition can hide evidence from later tokens. A CRF is discriminative and normalises once over whole sequences, which removes that label bias.

  • Z(x) is not the product of the per-position normalisers from 2.6. It is one sum over all complete label sequences, and it changes with x.
  • The score S(y, x) can be any real number; exp(S) is positive but not yet a probability. Divide by Z exactly once.
  • Set y₀ = START before the first token, or the first transition feature is missing. Do not drop the position i when a feature is position-specific.
  • Viterbi finds the highest-scoring label sequence. It does not compute Z, and the best path's score is not a probability until divided by Z.
  • Two labels and n tokens give 2ⁿ sequences, not 2n. For n = 3 the partition sum has 8 terms.

2.8 Named entity recognition

Named entity recognition (NER) finds the runs of words in a text that name a person, a place, an organisation, a date, an amount and similar things, and labels each run with its category. It exists because most later work — search, question answering, news indexing, form filling — needs the actors in a sentence, not only its grammar. A bank's KYC team, a newsroom sorting stories by company, and a chatbot reading 'pay ₹500 to Ravi on Friday' all sit on top of NER. It is usually solved as sequence labelling: one tag per token.

You need: 2.4 for the idea of a tagset, and 2.7 (with 2.5) for the sequence models that do the labelling.

The entity types

A named entity is a contiguous run of tokens that names one specific thing. NER does two jobs at once: it finds where each run starts and ends, and it puts the run into a category. Both jobs are hard. 'Apple' is a fruit in one sentence and a company in the next.

Four types cover most news and office text. PERSON (PER) names a person, for example 'Sundar Pichai'. LOCATION (LOC) names a place, natural or political, for example 'Godavari' or 'Pune'. ORGANISATION (ORG) names a company, team, agency or institution, for example 'IRCTC' or 'State Bank of India'. MISCELLANEOUS (MISC) is the leftover bin for named things that are none of these — events, laws, awards, nationalities, such as 'Kumbh Mela' or 'Bharat Ratna'.

A second family holds numeric and temporal expressions, where the value matters more than the name: DATE ('12 March 2024'), TIME ('4:30 pm'), MONEY ('₹2,500'), PERCENT ('18%'). A form-filling system wants these with a normalised value, so the tagger often writes 12/03/2024 next to the span.

The tagset is a design choice, not a law. Standard shared-task tagsets use PER, LOC, ORG and MISC; a bank may add ACCOUNT and IFSC_CODE. Changing the tagset changes the scores, so always report which one you used.

BIO encoding

A sequence labeller gives one tag per token, so the tag itself must carry both the type and the boundary. BIO does this with a prefix. B-X means 'this token begins an entity of type X'. I-X means 'this token continues an entity of type X'. O means 'outside every entity'.

The B prefix is not decoration. Two entities of the same type can sit side by side: 'Rao, Iyer and Bose' are three people. With only I-PER the three names merge into one span. B-PER restarts the span, so the boundary survives.

Some taggers use BIOES, also written BILOU. E-X ends an entity and S-X is a single-token entity. The end markers state the right boundary outright, so the model need not infer it from the next token being O. Sequences must obey the scheme: I-X may not follow O or B-Y when Y is not X. A CRF can be given a very large penalty for those transitions, so its decoder can never emit an illegal sequence.

The boundary problem appears whenever entities touch or one is a piece of another, as in 'Wipro Limited Chennai'. Exact-span scoring then punishes a run that is split differently from the gold data.

Three ways to build a tagger

Gazetteer and rules. Keep lists of known persons, cities and companies. Add patterns: a capitalised word after 'Mr' is PER; a listed city after 'in' is LOC; a capitalised word ending in 'Ltd' or 'Pvt' is ORG. This is fast and precise on known names, and it never generalises to a name that was added yesterday.

Features plus a classifier. Turn each token into a feature vector and predict its tag. Useful features: the word itself; its lowercase form; word shape (Xxx for 'Pune', XX for 'TCS', dd for a number); capitalisation flags; a prefix and suffix of three or four letters (-pur, -abad, -son, Ltd); the previous and next word; membership of a gazetteer; and the previous tag. The previous tag is what makes this sequence labelling — the classifier sees the decisions already made.

The CRF of 2.7 is the standard non-neural model here: it accepts overlapping features and scores the whole tag sequence, so neighbour tags enter each decision properly. A maximum entropy Markov model (2.6) also works and is cheaper to train. A plain HMM (2.5) sees only the current word through its emission table, so it cannot use the suffix of the next word or the previous two tags.

Scoring at entity level

Token accuracy is the wrong metric. A tagger that prints O everywhere scores about 90% on newswire, because most tokens lie outside entities, and it finds nothing at all.

Score whole entities with exact-span matching: a predicted entity is correct only if its start, end and type all match a gold entity. Let C be the number of such matches, N the number of predicted entities and G the number of gold entities.

P = C ÷ N, R = C ÷ G, F1 = 2PR ÷ (P + R)

P is precision, R is recall, C is the count of exactly matching entities, N is the number of entities the system predicted, G is the number of entities in the gold annotation; F1 is their harmonic mean, all three between 0 and 1.

One wrong boundary word costs twice: the gold entity is missed, which lowers recall, and a wrong span is invented, which lowers precision.

Worked example

Question. Tag the sentence 'Mr Rao of TCS met the team in Pune' with BIO labels over the types PER, ORG and LOC. A system then predicts the entities Rao (PER), TCS (ORG) and the span 'team in Pune' (LOC). The gold entities are Rao (PER), TCS (ORG) and Pune (LOC); 'the team' is not an entity. Compute precision, recall and F1 under exact-span matching.

  1. Tokenise: Mr, Rao, of, TCS, met, the, team, in, Pune — 9 tokens.
  2. Tag each token. The title 'Mr' is outside the span, so it is O. 'Rao' begins a person. 'TCS' begins an organisation. 'Pune' is a one-token location, which gets B-LOC (BIOES would write S-LOC). Full line: Mr/O Rao/B-PER of/O TCS/B-ORG met/O the/O team/O in/O Pune/B-LOC.
  3. Read off the gold spans: gold G = {(Rao, PER), (TCS, ORG), (Pune, LOC)}, so G = 3 entities.
  4. Read off the system spans: predicted N = {(Rao, PER), (TCS, ORG), (team in Pune, LOC)}, so N = 3 entities.
  5. Match exactly: (Rao, PER) and (TCS, ORG) match, giving C = 2. 'team in Pune' misses the gold boundary and type target, so it is a false positive, and Pune is a false negative.
  6. Precision P = C ÷ N = 2 ÷ 3 = 0.667 ≈ 0.67.
  7. Recall R = C ÷ G = 2 ÷ 3 = 0.667 ≈ 0.67.
  8. F1 = 2PR ÷ (P + R) = 2 × 0.667 × 0.667 ÷ (0.667 + 0.667) = 0.889 ÷ 1.333 = 0.667 ≈ 0.67.

Answer. Precision = 0.67, recall = 0.67 and F1 = 0.67; the wrong boundary on 'team in Pune' produces one false positive and one false negative at the same time.

Exam traps

  • Grading token by token: an all-O tagger gets about 90% token accuracy and F1 = 0. Reporting accuracy alone for NER is the classic mistake.
  • One boundary word wrong destroys a whole entity under exact-span matching, and it counts twice — one false positive and one false negative.
  • Writing I-PER on the first token of an entity is an illegal sequence; gold data never has I-X straight after O.
  • Partial overlap is not a match: 'Bank of India' against gold 'Reserve Bank of India' scores zero for that entity, not half.
  • Capitalisation features fail outside English and outside edited text — Hindi or Tamil has no case, and chat text is lowercase throughout.

Data-hungry teams generate weak labels from gazetteers and then clean them, or transfer a multilingual model. 2.9 Information extraction and relation extraction

A news sentence says Rao joined TCS in Pune, and a database needs a row: person Rao, employer TCS, city Pune. Information extraction does that reading. It pulls structured records out of free text, and relation extraction is the part that links two named entities into a triple of subject, relation and object. Banks use it to read KYC documents, job portals to parse resumes, and news monitors to build knowledge graphs that answer questions like who works where.

You need: 2.8 Named entity recognition, because a relation is defined between two entities that have already been found and typed.

From entities to triples

Information extraction (IE) takes free text and returns structured records with known fields. A program can then store the record in a table or a graph without reading the sentence again.

The IE family has three members. Entity extraction finds the spans naming people, places, organisations and dates; that is 2.8. Relation extraction takes two entity mentions and decides which relation, if any, holds between them. Template filling, also called slot filling, fills a fixed frame: a job-change record might have the slots PERSON, ORG, LOC and DATE.

The output of relation extraction is a triple (subject, relation, object). Subject and object are entity mentions with their types, and the relation comes from a small closed set such as works_for, based_in or joined_on. (Rao, works_for, TCS) means Rao is employed by TCS.

A set of triples is a knowledge base. Each triple has a head and a tail, so the knowledge base is a directed graph: nodes are entities, labelled edges are relations. Triples sharing an entity join up, so a query like "who works in Pune" becomes a two-hop walk.

Three ways to get relations

Hand-written patterns. Write a regular expression over words or over the POS tags of 2.4. For example, the pattern PER joined ORG in LOC requires all three slots but emits only (PER, works_for, ORG); LOC is kept as context and discarded. This is cheap and needs no training data, but it fires only on the phrasings its author thought of.

Bootstrapping, also called seed-based or semi-supervised learning. Start with a few seed triples. Find sentences where both entities of a seed occur, and take the words between them, or their POS pattern, as a candidate pattern. Keep patterns that match many seeds, apply them to new text to get new pairs, add those pairs to the seed set, and repeat. Precision falls with each round, so stop after a few rounds at a confidence threshold.

Supervised relation classification. Treat each candidate entity pair as one example. Features: the words between the two mentions, their POS tags, the entity types from 2.8, the distance in tokens, which entity comes first, and the head words on the path between them in a parse tree (see Unit 3, Syntax). Train a classifier over labels such as works_for, based_in, lives_in and NONE. NONE matters most, because nearly every entity pair in real text has no relation.

Distant supervision builds training data automatically: align a knowledge base with a corpus and label every sentence containing a known pair with that pair's relation, giving many noisy examples.

Precision, recall and stacked errors

Score on held-out text: sentences not used to write the patterns or train the classifier.

P = c / n,   R = c / t,   F1 = 2 × P × R / (P + R)

c is the number of triples produced that match a gold triple exactly; n is the total number of triples produced; t is the number of true relations present in the test text; P is precision, R is recall, F1 is their harmonic mean.

A triple is correct only when subject, relation and object all match the gold triple. Same subject and same relation but a different object is a wrong triple.

Relation extraction assumes the entities are already right. A wrong span, such as a person mention swallowing the verb, or a wrong type, such as TCS tagged as a person, hands the classifier a wrong argument, and the triple is lost even when the relation is recognised. If entity and relation errors are independent, end-to-end F1 is roughly their product: 0.90 × 0.80 = 0.72. Errors stack, they do not cancel.

This closes the subject's sequence: sequence labels (2.4 to 2.7), then entity spans (2.8), then relations between those spans.

Question. The sentence is "Rao joined TCS in Pune last Monday". (a) List the named entities with their types, and the triples the sentence yields. Exam traps

  • Measuring pattern precision on the sentences you used to write the pattern. Report it on held-out text only.
  • Counting a triple as correct when the subject and relation match but the object differs. All three parts must match the gold triple.
  • Confusing relation extraction with entity extraction: relation extraction receives the entities as input, so it cannot repair a wrong span or a wrong entity type.
  • Reporting recall as the number of patterns or seed pairs. Recall is correct triples divided by all true relations in the text, including those no pattern fires on.
  • Running bootstrapping for many rounds and quoting the first round's precision. Precision decays as drift enters the seed set.

A part-of-speech class that readily accepts new words, such as nouns and verbs.
Out-of-vocabulary token
A token in test data that is absent from the model vocabulary.
Part of speech
A grammatical category such as noun, verb, adjective or postposition.
Perplexity
A measure of how well a language model predicts a test text; lower is better.
Relation extraction
The task of identifying a relation between two entities and forming a triple.
Smoothing
A method that gives some probability to unseen events.
Tagset
The complete list of part-of-speech labels used in a tagging task.
Token
A word, punctuation mark or other small unit produced by tokenisation.
Tokenization
The process of splitting raw text into tokens.
Transition probability
The probability of moving from one hidden tag to the next.
Viterbi algorithm
A dynamic programming algorithm that finds the highest-probability hidden state path.
Vocabulary
The set of distinct tokens known to a model.
