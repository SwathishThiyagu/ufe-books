5.1 Principles of animation: squash and stretch, staging, timing

Animation is a sequence of still frames shown fast enough that the eye reads continuous motion. Film uses 24 frames per second (fps), PAL video 25 fps and NTSC video 30 fps; see Unit 4 for formats. A few old rules make that motion believable: a body changes shape but keeps its volume, a pose reads in one glance, and the spacing of frames controls weight and speed. These rules are used in cartoon films, UI motion, games and advertisements.

You need: Unit 1 on digital images, sampling and visual perception; chapter 5.2 shows how software fills the frames between keys.

Frames, motion and the twelve principles

An animation is a list of still images, called frames, played in order. If the frames arrive fast enough, the eye and brain join them into one moving scene. The threshold is roughly 10 to 12 frames per second (fps); below that the motion looks like a flicker of separate pictures. Film runs at 24 fps, PAL television at 25 fps and NTSC television at 30 fps. Unit 4 covers video formats and compression; here the frame is the unit of time. A 2-second action at 24 fps needs 48 frames. At 12 fps the same action needs 24 frames. Fewer frames means less drawing work but also less smooth motion.

The classic list has twelve principles. Some syllabi call them “Disney’s twelve rules” or “the principles of cartoon animation”. The twelve are squash and stretch, anticipation, staging, straight-ahead action and pose-to-pose, follow-through and overlapping action, slow-in and slow-out, arcs, secondary action, timing, exaggeration, solid drawing and appeal. This chapter develops three: squash and stretch, staging and timing. Anticipation, slow-in and slow-out and arcs appear as helpers because they change the spacing of frames, which is the heart of timing.

Squash and stretch: shape changes, volume stays

A moving body does not stay rigid. When a rubber ball hits the floor it flattens; when it falls fast it stretches along the direction of travel. Squash and stretch makes the material feel soft, heavy or springy. The rule is that the shape deforms, but the amount of material stays the same. In a 2D drawing, the area stays roughly constant. In a 3D model, the volume stays roughly constant.

For a simple 2D shape drawn as a rectangle, area A = w × h, where w is width and h is height. If the height changes from h₁ to h₂, the new width is w₂ = w₁h₁/h₂. A circle squashed into an ellipse keeps area when its horizontal semi-axis a and vertical semi-axis b satisfy a × b = r², where r is the original radius. For a 3D ball modelled as an ellipsoid, V = (4/3)πa²c, where a is the horizontal semi-axis, c is the vertical semi-axis and V is volume. If the original sphere has radius r, then a²c = r³. A squash that halves the height doubles the width in 2D; in 3D the width grows by √2 ≈ 1.41 if height halves. The exact factor depends on the axis you squeeze. Keep the shape rounded; a flat pancake with sharp corners loses the rubber feel.

a × b = r² and a²c = r³

a and b are the horizontal and vertical semi-axes of a 2D ellipse; c is the vertical semi-axis of a 3D ellipsoid; r is the original circle or sphere radius. The first equation keeps area, the second keeps volume.

Staging and timing: one clear pose, deliberate spacing

Staging means arranging the shot so the audience reads the action in one glance. Use a clear silhouette: if the character is filled in black, the pose should still tell you what is happening. Choose a camera angle that shows the action, not one that hides it. Keep the background quieter than the subject, by contrast, blur or empty space. Show one main action per shot; a second action competes for attention. In a cricket game animation, the batsman’s swing reads best from side-on with the field blurred behind him.

Timing is two decisions: how many frames an action takes, and how the in-between drawings are spaced inside those frames. Frame rate is fixed by the medium, such as 24 fps. Timing is chosen by the animator. Wide spacing means the object moves a large distance from one frame to the next; it reads fast and light. Tight spacing means small steps; it reads slow and heavy. Slow-in and slow-out put many drawings near the start and end of a move and few in the middle. Anticipation is a small opposite move before the main action: a bowler’s arm goes back before the ball leaves the hand. Arcs are curved paths, because few natural moves travel in a straight line. These three support timing: they change where the frames bunch and where they spread.

Worked example

Question. A rubber ball 1 unit wide hits the floor and squashes to 0.6 of its original height over 2 frames, then rounds back in 2 more. Find its width at maximum squash if it keeps volume, and say where the timing should be tight and where wide.

  1. Original ball is a sphere of diameter 1 unit, so radius r = 0.5 units. Volume V = (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)π(0.5)³ = 0.5236 cubic units.
  2. The squashed height is 0.6 × original height = 0.6 × 1 = 0.6 units. Treat the squash as an ellipsoid with vertical semi-axis c = 0.6/2 = 0.3 units. Let horizontal semi-axis be a; width is 2a.
  3. Keep volume: (4/3)πa²c = (4/3)πr³. Cancel (4/3)π: a²c = r³.
  4. Put r = 0.5 and c = 0.3: a²(0.3) = 0.125, so a² = 0.125/0.3 = 0.41667 square units.
  5. a = √0.41667 = 0.6455 units. Width = 2a = 1.2910 units, about 1.29 units.
  6. Timing: the squash and recovery each take 2 frames, so the shape changes a lot per frame. That is wide spacing: fast impact and rebound. Put tight spacing at the top of the bounce or in the settle, where the ball hangs or comes to rest; that reads slow and heavy. The squash itself should not be spread over many frames, or the ball looks soft and slow.

Answer. The ball widens to about 1.29 units at maximum squash; use wide spacing through the 2-frame impact and recovery, and tight spacing where the ball hangs or settles.

Exam traps

  • Scaling both axes by the same factor to make a squash. That shrinks the ball instead of keeping its volume. Squash one axis and widen the other.
  • Mixing timing with frame rate. Frame rate is frames per second, fixed by film, PAL or NTSC. Timing is how many frames and how they are spaced for one action.
  • Reversing spacing: wide spacing is fast and light, tight spacing is slow and heavy. Many students say the opposite in the exam.
  • Thinking squash and stretch applies only to balls. It applies to bodies, faces, cloth and even text motion.
  • Treating staging as just a nice background. Staging is pose, silhouette, camera angle and contrast working together so one action reads.

5.2 Keyframe animation and tweening

A keyframe is a value the animator fixes at one chosen frame. A raised arm at frame 0 and the same arm down at frame 12 are two keys, and every frame between them is computed. That computation is tweening, also called interpolation or in-betweening, and it works the same way for position, scale, rotation, opacity or colour. Instead of drawing every frame, the animator draws the poses that matter and states the timing; the software fills the gaps. Every 2D timeline tool, CSS transition and game-engine animation clip is built on this one idea.

You need Chapter 5.1 for why keys are spaced unevenly: the gaps between them carry the sense of weight and speed.

Keys fix values; tweens fill the frames

A keyframe is a frame at which a property's value is stored explicitly. An in-between is a frame whose value is computed from the keys on either side. A tween is the whole run of in-betweens joining two keys, plus the rule used to compute them. Animators say in-betweening; some syllabi say interpolation animation. All three words describe the same machinery.

Each property is a separate channel with its own keys. Position x, position y, scale, rotation angle, opacity and each colour component carry independent key lists, so an object can slide fast while it fades slowly. A channel is a function: give it a frame number f and it returns a value v. The animation is that function, sampled once per frame.

The simplest rule is linear interpolation. The normalised time u runs from 0 at the first key to 1 at the second, and the value moves in step with it.

u = (f − f₁)/(f₂ − f₁), v = v₁ + u(v₂ − v₁)

f is the frame being drawn; f₁ and f₂ are the key frames bounding it, with f₁ ≤ f < f₂; v₁ and v₂ are the property values at those keys, in the same unit (px, degrees, or 0 to 1 for opacity); u is normalised time; v is the value at frame f.

Hand-editing an in-between rarely sticks, because the next redraw recomputes it. To change the motion, move a key, add a key, or change the interpolation rule. Keys do not need to be dense; a two-key tween across 30 frames is normal.

Easing and spline curves

Linear motion starts and stops at full speed, which reads as mechanical. Easing reshapes u before it is used: the value is still the start value plus progress times the value gap, but progress is no longer u itself.

s(u) = u²  (ease-in);   s(u) = 1 − (1 − u)²  (ease-out);   s(u) = u²(3 − 2u)  (ease-in-out)

u is normalised time from 0 to 1 and s(u) is the eased progress, also 0 to 1. Ease-in starts slow and speeds up, ease-out does the reverse, ease-in-out does both. The value is v₁ + s(u)(v₂ − v₁). These three polynomials are the usual textbook choices; every tool ships a family of similar curves.

Easing changes speed, never the path. At the halfway time of an ease-in, s(0.5) = 0.25, so the object has covered only a quarter of the distance and looks like it is gathering momentum. That is slow-in slow-out from Chapter 5.1, written as arithmetic.

Spline interpolation goes one step further: each key also stores a tangent, the rate at which the value is changing as it passes. A cubic Bezier in (frame, value) space links two keys with continuous slope, so velocity has no kink at the key. The same Bezier maths as vector paths in Chapter 5.8 applies, with time on one axis instead of paper.

The graph editor is where this becomes visible: frames along the bottom, values up the side, keys as points, handles as tangent stalks. Flatten a handle and the object decelerates into that key; pull it steep and the object shoots through. Reading that curve tells you the motion without playing it back.

Shortest rotation, and duration counted in frames

Angles wrap at 360°, so plain subtraction lies. From 350° to 10° the raw difference is 10 − 350 = −340°, which spins the object most of a turn backwards. The short way is +20°. Wrap the difference into the range (−180°, 180°].

d = ((v₂ − v₁ + 180) mod 360) − 180,   v = v₁ + s(u)·d

v₁ and v₂ are the start and end angles in degrees; mod is the non-negative remainder; d is the signed shortest turn in degrees, between −180° and +180°; u is normalised time; s is the easing function. Add the interpolated turn to the start angle and wrap the result into 0°–360° for display.

Tools let the animator override the wrap: typing 710° instead of 350° as the end angle forces a full extra turn forward, which is what a spinning wheel or a coin flip needs. The number you store is the number that is interpolated.

Duration is a frame gap, not a key count. A ten-frame tween is ten frames at any frame rate, but the seconds change: 10 ÷ 24 = 0.417 s at 24 fps and 10 ÷ 30 = 0.333 s at 30 fps. Speed in px/s is Δvalue × fps ÷ Δframes. Exporting at a frame rate different from the timeline's silently changes how fast everything moves.

Two families get separate names. A motion tween interpolates properties of one object. A shape tween interpolates one outline into another and needs the two outlines matched point by point first — Chapter 5.3. Timeline panels, tween paths and motion blur belong to Chapter 5.9.

Worked example

Question. A logo slides across a banner. A keyframe at frame 0 holds x = 20 px; the next keyframe at frame 10 holds x = 120 px. The tween is linear and the project runs at 24 fps. Find x at frames 2, 4, 6 and 8, and the time at which x = 75 px.

  1. Frame gap: Δf = 10 − 0 = 10 frames. Value gap: Δx = 120 − 20 = 100 px.
  2. Linear step per frame = 100 px ÷ 10 frames = 10 px per frame, constant.
  3. Frame 2: x = 20 + 10 × 2 = 40 px. Frame 4: 60 px. Frame 6: 80 px. Frame 8: 100 px.
  4. For x = 75 px, normalised time u = (75 − 20) ÷ 100 = 0.55.
  5. Frame = 0 + 0.55 × 10 = 5.5. A renderer shows a whole frame, so it would output frame 5 (x = 70 px) or frame 6 (x = 80 px).
  6. Time at 24 fps: t = 5.5 ÷ 24 = 0.2292 s, about 229 ms after the first key.

Answer. x = 40, 60, 80 and 100 px at frames 2, 4, 6 and 8; x = 75 px at frame 5.5, which is 0.2292 s after the start at 24 fps.

Exam traps

  • Tweening rotation the long way round: 350° to 10° is +20°, not −340°.
  • Treating the number of keyframes as the duration. Duration is the frame gap between two keys, not how many keys you placed.
  • Editing an in-between by hand and expecting it to stay — it is recomputed on the next redraw.
  • Mixing frames and seconds. A 10-frame tween is 0.417 s at 24 fps and 0.333 s at 30 fps, so px/s depends on the frame rate.
  • Assuming easing bends the path. It only changes how fast the value travels along the same path.

5.3 Morphing between shapes and images

Morphing turns one shape or image into another: the outline of a face becomes another face, a letter A becomes a B, a car becomes a tiger. Two things happen together — the geometry moves and the colours cross over. A warp alone gives B's outline painted in A's colours. A fade alone gives A's outline with B's colour. Only both, driven by the same time value, read as one object changing into another. Film titles, advertisements, face-ageing apps and 3D character faces all use it. Some syllabi call it image metamorphosis or shape blending.

You need: digital images and pixels (Unit 1), linear interpolation as used for in-betweens in 5.2, Bezier curves for morphing outlines (Unit 3), and 2D transformations (Unit 2).

Two ingredients, joined by correspondence

Every morph runs two jobs at once. The geometric job changes the shape of A into the shape of B. The colour job changes A's colours into B's colours.

One number t drives both. t = 0 is the source, t = 1 is the target, and frames at t = 0.1, 0.2, … fill the gap.

The geometric job needs correspondence: a rule saying which part of A is which part of B. You place control points on the source — eye corners, the tip of the nose, the corners of a letter — and mark the matching points on the target. Or you lay a mesh of small triangles over the source and drag its nodes to the matching places on the target. The computer cannot guess that your ear should become the target's ear and not its chin; a person, or a face detector, supplies the pairs.

After that it is arithmetic. Each control point travels a straight line.

P(t) = (1 − t)·P_A + t·P_B

P_A is the point's position on the source, P_B its matched position on the target, t the time fraction from 0 to 1, and P(t) the intermediate position. This is the interpolation that fills in-betweens between keyframes (see 5.2), applied to one point instead of a whole drawing.

Moving the pixels: forward and inverse warping

Moving the control points does not move the pixels between them. Two ways to move those.

Forward warping. Visit each source pixel, compute where it lands at time t, write its colour there. When control points move apart, neighbouring source pixels land far from each other and leave unpainted holes. When points crowd together, several source pixels land on one output pixel and all but one are lost.

Inverse warping. Start from the output. For each output pixel, find the source position it came from, read the colour there, write it. The source-to-target map is inverted once, then every output pixel gets exactly one value, so there are no holes. The source position rarely falls on a whole pixel, so the colour is sampled from the nearest four pixels: nearest neighbour for speed, bilinear interpolation (a weighted average) for a smoother frame.

With a mesh, each triangle is warped by its own affine map — the map carrying its three source corners to their three interpolated positions (see Unit 2, 2D transformations). Affine maps keep straight lines straight, so the triangle stays a triangle and the mesh simply deforms. The warped pair is not blended yet; it is handed to the dissolve.

Cross-dissolve, and one t for both jobs

The cross-dissolve is a weighted average of the two warped images, pixel by pixel.

C(t) = (1 − t)·C_A + t·C_B

C_A is the colour of the warped source at that pixel, C_B the colour of the warped target at the same pixel, and t the same fraction used by the warp. Red, green and blue are blended separately.

The weights (1 − t) and t always add to 1, so a flat region keeps its brightness as it crosses over; it dims or brightens only if the sum is broken. Both jobs must use the same t. If the warp uses t = 0.3 while the dissolve uses 0.6, outline and shading drift apart — the silhouette sits in one place and its colour in another, and the eye catches it at once.

In 3D the same formula runs on vertices: V(t) = (1 − t)·V_A + t·V_B, where V_A and V_B are matched vertices. Vertex morphing needs identical topology — the same number of vertices with the same faces in the same order — because vertex 47 of A must move to vertex 47 of B. Normals and texture coordinates are interpolated by the same rule, and the interpolated normal is scaled back to unit length before it is used for lighting, so shading follows the deforming surface correctly.

What decides quality is correspondence, not the arithmetic. The arithmetic is one subtraction of t, then two multiplies and one add per coordinate — trivial next to the work of finding good correspondence.

Worked example

Question. Two images have matched control points. In the source image a control point sits at A(0, 0); in the target image the matched point sits at B(100, 40). Coordinates are in pixels, origin at the top-left. Find the intermediate position at t = 0.3, and give the weights the cross-dissolve uses at that same instant.

  1. t = 0.3, so the source weight is 1 − t = 0.7 and the target weight is t = 0.3.
  2. x(0.3) = (1 − 0.3)×0 + 0.3×100 = 0 + 30 = 30 px.
  3. y(0.3) = (1 − 0.3)×0 + 0.3×40 = 0 + 12 = 12 px.
  4. The interpolated control point is P(0.3) = (30, 12).
  5. The control point sits 30 px right and 12 px down from the source position, and 70 px left and 28 px up from the target position (about 75 px away in a straight line) — as expected, since t = 0.3 is nearer the source.
  6. Cross-dissolve weights: source 0.7, target 0.3. They add to 1.0, so brightness is preserved.
  7. A pixel colour there is C = 0.7·C_A + 0.3·C_B, per channel.

Answer. The control point is at (30, 12), and the cross-dissolve uses 0.7 of the warped source and 0.3 of the warped target.

Exam traps

  • Calling a plain cross-dissolve a morph. A fade moves no geometry; marks are awarded for naming the warp as well.
  • Mismatched or too few control points. The mesh folds or tears, and the examiner's favourite phrase is 'correspondence is the hard part'.
  • Confusing the two weights. The source uses (1 − t) and the target uses t. Writing t and t makes the total weight 2t, so the frames are dark near t = 0 and t = 1 and correct only at t = 0.5, and the colour crosses over on a wrong, non-linear schedule; writing t and (1 − t) reverses the direction.
  • Using one t for the warp and another for the dissolve, so outline and colour drift apart.
  • Forward warping leaves holes when control points spread apart; inverse warping with bilinear sampling is the standard fix, not a bigger mesh alone.

5.4 2D and 3D animation techniques and tools

A 2D cartoon and a 3D game character look different because they are made by different techniques, not because of different software. 2D animation draws flat pictures or slides separate parts around; 3D animation builds a model, gives it a skeleton, and moves the skeleton. The technique fixes what the tool must do: a sprite editor, a rig editor or a particle engine. This chapter covers the four 2D techniques, the six-stage 3D pipeline, the two ways to pose a skeleton, and the four ways to drive motion.

You need Unit 2 for composite transforms (rotating a limb about its joint) and Unit 3 for polygon meshes, lighting and rendering.

2D techniques: drawn frames and moved parts

Frame-by-frame animation, also called cel animation, draws every frame by hand. A cel is a transparent sheet: the moving character is painted on the cel and the background is painted once underneath, so only the character is redrawn. At 24 frames per second (fps), one second of film needs 24 drawings. That is why studios draw only key poses and let the software or an assistant fill the rest (see 5.2).

Rotoscoping traces live footage. The animator projects the film frame by frame and draws over each frame. Motion comes out human because it was filmed, but the cost is still one drawing per frame.

Sprite animation stores the moving object once as a small bitmap and stamps it at a new position on a fixed background each frame. Memory holds one sprite plus one background, not 240 full frames, which is why sprites suit games. Moving the sprite is a pure translation (Unit 2).

Cut-out animation, which some syllabi call puppet animation, builds the character from separate parts: head, upper arm, forearm. Each part sits on its own layer with its own transform. Moving a part means changing its rotation or position; nothing is redrawn. Hinging two parts at a shared point is the 2D version of a skeleton.

The 3D pipeline, in order

A 3D shot cannot start until the model exists. The stages run in a fixed order, and each stage hands a file to the next.

  1. Modelling: build the polygon mesh of vertices, edges and faces (Unit 3).
  2. Texturing: wrap images and material values on the surface to set colour, roughness and pattern.
  3. Rigging: build a skeleton of bones inside the mesh and bind each vertex to a bone with a weight.
  4. Animation: pose the rig at chosen frames and set the timing.
  5. Lighting: place lights and cameras for the shot (Unit 3).
  6. Rendering: compute the final 2D image for every frame. At 24 fps that is 24 renders for each second of film.

Rigging is the stage 2D does not have. A bone is a segment with a pivot. A vertex weighted 1.0 to a bone follows it exactly; a vertex weighted 0.5 between two bones moves halfway. Those weights are why a shoulder bends smoothly instead of tearing open.

Tool families follow the stages, not the brand: polygon modellers and sculpting tools for modelling; image and procedural texture tools for texturing; rig editors for skeletons; animation timelines with curve editors; renderers; and compositors that combine renders with live plates.

Forward and inverse kinematics

A rig is a chain: shoulder to elbow to wrist. Each joint carries an angle, and kinematics is the arithmetic that turns those angles into a hand position.

x = L₁ cos θ₁ + L₂ cos(θ₁ + θ₂),   y = L₁ sin θ₁ + L₂ sin(θ₁ + θ₂)

x, y are the hand position in metres measured from the shoulder; L₁ and L₂ are the upper-arm and forearm lengths in metres; θ₁ is the shoulder angle from the horizontal in radians; θ₂ is the elbow angle relative to the upper arm in radians.

Forward kinematics (FK) walks outward from the root: set θ₁ and θ₂, then compute the hand. The arithmetic is direct and gives exactly one answer, but landing the hand on a cup means guessing angles by trial.

Inverse kinematics (IK) walks inward from the hand: the animator drags the wrist to a target and the solver finds θ₁ and θ₂ backwards. The answer is not unique, because the elbow can point up or down, so IK needs constraints such as a joint limit or a pole vector. Use FK for waving, tails and free arcs; use IK for a foot planted on the ground or a hand gripping a rail.

Four ways to drive the motion

  • Keyframe: the animator sets poses at chosen frames and the software interpolates every property between them (see 5.2).
  • Procedural: a rule or formula sets the value on every frame. A bobbing float is h = A sin(2πft); a flag flap is a wave travelling along the cloth. No poses are stored, only the formula and its parameters. Some syllabi call this expression-driven animation.
  • Motion capture: a performer wears reflective markers or a sensor suit, cameras record marker positions in 3D many times per second, and software maps that data onto the rig. It captures subtle human motion fast but needs cleanup and a matching skeleton.
  • Particle systems: fire, smoke, rain and sparks. Each particle is a point with position, velocity, lifetime and size. Every frame the system applies forces such as gravity and wind, updates velocity and position, spawns new particles and kills old ones, then draws each as a sprite or a small mesh. The animator sets the rules, not the frames.

A 3D animation that never touches motion capture is still 3D animation. The rig with keyframes is the normal case; mocap is one input method among four.

Worked example

Question. A 2D cel animation runs for 10 s at 24 fps. The animator draws one key pose every 6 frames. Find the total number of frames, the number of key poses and the number of in-between frames.

  1. Frames: N = fps × time = 24 frames/s × 10 s = 240 frames.
  2. Key poses: one pose every 6 frames, so K = 240 ÷ 6 = 40 key poses.
  3. In-betweens: the frames that are not key poses, B = N − K = 240 − 40 = 200 in-between frames.
  4. Check: 40 key poses + 200 in-betweens = 240 frames, which matches the total.

Answer. The clip has 240 frames, 40 key poses and 200 in-between frames.

Worked example 2

Question. A flag is animated procedurally with height h = A sin(2πft). Take A = 0.4 m and f = 0.5 Hz. Find h at t = 0.5 s and state how many frames that is at 25 fps.

  1. Angle: 2πft = 2 × π × 0.5 × 0.5 = π/2 rad.
  2. sin(π/2) = 1, so h = 0.4 m × 1 = 0.4 m.
  3. Frames: 0.5 s × 25 frames/s = 12.5 frames, that is frame 12 or 13 depending on rounding.

Answer. At t = 0.5 s the flag height is 0.4 m, which falls at frame 12 or 13 of a 25 fps clip.

Exam traps

  • Swapping forward and inverse kinematics. 'Rotate the shoulder and see where the hand lands' is FK; 'put the hand here and let the software bend the elbow' is IK.
  • Assuming every 3D animation needs motion capture. A hand-keyed rig on a timeline is ordinary 3D animation; mocap is only one input method.
  • Counting in-betweens wrongly. The key poses are part of the total, so B = N − K; do not add one, and do not count in-betweens as the total.
  • Calling cut-out animation 3D because the parts sit on separate layers. If every transform applied is 2D, the output is 2D.
  • Jumbling the pipeline order. Rigging comes after the model and before animation, and rendering is always last and always done per frame.

5.5 Authoring metaphors and tools: card, icon, time based

An authoring metaphor is the tool's mental model for a title. It decides whether you build a stack of cards, a flow of icons or a timeline of layers. The choice changes how you store media, how you add interactivity and how hard a late change becomes. Card tools link screens, icon tools link objects in a flowchart, and time tools place objects on a clock. The same training title can be rebuilt in all three. Use the metaphor that matches the job: presentation, simulation or fixed playback.

You need: the media elements from Unit 4, and chapter 5.2 for the idea that software fills frames between keys.

Card and page tools: a stack of screens

An authoring metaphor is the mental model the tool gives the author. It decides where the title lives: in a stack of cards, a flow of icons or a timeline of layers. Card-based tools, also called page-based tools, treat the title as a deck of full screens. One card fills the display at a time. The author places text, images, audio and buttons on each card. A button or hot spot carries a link to another card. A menu card links to lesson cards; a quiz card links back to a lesson or forward to a score card. This is hypermedia authoring: nodes (cards) joined by links. The reader moves by clicking, not by waiting. Structure is easy to see: one box per screen. Change one card and every link to it sees the new version. Interactivity is a link: go to card 7, or if answer = B go to card 12. A card stack can branch, loop and skip. The cost is that every screen is separate; a small change to a shared banner must be repeated unless the tool has a master page.

Icon and object tools: a flowchart of objects

Some syllabi call these flowchart tools. Icon-based tools show the title as a diagram. Each icon is an object: a display, a sound, a video, a decision, a calculation. The author drags icons onto a flowline and connects them with arrows. Double-clicking an icon opens its properties: file, position, duration, trigger. Media files live in a warehouse or cast, a central store. The flowline is the program: start → show video → ask question → if correct go to praise, else go to hint. This flowchart authoring suits simulations because a decision icon can read learner input and choose the next path. A simulation is not a fixed sequence; it reacts. The icon graph also makes reuse easy: one icon can point to a shared media object, so replacing that object updates every use. The cost is that a large graph becomes a tangle; you need naming and grouping. Interactivity is expressed as control flow between icons, not as a button on a page.

Time-based tools: layers on a clock

Time-based tools organise the title on a timeline. The screen is split into layers, like transparent sheets. Each layer holds objects: background, text, video, sound. The playback head moves left to right in seconds. A frame is one instant; a keyframe marks a change. The author sets where an object starts, how long it stays and what it does. This is close to video editing. The tool knows clock time: at 12.0 s the video starts, at 18.5 s the caption appears. A cast holds reusable objects. Change the cast member and all its instances update. Interactivity is a button or a pause point that jumps to another frame, but the main structure is linear. A time-based rebuild of a branching quiz loses the branches unless the tool adds separate timelines or scenes.

T = N × d

T is total play time in seconds; N is the number of screens or cards; d is the display time per screen in seconds.

For 19 screens at 30 s each, T = 570 s = 9.5 min.

Choosing, comparing and publishing

Choose by the job. A card tool suits a catalogue, a presentation or a course with clear screens and many links. An icon tool suits a simulation, a game-like drill or a process with decisions. A time tool suits a demo, a title sequence or a narrated lesson that plays in order. Cross-platform authoring publishes one project to desktop, web and mobile. The project keeps one structure; the tool adapts window size, input and file packaging. You do not redraw the title three times. Use card tools for interactive presentations, icon tools for virtual learning modules and simulations, and time tools for fixed demonstrations. Some syllabi call page-based tools hypermedia authoring and icon-based tools flowchart authoring. A simulation is the sharp test: it must react to learner input, so the author needs a graph or a script, not a fixed strip of frames.

5.6 Painting, drawing and image editing tools

Every editing tool answers one question first: does it store a grid of pixels, or a list of objects? Paint tools change pixels, so the picture is frozen at the resolution it was made. Drawing tools store shapes you can re-edit, so they stay sharp at any zoom. Image editing tools act on the whole picture — crop, resample, colour shift. That one storage choice fixes resolution, editing power and file size, and it separates the tool families in an authoring package: painting, drawing, image editing, 3D modelling, animation, audio and movie tools. Only a few universities teach this chapter separately.

You need: Unit 1 on raster versus vector graphics and colour models, and Unit 4 on image file formats.

Paint tools write into the pixel grid

A paint program keeps a canvas, which is a grid of pixels. A brush of diameter d pixels stamps colour into every pixel its path touches, so a soft edge is really a row of partly transparent pixels. An airbrush sprays with a density setting: repeated passes build up opacity. An eraser writes the background colour, or alpha 0 on a transparent canvas. Bucket fill floods the connected region of pixels whose colour lies within a tolerance of the clicked pixel; a small tolerance leaves a halo at anti-aliased edges.

Any shape drawn in a paint program — rectangle, ellipse, text — is rendered into the bitmap the moment you release the mouse. Its corner radius cannot be changed afterwards. The file is a bitmap, so its uncompressed size depends only on width, height and bit depth, and its resolution is fixed when the canvas is created.

Drawing tools store objects

A drawing program stores each object as numbers: the endpoints of a line, the centre and radii of an ellipse, the anchors of a curve, plus a stroke colour, a stroke width and a fill colour. Nothing becomes pixels until the picture is displayed or exported. Move a node, change a fill, scale the drawing up to a banner — the software re-renders from the same numbers and the edges stay clean. A 20 mm circle on a poster and the same circle on a phone screen are one object with two renderings. Paths and the pen tool are covered in 5.8.

Image editing: crop, resample, resize

A pixel-based image editor works on a photograph or a finished bitmap. You select a region — rectangle, lasso, or magic wand by colour similarity — then apply one operation.

Crop keeps the selected rectangle and discards the rest. The pixel count falls, the dots per inch (dpi) value stays, so the printed size shrinks.

Resample changes the number of pixels. Downsampling throws pixels away. Upsampling must invent them by interpolation: nearest neighbour copies the closest pixel and looks blocky, bilinear averages four neighbours and looks soft, bicubic fits a smooth surface and keeps edges crisper. Resize keeps the pixel count and changes only the print size, which changes the dpi.

S = W × H × b ÷ 8

S is the uncompressed size in bytes; W and H are the pixel width and height; b is the bit depth, equal to 24 for 8-bit RGB and 8 for greyscale or an indexed palette.

Bit depth sets how many values a channel can take: 8 bits give 256 levels, so 24-bit RGB gives 16,777,216 colours, and an 8-bit indexed image holds one of 256 palette entries per pixel. Brightness adds a constant to every channel. Contrast scales values away from mid-grey. A hue shift rotates the hue angle θ in HSV, which is how a red shirt turns blue without touching saturation.

Exam traps

  • Confusing resample with resize. Lowering dpi alone keeps every pixel, so the file size does not change; resampling throws pixels away and does change it.
  • Writing dpi as a property of the file's quality. A 1200 × 1200 px image is the same data at 72 dpi and at 300 dpi; only the print size differs.
  • Mixing up bit depth and colour mode. 24-bit RGB and 8-bit indexed both hold one byte per channel or per palette index, but the indexed image has only 256 colours.
  • Assuming crop reduces resolution. Crop reduces pixel count and printed size while the dpi value stays as it was.
  • Using a tight bucket-fill tolerance on a jagged edge and reporting the leftover fringe as a bug in the fill tool; it is the tolerance working as set.

5.7 Photo retouching with layers, adjustments and filters

Photo retouching keeps every edit separate from the original pixels. The photo sits on the bottom layer. Retouch strokes go above it, colour changes go in adjustment layers, and masks decide where effects appear. Filters blur, sharpen or add noise by changing small neighbourhoods. Because the correction is stored as a recipe, you can reopen it next week. Studios and news desks use this workflow to clean images safely.

You need: Chapter 5.6 for pixel tools, and the RGB colour model from Unit 1.

Layer stack, alpha and masks

An image opens as a stack of layers. The bottom layer holds the original photo. Each layer stores a colour C and an alpha α at every pixel; α runs from 0 (transparent) to 1 (opaque). The software draws bottom first, so a layer covers the result below by its opacity.

C = clamp(round(α·C_top + (1 − α)·C_below), 0, 255)

C_top and C_below are upper and lower channel values; α is effective alpha after layer opacity and mask; round gives an integer and clamp keeps 0–255.

A mask stores grey m: white (m = 1) shows, black (m = 0) hides, grey blends. Effective alpha is α_eff = α_layer × m. You retouch through the mask; painting black hides the retouch at that spot; paint white over a blemish to show the correction.

Blend modes replace normal mixing: Normal C = C_top; Multiply C = C_top·C_below/255 (darkens); Screen C = 255 − (255−C_top)(255−C_below)/255 (lightens); Overlay C = 2·C_top·C_below/255 when C_below < 128, else 255 − 2(255−C_top)(255−C_below)/255 (adds contrast).

Adjustment layers and filters

An adjustment layer stores a correction, not a result. Levels, curves and hue/saturation are common. The adjustment affects every layer below it unless you clip it. Clipping makes it affect only the layer directly beneath, which is the local fix.

Filters are neighbourhood operations. Blur replaces a pixel by a weighted average of nearby pixels. Sharpen increases local contrast. Noise adds random variation. Unsharp mask forms original minus a blurred copy, then adds a scaled version of that difference back to the original to strengthen edges. Keep filters on a separate layer so the amount can be changed later.

Order matters. Put the retouch layer above the background, then a curves adjustment above it to correct both. If the curves must not touch the retouch, move the curves adjustment below the retouch layer (directly above the background) or clip it to the background layer. Only a few universities teach this chapter, so focus on the model, not menus.

  1. Layer opacity α_layer = 50/100 = 0.5; mask m = 40/100 = 0.4.

  5. Blue: 0.2 × 255 + 0.8 × 50 = 51 + 40 = 91.

5.8 Vector drawing with paths and the pen tool

A vector path is a list of anchor points joined by Bezier segments. The pen tool places an anchor; dragging before release pulls out handles. Those handles set the tangent, so the next segment leaves and arrives at the angle you want. Designers use paths for logos, icons, fonts and maps because the artwork is stored as geometry, not pixels. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need Unit 1 on raster versus vector graphics and Unit 3 on Bezier curves.

Anchors, handles, segments

A path has three parts. An anchor point is a location on the path. A handle (control point) pulls the curve and is not on it. A segment is the curve between two anchors. Some syllabi call anchors nodes and handles direction lines. At a smooth point the two handles lie on one straight line, so the curve passes through without a kink. At a corner point they do not, so the path turns sharply. Dragging a handle after placing an anchor sets the tangent; clicking with no drag gives a straight corner. An open path has two ends; a closed path joins its last anchor to its first. Fill paints the inside; stroke paints the outline. Union, subtract and intersect combine two closed paths into a new path. Converting a path to a selection makes a marching-ants region; the path stays a separate object. The pen tool is the usual way to place these parts.

The cubic Bezier formula

Each curved segment is a cubic Bezier with four points: anchors P₀ and P₃, handles P₁ and P₂. For 0 ≤ t ≤ 1,

B(t) = (1 − t)³P₀ + 3t(1 − t)²P₁ + 3t²(1 − t)P₂ + t³P₃

B(t) is the point at parameter t. P₀ and P₃ are the start and end anchors. P₁ is the handle leaving P₀; P₂ is the handle arriving at P₃. Each term is a weight times a control point, and the four weights sum to 1.

To evaluate by de Casteljau, split P₀P₁, P₁P₂ and P₂P₃ at ratio t, join the three new points, split those two lines again, then split the last line once more; that point is B(t). Direct substitution gives the same answer. Differentiate: B′(0) = 3(P₁ − P₀). The tangent at t = 0 points along P₁ − P₀. Vector art scales because the file stores anchors, handles and fill rules as numbers. At any zoom the renderer recomputes the curve and paints fresh pixels. Nothing is enlarged, so no pixel blocks appear. A raster logo stretched to billboard size shows squares; the same logo as a path stays sharp. This is why fonts, icons and maps ship as paths. The trade-off is more computation at render time, not more storage.

Worked example

Question. A cubic Bezier has P₀(0, 0), P₁(0, 50), P₂(50, 50), P₃(50, 0), in millimetres. Find B(0.5) and the tangent direction at t = 0.

  1. At t = 0.5, the weights are (1 − t)³ = 0.125, 3t(1 − t)² = 0.375, 3t²(1 − t) = 0.375 and t³ = 0.125.
  2. x = 0.125×0 + 0.375×0 + 0.375×50 + 0.125×50 = 25 mm.
  3. y = 0.125×0 + 0.375×50 + 0.375×50 + 0.125×0 = 37.5 mm.
  4. B′(0) = 3(P₁ − P₀) = 3(0, 50) = (0, 150). The direction is +y, so it is vertical upward.

Answer. B(0.5) = (25, 37.5) mm; the tangent at the start has direction (0, 150), vertically upward.

Exam traps

  • A handle pulls the curve, but the curve does not pass through it; only anchors lie on the path.
  • A path is not a selection. Converting a path to a selection creates a pixel-edge mask and leaves the path separate.
  • Smooth anchors have collinear handles; corner anchors do not.
  • The start tangent is 3(P₁ − P₀), not 3(P₀ − P₁); reversing the subtraction flips the arrow.

5.9 Timeline animation with motion tweens and motion blur

Timeline animation puts objects on layers and moves a playhead through frames. You set a few keyframes; a motion tween computes frames between them by changing a symbol instance's position, scale, rotation, colour, alpha or filters. Motion blur smears a fast object over the shutter time. Only a few universities teach this advanced topic. It appears in cartoon shorts, app intros and game menus.

You need: keyframes and interpolation, 2D/3D techniques, and frame rates from Unit 4.

Timeline, layers and two kinds of tween

The timeline is a grid of frames and layers. Columns are frames numbered from 1. Rows are layers, so independent objects animate without touching each other. A keyframe stores a property value. A blank keyframe is empty, so nothing from the previous frame carries over; you draw or place the new object in it, and the next keyframe stores that object's property values. The playhead marks the frame on screen. Frame rate is frames per second (fps): 24 frames at 24 fps last one second. Use a motion tween for movement and a shape tween for outline morphing. A motion tween needs a symbol instance. A symbol is a reusable object in the library; an instance is one placement of it on the stage. You set a start keyframe and an end keyframe, and the software computes the frames between. It can tween x and y position, scale, rotation, alpha (opacity), colour tint and filters such as blur. A shape tween instead morphs raw drawing shapes; it does not move a symbol instance.

The tween path, easing and nesting

The tween path is the route the symbol centre follows. For a straight path, x(t) = x₀ + (x₁ − x₀)t and y(t) = y₀ + (y₁ − y₀)t, where t runs from 0 at the first keyframe to 1 at the last; x₀, y₀ are the start point and x₁, y₁ the end point. Curved paths use Bezier handles. Easing changes t before interpolation. Ease-in starts slow; ease-out ends slow. The easing curve plots progress against time, so it changes speed along the path. Swapping the tween target replaces the symbol instance in the keyframes with another library instance. The path and timing remain; only the drawing changes. A nested animation puts a symbol's own inner timeline inside the outer tween: the outer moves the instance while the inner runs a loop, such as a blinking eye. You can tween 3D position along z; perspective changes apparent size with distance, so check the camera convention in your tool (see Unit 2).

Motion blur and frame-rate testing

Motion blur fakes speed by smearing the object over the time the shutter is open. Shutter angle θ is the open part of 360°; shutter time = (θ ÷ 360°) ÷ fps. A 180° shutter at 24 fps is open for 0.5 ÷ 24 = 1/48 s. Blur length ≈ speed × shutter time: speed is in pixels per second, shutter time in seconds, so blur length is in pixels. A fast object or a long shutter gives a longer smear. A 360° shutter would blur over the whole frame interval. Motion blur is a render or filter effect; it does not change the tween path. Test the movie at the target frame rate on the phone or projector. A tween that looks smooth at 30 fps may stutter at 24 fps if too much is redrawn. Use the same fps for authoring and playback. Buttons, stop actions, ActionScript and publishing are named only; they are outside this unit's scope.

Worked example

Question. A symbol tweens from x = 100 px to x = 400 px over 24 frames at 24 fps. Find average speed and motion blur length with a 1/48 s shutter.

  1. Duration = 24 frames ÷ 24 fps = 1 s.
  2. Distance = 400 − 100 = 300 px.
  3. Average speed = 300 px ÷ 1 s = 300 px/s.
  4. Shutter time = 1/48 s.
  5. Blur length ≈ 300 × 1/48 = 6.25 px.

Answer. Duration 1 s, average speed 300 px/s, blur length 6.25 px.

Exam traps

  • Putting a motion tween on a raw shape: it needs a symbol instance; shape tween is for raw shapes.
  • Reporting tween length in frames when asked for seconds: divide frames by fps.
  • Confusing shutter angle with shutter time: shutter time = (θ ÷ 360°) ÷ fps.
  • Using distance as blur length: blur length = speed × shutter time.

Analyze Linear versus ease-in tween: compare blur across the tween. 5 marks

Average speed and total duration are the same for both. In an ease-in the object moves slowly at the start and quickly near the end, so blur is short in the early frames and longest near the last frames. A linear tween gives the same blur on every frame, blur = average speed × shutter time. At the exact mid-time the ease-in value may be below, equal to or just above the linear value depending on the easing curve, so do not claim 'shorter' without fixing the curve.

Takeaways

  1. Squash and stretch, staging and timing are three separate checks, and a motion usually looks wrong because one of them is missing.
  2. A keyframe stores a value; a tween is the rule that fills the frames in between, so animators set poses and the software computes motion.
  3. Motion tweens change properties of one object, shape tweens change the outline itself, and the two fail in different ways.
  4. Morphing needs correspondence first, then warping, then a colour cross-dissolve — skipping correspondence gives a melting, soupy result.
  5. Forward warping leaves holes in the output, so practical morphing loops over output pixels and uses inverse warping.
  6. 2D and 3D animation are not competitors but toolkits: cels, sprites, cut-outs and rotoscoping for one look, model-rig-animate-render for another.
  7. The authoring metaphor (card, icon, time-based) decides how a title is organised before any content is drawn, and changing it later is expensive.
  8. Raster tools store pixels and vector tools store objects; that single choice controls resolution, file size and what kind of editing is possible.
  9. Retouching stays reversible when every edit gets its own layer and colour changes live in adjustment layers.
  10. A Bezier path is a short list of anchors plus handles, which is why a vector curve scales to any size without breaking.
  11. On a timeline, easing and motion blur do most of the work of making a tween look like real motion rather than a slide.

Glossary

Animation
Producing the illusion of motion by showing a sequence of slightly different still images fast enough for the eye to blend them.
Anti-aliasing
Softening the edges of drawn shapes by blending boundary pixels with the background, reducing jagged stair-steps.
Authoring tool
Software used to assemble text, graphics, sound, video and interactivity into a finished interactive title.
Bezier curve
A curve defined by two end anchors and handles that set the tangent direction and strength at each anchor.
Cel animation
Drawing or painting characters on transparent sheets laid over a painted background, so the background is not redrawn each frame.
Cross-dissolve
Blending two images by raising the opacity of one while lowering the other, used at the end of a morph.
Cut-out animation
Building a character from separate pieces and moving the pieces to animate, instead of drawing new frames.
Easing
Making the interpolation speed up or slow down instead of running at constant rate, so motion starts and stops naturally.
Forward kinematics
Setting each joint angle in a chain by hand so the end of the chain lands where the animator wants.
Frame rate
The number of frames shown per second; film commonly 24, television 25 or 30, measured in frames per second (fps).
Icon-based authoring
An authoring model where the title is a flow chart of icons, each carrying an event and a resulting action.
Interpolation
Computing intermediate values between two known values, which is how tweening fills in-between frames.
Keyframe
A frame where the animator fixes the value of a property by hand.
Layer mask
A grayscale channel attached to a layer that hides or reveals parts of it without deleting pixels.
Mesh warp
Warping controlled by a grid of control points laid over the image, each point dragged to a new position.
Morphing
Smoothly turning one image or shape into another by warping geometry and blending colour over time.
Onion skinning
Showing faint copies of neighbouring frames under the current one so the animator can judge the spacing of a motion.
Persistence of vision
The tendency of the eye and brain to hold an image briefly, which lets separate frames appear as continuous motion.
Procedural animation
Motion produced by rules or formulas rather than keyframes, such as physics-driven or noise-driven movement.
Rendering
Turning the scene or timeline into final output pixels, with lighting, shading and effects applied.
Rigging
Building the skeleton and controls that let an animator pose a 3D model.
Shutter interval
The fraction of a frame time during which the virtual camera is open, which sets how long a motion-blur smear becomes.
Time-based authoring
An authoring model where content sits on layers along a timeline, giving frame-level control of timing.
Tween
A generated set of frames between two keyframes, produced by interpolating one or more properties.
Warping
Moving pixel positions according to a mapping, so a shape deforms without being redrawn.
