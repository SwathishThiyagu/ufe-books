4.1 Multimedia: definition, elements and applications

Multimedia is the use of a computer to present several kinds of media together — words, drawings, sound, moving pictures, animation — as one coordinated presentation. The computer controls what appears and when, and the user can often act on what is shown. That mix of many media, computer control and user action is what separates multimedia from a plain video clip. You meet it in railway enquiry kiosks, phone learning apps, hospital scan viewers and web advertisements. This chapter defines multimedia, lists its elements, and surveys where it is used.

Definition: many media under computer control

Multimedia means presenting information through several media at the same time, with a computer controlling what appears and when. A medium is a channel of information: words, a picture, a sound, a moving picture. Two media become multimedia only when a computer holds them together as one unit.

Three conditions must hold. First, at least two media types appear in the same presentation. Second, a computer, not a person changing slides, controls the order and the timing. Third, the media are integrated: the audio and the caption belong to the same scene, not to two unrelated files opened side by side.

When the presentation runs from start to end and the viewer only watches, it is linear multimedia. A cinema advertisement, or a health-awareness video looping on a hospital screen, is linear. When the viewer can decide what happens next — touch a button, click a train name, jump to a chapter — it is interactive multimedia. Interactivity is the feature examiners look for when the phrase is used.

Some syllabi call the elements 'media types'. Some also use hypermedia for interactive multimedia whose pieces are joined by links, as on a web page. The links are the extra idea; they are not a new media type.

Seven elements are usually listed. Each has its own digital unit, which Chapter 4.2 explains in full.

Text is the cheapest element: a page of characters costs a few kilobytes. Graphics are stored as commands — draw this line, draw this curve — so a map stays sharp at any zoom. Images are stored as pixels and lose sharpness when enlarged. Unit 1, Graphics Systems and Output Primitives, covers this raster-versus-vector split.

Audio and video are timed media. Each carries a rate: audio samples per second, video frames per second. Animation is motion generated by the computer from key frames rather than captured by a camera. Interactivity is not a file at all; it is the program's response to a tap, a click, a key.

Where multimedia is used

  • Education: a chemistry reaction shown as animation with a voice-over lets a student see the motion and hear the explanation at the same time, which a still page cannot do.
  • Entertainment: films with computer-generated effects, games, and streaming services that carry video, audio and subtitles together.
  • Advertising: a shop's web banner that plays a short clip and offers a 'Buy' button, so the message and the action sit in one place.
  • Medicine: a radiologist scrolls through CT slices as images, marks a region, and records a spoken note; telemedicine sends all three to a distant doctor.
  • Training: a flight simulator, or a repair module that shows a machine part in 3D and lets a technician rotate it.
  • Public information: a Chennai railway enquiry kiosk, a museum touch guide, or a bank branch screen that walks a customer through a form.

The common thread is control. The user picks a path instead of watching a fixed film. The price is size and bandwidth: a minute of video is thousands of times larger than a minute of text, which is why compression and codec hardware matter so much, and why the pieces must stay in step, the subject of Chapter 4.9. Full system design and delivery belong to Unit 6, Multimedia Systems and Applications.

Worked example

Question. A Chennai railway enquiry kiosk shows a train name in text, a route map as graphics, a 20 s audio announcement, a 15 s video advertisement, a 2 s blinking icon as animation, and a touch button for the next page. (a) List the media elements present. (b) State why the kiosk is multimedia. (c) Find the total duration of the timed media in one play of the loop.

  1. Read the question and list every item named: train name, route map, 20 s announcement, 15 s advertisement, 2 s blinking icon, touch button.
  2. Train name in characters is text.
  3. Route map drawn as lines and curves is graphics.
  4. The 20 s announcement is audio; its duration is 20 s.
  5. The 15 s advertisement is video; its duration is 15 s.
  6. The 2 s blinking icon, generated by the program, is animation; its duration is 2 s.
  7. The touch button is interactivity. It is a control, not an information channel, so it has no duration.
  8. Check computer control and integration: all items sit on one screen and are timed by the kiosk software, so the conditions hold. The user acts on the screen, so this is interactive multimedia.
  9. Add the timed media: 20 s + 15 s + 2 s = 37 s.

Answer. The kiosk uses text, graphics, audio, video, animation and interactivity; the media are integrated under computer control and the user can act on them, so it is interactive multimedia, and one play of its timed media lasts 20 + 15 + 2 = 37 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A 3-minute narration, a 45 s animation and one still diagram appear in an e-learning module. Which items are timed media, and what is the total timed duration in seconds?

  1. Convert the narration to seconds: 3 min × 60 s/min = 180 s.
  2. The narration is audio, a timed medium: 180 s.
  3. The animation is a timed medium: 45 s.
  4. The still diagram is an image; it has no duration of its own, so it is not timed media.
  5. Total timed duration = 180 s + 45 s = 225 s.

Answer. The narration and the animation are the timed media; together they last 180 + 45 = 225 s, that is 3 min 45 s.

Exam traps

  • A video clip alone is not multimedia. The examiner wants at least two media types plus computer control; interactivity is needed only when the phrase 'interactive multimedia' is used.
  • Do not list the keyboard, mouse or touch screen as media. They are input devices that support interactivity, not channels of information.
  • Text counts as a medium even though it feels ordinary. A slide with text and one photograph already carries two media.
  • Linear multimedia is still multimedia. Missing interactivity does not break the definition unless the question asks specifically for interactive multimedia.
  • Hypermedia is not a seventh element sitting beside audio and video. It is linked interactive multimedia, so do not add it to the element list.

4.2 Media types: text, graphics, images, audio, video

Every multimedia file is built from a small set of digital units. A character is a code number, a drawing is a list of commands, an image is a grid of pixels, a sound is a sequence of samples, and a video is a sequence of frames. Once you know the unit and its size, you can estimate storage and transmission needs before writing any code. This chapter defines those units and separates media that have a time axis from media that do not.

You need: Unit 1 digital images and sampling, and Chapter 4.1 for multimedia elements.

Two families of media

Media are grouped by whether they change with time. Text, graphics and images are non-temporal (discrete) media: a page, logo or photograph can be shown and held. Audio, video and animation are temporal (continuous) media: their meaning depends on playback over time. Some syllabi call these 'discrete' and 'continuous' media. Every medium has a digital unit. Text uses a character code. Vector graphics use a drawing command such as 'line to (x, y)'. An image uses a pixel value. Audio uses a sample. Video uses a frame. The unit fixes how size is counted: characters, commands, pixels, samples or frames. A presentation can mix both families: a narrated slide has non-temporal text and temporal audio, kept aligned by synchronization (see Chapter 4.9). Knowing the unit lets you estimate storage before compression; compression only reduces the count (see Chapter 4.6).

Text: character codes and fonts

Text is stored as character codes, not as pictures. ASCII maps 128 characters to 7-bit codes; extended ASCII uses 8 bits for 256 codes. Unicode assigns a code point to characters in most writing systems; UTF-8 stores a code point in 1 to 4 bytes. For plain English, ASCII is enough. For Hindi, Tamil or mixed scripts, Unicode is required. A font is the visual design of glyphs. A bitmapped font stores each glyph as a small pixel pattern for one size; scaling it makes edges jagged. An outline font stores lines and curves plus hints; the rasterizer fills pixels at the requested size, so it stays sharp. Use bitmap fonts for tiny fixed displays, outline fonts for documents and screens. Text size depends on characters and encoding: a page of 2,000 ASCII characters is about 2 kB before formatting. The font file is separate from the text file.

Graphics and images: commands and pixels

Graphics and images both produce pictures but store different units. Vector graphics store drawing commands: move to a point, draw a line or curve, fill a shape, set colour and width. The renderer turns commands into pixels at the display resolution. A raster image stores a grid of pixels. Resolution is the grid size, such as 640×480 pixels. Colour depth is bits per pixel; with d bits, each pixel can take one of 2^d values. A 24-bit RGB pixel uses 3 bytes, one byte each for red, green and blue. Raw image size is W×H×d÷8 bytes, before headers. A 1,000×1,000 image at 24 bits is 3,000,000 B, or 3 MB with 1 MB = 10^6 B. Vector art stays sharp when enlarged because commands are re-rendered; a raster image shows pixel blocks when enlarged beyond its grid. Vector suits logos, charts and fonts; raster suits photographs and scanned pages. File formats are separate (see Chapter 4.4); colour models are in Unit 1.

Audio and video: samples and frames

Audio is a continuous pressure wave. An analog-to-digital converter samples the wave at a sampling rate f_s, measured in hertz (sample times per second per channel), and rounds each sample to a bit depth b. With b bits there are 2^b quantization levels. Channels N_ch is 1 for mono and 2 for stereo. The Nyquist rule: to reconstruct a signal up to frequency f_max, sample above twice f_max, so f_s > 2 f_max. Human hearing reaches about 20 kHz; 44.1 kHz satisfies this with room for filters. A higher bit depth reduces quantization noise but increases raw size. Compressed audio and perceptual coding come later (see Chapter 4.7). Video is a sequence of frames. Frame rate is frames per second (fps); resolution is frame width×height; colour depth is bits per pixel. Progressive video draws every line of each frame. Interlaced video splits a frame into two fields, odd lines then even lines; it saves bandwidth on old television but shows combing on fast motion. Raw sizes use the formulas below.

R_audio = f_s × N_ch × (b ÷ 8)

R_audio is raw audio bytes per second; f_s is samples per second per channel; N_ch is number of channels; b is bits per sample; 8 bits = 1 byte.

S_frame = W × H × (d ÷ 8),   R_video = S_frame × F

S_frame is bytes per frame; W and H are width and height in pixels; d is bits per pixel; F is frames per second; R_video is raw video bytes per second.

Worked example

Question. A one-minute stereo recording uses 44.1 kHz and 16 bits per sample. A video frame is 640×480 pixels at 24 bits per pixel and plays at 25 frames/s. Find the raw audio size in MB, the raw size of one frame in MB, and the raw video data rate in MB/s. Use 1 kB = 1000 B and 1 MB = 10^6 B.

  1. Audio: b = 16 bits = 2 bytes per sample. f_s = 44,100 samples/s per channel, N_ch = 2.
  2. Audio byte rate = 44,100 × 2 × 2 = 176,400 B/s.
  3. One minute = 60 s, so size = 176,400 × 60 = 10,584,000 B = 10.584 MB.
  4. Frame: d = 24 bits = 3 bytes/pixel. One frame bytes = 640 × 480 × 3 = 921,600 B = 0.9216 MB.
  5. Video rate = 921,600 B/frame × 25 frames/s = 23,040,000 B/s = 23.04 MB/s.

Answer. One minute of stereo audio is 10.584 MB; one frame is 0.9216 MB; the raw video rate is 23.04 MB/s.

Exam traps

  • Sampling rate is per channel. Stereo at 44.1 kHz stores two samples at each sample time, so total samples per second is 88,200, not 44,100.
  • Do not mix bits and bytes. A 16-bit sample is 2 bytes; a 24-bit pixel is 3 bytes.
  • Video rate needs frames per second. A frame size in MB is not a data rate; multiply by fps.
  • Nyquist says greater than twice the highest frequency, not equal to it. 44.1 kHz is chosen above 40 kHz for 20 kHz audio.
  • Non-temporal does not mean 'not digital'. Text, graphics and images can be files; they simply have no playback time axis.

Analyze A signal has components up to 12 kHz. Is 20 kHz sampling enough? Justify using Nyquist. 4 marks

No. Nyquist requires f_s > 2 × 12 kHz = 24 kHz. The 20 kHz rate is below 24 kHz, so aliasing can occur.

Understand Why is image colour depth measured in bits per pixel, while audio bit depth is measured in bits per sample? 3 marks

An image stores one colour value for each pixel, so its precision is bits per pixel. Audio stores one quantized amplitude value for each sample, so its precision is bits per sample.

4.3 Multimedia hardware and software

A multimedia system is a stack, not one device. An authoring app edits media. Codecs compress and decompress. OS drivers control hardware. Hardware captures, stores and presents media. The stack exists because raw video and audio are too large and too fast for software alone. It is used in a phone camera, a video upload, an IRCTC announcement and a hospital scanner. Each layer hides the layer below: the editor asks a codec, the codec asks a driver, and the driver talks to the device.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for media representation.

A four-layer working model

The figure gives the working model. At the top is the authoring application: a video editor, audio editor or presentation tool. It never touches a camera register. Below it sit codecs and multimedia APIs. A codec is a coder-decoder: it converts raw pixels or samples to a compressed stream and back. An API is an application programming interface, a named set of calls such as “open camera” or “decode frame”. Below that are the operating system and drivers. A driver is software that knows a device’s control registers and data queues. At the bottom is hardware: CPU, GPU, camera, microphone, disk and display. The separation lets one editor work with many cameras. The cost is copying: data moves from camera buffer to system memory to GPU memory. A good system keeps that path short.

Capture, storage, display and the GPU

Capture devices turn a signal into numbers. A camera sensor gives a frame: a grid of pixels with colour samples. A microphone gives audio samples at a fixed sample rate. A scanner gives a still image line by line. Storage holds the result. A hard disk uses spinning platters; an SSD uses flash memory; an optical disk uses a laser. Hard disks are cheap per gigabyte, SSDs are fast, and optical disks suit archive but are slow. Displays present media: an LCD or OLED panel refreshes pixels, and a projector throws light on a screen. Audio devices include speakers, headphones and sound cards. A GPU is a graphics processing unit: many cores that draw pixels and run video encode and decode. A phone records H.264 or H.265 video in a dedicated block, so the CPU stays cool. USB carries data and also power to small devices; HDMI and audio cables carry signals between boxes. Only their role matters, not their protocols.

Software and a capture-to-playback path

Software is layered too. A media player opens a container, finds streams and calls the right decoder. An editor adds cuts, titles and effects. An authoring tool arranges media into a presentation or game level. A codec library does compression. Multimedia APIs sit between applications and the OS: camera, audio and graphics APIs. Drivers sit below APIs. A typical capture-to-playback path has six steps.

  1. App asks camera API for frames.
  2. Driver copies sensor frames to memory.
  3. Codec encodes video frames and audio samples.
  4. Muxer writes streams into a container (see Chapter 4.5).
  5. Storage writes the file.
  6. Player demuxes, decodes and presents with sync (see Chapter 4.9).

A slow driver drops frames; a missing codec refuses to play; a weak GPU stutters during effects. The fix is to find the layer that is late. The app never needs to know the file system.

Worked example

Question. 1080p30 video uses 24 bits/pixel; stereo audio uses 48 kHz, 16 bits/sample; record for 2 h. Find raw GB (1 GB = 10^9 B) and one storage choice.

  4. Video = 186,624,000×7,200 = 1,343,692,800,000 B = 1,343.6928 GB.
  5. Audio rate = 48,000 samples/s × 2 channels (stereo) × 2 B/sample (16 bits) = 192,000 B/s.
  6. Audio = 192,000×7,200 = 1,382,400,000 B = 1.3824 GB.

Exam traps

  • 1080p30 is pixel size and frame rate, not file size.
  • 16-bit audio is 2 bytes per sample, not 16.
  • 1 GB is 10^9 B; 1 GiB is 2^30 B.
  • A container is not a codec; MP4 may hold H.264 and AAC.
  • GPU encode aids the CPU; it does not replace driver or CPU.

4.4 Image, graphics and animation file formats

A file format is an agreement about how an image is stored: where the header is, how colour is coded, whether pixels or drawing commands are kept, and how the data is compressed. The format decides which software can open the file and how large it is. Web pages, phone galleries, print shops and animation tools all depend on this choice. This chapter maps the common still-image, vector, document and animation formats, and shows how colour mode, transparency, lossless or lossy coding, and resolution guide the choice.

You need: Unit 1 raster versus vector graphics and colour models, and Chapter 4.2 media types.

The format is a contract

Raster images store a grid of pixels. Vector images store drawing commands: move to a point, draw a line, fill a shape. A file format fixes which of these is written, plus a header with width, height, colour mode, resolution and metadata. It also fixes how the data is packed and compressed. The extension is only a label; the bytes inside decide the real format.

Colour mode is the way each pixel's colour is recorded. RGB stores red, green and blue levels. Grayscale stores one brightness value. Indexed colour, which some syllabi call palette colour, stores a small number that points into a table of colours. CMYK stores cyan, magenta, yellow and black for print. An alpha channel stores transparency, so a pixel can blend with the page behind it.

Lossless compression reconstructs exact pixels. Lossy compression discards detail to make a smaller file. The algorithm details belong to Chapter 4.6. At file level, ask what is stored and what is lost.

S ≈ W × H × B + O

S is the file size in bytes; W and H are the pixel counts; B is the average bytes per pixel after compression; O is the header and metadata size. This is a planning estimate; content changes the real size.

Raster still-image formats

BMP is a simple bitmap. It often stores pixels with no compression, so it is large but easy to read. It can hold RGB or indexed colour and is used inside Windows and for small test images, rarely on the web.

JPEG is lossy and built for photographs. It stores RGB or grayscale, usually no alpha, and gives small files for smooth tonal scenes. Sharp text and line art suffer visible blocks and ringing, so JPEG is a poor choice for a logo or a screenshot.

GIF stores indexed colour with at most 256 colours. It supports one transparent colour, not partial alpha, and can hold several frames for simple animation. It suits flat diagrams, small icons and short loops.

PNG is lossless and supports RGB, grayscale, indexed colour and full alpha. It is the safe web choice for logos, screenshots and images with transparency. For a photograph, PNG is often larger than JPEG.

TIFF is a flexible container used in scanning, print and archiving. It can store RGB, grayscale or CMYK, many pages and lossless or lossy data. Print workflows value CMYK TIFF because the file states ink colours, not screen colours.

WBMP is a 1-bit format: each pixel is black or white. It was made for small wireless images and is tiny, but it has no colour or grey levels.

Web images are sized in pixels. Print needs enough pixels per inch, ppi, at the final physical size: a 1200×800 image prints 4×2.67 inches at 300 ppi. At 96 ppi the same file would be about 12.5×8.33 inches, but with fewer pixels per inch.

Vector, document and animation formats

SVG is a vector web format written as text. It stores paths, shapes, colours and text as drawing commands, so it scales without pixelation. A simple logo as SVG is small and clear at any size; a photograph as SVG is a bad fit because it must embed or trace millions of details.

PDF is a page description format. It can hold vector text, raster images and fonts, and it is used for printing and sharing fixed-layout documents. A PDF is not a single image; it is a program-like page description that a reader renders.

PSD is an editing document format. It keeps layers, masks, adjustment settings and the colour mode, so an editor can reopen and change the work. It is not a delivery format for a web page or a print shop, which expect flattened PNG, JPEG, TIFF or PDF.

Animation formats store frames over time. GIF animation uses the same 256-colour limit and binary transparency; it is common for short loops. APNG is an animated PNG. It keeps PNG's lossless coding and full alpha, so it is better for smooth edges and transparency, but support and file size vary.

For the web, the common still formats are JPEG, PNG, GIF and SVG. Choose by content: photograph to JPEG, flat graphic or transparency to PNG, simple vector to SVG, short simple loop to GIF or APNG. Long video belongs to Chapter 4.5.

Worked example

Question. A web page needs a 1200×800 photograph and a 200×200 logo with a transparent background. Choose a format for each and give a reason. The photograph is saved as JPEG at an average of 0.5 byte per pixel. Find its file size in kB, using 1 kB = 1000 bytes.

  1. Photograph: choose JPEG. A photograph is continuous-tone, and lossy JPEG gives a small file. JPEG has no alpha, but the photograph does not need transparency.
  2. Logo: choose PNG or SVG. PNG supports full alpha and lossless flat colour. SVG is better if the logo is a simple vector and must scale to any size.
  3. Pixel count = 1200 × 800 = 960,000 pixels.
  4. Bytes = 960,000 pixels × 0.5 B/pixel = 480,000 B.
  5. kB = 480,000 ÷ 1000 = 480 kB.

Answer. JPEG for the photograph and PNG or SVG for the logo; the photograph is 480 kB.

Exam traps

  • JPEG is for photographs, not line art or text. Its lossy coding creates blocks and ringing around sharp edges.
  • GIF has at most 256 indexed colours and only one transparent colour, not partial alpha.
  • PNG is lossless, so for a photograph it is often larger than JPEG; it is not a smaller JPEG.
  • A .jpg extension does not prove JPEG coding. The file header and bytes decide the real format.
  • CMYK is for print, RGB is for screens. Sending a CMYK image to a web page can shift the colours.

4.5 Digital audio and video file formats

A digital audio or video file is usually two things at once: a container and the codecs inside it. The container is the box; the codec is the compressor. This separation lets one file format carry different codecs and lets a player choose the right decoder. It is why a .mp4 from a phone may play on a laptop, and why a .mkv may need a different player. This chapter explains the file formats and the numbers that describe their audio and video streams, with the compression algorithms left to later chapters.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for audio samples and video frames, and Chapter 4.4 for how a file format stores media and metadata.

A container is not a codec

A digital video file usually carries two compressed streams: one for pictures, one for sound. A codec (coder-decoder) compresses or decompresses one stream. A container, also called a wrapper format, packs those streams with timing information, an index and metadata into one file. A muxer writes the container. A player demuxes (separates) the streams and sends each to its decoder.

MP4 is a container. H.264 is a codec. The same .mp4 file can hold H.264 or H.265 video and AAC or MP3 audio. So the extension is a hint, not a promise. Audio-only formats include WAV, AIFF, MP3, AAC (.m4a), FLAC and Ogg Vorbis. Video containers include AVI, MP4, MOV, MKV and WebM.

A .mkv file can hold almost any codec; a .webm file is restricted to web codecs. The container decides what streams and metadata fit; the codec decides how small each stream becomes.

Audio: samples, channels and bitrate

Audio is a sequence of samples. The sample rate fs is the number of samples per second, in hertz (Hz). CD audio uses 44,100 Hz. Bit depth b is the number of bits per sample; 16-bit is common. Channels C is 1 for mono and 2 for stereo. Uncompressed PCM audio stores every sample, so its bitrate is fixed:

R = fs × b × C

R is the bitrate in bits per second (bit/s); fs is the sample rate in samples per second (Hz); b is the bit depth in bits per sample; C is the number of channels.

For CD audio: R = 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bit/s = 1.4112 Mbps. WAV and AIFF often hold PCM. WAV is common on Windows; AIFF on Apple systems. MP3 is lossy and common at 128 kbps. AAC is a newer lossy audio codec, often inside MP4. FLAC is lossless: it reconstructs the exact PCM samples. Ogg Vorbis is lossy and usually sits in an Ogg container.

Bitrate can be constant (CBR) or variable (VBR). VBR spends more bits on difficult passages and fewer on silence. Sample rate is samples per second; bitrate is bits per second. They are different numbers.

Video: resolution, frame rate and containers

Video adds space and time. Resolution is width × height in pixels, such as 1920 × 1080. Frame rate is frames per second (fps). Full motion video is about 24 to 30 fps; cinema uses 24 fps, and many phone videos use 30 fps.

Compressed video bitrate is the number of bits per second for the video stream. A 1080p H.264 stream may use 4 to 8 Mbps; H.265 can reach similar quality at a lower bitrate. VBR changes the video bitrate with scene complexity; CBR holds it roughly fixed. Containers differ in the codecs they expect:

AVI is older and less flexible. MP4 is widely used for phones, cameras and the web. MOV is Apple's container. MKV is open and can hold many streams, subtitles and chapters. WebM targets the web with VP9 or AV1 video and Opus or Vorbis audio. H.264 (AVC) and H.265 (HEVC) are codecs, not containers.

Worked example

Question. An MP4 file holds H.264 video at 2 Mbps and AAC audio at 128 kbps. The video lasts 3 minutes. Find the file size, ignoring container overhead. Use 1 MB = 10^6 bytes.

  1. Duration = 3 min = 3 × 60 = 180 s.
  2. Video bits = 2,000,000 bit/s × 180 s = 360,000,000 bits.

  5. Audio bytes = 23,040,000 ÷ 8 = 2,880,000 B = 2.88 MB.
  6. Total size = 45 MB + 2.88 MB = 47.88 MB.

Answer. The file size is 47.88 MB, ignoring container overhead.

  • A .mp3 file is audio only; it cannot carry a video stream.

4.6 Compression basics: lossless and lossy methods

Compression rewrites data in fewer bits by removing redundancy. Redundancy is repeated or predictable information: repeated pixels, repeated frames, uneven symbol frequencies, or detail the eye ignores. Lossless compression reconstructs the exact original bytes. Lossy compression discards some detail, so the original cannot be recovered exactly. Every image, song and video on a phone uses these ideas. A photo gallery, a UPI receipt PDF and a video call all depend on compression to fit storage and network limits. This chapter defines redundancy, compression ratio and the main lossless and lossy methods.

You need: data sizes from Chapter 4.2 and file formats from Chapter 4.4 and Chapter 4.5.

Redundancy and compression ratio

Compression is the process of reducing the number of bits used to store or transmit data. Decompression reverses it. The need is simple: a 12-megapixel colour photo has about 36 million bytes of raw pixel data, too large for a chat app to send quickly. Compression works by finding redundancy.

Redundancy is data that can be predicted from other data. Four kinds matter. Spatial redundancy: neighbouring pixels in an image are often similar, as in a clear sky. Temporal redundancy: successive video frames are similar, as in a static cricket pitch. Coding redundancy: some symbols occur more often than others, so fixed-length codes waste bits. Psychovisual redundancy: the eye and ear ignore some detail, such as very fine texture or a quiet tone next to a loud one.

compression ratio = original size ÷ compressed size

original size is bits before compression; compressed size is bits after compression. Ratio above 1 means the compressed file is smaller.

saving % = (1 − compressed size ÷ original size) × 100

original size and compressed size use the same unit. A file of 1000 bytes compressed to 250 bytes has ratio 4 and saving 75%.

Lossless methods: RLE, Huffman and LZW

Lossless compression reconstructs the exact original data. It is used when a changed byte matters: text files, source code, bank statements, medical scans, ZIP archives. Three classic methods appear in syllabi.

Run-length encoding (RLE) replaces a run of the same value by one value and a count. The string AAAABBBCCDAA becomes 4A3B2C1D2A. RLE is simple and fast. It works well on images with large flat areas, such as a cartoon or a scanned document. It can expand data when runs are short.

Huffman coding assigns short codes to frequent symbols and long codes to rare symbols. It builds a binary tree by repeatedly combining the two smallest weights. The code must be prefix-free: no code is the start of another code. This lets the decoder read bits left to right without separators. A fixed 3-bit code for six symbols always uses 3 bits per symbol. Huffman uses fewer bits on average when frequencies are uneven. The worked example below shows the calculation.

LZW (Lempel–Ziv–Welch) builds a dictionary of repeated strings while reading the data. It starts with single characters. When a string repeats, LZW adds it to the dictionary and later sends the dictionary index instead of the string. The decoder rebuilds the same dictionary, so no dictionary needs to be stored. LZW is used in GIF images and in some document formats. It is good at repeated words and patterns.

All three methods are lossless. Entropy coding is the general name for Huffman, arithmetic coding and similar final stages that pack symbols into bits. In a lossy pipeline, entropy coding is still the last lossless step.

Lossy methods: quantization, DCT and perceptual coding

Lossy compression discards detail that is less important to human perception. It cannot reconstruct the exact original. Some syllabi call it irreversible compression. It is used for photos, music and video, where a small loss is acceptable and the saving is large.

Quantization reduces the precision of numbers. A value 137 can be stored as 136 or 144 if the step size is 8. The decoder gets an approximation. Quantization is the main lossy step in image and audio codecs. A larger step gives a smaller file and more visible or audible error.

The discrete cosine transform (DCT) converts a block of pixels into frequency coefficients. Low-frequency coefficients describe broad shapes and smooth colour; high-frequency coefficients describe fine detail and edges. The codec quantizes high-frequency coefficients more coarsely because the eye notices them less. The inverse DCT rebuilds an approximate block. This is why JPEG can shrink a photo while keeping it recognisable. Details of the JPEG pipeline belong to Chapter 4.7.

Perceptual coding uses a model of hearing or vision. In audio, a loud tone masks a nearby quiet tone; the quiet tone can be removed without being heard. In video, the eye is less sensitive to fine colour detail than to brightness. The codec spends fewer bits on masked or less visible components. This is the basis of MP3 and AAC audio, and of video codecs.

After quantization, the remaining symbols are often entropy-coded with Huffman or arithmetic coding. That last stage is lossless; the loss already happened in quantization. Standards for MPEG audio and video are in Chapter 4.8.

Worked example

Question. Huffman-code the six symbols with frequencies A:45, B:13, C:12, D:16, E:9, F:5. Give one valid prefix-free code, the average code length, and compare the total bits with a fixed 3-bit code for 100 symbols. Find the compression ratio and saving percentage.

  1. Total symbols = 45 + 13 + 12 + 16 + 9 + 5 = 100.
  2. Sort weights ascending: F 5, E 9, C 12, B 13, D 16, A 45.
  3. Merge the two smallest: F 5 + E 9 = N1 14.
  4. Remaining: C 12, B 13, N1 14, D 16, A 45. Merge C 12 + B 13 = N2 25.
  5. Remaining: N1 14, D 16, N2 25, A 45. Merge N1 14 + D 16 = N3 30.
  6. Remaining: N2 25, N3 30, A 45. Merge N2 25 + N3 30 = N4 55.
  7. Merge A 45 + N4 55 = root 100.
  8. Assign 0 to each left child and 1 to each right child. One valid code: A = 0, B = 101, C = 100, D = 111, E = 1101, F = 1100.
  9. Code lengths: A 1 bit, B 3 bits, C 3 bits, D 3 bits, E 4 bits, F 4 bits.
  10. Total compressed bits = 45×1 + 13×3 + 12×3 + 16×3 + 9×4 + 5×4 = 45 + 39 + 36 + 48 + 36 + 20 = 224 bits.
  11. Average length = 224 ÷ 100 = 2.24 bits/symbol.
  12. Fixed 3-bit code total = 100 × 3 = 300 bits.
  13. Compression ratio = 300 ÷ 224 = 1.3428… ≈ 1.34.
  14. Saving = (300 − 224) ÷ 300 × 100 = 76 ÷ 300 × 100 = 25.333…% ≈ 25.3%.

Answer. The Huffman code has average length 2.24 bits/symbol, total 224 bits against 300 bits for the fixed code, compression ratio about 1.34 and saving about 25.3%.

Exam traps

  • A Huffman code must be prefix-free. If one code is the start of another, the decoder cannot split the bit stream.
  • A compression ratio above 1 means the compressed file is smaller. A ratio below 1 means the 'compressed' file is larger than the original.
  • Lossy compression cannot recover the exact original. Do not call it lossless even if the output looks or sounds the same.
  • In a lossy codec, entropy coding is lossless. The loss comes from quantization, not from Huffman coding.
  • Saving percentage uses the original size in the denominator, not the compressed size.

4.7 Audio and video compression; motion compensation

Audio and video are large streams of samples and frames. Compression makes them small enough to store and send. Audio compression removes what the ear will not notice, using masking. Video compression removes what the eye will not notice and also predicts each frame from earlier frames. Motion compensation is the prediction step: the coder finds where a block moved and sends only the difference. Phone calls, music streaming, video calls and OTT video use these ideas.

You need: Chapter 4.6 for lossy compression and Chapter 4.2 for audio samples and video frames.

Audio compression and masking

PCM is the baseline for digital audio. A microphone signal is sampled at a fixed rate, and each sample is stored with a fixed number of bits. A mono CD signal uses 44 100 samples per second and 16 bits per sample, so one second needs 705 600 bits, or 88.2 kB. PCM keeps every sample. Lossy audio compression sends fewer bits by removing parts the ear cannot hear well.

The ear behaves like a frequency analyser. A loud tone raises the hearing threshold near its frequency. A quiet tone close in frequency becomes inaudible. This is frequency masking. A loud sound also hides a quiet sound just before and just after it. This is temporal masking. A psychoacoustic model estimates these masked regions from the signal.

The coder splits the signal into short overlapping blocks and applies the modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT). The MDCT turns each block into frequency coefficients. Coefficients inside masked regions get coarse quantization or zero bits. Coefficients outside masked regions get fine quantization. MP3 and AAC use this scheme.

Speech codecs often use a different model. They predict the vocal tract with a linear filter and send the filter parameters plus a small residual. This works at low bit rates for speech.

R = fs × b × C

R is the bit rate in bits per second; fs is the sample rate in samples per second; b is the bits per sample; C is the number of channels.

Video frames and motion compensation

A video is a sequence of frames. An I-frame (intra frame) is coded from itself only, like a still image. A P-frame (predicted frame) is coded from an earlier reference frame. A B-frame (bidirectional frame) may use an earlier and a later reference frame. A group of pictures (GOP) is the repeating pattern from one I-frame to the next I-frame.

For a P-frame, the coder splits the current frame into blocks, often 16×16 macroblocks, which are blocks of pixels. For each block it searches the reference frame for a similar block. The search is motion estimation. The displacement from the current block to the matched block is the motion vector. If the matched block starts at (x+5, y−3) when the current block starts at (x, y), the motion vector is (5, −3). It is a displacement, not a position.

Motion compensation uses that vector to copy the matched block as a prediction. The coder subtracts the prediction from the current block. The difference is the residual. If the match is good, the residual has little energy and few nonzero values. The coder sends the motion vector and the coded residual. The decoder copies the same prediction and adds the residual back. Some syllabi call motion compensation motion-compensated prediction.

B-frames need two reference frames, so they need more memory and introduce delay. A GOP balances random access, compression and delay.

Coding the residual to a bitstream

The residual block goes through a two-dimensional discrete cosine transform (DCT). The DCT turns the block into coefficients: one low-frequency term and many higher-frequency terms. Natural images have most energy in low frequencies, so many high-frequency coefficients are small. Quantization divides each coefficient by a step size and rounds to an integer. A larger step gives more zeros and fewer bits, but more error. The quantized coefficients are scanned in an order that puts low frequencies first, then run-length coded and entropy coded. Entropy coding assigns short codes to common symbols.

The decoder reverses the steps. It entropy-decodes the coefficients, dequantizes them, applies the inverse DCT to get the residual, and adds the motion-compensated prediction. It stores the rebuilt frame as the reference for later frames.

The encoder path runs as follows. Motion estimation uses the current frame and the reference frame. Motion compensation forms the residual. DCT and quantization reduce it to coefficients. Entropy coding writes the bitstream. The motion vector is also sent, because the decoder needs it to rebuild the prediction.

Worked example

Question. A 16×16 macroblock in the current frame starts at (x, y). It matches a block starting at (x+5, y−3) in the reference frame. Each pixel uses 8 bits. Each motion-vector component uses 8 bits. After motion compensation the residual has 40 nonzero coefficients, and each coefficient is coded with 6 bits. Ignore headers. (a) Write the motion vector. (b) Find the bits and bytes for the coded block. (c) Compare with the raw block and give the ratio.

  1. The matched block starts 5 columns to the right and 3 rows in the negative direction from the current block. Motion vector = (5, −3).
  2. Vector bits = 8 bits for x + 8 bits for y = 16 bits.
  3. Residual bits = 40 nonzero coefficients × 6 bits = 240 bits.
  4. Total coded bits = 16 + 240 = 256 bits. Total coded bytes = 256 ÷ 8 = 32 B.
  5. Raw block = 16 × 16 = 256 pixels. Raw bits = 256 × 8 = 2048 bits = 256 B.
  6. Ratio = raw bits ÷ coded bits = 2048 ÷ 256 = 8, so 8:1.

Answer. The motion vector is (5, −3); the block needs 256 bits = 32 B, an 8:1 reduction from the 2048-bit raw block.

Exam traps

  • A motion vector is a displacement, not the absolute position of the matched block. (5, −3) means five columns right and three rows in the negative direction from the current block.
  • An I-frame does not use motion compensation. Do not add motion-vector bits to an I-frame.
  • Quantization is the main lossy step. The DCT and entropy coding are reversible; do not blame them for the lost detail.
  • A B-frame can use a later reference frame, so its decoding order may differ from its display order.
  • Masking is statistical for a population, not a promise for every listener. A coefficient marked masked may still be sent when bits are available.

4.8 MPEG standards and MPEG-7 overview

MPEG is a family of standards from the Moving Picture Experts Group. MPEG-1 and MPEG-2 made compressed video fit on CDs and DVDs. MPEG-4 adds object-based coding and the H.264/AVC codec used in phones and streaming. MPEG-7 is different: it does not compress media. It standardizes descriptions of multimedia content, so software can search, filter and manage audio, video and images. This chapter gives an overview of the family and the role of descriptors and description schemes in MPEG-7.

You need: Chapter 4.7 for motion compensation and perceptual audio coding, and Chapter 4.5 for the difference between a codec and a container.

MPEG-1 and MPEG-2: discs and broadcast

MPEG standards define a compressed bitstream and the decoder rules. A compliant encoder produces bits that a compliant decoder can read. The standard does not fix one encoder chip or one file extension.

MPEG-1 targeted about 1.5 Mbps video plus audio for Video CD. Its audio part has three layers; Layer III is the MP3 format used for music. MPEG-1 video uses motion-compensated prediction and a discrete cosine transform on 8×8 blocks.

MPEG-2 extends this to higher bit rates, interlaced television and transport streams. DVD-Video uses MPEG-2 video and MPEG-1 or MPEG-2 audio; digital television also uses MPEG-2. A size estimate uses the bit rate:

total bits = R × t

R is the bit rate in bits per second; t is the duration in seconds. Divide by 8 for bytes, then by 10⁹ for GB.

MPEG-4: objects and H.264/AVC

MPEG-4 is a family, not a single codec. It models a scene as audio-visual objects: a speaker, a background, a logo. Each object can be coded separately and composed at the decoder. This object-based idea supports interactive scenes and synthetic content. Many real files use MPEG-4 only as a simple video codec.

Part 10 of MPEG-4 is H.264/AVC, a widely used video codec. AVC adds better motion prediction, smaller transform blocks and in-loop filtering. It gives good quality at lower bit rates, so it appears in phones, video calls, streaming and broadcast. MPEG-4 audio includes AAC, used in phones and streaming.

The .mp4 file is a container from MPEG-4 Part 14; it can hold AVC video, AAC audio and metadata. The container is not the standard itself. An .mp4 extension alone does not prove the video codec; you must inspect the stream.

MPEG-7: Multimedia Content Description Interface

MPEG-7 does not compress audio or video. It standardizes descriptions of content. Some syllabi call it the Multimedia Content Description Interface. A description can say that a photo has a red shirt in the centre, that an audio clip is speech, or that a video shows a cricket ball moving left to right.

The basic unit is a descriptor (D). A descriptor represents a feature, such as colour, texture, shape, motion or audio timbre. A description scheme (DS) combines descriptors and states relationships: a shot contains a moving object; an image has regions; a segment has a title.

MPEG-7 uses a Description Definition Language, based on XML, to define descriptors and description schemes. Descriptions support search, filtering, browsing and rights metadata. They are not the media samples themselves. A search engine compares a query descriptor with stored descriptors, then ranks results.

How the family fits together

MPEG-1, MPEG-2 and MPEG-4 are compression families. MPEG-7 is a description standard. MPEG-21 is a framework for delivery and rights; this chapter only notes it. Keep the names separate: MP3 is MPEG-1 Audio Layer III; MP4 is a container; MPEG-4 is a standard family.

Use MPEG-1 for old Video CD material, MPEG-2 for DVD and broadcast, MPEG-4/AVC for modern delivery, and MPEG-7 descriptions when you need to search or manage media. A player needs the right decoder for each stream, even when the container is supported.

In an exam, a question may give bit rate and time. Keep bits, bytes and GB separate. The worked example below uses 6 Mbps video and 448 kbps audio for one hour. Note that 6 Mbps = 6,000,000 bits/s, not 6,000,000 bytes/s. A 4.7 GB single-layer DVD has 4.7×10⁹ B, so the example fits with room for menus and error correction.

Worked example

Question. A one-hour DVD video uses MPEG-2 video at 6 Mbps and audio at 448 kbps. Find the total size in bytes and in GB. Use 1 GB = 10⁹ B and 1 h = 3600 s.

  4. Video bytes = 21,600,000,000 ÷ 8 = 2,700,000,000 B = 2.7 GB.
  5. Audio bit rate: R_a = 448 kbps = 448,000 bits/s.
  6. Audio bits = 448,000 × 3600 = 1,612,800,000 bits.
  7. Audio bytes = 1,612,800,000 ÷ 8 = 201,600,000 B = 201.6 MB.
  8. Total bytes = 2,700,000,000 + 201,600,000 = 2,901,600,000 B.
  9. Total size = 2,901,600,000 ÷ 10⁹ = 2.9016 GB. This is below 4.7 GB.

Answer. The one-hour stream needs 2,901,600,000 B, or 2.9016 GB, so it fits a 4.7 GB single-layer DVD.

Exam traps

  • MPEG-7 is not a compression codec. A file described by MPEG-7 still needs a codec such as MPEG-2 or AVC for its media.
  • MP4 is a container, not the MPEG-4 compression standard. An .mp4 file can carry AVC video and AAC audio.
  • MP3 is MPEG-1 Audio Layer III. It is audio, not MPEG-2 video, and not a video codec.
  • Bit rate is in bits per second. Divide by 8 before you compare a size with a storage capacity in bytes or GB.
  • An .mpg or .mpeg extension does not identify the codec. It may hold MPEG-1 or MPEG-2 video.

4.9 Synchronization in multimedia presentations

Multimedia presentations run several timed streams together: a video call has voice and lips, a class recording has slides and narration, a film has dialogue and subtitles. Synchronization keeps these media at the intended relative times during capture, transmission and presentation. Without it, lips move after the voice, slides jump ahead of the teacher, and subtitles spoil the next line. Players, editors, video-conferencing apps and e-learning systems use it every time they align a soundtrack to a picture.

You need: media types for samples and frames, and audio and video streams in containers.

Stream timing and delay

Intra-stream synchronization keeps one stream's units evenly spaced. Video at 25 frames per second needs a frame every 1/25 s = 40 ms. Audio at 8 kHz needs a sample every 1/8000 s = 0.125 ms. If units arrive in a burst, the stream is wrong even when every unit is present.

Inter-stream synchronization keeps relative timing between streams, such as voice and lips. A synchronization point is a pair of timestamps that should align, such as one video frame and one audio block.

Delay is the time from capture to presentation. Jitter is the variation in delay: one packet takes 20 ms, the next 70 ms. Buffering holds data briefly so late packets play smoothly; it adds delay and cuts jitter. Skew is the remaining time difference between matching points after buffering. The goal is stable relative time, not zero delay. A player can hide small jitter with a buffer. The buffer can reorder units that arrive out of order but before their deadline; it cannot fix a unit that arrives after its deadline or is lost.

Skew, intervals and the scheduler

skew = taudio − tvideo

taudio is the audio presentation timestamp in ms; tvideo is the video presentation timestamp in ms; positive skew means audio is later than video.

Test sync with |skew| ≤ S, where S is the allowed skew in ms. A presentation interval is the window [t − S, t + S] in which a unit may be shown without breaking sync.

  1. Read each unit's timestamp.
  2. Find its matching unit in the other stream.
  3. Compute skew and compare |skew| with S.
  4. If |skew| ≤ S, present both.
  5. If audio is later by more than S, delay video by the skew when a buffer exists.
  6. If audio is later, delay video; with no buffer, drop audio samples or repeat/freeze one video frame. If audio is earlier, delay audio; with no buffer, drop or duplicate video frames or audio samples under policy.

Buffering reduces jitter, not a permanent skew. Lip sync is the main test: the sound of a syllable and the mouth shape must fall in the same interval. Speech uses a tighter S than a slide show.

Worked example

Question. A video frame has timestamp 120 ms and its matching audio sample has timestamp 150 ms. The allowed skew is S = 80 ms. (a) Find the skew and decide if they are in sync. (b) Repeat for audio timestamp 230 ms and state the player action.

  1. t_V = 120 ms, t_A = 150 ms.
  2. skew = t_A − t_V = 150 − 120 = +30 ms.
  3. |skew| = 30 ms ≤ 80 ms, so the pair is in sync.
  4. For t_A = 230 ms: skew = 230 − 120 = +110 ms.
  5. |skew| = 110 ms > 80 ms, so they are out of sync; audio is later.
  6. Delay video by 110 ms if a buffer exists; otherwise drop audio samples to move audio earlier, or repeat/freeze one video frame to delay the picture. Do not skip video frames when audio is late.

Answer. Skew is +30 ms, inside the ±80 ms limit; for 230 ms the skew is +110 ms, so delay video by 110 ms if a buffer exists; otherwise drop audio samples or repeat/freeze one video frame.

Exam traps

  • Use |skew| against S; a negative skew can still be inside the limit.
  • Positive skew means audio is later than video, not earlier.
  • Buffering cuts jitter; it does not remove a fixed offset.
  • Convert frame rate to period: 25 fps is 40 ms per frame, not 25 ms.

Takeaways

  1. Multimedia means several media types integrated under computer control, and interactivity is what makes it different from broadcast.
  2. Every medium reduces to one digital unit: a character code, a pixel, a drawing command, an audio sample or a video frame.
  3. A multimedia system is layers: capture and display hardware, operating-system drivers, codecs, and authoring or playback software on top.
  4. A file format decides how pixels or drawing commands, colour information and metadata are laid out on disk, often with compression built in.
  5. A container holds streams; a codec compresses each stream; the two are chosen independently (an MP4 file can carry H.264 video and AAC audio).
  6. Compression works by removing redundancy: lossless methods rebuild exact data, lossy methods discard detail the eye or ear notices least.
  7. Audio compression exploits frequency masking; video compression adds inter-frame prediction and motion compensation on top of intra-frame coding.
  8. The MPEG family standardises how audio and video are compressed for interchange, while MPEG-7 standardises descriptions used for searching.
  9. Synchronization must be maintained at capture, over the network and at playback, and drift is the error you must bound.

Glossary

Alpha channel
Extra per-pixel data giving opacity, so an image can be composited over another.
Animation
A sequence of images shown in order so that objects appear to move or change.
Bit rate
The number of bits a stream carries per second, usually stated in kbit/s or Mbit/s.
Codec
The algorithm that compresses a media stream and reconstructs it for playback.
Colour depth
The number of bits used to store the colour of one pixel, e.g. 24 bits for 8 bits each of red, green and blue.
Compression ratio
Original data size divided by compressed data size, quoted as a ratio such as 11 : 1.
Container
A file structure that carries compressed streams, timing information and metadata; it is not itself a codec.
Drift
The growing difference between the intended and actual presentation time of a media unit.
Group of pictures
The ordered set of I, P and B frames between two independently decodable points in a video stream.
Hypermedia
Multimedia content joined by links that the reader follows, as on a web page.
Inter-frame coding
Compressing a frame by predicting it from other frames in the sequence.
Interactivity
The property that user input changes what the system presents next.
Intra-frame coding
Compressing a frame using only its own pixels, as a still image is compressed.
Jitter
Variation in the delay of packets, which must be absorbed by a buffer before playback.
Lossless compression
Compression after which the original data can be rebuilt exactly.
Lossy compression
Compression that permanently discards detail to gain a much smaller file.
Motion vector
The displacement that points from a block in the current frame to the best matching region in a reference frame.
Multimedia
A computer-controlled presentation that combines two or more media types and lets the user interact with it.
Non-temporal media
Media such as text, graphics and still images whose meaning does not depend on when they are shown.
Palette
A small table of selected colours where each pixel stores an index into the table instead of a full colour value.
Quantisation
Rounding each sample or coefficient to a limited set of levels, which introduces error and is the main lossy step.
Raster graphics
Images stored as a grid of pixels, tied to a fixed resolution.
Sampling rate
How many samples of a signal are taken per second, e.g. 44 100 Hz for CD audio.
Streaming
Delivering media so playback can begin before the whole file has arrived.
Temporal media
Media such as audio, video and animation whose meaning depends on the timing of playback.
Vector graphics
Images stored as geometric commands that are rendered at the current resolution.
