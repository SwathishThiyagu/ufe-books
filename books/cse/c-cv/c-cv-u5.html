5.1 Corner and blob detection: Harris detector

A feature is a small image patch that a program can find again in another image. A flat patch matches everywhere, an edge matches along its length, but a corner matches at one place only. Harris turns this idea into arithmetic: measure how brightness changes in a small window, put the changes into a 2×2 matrix, and score every pixel. The detector still runs inside phone panorama stitchers, visual odometry and tracking code. Blob detectors extend the same search from points to regions.

You need Unit 2 (convolution and gradients) and Unit 4 (edge detection); the Sobel operator from Unit 4 is reused here.

Features: edges, corners, blobs

An edge is a line where brightness changes sharply across the line but stays nearly constant along it. A corner is a point where brightness changes sharply in two different directions, so the patch looks different when the window moves any way. A blob is a small region whose brightness differs from its surroundings.

Matching a flat patch is impossible because every flat patch looks the same. Matching an edge has the aperture problem: slide the window along the edge and the pixels do not change, so the position along the line is unknown. A corner pins down both coordinates.

E(u, v) = Σ w(x, y) [I(x+u, y+v) − I(x, y)]²

I(x, y) is the intensity in grey levels at pixel (x, y); w(x, y) is the window weight (1 inside a box window, 0 outside); (u, v) is the shift in pixels; E is the sum of squared differences.

Harris asks one question at each pixel: if I shift a small window by (u, v), does the content change in all directions? If yes, the pixel is a corner.

Gradients and the structure matrix H

The derivatives Ix = ∂I/∂x and Iy = ∂I/∂y come from Sobel filters (Unit 4). For a small shift, the squared-difference sum becomes a quadratic form in the shift.

E(u, v) ≈ [u v] H [u v]ᵀ,   H = Σ w(x, y) × [[Ix², IxIy], [IxIy, Iy²]]

H is the 2×2 structure matrix; the sum runs over the window; w is the window weight; Ix and Iy are gradients in grey levels per pixel.

H is symmetric, so it has two real eigenvalues λ1 and λ2. They are the weighted sum of the squared intensity change along the two principal directions of the window; with unit weights inside a box window, this is simply the sum. The eigenvectors give those directions.

λ1λ2Meaning
LargeLargeCorner: change in both directions
LargeSmallEdge: change across the edge only
SmallSmallFlat: little change anywhere

The actual size of λ depends on contrast and on the window size, so only the pattern matters. Some syllabi call H the second-moment matrix.

Harris response, threshold and NMS

Computing eigenvalues at every pixel is slow, so Harris combines them into one number.

R = det H − k (trace H)²

R is the Harris response in (grey levels)⁴ per pixel⁴, the same unit as det H and (trace H)²; det H = λ1λ2; trace H = λ1 + λ2; k is a constant, usually 0.04 to 0.06.

For a corner both eigenvalues are large: det H is large and the subtracted trace term is smaller, so R is large and positive. For an edge one eigenvalue is tiny: det H stays small but (trace H)² is large, so R is negative. For a flat area both are small and R is near zero. Shi-Tomasi uses the simpler response R = λmin.

  1. Smooth the image with a small Gaussian to reduce noise.
  2. Compute Ix and Iy with Sobel filters.
  3. At each pixel, sum Ix², Iy² and IxIy over a 3×3 or 5×5 window.
  4. Form H and compute R.
  5. Threshold R, for example keep R > 0.01 × max R.
  6. Apply 3×3 non-maximum suppression: keep a pixel only if its R is the largest in its neighbourhood.

NMS stops one corner from producing a cluster of keypoints; the threshold stops weak noise responses. Harris is rotation invariant: rotating the image rotates the eigenvectors but leaves the eigenvalues unchanged. It is not scale invariant, because the window size is fixed.

Blobs in brief

A blob is a region, not a point: a dark spot on a bright background, a cell, a road sign. To find blobs of unknown size, filter the image with a Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG), a second-derivative filter shaped like a Mexican hat.

The response is strongest when the filter's central lobe matches the blob. If the Gaussian has standard deviation σ in pixels, the blob radius is about √2 σ. Run the filter at many σ, a scale space, and keep the (x, y, σ) where the response peaks in space and scale. Difference of Gaussians (DoG) is a fast approximation: subtract two Gaussians with σ and 2σ. SIFT builds on DoG, as the next chapter shows.

Worked example

Question. A 2×2 window has equal weights w = 1. The horizontal gradient is Ix = [[2, 2], [−2, −2]] and the vertical gradient is Iy = [[2, −2], [2, −2]]. Find H, its eigenvalues, det H, trace H and the Harris response with k = 0.04. Is the point a corner?

  1. 1. Square the horizontal gradients and add: Σ Ix² = 2² + 2² + (−2)² + (−2)² = 16.
  2. 2. Square the vertical gradients and add: Σ Iy² = 2² + (−2)² + 2² + (−2)² = 16.
  3. 3. Multiply the pairs and add: Σ IxIy = (2)(2) + (2)(−2) + (−2)(2) + (−2)(−2) = 4 − 4 − 4 + 4 = 0.
  4. 4. Equal weights give H = [[16, 0], [0, 16]].
  5. 5. Solve det(H − λI) = (16 − λ)² = 0, so λ1 = λ2 = 16.
  6. 6. det H = 16 × 16 − 0 × 0 = 256. trace H = 16 + 16 = 32.
  7. 7. R = 256 − 0.04 × 32² = 256 − 0.04 × 1024 = 256 − 40.96 = 215.04.
  8. 8. R is large and positive and both eigenvalues are large, so the point is a corner.

Answer. H = [[16, 0], [0, 16]], λ1 = λ2 = 16, det H = 256, trace H = 32 and R = 215.04; the point is a corner.

Exam traps

  • Using one pixel's gradients instead of summing over the window. The Σ over w is the detector; a single-pixel H labels noise as a corner.
  • Skipping non-maximum suppression. Every pixel on a thick corner scores high, so you get clusters instead of one keypoint per corner.
  • Swapping det and trace. det H = λ1λ2 (256 in the example) and trace H = λ1 + λ2 (32); exchanging them changes the sign of R.
  • Claiming scale invariance. Harris uses a fixed window, so zooming in makes a corner flatter and the response falls.
  • Choosing k too small, or using a negative sign, leaves edges with R > 0, so they are wrongly kept as corners.
  • Choosing k too large pushes even true corners to R < 0, so corners are missed.

5.2 Feature descriptors: SIFT and SURF

A feature descriptor is a short vector that describes the patch around a keypoint. SIFT and SURF build that vector from local gradient patterns, so the same physical point gives a similar vector after zoom, rotation and small viewpoint change. The detector finds the point; the descriptor names it. Software then matches vectors between two images to stitch a panorama, locate an object, or build a 3D model. SIFT is the careful reference method. SURF is a faster approximation built from box filters. Both finish with a distance test between vectors.

You need: 5.1 keypoints, Unit 2 image pyramids and Gaussian filtering, and Unit 4 gradients.

Scale space and DoG keypoints

Scale space is a stack of blurred copies of the same image. Small blur finds fine detail; large blur finds coarse structure. The Gaussian blurred image at scale σ is:

L(x,y,σ) = G(x,y,σ) * I(x,y)

I is the input image; G is a Gaussian kernel with standard deviation σ; * is convolution; (x,y) is a pixel position; L is the blurred image.

SIFT builds several octaves. Each octave doubles σ and halves the image size. The Difference of Gaussians subtracts two nearby scales:

D(x,y,σ) = L(x,y,kσ) − L(x,y,σ)

k is a fixed scale factor, commonly 2^(1/3) in SIFT; D is the DoG image.

  1. Build the Gaussian scale space and the DoG pyramid.
  2. Test each DoG pixel against its 26 neighbours: 8 in the same scale, 9 below, 9 above.
  3. Keep candidates whose |D| passes a contrast threshold.
  4. Reject points on edges using the ratio of principal curvatures.

Orientation and the 128-D SIFT descriptor

Each keypoint gets one or more orientations. The gradient at a pixel has magnitude m and angle θ:

m(x,y) = √((L(x+1,y)−L(x−1,y))² + (L(x,y+1)−L(x,y−1))²),  θ(x,y) = atan2(L(x,y+1)−L(x,y−1), L(x+1,y)−L(x−1,y))

L is the blurred image at the keypoint scale; atan2 returns the angle in radians from −π to π.

SIFT builds a 36-bin histogram of θ over a window around the keypoint. Each sample is weighted by m and by a Gaussian window. The highest peak is the keypoint orientation. Any other peak above 80% of the highest peak creates another keypoint with that orientation.

The descriptor uses a square patch rotated to the keypoint orientation. The patch is divided into 4×4 cells. Each cell has an 8-bin gradient-orientation histogram. 4×4×8 = 128 values form the SIFT descriptor. Values are smoothed by trilinear interpolation, then normalized:

d′ = d / ||d||₂, ||d||₂ = √(Σ dᵢ²)

d is the 128-D descriptor vector; dᵢ is one component; ||d||₂ is the L2 norm. SIFT then clips each component at 0.2 and renormalizes.

SURF: box filters and Haar wavelets

SURF replaces Gaussian derivatives with box filters and uses an integral image:

IΣ(x,y) = Σ x′≤x, y′≤y I(x′,y′)

IΣ is the integral image; the sum runs over all pixels above and to the left of (x,y). Any rectangular sum is four additions, so box filters are fast at many scales.

SURF uses the Hessian matrix. Its determinant is approximated as:

det(H) ≈ D_xx D_yy − (0.9 D_xy)²

D_xx, D_yy and D_xy are box-filter responses that approximate second derivatives; the factor 0.9 corrects the box-filter approximation. Keypoints are extrema of this determinant in scale and space.

For orientation, SURF sums Haar wavelet responses in x and y inside a circular window. The longest sum vector gives the dominant direction. The descriptor uses a 4×4 grid of subregions. In each subregion it sums dx, dy, |dx| and |dy|, giving 4×4×4 = 64 values. The upright SURF variant skips rotation and is faster.

Matching with the ratio test

Matching compares descriptors from two images. For a descriptor p in the first image, find its nearest neighbour q1 and second nearest neighbour q2 in the second image by Euclidean distance. The ratio test accepts the match only if:

||p − q1|| / ||p − q2|| < 0.8

||·|| is Euclidean distance; 0.8 is a typical threshold. A smaller threshold is stricter.

A true match usually has q1 much closer than q2. An ambiguous match has q2 almost as close, so the ratio is near 1 and the match is rejected. This removes many false matches before RANSAC or bag-of-words. See 5.4 for what comes next.

Worked example

Question. A SIFT descriptor is being built. One 4×4 spatial cell has an 8-bin orientation histogram. Four of its bins have counts [1, 3, 0, 2]; the other four are zero. Take only these four bins. (a) Compute the L2 norm. (b) Normalize the four-bin vector. (c) State what SIFT does to the full 128-D vector after normalization.

  1. The four-bin vector is d = [1, 3, 0, 2].
  2. Square each component: 1² = 1, 3² = 9, 0² = 0, 2² = 4.
  3. Sum the squares: 1 + 9 + 0 + 4 = 14.
  4. L2 norm: ||d||₂ = √14 = 3.7417 (rounded to 4 decimals).
  5. Divide each component by 3.7417.
  6. 1 ÷ 3.7417 = 0.2673.
  7. 3 ÷ 3.7417 = 0.8018.
  8. 0 ÷ 3.7417 = 0.0.
  9. 2 ÷ 3.7417 = 0.5345.
  10. Normalized vector = [0.2673, 0.8018, 0.0, 0.5345].
  11. For the full SIFT descriptor, the 128 values are normalized, clipped at 0.2, and renormalized.

Answer. The L2 norm is 3.7417, and the normalized four-bin vector is [0.2673, 0.8018, 0.0, 0.5345].

Exam traps

  • The descriptor is not the raw histogram count. Raw counts change with contrast; SIFT normalizes, clips and renormalizes to reduce illumination effects.
  • A 4×4 grid of cells with 8 bins is 128 values, not 4×4 = 16. Each cell contributes 8 numbers.
  • Orientation assignment happens before descriptor building. Skip it and the descriptor is not rotation invariant.
  • SURF is not SIFT with faster code. SURF uses a Hessian-determinant detector and Haar-wavelet descriptor; upright SURF is not rotation invariant.
  • In the ratio test, a smaller threshold is stricter. Do not accept a match just because the nearest distance is small; compare it with the second nearest.

5.3 Histogram of oriented gradients (HOG)

HOG describes local shape by counting gradient directions. It divides an image window into small cells, builds a histogram of unsigned gradient orientations in each cell, groups cells into overlapping blocks, and normalizes each block. The normalized histograms joined into one vector are the descriptor. HOG exists because raw pixel values change with lighting, while edge-direction patterns are steadier. It is used in sliding-window object detection, most famously pedestrian detection, and as a classical baseline before deep learning.

You need: Unit 4, detection of points, lines and edges (gradients); 5.2 Feature descriptors.

Gradients vote into cells

Compute horizontal and vertical gradients Gx and Gy at each pixel, usually with a simple derivative filter. Each pixel gives a magnitude and an orientation.

G = √(Gx² + Gy²), θ = atan2(Gy, Gx) mod 180°

Gx and Gy are horizontal and vertical gradients; G is magnitude; θ is unsigned orientation; atan2 returns the angle.

Unsigned means opposite directions are the same line: 10° and 190° both become 10°. Classic HOG uses 9 bins covering 0° to 180°, each 20° wide. Bin k, for k = 1 to 9, collects pixels with (k−1)·20° ≤ θ < k·20°. A pixel adds its magnitude G to its bin, not 1. A strong edge votes more than a weak one.

The image window is split into cells, for example 8×8 pixels. Each cell produces one 9-bin histogram. A cell is the local unit; it is small enough to keep shape detail and large enough to tolerate a little movement. Real implementations often interpolate a vote between neighbouring bins and cells. The worked example uses no interpolation, which keeps the arithmetic clear.

Blocks and L2-Hys normalization

Neighbouring cells are grouped into blocks. A common block is 2×2 cells, or 16×16 pixels. Blocks overlap: a stride of one cell means each cell appears in up to four blocks. Overlap makes the descriptor robust to small shifts.

Each block concatenates its cell histograms into a vector v. Normalization removes the effect of overall contrast.

||v||₂ = √(Σ vᵢ²), v̂ = v / (||v||₂ + ε)

vᵢ is one histogram entry; ||v||₂ is the L2 norm; ε is a tiny number that avoids division by zero.

L2-Hys adds a clamp: limit each v̂ᵢ to 0.2, then normalize again. The clamp stops one very strong gradient from dominating. The final HOG descriptor is the list of normalized block vectors in scan order.

  • Cell histogram: one cell, 9 numbers.
  • Block: 2×2 cells, 36 numbers before normalization.
  • Descriptor: all blocks joined. For a 64×128 window with 8×8 cells and 8-pixel stride, there are 7×15 = 105 blocks, so 105 × 36 = 3780 numbers.

Sliding windows and relatives

Detection uses a fixed-size window, say 64×128 for a person. Slide the window over the image at several scales. Compute HOG for each window. A classifier trained beforehand, often an SVM, scores the vector. High scores are detections; non-maximum suppression merges overlapping boxes. Classifier training belongs to Unit 6.

HOG is dense: it describes every cell in the window, with no keypoint detector and no keypoint orientation. SIFT in 5.2 first finds keypoints and builds a descriptor around each one, so it is sparse and scale/rotation-aware. HOG is computed on a regular grid. It is not rotation invariant by design, which suits upright people and cars. It can still handle small deformation because cells and blocks overlap.

LBP is a related texture descriptor that compares a pixel with its neighbours; some syllabi mention it beside HOG. A traffic-camera pedestrian detector is a typical use: the person is upright, lighting changes, and a fast fixed-length descriptor helps.

Worked example

Question. One 8×8 cell has four gradient samples (angle, magnitude): (10°, 2), (50°, 1), (110°, 3), (170°, 2). Use 9 unsigned bins of 20° with no interpolation. Build the histogram and L2-normalize it.

  1. Unsigned orientation range is 0° ≤ θ < 180°. Bin width is 20°. Bin number = floor(θ/20) + 1.
  2. 10°: floor(10/20)+1 = 1. Add magnitude 2 to bin 1.
  3. 50°: floor(50/20)+1 = 3. Add magnitude 1 to bin 3.
  4. 110°: floor(110/20)+1 = 6. Add magnitude 3 to bin 6.
  5. 170°: floor(170/20)+1 = 9. Add magnitude 2 to bin 9.
  6. Histogram bins 1 to 9 = [2, 0, 1, 0, 0, 3, 0, 0, 2].
  7. Norm = √(2² + 0² + 1² + 0² + 0² + 3² + 0² + 0² + 2²) = √18 = 4.243.
  8. Divide each bin by 4.243, rounded to 3 decimals: [0.471, 0, 0.236, 0, 0, 0.707, 0, 0, 0.471].

Answer. The L2 norm is 4.243, and the normalized 9-bin cell histogram is [0.471, 0, 0.236, 0, 0, 0.707, 0, 0, 0.471].

Exam traps

  • Signed vs unsigned: classic HOG uses 0°–180°. Using 0°–360° doubles the bins and changes the vector length.
  • 170° is bin 9, not bin 1: floor(170/20)+1 = 9. With interpolation it also spills toward bin 1, but do not wrap it in a no-interpolation question.
  • A cell histogram is not the descriptor. Normalize overlapping blocks and join them.
  • Do not confuse cell size with block size. An 8×8 cell inside a 2×2-cell block covers 16×16 pixels.
  • SIFT finds keypoints; HOG does not. Calling HOG rotation invariant is wrong.

5.4 Bag of visual words

Bag of visual words (BoVW) describes an image by which local features it contains and how many of each, and ignores where they sit. Take SIFT descriptors from many training images, cluster them into k groups with k-means, and call each cluster centre a visual word. Any image then becomes a histogram of length k. Classifiers need fixed-length inputs and images give different numbers of descriptors, so this step is needed. BoVW powered image search and scene classification before deep learning, and it is still a fast baseline.

You need: 5.2 Feature descriptors: SIFT and SURF for the local descriptors, and basic k-means plus a standard classifier such as k-NN or SVM (see Unit 6, Recognition and Deep Learning for Vision).

From descriptors to a vocabulary

A SIFT descriptor is a list of 128 numbers describing one small patch around a keypoint. One image gives a few hundred such vectors, and the count changes with image size, texture and the detector threshold. A classifier such as an SVM needs the same number of inputs for every image. BoVW turns the whole set into one vector of fixed length k.

The method borrows an idea from text search. A document is a bag of words when we count its words and ignore their order. Here each descriptor plays the role of a word, and the vocabulary of words is learned once from training images.

  1. Collect SIFT or SURF descriptors from a large set of training images. Keep only the vectors, not their positions.
  2. Choose k, the number of visual words. A few hundred to a few thousand is typical.
  3. Run k-means on the descriptors: start with k centres, assign each descriptor to the nearest centre, move each centre to the mean of its assigned descriptors, repeat until the centres stop moving.
  4. Store the k final centres. Each centre is one visual word; the set of centres is the vocabulary.
J = Σj=1k Σx∈Cj ‖x − μj‖²

x is one descriptor vector (128 numbers for SIFT); Cj is the set of descriptors assigned to word j; μj is the centre of word j; ‖·‖ is Euclidean length; J is the total squared error that k-means shrinks.

The vocabulary is built once, offline, from training images. Every later image, training or test, is described with this same frozen vocabulary.

Assigning words and building the histogram

To describe a new image, detect keypoints as in 5.1, compute one descriptor per keypoint, and assign each descriptor to the nearest word by Euclidean distance. Then count how many descriptors fell on each word.

hi = ni / N

hi is the i-th entry of the histogram; ni is the number of descriptors assigned to word i; N is the total number of descriptors in the image. The entries sum to 1.

The vector [h1, h2, …, hk] is the image's BoVW feature. Its length is always k, whatever the image size. A classifier such as k-NN or an SVM is then trained on these vectors (Unit 6).

The numbering of words is arbitrary: word 1 may be a patch of grass and word 3 a patch of window. It must not change after training, because the classifier learns one weight per histogram position. A zero entry is not a missing value; it only means the image used no descriptor of that word.

Weighting, layout and a compact variant

Some words are useless. If a texture appears in almost every training image, its histogram entry is large everywhere and separates nothing. Text search solved this with tf-idf weighting, and BoVW borrows it:

wi = (ni / N) · log(Nimg / dfi)

ni/N is the term frequency of word i in this image; dfi is the number of training images that contain word i at least once; Nimg is the total number of training images; wi is the weighted entry. A word found in every image gets log(1) = 0, so it is switched off.

The plain histogram throws away position. Spatial pyramid matching puts coarse position back: split the image into 1×1, 2×2 and 4×4 grids, build one histogram per cell, and concatenate them. That gives (1 + 4 + 16)k numbers, with the levels often weighted 1/4, 1/4 and 1/2.

VLAD is a compact extension that keeps more than counts. For each word, add up the residuals of the descriptors assigned to it:

vi = Σx → i (x − μi)

x → i means x is a descriptor whose nearest word is i; μi is that word's centre; vi is the summed residual for word i. The k blocks are concatenated into a vector of length k·d (d = 128 for SIFT) and L2-normalised.

Worked example

Question. A vocabulary has k = 3 visual words. One image gives six SIFT descriptors, which are assigned to words [1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 3]. (a) Count the descriptors per word. (b) Build the normalised BoVW histogram. (c) Word 2 occurs in 400 of the 1000 training images. Using log base 10, find its tf-idf weight.

  1. Count the assignments: word 1 appears at positions 1 and 2, word 2 at position 3, word 3 at positions 4, 5 and 6. So n₁ = 2, n₂ = 1, n₃ = 3.
  2. Total descriptors N = 2 + 1 + 3 = 6.
  3. Divide each count by N: h₁ = 2/6 = 0.333, h₂ = 1/6 = 0.167, h₃ = 3/6 = 0.500. The histogram is [0.333, 0.167, 0.500] and sums to 1.000.
  4. Term frequency of word 2: tf = n₂/N = 1/6 = 0.167.
  5. Inverse document frequency: idf = log₁₀(Nimg / df₂) = log₁₀(1000/400) = log₁₀(2.5) = 0.398.
  6. Weight: w₂ = tf × idf = 0.167 × 0.398 = 0.066.

Answer. The normalised histogram is [0.333, 0.167, 0.500], and the tf-idf weight of word 2 is 0.066.

Exam traps

  • Re-running k-means on each test image. The word numbers change, so the new histogram cannot be compared with the training histograms. Fit the vocabulary once and freeze it.
  • Believing BoVW keeps layout. The histogram is blind to position; only a spatial pyramid adds coarse geometry.
  • Comparing histograms built from two different vocabularies, or training on raw counts and testing on unit-sum histograms. Use one vocabulary and one normalisation everywhere.
  • Mixing up the vocabulary size k with the descriptor length d. SIFT has d = 128 numbers per descriptor, while k is the number of words and the histogram length.
  • Treating the order of words as meaningful across runs. Two correct k-means runs may swap word 1 and word 3; only the pairing of vocabulary and histogram matters.

5.5 Camera models and camera calibration

A camera model is a set of equations that turns a 3D point into a pixel. The pinhole model uses two steps: a rigid transform from world to camera, then a perspective divide and scale to image coordinates. Calibration estimates the scale and origin (intrinsic) and the pose (extrinsic). Phone cameras, AR apps, robot arms and drone mapping need these numbers; without them a measured pixel cannot become a direction in space.

You need: image formation (see Unit 1, Foundations) and matrix multiplication with homogeneous coordinates.

Pinhole projection and homogeneous coordinates

A pinhole camera has a small opening and an image plane behind it. Light from a 3D point goes straight through the opening and hits one pixel. Put the camera centre at the origin of a camera frame, with the optical axis along +Z. The point has camera coordinates (X, Y, Z) in metres.

Similar triangles give the pixel. The pixel u grows with X and shrinks with Z: u = fx·X/Z + cx. The pixel v grows with Y and shrinks with Z: v = fy·Y/Z + cy. Here fx and fy are focal lengths in pixels; (cx, cy) is the principal point, the pixel where the optical axis meets the image. The division by Z is the perspective divide. A far object has large Z, so it moves few pixels.

u = fx·X/Z + cx, v = fy·Y/Z + cy

(X, Y, Z) is the point in camera coordinates in metres; fx, fy are focal lengths in pixels; (cx, cy) is the principal point in pixels; (u, v) is the pixel coordinate.

Homogeneous coordinates add a 1: (X, Y, Z, 1). A 4×4 matrix can then hold translation. A 3×4 projection matrix maps a world point to homogeneous pixel (u', v', w'); dividing by w' gives (u, v). That matrix is P = K[R|t].

Intrinsic and extrinsic matrices

The intrinsic matrix K holds the camera's inside numbers: K = [[fx, 0, cx], [0, fy, cy], [0, 0, 1]]. fx and fy are in pixels, not millimetres. If the physical focal length is f metres and one pixel is p metres wide, fx = f/p. A phone camera may have fx = fy = 800 px, cx = 320 px, cy = 240 px on a 640×480 image. The principal point is near the image centre, but calibration measures it.

The extrinsic matrix holds the camera pose. Rotation R (3×3) and translation t (3×1) map a world point Xw to camera coordinates: Xc = R·Xw + t. The camera centre in world coordinates is C = −Rᵀ·t. Extrinsic comes first, then intrinsic: move the world point into the camera frame, then project it to pixels.

P = K [R | t],   Xc = R Xw + t,   [u, v, 1]ᵀ ∝ K Xc

P is the 3×4 camera matrix; K is the 3×3 intrinsic matrix; R is the 3×3 rotation; t is the 3×1 translation; Xw is a world point; Xc is the same point in camera coordinates; ∝ means equal after dividing by the third coordinate.

Some syllabi call finding R and t from image points camera resection. The same equations let a robot arm move to a point seen by a fixed camera.

Distortion and checkerboard calibration

A real lens bends straight lines near the image edge. Radial distortion scales the offset of a pixel from the principal point. First convert to dimensionless normalized image coordinates: x = (u − cx)/fx and y = (v − cy)/fy. Then r² = x² + y². The undistorted pixel is uundist = cx + (u − cx)·(1 + k1·r² + k2·r⁴), and vundist = cy + (v − cy)·(1 + k1·r² + k2·r⁴). Here k1 and k2 are radial coefficients; they apply to the normalized coordinates x and y. Tangential distortion adds p1 and p2 because the lens is not perfectly parallel to the sensor. These numbers are fitted after K and pose.

Checkerboard calibration uses a board with known square size, say 25 mm. The camera takes 10 to 20 images at different tilts. A corner detector finds inner corners; their 3D positions on the board are known. A direct linear transform (DLT) solves a projection matrix from six or more point pairs. Zhang's method uses each planar view's homography to constrain K, giving a closed-form first estimate; nonlinear least squares refines K, R, t and distortion. Only a few universities teach the closed-form steps in detail.

Reprojection error judges the fit: project every known 3D corner with the fitted camera and compare with the detected pixel. The per-corner error is the Euclidean distance in pixels between the detected and projected corner: ei = √((uobs,i − upred,i)² + (vobs,i − vpred,i)²). Calibration tools report the root-mean-square error over all N corners, e = √((1/N)·Σ(du² + dv²)), where du and dv are the observed minus projected pixel differences. A good calibration often reaches well under 1 px. Large error means bad corners, too few views, or a board that was not rigid.

Worked example

Question. A camera has fx = fy = 800 px, cx = 320 px, cy = 240 px. A point in camera coordinates is (X, Y, Z) = (1.0 m, 0.5 m, 2.0 m). Find its pixel (u, v).

  1. The point is already in camera coordinates, so R and t are not needed.
  2. Compute X/Z = 1.0 m / 2.0 m = 0.5, and Y/Z = 0.5 m / 2.0 m = 0.25.
  3. u = fx·X/Z + cx = 800 × 0.5 + 320 = 400 + 320 = 720 px.
  4. v = fy·Y/Z + cy = 800 × 0.25 + 240 = 200 + 240 = 440 px.

Answer. The point projects to pixel (u, v) = (720 px, 440 px).

Exam traps

  • Putting focal length in millimetres into K. fx and fy in K are in pixels; divide the physical focal length by pixel pitch.
  • Forgetting the divide by Z. The raw homogeneous values are not pixels until you divide by the third coordinate.
  • Mixing world and camera coordinates. R and t must move the point into the camera frame before K is applied.
  • Using the image centre from sensor size as cx, cy. Calibration returns the measured principal point, which can be several pixels away.
  • Fitting distortion before K and pose. The usual order is K and pose from a linear model, then distortion, then joint refinement.

5.6 Epipolar geometry, stereo and multi-view geometry

Two photographs of the same scene are not independent. A 3D point seen in the left image must appear somewhere on a specific line in the right image, called the epipolar line. This constraint, captured by the fundamental matrix F, reduces matching from a 2D search to a 1D search. When the cameras are parallel, the line becomes a row shift called disparity, and depth follows from Z = f B / d. The same geometry gives camera motion, 3D triangulation and the base for structure from motion.

You need: Chapter 5.5 for the pinhole camera and intrinsic matrix K, plus matrix multiplication, SVD and least squares.

Epipoles, epipolar planes and lines

Two cameras at centres C1 and C2 see a 3D point X. X projects to x1 in the left image and x2 in the right image. The plane through C1, C2 and X is the epipolar plane.

The ray from C1 through X can slide while x1 stays fixed. As it slides, the epipolar plane turns about the baseline C1C2. In the left image every such plane cuts a line through one fixed point e1, the epipole. e1 is the image of C2 in camera 1. In the right image the lines pass through e2, the image of C1.

Knowing x1, the match x2 must lie on the epipolar line l2 through e2. The search is now one-dimensional, and it does not depend on the depth of X. When the image planes are parallel, the epipoles go to infinity and the lines become horizontal; this is rectified stereo.

  1. Detect a keypoint x1 in the left image.
  2. Compute its epipolar line in the right image using F.
  3. Search for the matching descriptor only along that line.
  4. Reject weak matches with a ratio test or RANSAC.

The fundamental matrix and the essential matrix

Write image points as homogeneous vectors x1 = (u1, v1, 1)ᵀ and x2 = (u2, v2, 1)ᵀ, with u and v in pixels. The fundamental matrix F is the 3×3 matrix that encodes the epipolar geometry of the pair.

x2ᵀ F x1 = 0

x1 and x2 are matching pixel points in homogeneous coordinates; F is the 3×3 fundamental matrix. The line l2 = F x1 is the epipolar line in image 2, and l1 = Fᵀ x2 is the line in image 1.

F has rank 2 and is defined only up to scale. It has 7 degrees of freedom: 9 entries minus one for scale, minus one for the rank constraint. If the points are in normalized camera coordinates x̂ = K⁻¹x, the matrix is the essential matrix E, with x̂2ᵀ E x̂1 = 0. E = [t]× R, where R is the rotation and t the translation from camera 1 to camera 2, and [t]× is the cross-product matrix of t. E has 5 degrees of freedom: 3 for rotation and 2 for the translation direction. The link is F = K2⁻ᵀ E K1⁻¹.

Eight-point algorithm: each match gives one linear equation in the nine entries of F. Stack eight or more matches into a matrix A and take the singular vector for the smallest singular value. Enforce rank 2 by zeroing the smallest singular value of the result. Real matches contain outliers, so run the fit inside RANSAC: sample eight matches, fit F, count inliers with |x2ᵀ F x1| near zero, and keep the best model. Normalize pixel coordinates before the SVD for stability.

Rectification, disparity and depth

Rectification warps the two images so that epipolar lines become horizontal image rows. After rectification, a point at pixel (u1, v) in the left image has its match at (u2, v) in the right image. The horizontal shift is the disparity d = u1 − u2, measured in pixels.

Z = f B / d

Z is the depth of the point in metres; f is the focal length in pixels; B is the baseline between the two camera centres in metres; d is the disparity in pixels. Pixels × metres ÷ pixels gives metres.

A small disparity means a large depth, and a point at infinity has d = 0. Halving the depth doubles the disparity. For an unrectified pair, triangulate instead: back-project x1 and x2 as rays from C1 and C2 using the camera matrices P1 and P2, and find the 3D point X where they meet. Noise makes the rays miss, so solve a least-squares problem, typically by the direct linear transform. Triangulation needs the relative camera pose; rectified stereo is the special case of a pure horizontal translation.

Multi-view projective geometry

A camera matrix P = K[R | t] maps a homogeneous 3D point X to a homogeneous image point x = P X. Two views of the same point give two such equations. Eliminating X gives the epipolar constraint, so F is a property of the camera pair, not of the scene. This is why the same F works for every point in the scene.

One camera loses depth: X and every point along the ray from the camera centre project to the same pixel. Two views fix depth only up to the baseline length. Three or more views add constraints; the trifocal tensor plays the role of F for three views, and longer sequences are handled by structure from motion. Some syllabi call F the bifocal tensor. The same equations support pose estimation for a moving robot and augmented reality that must place a virtual object at the right depth.

Worked example

Question. A rectified stereo pair has focal length f = 800 px and baseline B = 0.12 m. A matched point has disparity d = 24 px. Find its depth Z. State the units at each step.

  1. Rectified pair, so the disparity is measured in pixels along a row: d = 24 px.
  2. Formula: Z = f B / d.
  3. Substitute: Z = 800 px × 0.12 m / 24 px.
  4. 800 × 0.12 = 96 px·m, and 96 / 24 = 4.0 m.

Answer. Z = 4.0 m.

Exam traps

  • F and E are not interchangeable: F acts on pixel coordinates, E on normalized camera coordinates x̂ = K⁻¹x. Mixing them gives wrong epipolar lines.
  • Disparity is in pixels, not metres. f must also be in pixels in Z = f B / d, while B and Z are in metres.
  • Z = f B / d assumes rectified images. On an unrectified pair, compute F and rectify first, or triangulate with P1 and P2.
  • The fundamental matrix has rank 2. A full-rank eight-point result does not give a single epipole; zero the smallest singular value.
  • The epipole is the image of the other camera centre, not a 3D point and not the same thing as an epipolar line.

5.7 Structure from motion and 3D reconstruction

Structure from motion (SfM) takes a set of photographs of a still scene and estimates, at the same time, where each camera was and where each visible 3D point lies. It exists because one image gives only directions; many images from different positions turn those directions into depth by triangulation. SfM is used to build 3D models from phone photos, to localise robots and drones, and to make maps inside augmented-reality and mapping apps.

You need: 5.2 for repeatable keypoints and descriptors, and 5.6 for epipolar geometry, the essential matrix and triangulation.

From features to a reconstruction

A feature track is a list of pixel observations of one physical 3D point, one observation per image where it was seen. Good tracks are long and consistent. SfM builds them before it estimates any geometry.

  1. Detect keypoints in every image with a corner or blob detector (see 5.1) and describe each with a descriptor such as SIFT (5.2).
  2. Match descriptors between image pairs and keep only mutual nearest matches that pass a ratio test.
  3. Link pair matches into tracks: if feature a in image 1 matches b in image 2, and b matches c in image 3, then a, b, c form one track.
  4. Pick an initial pair with a wide baseline and many reliable matches. Estimate its relative pose from the essential matrix and triangulate the tracks seen in both images.
  5. For each remaining image, match its features to tracks that already have 3D points. Solve perspective-n-point (PnP) to get that camera's pose.
  6. Triangulate tracks that now appear in at least two posed images, then run bundle adjustment over all poses and points.

The loop in step 5 and step 6 repeats until no image is left or no new track is reliable. The result is a sparse reconstruction: camera poses plus a 3D point for each surviving track.

Two-view start and adding a camera

The initial pair fixes the coordinate frame. For calibrated cameras, a 3D point X projects to normalized image coordinates u = X/Z and v = Y/Z. The camera centre is the origin of its own coordinates, and Z is depth along the optical axis.

Z = B / (u₁ − u₂)

B is the baseline between two cameras that look along +Z and are separated along X; u₁ and u₂ are the horizontal normalized coordinates of the same point in camera 1 and camera 2.

This is triangulation in its simplest form. The difference u₁ − u₂ is the disparity; a small disparity means a far point, and a zero disparity means the point is at infinity for that pair. Real systems use the essential matrix and a general triangulation for arbitrary motion.

PnP (perspective-n-point) is the reverse problem: known 3D points and their 2D pixels in a new image, find the camera rotation R and translation t. The minimal case P3P uses 3 points; practical solvers use 4 or more and RANSAC to reject outliers. Once the new camera is posed, new tracks are triangulated and added to the map.

Bundle adjustment and the two kinds of reconstruction

Every measured pixel is noisy, so the first estimate drifts. Bundle adjustment refines all camera poses and all 3D points together by minimising reprojection error: the distance between where a point actually appears in an image and where the current model projects it.

E = Σᵢ Σⱼ ρ( ‖ xᵢⱼ − π(K, Rᵢ, tᵢ, Xⱼ) ‖² )

xᵢⱼ is the measured pixel of point j in image i; π projects 3D point Xⱼ through camera i with intrinsics K and pose (Rᵢ, tᵢ); ρ is a robust loss that reduces the pull of wrong matches; the sum runs over all observations.

The unknowns are the camera poses and the 3D points. Levenberg-Marquardt solves the nonlinear least-squares problem by repeated linearisation. The normal equations are sparse because one point is seen in only a few images, so a solver can handle thousands of cameras. Only a few universities teach the normal equations in detail; the exam usually asks for the cost function and the meaning of each term.

SfM itself is sparse: it reconstructs points only at tracked keypoints. Dense reconstruction, usually called multi-view stereo (MVS), takes the estimated poses and computes a depth map per pixel, then fuses the maps into a mesh or dense cloud. MVS is a separate stage; SfM gives it the camera geometry.

Worked example

Question. Two calibrated cameras look along +Z. Camera 1 is at C₁ = (0, 0, 0) m and camera 2 is at C₂ = (1, 0, 0) m. A 3D point has normalized image coordinates u₁ = 0.2 in camera 1 and u₂ = −0.1 in camera 2; both have v = 0. Find the point's X and Z coordinates in metres.

  1. Both cameras have the same orientation. In camera 1, u₁ = X/Z = 0.2 and v = Y/Z = 0, so Y = 0.
  2. Camera 2 is shifted by B = 1 m along X. In camera-2 coordinates the point is at X − 1, so u₂ = (X − 1)/Z = −0.1.
  3. Subtract the two normalized equations: u₁ − u₂ = X/Z − (X − 1)/Z = 1/Z.
  4. So Z = B/(u₁ − u₂) = 1/(0.2 − (−0.1)) = 1/0.3 = 3.333 m.
  5. Then X = u₁ Z = 0.2 × 3.333 = 0.667 m, and Y = 0 m.

Answer. The 3D point is at (0.667, 0, 3.333) m in camera-1 coordinates.

Exam traps

  • Monocular SfM has unknown scale. A reconstruction scaled by any positive factor gives exactly the same pixels, so do not quote metres unless a known baseline, an object of known size, or an IMU/GPS fixes scale.
  • Bundle adjustment minimises reprojection error in pixels, not Euclidean distance between 3D points. A model can lower pixel error while 3D error grows in weakly observed regions.
  • A narrow-baseline first pair gives unstable triangulation: small pixel noise produces a large depth error. Choose a wide baseline with many inlier matches.
  • PnP needs 3D-to-2D correspondences. Two-view epipolar geometry uses 2D-to-2D matches; do not use PnP before the 3D points exist.
  • One wrong match inside a long track can pull a 3D point away from the true surface and corrupt the initial map. Use a ratio test and RANSAC before triangulation.

5.8 Optical flow and motion estimation

Optical flow is the apparent motion of brightness patterns between two video frames. It gives a vector (u, v) at each pixel: how far that pixel seems to move in one frame interval. The idea exists because true object motion is hidden, but brightness patterns are observable. Flow drives video compression, stabilisation, tracking, and driver-assistance systems. It is not the same as the motion field, the true 3D motion projected on the image. Textures, lighting and occlusion make the two differ.

You need: image gradients and convolution from Unit 2, the local window matrix from Harris corner detection, and basic linear algebra (2×2 matrix inverse).

Brightness constancy and the gradient equation

Let I(x, y, t) be brightness at pixel (x, y) at frame time t. A small pattern moves by u pixels in x and v pixels in y in one frame. Brightness constancy assumes the pattern keeps the same brightness: I(x + u, y + v, t + 1) = I(x, y, t). A first-order Taylor expansion gives I(x, y, t) + Ixu + Iyv + It = I(x, y, t). Cancel I(x, y, t).

Ixu + Iyv + It = 0

Ix = ∂I/∂x and Iy = ∂I/∂y are spatial brightness gradients; It = ∂I/∂t is the change per frame; u and v are flow in pixels/frame.

One equation has two unknowns. At a single pixel we can only see motion along the brightness gradient. This is the aperture problem. A straight edge moved along itself looks unchanged, so motion along the edge is invisible. A corner has gradients in two directions and fixes both components.

Optical flow is not always the true motion field. A moving white wall has no texture, so flow is zero while the wall moves. Specular highlights and shadows also move differently from surfaces. Some syllabi call this motion estimation.

Lucas-Kanade local least squares

Lucas-Kanade assumes u and v are constant in a small window around the pixel. For each pixel i in the window: Ixiu + Iyiv = −Iti. Stack all rows into A d = b, where d = [u, v]T, row i of A is [Ixi, Iyi], and b is the column of −Iti.

d = (ATA)−1ATb

A holds spatial gradients in the window; b holds negative temporal gradients; d = (u, v) is the flow in pixels/frame.

ATA is the same second-moment matrix used by Harris corners (5.1). It is invertible only when the window has gradients in two directions. Flat window: no solution. Straight edge: one reliable direction. Corner: both.

  1. Compute Ix, Iy and It by finite differences between frames.
  2. For each pixel, sum Ix2, IxIy, Iy2, IxIt and IyIt over the window.
  3. Solve the 2×2 normal equations for u and v.
  4. For large motion, build an image pyramid: solve at the coarsest level, upsample the flow, and refine at finer levels.

Horn-Schunck smoothness and applications

Horn-Schunck adds a global smoothness assumption: neighbouring pixels should have similar flow. It minimises an energy with two terms.

E = Σ (Ixu + Iyv + It)2 + α2(‖∇u‖2 + ‖∇v‖2)

the first term enforces brightness constancy; α is a positive smoothness weight; ∇u and ∇v are spatial gradients of the flow field; Σ runs over all pixels.

Only a few universities teach the full variational derivation. The result is an iterative update that fills flat regions but blurs flow at object boundaries. Lucas-Kanade is local and sparse; Horn-Schunck is global and dense. Pyramidal Lucas-Kanade with tracking is common for feature points. Dense variational methods are used when every pixel is needed.

  • Video compression: MPEG stores motion vectors for blocks.
  • Camera stabilisation and frame interpolation use flow to shift pixels.
  • Driver assistance uses flow to detect approaching objects.
  • Medical imaging uses flow to measure tissue motion between scans.

Exam traps

  • Using one pixel equation for two unknowns: Ix u + Iy v + It = 0 alone cannot give both u and v unless one gradient is zero and you accept the other component as unknown.
  • Confusing optical flow with true object motion: a moving untextured wall gives zero flow, and a moving shadow gives flow where no surface moves.
  • Sign slip: from Ix u + Iy v + It = 0, a positive It with positive Ix gives negative u, not positive u.
  • Unit slip: u and v are pixels/frame; Ix is intensity per pixel, It is intensity per frame. Mixing frame steps scales the answer wrongly.
  • Using a small Lucas-Kanade window for large motion: the displacement leaves the window and the solution is wrong. Use a pyramid.

5.9 Image registration

Image registration finds a spatial transform that aligns a moving image to a fixed image. It exists because two pictures of the same scene rarely share a pixel grid: a patient moves between scans, a phone shifts between shots, or two satellites pass at different times. Registration is used in medical imaging to compare scans, in remote sensing to stack satellite images, and in vision to stitch panoramas. Some syllabi call this image alignment. The result is a transform plus an aligned image resampled on the fixed grid.

You need: geometric operations and interpolation from Unit 1, and feature matching from 5.2 Feature descriptors: SIFT and SURF.

Fixed, moving and transform types

Registration takes two images. The fixed image F is the reference and stays on its grid. The moving image M is warped by a transform T until it matches F. Direction matters: T maps coordinates in M to coordinates in F. To build the aligned image on the fixed grid we sample M at T⁻¹(x).

T(x) = A x + t,  A = [[a, b], [c, d]],  t = [t_x, t_y]ᵀ

x = (x, y) is a pixel coordinate in the moving image; T(x) is the matching coordinate in the fixed image; A is a 2×2 matrix for rotation, scale and shear; t is translation in pixels; ᵀ means transpose.

  • Translation: t only, 2 degrees of freedom (DOF) in 2D.
  • Rigid: rotation and translation, 3 DOF in 2D.
  • Affine: A and t, 6 DOF in 2D; parallel lines stay parallel.
  • Projective: homography, 8 DOF in 2D; straight lines stay straight but parallel lines may meet.
  • Nonrigid: a local deformation field with many DOF; used for soft tissue and breathing.

Feature-based registration

Feature-based registration uses a small set of matching points. It is fast and can work when the two images have different brightness. Detect keypoints, which are distinctive image points, with a detector such as Harris or SIFT. Describe each keypoint and match descriptors. See 5.1 Corner and blob detection: Harris detector.

  1. Detect keypoints in F and M; keep the strongest N points.
  2. Match descriptors; keep mutual nearest neighbours.
  3. Pick a transform type and its minimum pairs: 1 pair for translation, 2 for rigid, 3 non-collinear pairs for affine, 4 for projective.
  4. Run RANSAC: sample the minimum pairs, fit T, count inliers within a pixel threshold, repeat, then refit T on all inliers.
  5. Warp M with the final T and resample it on the fixed grid.

RANSAC is robust because each hypothesis is fitted from a minimal sample, so an outlier usually lands in a sample whose inlier count is low, and that hypothesis is discarded. The final transform is refitted only on the inlier set. The inlier threshold is in pixels. Too large a threshold admits wrong matches; too small a threshold rejects correct ones.

Intensity-based registration and evaluation

Intensity-based registration uses all pixels. It defines a cost C(θ) from F and the warped M, then searches transform parameters θ.

SSD(θ) = Σ_x [F(x) − M(T_θ⁻¹(x))]²

F(x) is fixed intensity; M(T_θ⁻¹(x)) is the moving intensity sampled at the moving coordinate that lands on fixed pixel x; θ are transform parameters; x runs over overlapping pixels. Lower SSD is better, but SSD is sensitive to brightness and modality.

NCC = Σ_x (F(x)−F̄)(M(T_θ⁻¹(x))−M̄) / √(Σ_x (F(x)−F̄)² Σ_x (M(T_θ⁻¹(x))−M̄)²)

F̄ and M̄ are means over the overlap; NCC is 1 for a perfect positive match, −1 for inverted contrast, and 0 for no linear relation.

MI(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y)

H is Shannon entropy in bits; X and Y are intensity values; joint entropy H(X,Y) uses their 2D histogram. MI is high when one image predicts the other; it works when intensities differ, as in CT and MRI.

An optimizer changes θ to minimize C(θ) or maximize similarity. Common choices are gradient descent, Powell and Nelder-Mead simplex. Bilinear interpolation gives M at non-integer coordinates. Resampling writes the aligned image W(x)=M(T_θ⁻¹(x)) on the fixed grid. Evaluate by overlap for labels: Dice = 2|P∩Q|/(|P|+|Q|), where P and Q are label masks. For landmarks, target registration error (TRE) is the mean distance between corresponding points after transform, in mm or pixels. Lower TRE is better. In hospitals, DICOM files store scan pixels and metadata; PACS stores and serves them; NIfTI is a common research file. None of these is a registration method.

Worked example

Question. Three corresponding points are moving (0,0), (1,0), (0,1) and fixed (1,1), (3,1), (1,3). Find the affine transform that maps moving to fixed in homogeneous form.

  1. Write the affine form x_f = a x_m + b y_m + c and y_f = d x_m + e y_m + f.
  2. Use the first pair (0,0) → (1,1): c = 1 and f = 1.
  3. Use the second pair (1,0) → (3,1): a + c = 3, so a = 2; d + f = 1, so d = 0.
  4. Use the third pair (0,1) → (1,3): b + c = 1, so b = 0; e + f = 3, so e = 2.
  5. Write the homogeneous matrix: [[a, b, c], [d, e, f], [0, 0, 1]] = [[2, 0, 1], [0, 2, 1], [0, 0, 1]].

Answer. The affine transform is x_f = 2x_m + 1, y_f = 2y_m + 1, with matrix [[2, 0, 1], [0, 2, 1], [0, 0, 1]], a uniform scale of 2 plus translation (1,1) pixels.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the direction: registration maps moving to fixed, so building the aligned image needs T⁻¹, not T.
  • Choosing a transform with too few DOF. A translation cannot remove rotation; an affine cannot bend soft tissue.
  • Using SSD when brightness changes or when the two images come from different modalities. Use NCC for brightness shifts and mutual information for multimodal scans.
  • Ignoring interpolation. Nearest-neighbour resampling gives jitter; bilinear interpolation is smoother but blurs fine detail.
  • Setting the RANSAC threshold badly: too large admits outliers and biases the transform; too small rejects true inliers.

Takeaways

  1. A corner is defined by change in two directions, so it needs both eigenvalues of a small matrix, not one gradient magnitude.
  2. Harris is rotation-invariant but not scale-invariant; scale space fixes that by searching over blur levels as well as over position.
  3. SIFT and SURF produce descriptors that stay close under rotation, scale change and small viewpoint change, which is what makes matching possible.
  4. HOG ignores which pixel is which inside a cell, so it tolerates small deformation; block normalization makes it robust to lighting.
  5. Bag of visual words converts a variable number of descriptors into one fixed-length histogram, which any classifier can use.
  6. The pinhole model with K, R and t is the single framework behind calibration, stereo, SfM and augmented reality.
  7. Two views of one point are not independent: the match is constrained to an epipolar line, which turns a 2D search into a 1D search.
  8. Stereo depth follows Z = f·B / d, so a small baseline or a small disparity gives a poor depth estimate.
  9. Structure from motion solves for points and poses together; bundle adjustment is what keeps the drift from piling up.
  10. Optical flow gives per-pixel motion but only the component perpendicular to an edge is locally measurable; smoothness priors supply the rest.

Detect and describe features (SIFT), match descriptors between image pairs; Filter matches with a ratio test; estimate the fundamental or essential matrix by RANSAC; keep inlier tracks; Choose an initial pair with a wide, well-conditioned baseline; triangulate its points; Resect new cameras with 3D-2D correspondences (P3P or PnP with RANSAC); Triangulate new points; repeat with bundle adjustment over growing subsets; Fix gauge freedom: overall scale, and the 7-dof global similarity; Failure modes: textureless surfaces, repeated structure, pure rotation (no baseline, no depth), drift, and error from wrong matches.

Evaluate You must align a CT scan to an MRI scan of the same patient. Discuss why intensity-difference measures are unsuitable and how mutual information solves it, and how you would evaluate the result. 13 marks

CT and MRI grey values have no fixed relationship; the same tissue can be bright in one and dark in the other; SSD/NCC assume a linear intensity relationship, so they mislead; Mutual information measures shared information: MI(A,B) = Σ p(a,b) log₂ [p(a,b) / (p(a)p(b))]; Normalized MI reduces sensitivity to overlap size; joint histogram computed over the overlapping region only; Rigid or affine transform first; optimization by Powell or gradient-based search with multi-resolution; Evaluate with target registration error on anatomical landmarks, or overlap measures such as Dice if a segmentation is available.

Glossary

Aperture problem
The ambiguity that a small window on a straight edge cannot tell motion along the edge from no motion.
Blob detector
A method that finds roughly uniform regions differing from their surroundings by locating extrema of a Laplacian-like response over both position and scale.
Bundle adjustment
Simultaneous nonlinear least-squares optimization of camera parameters and 3D point coordinates.
Corner
A point whose local intensity pattern changes in two independent directions, making it a repeatable anchor for matching.
Descriptor
A fixed-length numeric vector summarizing the neighbourhood of a keypoint so that two keypoints can be compared.
Epipolar geometry
The geometry relating two views of the same scene, captured by the fundamental or essential matrix.
Essential matrix
The 3×3 matrix E = [t]× R linking matched points whose cameras are fully calibrated.
Extrinsic parameters
The rotation and translation placing the camera in the world coordinate system.
Harris response
The scalar R = det(M) − k(trace M)² used to rank candidate corners without computing eigenvalues.
HOG
Histogram of Oriented Gradients: local gradient-direction histograms over cells, contrast-normalized over blocks.
Homography
An invertible 3×3 matrix mapping one projective plane onto another; exact for planar scenes and pure rotation.
Image registration
Estimating the spatial transformation that brings one image into geometric agreement with another.
Integral image
A precomputed running sum of pixel values so any rectangle sum costs four lookups.
Intrinsic parameters
The internal camera numbers — focal lengths in pixels, principal point, skew, distortion — that do not change when the camera moves.
Keypoint
A detected image location, often with an associated scale and orientation, that is stable across views.
Mutual information
A similarity measure based on the reduction in uncertainty about one image's intensities given the other's.
Optical flow
The apparent per-pixel velocity field between two consecutive frames of a sequence.
Pinhole camera model
An idealized camera in which every scene point projects through the optical centre onto the image plane as x = f·X/Z.
Principal point
The pixel where the optical axis meets the image plane, roughly but not exactly the image centre.
Radial distortion
Bending of straight lines near the image borders caused by the lens; modelled with coefficients k₁, k₂ in a polynomial of r.
RANSAC
Random Sample Consensus: repeatedly fit a model to a minimal random subset and keep the fit with the most inliers.
Scale space
A family of progressively blurred images indexed by σ, allowing features to be detected at their natural size.
SIFT
Scale-Invariant Feature Transform: DoG keypoint detection plus a 128-value gradient-histogram descriptor.
Structure tensor
The 2×2 windowed matrix of products of image gradients; its eigenvalues measure local variation along two directions.
SURF
Speeded-Up Robust Features: a Hessian-based detector and 64-value descriptor built with integral images for speed.
Triangulation
Recovering a 3D point from two or more image projections of it, given the camera matrices.
Visual vocabulary
The codebook of all cluster centres obtained by clustering descriptors; also called the visual dictionary.
Visual word
A representative descriptor taken as a cluster centre, analogous to a word in a text document.
VLAD
Vector of Locally Aggregated Descriptors: for each visual word, store the sum of residuals of its assigned descriptors.
