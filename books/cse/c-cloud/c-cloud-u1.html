1.1 Evolution: cluster, grid and utility computing

Cloud computing did not arrive in one step. It is the commercial end of a long habit: pool many computers so one big job can use them all, and charge each user for the share they take. Three earlier forms of that habit — cluster, grid and utility computing — each solved one part. Clusters pooled machines inside one room. Grids pooled them across organisations. Utility computing put a meter on the pool. Today the same machinery runs a ticket-booking site, a film studio's rendering farm and a bank's overnight batch jobs.

The pooling idea

One computer has a ceiling: its memory and cores are fixed, so a job needing more will never finish, however long you wait. The fix is to join many machines with a network and let one job spread across them. The joined machines form a pool, and the network is the pool's weakest link.

The first pooling was time-sharing on a mainframe. One large machine ran many programs while users at cheap terminals took turns. Each user felt they had a private computer, and the costly mainframe never sat idle while someone typed. The habit of charging for a share rather than a machine started here.

In the 1980s and 1990s, cheap personal computers and fast office networks allowed a second version: buy many ordinary machines instead of one expensive one, and join them. That is a cluster.

Three words recur. Homogeneous: the machines are of the same kind. Heterogeneous: they differ in processor, speed or operating system. Middleware: software that sits between the application and the machines and hides their differences.

Cluster computing: one owner, one room

A cluster is a group of similar computers in one place, joined by a fast local network, owned by one organisation and run by one administrator, with software that makes the group look like a single large computer. One machine in it is a node.

A cluster needs a manager. The head node accepts the job, cuts it into parts, sends each part to a worker node and collects the results. Because every node has the same hardware and operating system, the head node can predict how long a part takes and place it fairly.

The network is a LAN, a local area network: fast, cheap and private. Each node keeps its own memory. Pieces of the same job exchange data by passing messages over the LAN, which is fast enough for them to work together while they run. Some clusters add special interconnects that allow one node to reach another node's memory directly, but a plain LAN does not.

  • Rendering an animated film, one frame per node.
  • Weather, crash and structural simulation.
  • Overnight batch runs, such as a bank clearing its transactions.

The limit is fixed size. A cluster is bought, not rented. Its capacity is whatever was installed, and it idles once the big job finishes.

Grid computing: many owners, middleware

A cluster stops at the edge of its owner's budget. A grid goes past it: it joins machines of different kinds, in different organisations and cities, so a job can use capacity that would otherwise sit idle.

No single administrator runs a grid, so nothing can be assumed. Machines are heterogeneous, logins differ, and each owner has rules about who may use what. Middleware handles this: it signs a user in once, finds machines that user may use, and hides differences in hardware and operating system.

Users are grouped into a virtual organisation (VO): people from several institutions given access to a shared set of machines by agreement. Membership comes from the agreement, not from who employs you.

The resource broker is the middleware part that matches. It reads the job's needs — cores, memory, disk, time — compares them with machines on offer, and decides where each piece runs. Globus-style toolkits are the classic middleware here; only a few universities teach that layer.

The cost is distance. Sites in different cities talk over a WAN, a wide area network, which is slower and less predictable than a LAN. Sharing also means trusting other owners' machines with your data.

Utility computing, then the cloud

Utility computing means selling computing the way an electricity board sells power: you do not buy the generator, you pay for the units you consume. The provider owns the machines and records your use.

The record is a meter. Common metered quantities are server-hours (one server switched on for one hour), gigabytes stored per month, and gigabytes transferred. The bill adds quantity × rate for each item.

Billing alone is not a cloud. Two more pieces are needed. Self-service: you order capacity from a web console, with no e-mail to a salesperson. Elasticity: capacity grows when load grows and shrinks when load falls, so the meter never runs on machines nobody needs. A cloud is utility billing plus self-service plus elasticity on shared hardware; chapter 1.6 shows what exactly is rented.

The order is this. Mainframe time-sharing pooled one machine. Clusters pooled many machines for one owner. Grids pooled many owners' machines. Utility computing put a meter on the pool. The cloud added a self-service switch and automatic scaling. Each stage kept what the one before it built. This is the order in which the ideas build on each other, not a strict timeline: metered charging was talked about in the mainframe years, and grids came later as a way to reach capacity outside one owner's walls.

Worked example

Question. A ticket-booking site needs 300 servers for 5 working days each month, and 20 servers for the other 20 working days. One server costs ₹80,000 to buy, and running 300 servers costs ₹3,00,000 per month in power, cooling and staff. Renting costs ₹30 per server-hour, charged only for servers that are switched on. Compare the two options over the first 12 months and state the saving from renting.

  1. Peak server-hours per month = 300 servers × 5 days × 24 hours = 36,000 server-hours.
  2. Off-peak server-hours per month = 20 servers × 20 days × 24 hours = 9,600 server-hours.
  3. Total billed server-hours per month = 36,000 + 9,600 = 45,600 server-hours.
  4. Rented cost per month = 45,600 × ₹30 = ₹13,68,000.
  5. The ₹30 per server-hour rent is taken to include power, cooling and staff, so the rented side carries no separate running cost; the bought side pays ₹3,00,000 per month on top of the purchase price.
  6. Rented cost for 12 months = ₹13,68,000 × 12 = ₹1,64,16,000.
  7. Purchase cost once = 300 servers × ₹80,000 = ₹2,40,00,000.
  8. Running cost for 12 months = 12 × ₹3,00,000 = ₹36,00,000.
  9. Total bought cost for 12 months = ₹2,40,00,000 + ₹36,00,000 = ₹2,76,00,000.
  10. Saving from renting = ₹2,76,00,000 − ₹1,64,16,000 = ₹1,11,84,000.

Answer. Renting costs ₹1,64,16,000 over 12 months and buying costs ₹2,76,00,000, so renting saves ₹1,11,84,000 because the bought servers sit idle for 20 of the 25 working days each month.

Exam traps

  • Grid and cluster are not the same. Both spread one job over many machines, but a cluster has one owner, one LAN and similar nodes, while a grid crosses organisations and needs middleware to negotiate access.
  • Utility computing is a billing model, not a cloud. Metered pricing without self-service and elasticity is just a hosting contract with an itemised bill.
  • Middleware is not the operating system. The OS runs one machine; middleware negotiates access across machines that each keep their own OS and their own rules.
  • In the worked example, do not charge all 300 servers for the month. Only the 5 peak days carry 300; the other 20 working days carry 20.
  • Homogeneous means same kind of hardware, not same job. The head node of a cluster is still homogeneous with its workers, yet it schedules while they compute.

1.2 Parallel vs distributed computing; eras of computing

One processor cannot get much faster, so since the 1960s we buy more of them. Parallel computing puts many processors on one job and lets them share memory, so they cooperate cheaply. Distributed computing joins separate computers that share nothing but a network, so they must send messages. The cloud is the second kind — a distributed system of data-centre machines — wrapped in a business layer that rents them by the second, from an IRCTC tatkal surge to a startup's first server.

You need chapter 1.1 for the habit of pooling computers into clusters, grids and utilities.

Parallel versus distributed: the memory decides

Parallel computing is one job spread over several processors inside one machine. The processors share one memory, so a processor can leave a partial result in a variable and another processor reads it directly. That exchange costs nanoseconds. They also share one clock, one operating system and one power supply.

Distributed computing is several independent computers, each with its own memory, its own clock and often its own administrator, joined only by a network. To cooperate they send messages: a request goes out, a reply comes back. A message costs microseconds to milliseconds, thousands of times more than a memory read.

That cost forces a different design. A parallel program can rely on a lock in shared memory. A distributed program must tolerate a message that never arrives, a node that dies while the others keep running (partial failure), concurrent updates from users it does not control, and clocks that disagree by seconds. No global clock means you cannot order two events on different machines by their timestamps alone.

Flynn's four classes

Flynn classified machines by how many instruction streams and data streams they handle at once. An instruction stream is the sequence of operations a processor executes; a data stream is the set of operands those operations touch. Some syllabi call this the classification of parallel systems.

  • SISD — single instruction, single data. One core working through one array. A basic laptop.
  • SIMD — single instruction, multiple data. One add applied to 32 numbers at once. A GPU, a vector unit, a CPU's SSE lane.
  • MISD — multiple instruction, single data. Several processors act on the same data. Almost no commercial machine; systolic arrays and triple-redundant flight computers are the textbook examples.
  • MIMD — multiple instruction, multiple data. Independent cores running independent code. A multicore server, a cluster, the cloud itself.

MIMD splits again by how the processors talk. In shared memory they read and write one address space and synchronise with locks. In message passing each has private memory and calls send and receive, so the programmer must move data explicitly. The cores inside one cloud server are shared memory; the servers inside one rack are message passing. The interconnection network — the bus, the crossbar switch or the data-centre fabric — decides how fast either can be.

Amdahl's law: the serial part sets the bill

Split a job into a fraction p that can run in parallel and a fraction 1 − p that cannot: reading the input, a final merge, a sequential loop. With n processors the best possible speedup is bounded by Amdahl's law.

S = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/n)

S is the speedup (serial time ÷ parallel time, a pure ratio), p is the fraction of the work that can run in parallel (0 to 1), n is the number of processors, and 1 − p is the serial fraction. It assumes perfect load balance and zero communication cost.

As n grows, p/n shrinks to zero and S stops at the ceiling 1/(1 − p). That is the serial fraction's revenge: p = 0.95 caps you at 20×, p = 0.90 at 10×, p = 0.75 at 4×.

The same arithmetic decides what to parallelise in a cloud. Task parallelism gives different tasks to different servers — 200 product pages rendered at once. Data parallelism splits one dataset into chunks — a 500 GB log file into 200 blocks, each counted by one worker — the same shape as SIMD, one operation over many data items. The programming models for both are in Unit 5.

Eras of computing

  • Batch mainframe, 1950s–60s. One costly machine in a locked room. Jobs on punched cards, results the next morning. Processing is central and users queue.
  • Time-sharing, 1960s–70s. Terminals hang off one mainframe, each user gets a time slice and works interactively. The pool is shared and billed by connect time.
  • Personal computer, 1980s. One user per machine. Cheap and private, but idle most of the day and owned by the user.
  • Client-server and the web, 1990s. The application sits on the desktop, data and logic on a server; then the browser becomes the client and server farms grow behind it.
  • Cloud and mobile, 2000s onwards. Computing returns to a shared pool, now metered per second and elastic. The phone and browser are terminals again, and the pool belongs to someone else.

The shape is central, then decentralised, then central again — but the new centre is rented, not owned. That is why the parallel-versus-distributed distinction matters commercially: a provider amortises one distributed data centre over thousands of tenants, and a tenant gets parallelism on demand without buying a machine. Chapter 1.3 turns this into a test.

Worked example

Question. A batch job has 80% of its work that can run in parallel, so p = 0.8. Find the speedup on n = 4, n = 16 and n = 1000 processors, and state the maximum speedup any number of processors could give.

  1. Serial fraction: 1 − p = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. This part runs on one processor whatever n is.
  2. n = 4: p/n = 0.8/4 = 0.2, so the denominator is 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4 and S = 1/0.4 = 2.5.
  3. n = 16: p/n = 0.8/16 = 0.05, denominator 0.2 + 0.05 = 0.25, S = 1/0.25 = 4.0.
  4. n = 1000: p/n = 0.8/1000 = 0.0008, denominator 0.2 + 0.0008 = 0.2008, S = 1/0.2008 ≈ 4.98.
  5. Ceiling as n → ∞: S → 1/(1 − p) = 1/0.2 = 5.

Answer. Speedups of 2.5×, 4.0× and about 4.98×, with a hard ceiling of 5× however many processors are bought.

Exam traps

  • Writing n as the number of tasks or threads instead of the number of processors. Amdahl's n is processors.
  • Forgetting the ceiling 1/(1 − p) and claiming speedup keeps growing; no answer above n is possible without superlinear effects from cache, which the model ignores.
  • Answering 'many machines' for parallel versus distributed. The real differences are shared memory versus a network, and one clock versus no global clock.
  • Treating MISD as a real machine class. It is theoretical; the ones you meet are SISD, SIMD and MIMD.
  • Using total time in Amdahl's law instead of the parallel fraction p. Convert first: p = parallel time ÷ total serial time.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Amdahl's law gets its practical partner in Gustafson's law, which asks how large a problem you can finish in fixed time as processors grow rather than how fast one fixed problem runs. Real clouds are judged by the tail latency of the slowest of hundreds of parallel sub-requests, which is why providers engineer for p99 and not the mean. Modern data-centre fabrics and remote direct memory access push message costs towards memory costs, blurring the shared-memory and message-passing line. 1.3 NIST definition and essential characteristics

Cloud computing is not a thing you can point at. It is a way of selling computing, and "cloud" was a marketing word before it was a technical one. In 2011 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology published a short document, SP 800-145, that turned the word into a test: one definition, five essential characteristics, and a rule that all five must hold at the same time. Engineers, buyers and auditors still use that test to decide whether a service is really a cloud or just a rented server wearing a modern logo.

You need: the idea of pooled resources from chapter 1.1, and the difference between parallel and distributed systems from chapter 1.2.

The definition, one sentence long

The standard definition, in paraphrase, says: cloud computing is a model for enabling on-demand network access to a shared pool of configurable computing resources — networks, servers, storage, applications and services — that can be rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort or service-provider interaction.

Read it in four pieces. Model: a way of organising and selling computing, not a single product. On-demand network access: you reach the resources over a network, at the moment you ask. Shared pool of configurable resources: many customers draw from one stock of hardware that can be reshaped into whatever each needs. Rapidly provisioned and released with minimal management effort: no human at the provider has to act for you to start, and none has to act for you to stop.

The same document then lists five essential characteristics, three service models (software, platform and infrastructure as a service) and four deployment models (private, community, public and hybrid). Those two lists belong to chapter 1.6 and chapter 1.7. The characteristics decide yes or no.

Without a definition, every hosting company is a cloud. With one, a buyer or an examiner can check item by item instead of trusting an adjective.

The five essential characteristics

Each characteristic is a question you ask of the service. The names below are the standard ones.

  • On-demand self-service. You obtain computing capability — server time, storage, a database — by yourself, through a console, a form or an API, without a human at the provider. A developer creating a database at 2 a.m. from a web page is the picture to hold.
  • Broad network access. The service is reachable over the network by standard mechanisms, from ordinary clients: laptops, phones, tablets. The network may be the public internet or a private link; the test is standard access, not open-to-the-world access.
  • Resource pooling. The provider's resources are pooled to serve many customers at once, a multi-tenant model. Physical and virtual resources are assigned and reassigned as demand moves between customers. You usually do not know which machine holds your workload; you may be allowed to choose a region or a country.
  • Rapid elasticity. Capacity can be provisioned and released quickly, sometimes automatically, to match demand. To you the supply looks unlimited and available in any quantity at any time. Think of a results-day surge on an exam portal: fifty servers at 9 a.m., three by noon.
  • Measured service. Usage is metered at a level that suits the service — storage in GB-months, compute in vCPU-hours, bandwidth in GB, or API calls — and reported to both provider and customer. The meter turns usage into a bill.

Virtualisation is the machinery underneath. Pooling needs a way to slice one physical machine into many isolated pieces and hand those pieces to different customers, and elasticity needs the pieces to be created and destroyed in seconds. That machinery belongs to Unit 3; here you only need the link.

Applying the test

Test the service, not the brochure. Ask what the customer actually does and what the provider actually does.

  1. Self-service: can capacity be created with no person at the provider in the loop?
  2. Broad network access: can standard clients reach it over a network from outside one building?
  3. Pooling: are many customers served from one pool of hardware, reassigned as demand shifts between them?
  4. Elasticity: can capacity rise and fall quickly with demand, ideally without a request?
  5. Measured service: is usage metered at a fine grain, reported, and reflected in the bill?
  6. If all five fully hold, it is a cloud by this definition. If any one collapses, it is not.
score = Σ cᵢ for i = 1 to 5, with cᵢ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}

cᵢ is the credit for characteristic i: 1 if it fully holds, 0.5 if partly, 0 if not. The service is a cloud only when every cᵢ = 1, that is min(cᵢ) = 1. The sum is a convenience score out of 5, not a pass mark — four and a half out of five is still a no.

The habit to build: give a verdict, then give the characteristic that fails. "Not a cloud, because self-service and measured service fail" is an answer. "Not a cloud, it's just hosting" is a guess.

Worked example

Question. A college offers its students a login on one server in the campus data centre. The fee is a flat ₹500 per student per year. The service is reachable only from inside the campus Wi-Fi. Accounts are created by emailing the administrator. Score it against the five essential characteristics and decide whether it is a cloud.

  1. The five characteristics: self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, measured service.
  2. Self-service: accounts are created by a human reading email, so the customer cannot act alone. Credit c₁ = 0.
  3. Broad network access: reachable only from campus Wi-Fi, not from a phone on mobile data. Credit c₂ = 0.
  4. Resource pooling: one server serves many students, so hardware is shared, but there is no pool that shifts between tenants. Credit c₃ = 0.5.
  5. Rapid elasticity: the single server has fixed capacity, so a load of 60 students cannot grow to 600 within a few minutes, and no extra machines are created on demand. Credit c₄ = 0.
  6. Measured service: the fee is flat ₹500 per student per year; nothing is metered per hour, per GB or per request, and no usage report reaches the customer. Credit c₅ = 0.
  7. Total score = 0 + 0 + 0.5 + 0 + 0 = 0.5 out of 5, that is (0.5 ÷ 5) × 100 = 10 percent.
  8. Verdict: not a cloud. One partial credit and four full misses, and the definition needs all five.

Answer. Score 0.5 out of 5 (10 percent); the service is a hosted campus facility, not a cloud, because self-service, broad network access, elasticity and measured service all fail outright.

Exam traps

  • Calling any virtual machine a cloud. A VM on your laptop fails all five: no provider pool shared with other customers, no network access from standard clients, no elastic capacity, no self-service and no meter. Carving several VMs out of one machine is virtualisation; pooling begins only when those slices are handed to different customers. Recite all five, then judge.
  • Mixing up pooling and elasticity. Pooling is many tenants sharing one pool at the same moment; elasticity is the size of your slice changing over time. One is about sharing, the other about speed of growth.
  • Reading broad network access as "open to the public internet". A service behind a corporate VPN still passes, because the test is standard clients and standard mechanisms, not anonymity.
  • Listing service or deployment models as characteristics. SaaS, PaaS, IaaS and public, private, hybrid, community answer different questions; they are not part of the five.
  • Accepting four out of five as close enough. The definition is a conjunction: every characteristic must hold. Examiners award the mark for the word "all".

Elasticity must raise and lower capacity automatically, and an automatic decision needs a live measure of demand — requests per second, queue length, CPU load. Metering and monitoring usually share one stream of measurements, but the bill alone does not scale anything.

No. Broad network access asks for standard network mechanisms and standard client devices, which a bank's internal network provides. Deployment model (private) and characteristic (broad access) are separate questions; all five still apply.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. The NIST definition is a starting point, not the only vocabulary: ISO/IEC 17788 gives a wider international vocabulary for cloud computing, and newer national and regional schemes add requirements the 2011 document never imagined. On the cost side, measured service has grown into a whole practice, FinOps, that manages the meter as a budget.

1.4 Benefits, risks and challenges of cloud

Cloud computing moves spending from buying servers to renting them by the hour, and moves control from your building to a provider's. That trade is the whole chapter: the same contract that gives elasticity and global reach also gives lock-in and a bill that can move without your data moving. Use this ledger when you choose a deployment model, size a budget, or design for failure.

You need: the five essential characteristics from Chapter 1.3, so each benefit can be matched to the characteristic that produces it.

Benefits: capital cost becomes operating cost

Capital expenditure (capex) is money spent once on an asset you own: a server, a rack, a generator. Operating expenditure (opex) is money spent as you consume a service. The cloud turns the first into the second. Instead of buying a server for a peak you may never reach, you rent capacity by the hour or the month.

Elasticity removes over-provisioning. Over-provisioning means buying for the busiest hour and leaving the machine idle for the rest. A ticket site that sees a Tatkal rush at 10 a.m. can add instances for that hour and remove them after. You pay for the demand you had, not for the demand you feared.

The start is faster because there is no procurement cycle. A team can create a database and a web server in minutes, not weeks. Global reach comes from regions: a provider already runs data centres in many places, so you can place a copy near your users without building a site.

The provider also carries routine work: patching the hypervisor, replacing failed disks, keeping redundant power. Your team can spend its time on the product, not on the machine room. These gains are the benefits side of the ledger; each one creates a matching risk on the other side.

Risks and challenges: the other side of the same contract

Vendor lock-in means leaving is expensive. It grows from proprietary APIs, special data formats, and egress fees. If your code calls one provider's queue by name, moving to another provider is a rewrite, not a copy.

Data security and privacy stay your job. The provider secures the building and the hypervisor; you secure your accounts, keys and data. A shared disk is split by software, so tenant isolation must hold. Tenant isolation means one customer's virtual machine cannot read another's memory or disk.

Regulatory compliance and data location add a legal risk. Some regulators require certain data to stay inside the country. The provider may have a region there, but you must choose it and prove it in an audit.

Outages happen, and dependency on the link is real. A provider outage can stop your service; a cut in your office fibre can stop your access even when the cloud is healthy. Latency grows with distance, so a region far from users adds delay to every request.

The bill can surprise. Egress charges are payments for data leaving the provider's network; storage charges continue for data you keep but do not read. Low-level control is limited: you cannot tune the physical network or the host kernel in a public cloud. A skills gap is common: the team must learn identity, networking and cost controls that the old data centre hid.

Threat types and the benefit-to-risk ledger

Four threat types appear in almost every cloud risk register. A data breach is unauthorised access to stored data. Account hijack is stolen credentials used to run resources in your name. An insecure interface is an API or console with weak authentication or missing input checks. Denial of service is traffic that exhausts a service so real users cannot reach it. Attack vectors and controls belong to Unit 6; cost models belong to Unit 4.

Use a ledger, not a list. For every benefit, name the risk it creates and the mitigation that keeps the risk acceptable.

Worked example

Question. A startup stores 1 TB of photos in the cloud at ₹2 per GB per month and serves 3 TB of downloads each month at ₹6.50 per GB. (a) Compute the monthly bill and the share of it caused by downloads. (b) If a video goes viral and downloads double, compute the new bill.

  1. Storage volume = 1 TB = 1,024 GB. Storage cost = 1,024 GB × ₹2 per GB = ₹2,048 per month.
  2. Download volume = 3 TB = 3,072 GB. Download cost = 3,072 GB × ₹6.50 per GB = ₹19,968 per month.
  3. Total bill = ₹2,048 + ₹19,968 = ₹22,016 per month.
  4. Download share = 19,968 ÷ 22,016 = 0.906976... = 90.7% to one decimal place.
  5. If downloads double, download volume = 6 TB = 6,144 GB. New download cost = 6,144 GB × ₹6.50 per GB = ₹39,936. New total = ₹2,048 + ₹39,936 = ₹41,984 per month. Stored data is still 1 TB, so storage cost is unchanged.

Answer. The monthly bill is ₹22,016, of which 90.7% is download; if downloads double the bill becomes ₹41,984 and storage cost stays at ₹2,048.

  • Calling egress charges a security risk; they are a cost risk, and the category carries marks.
  • Saying cloud removes all management; the provider manages the layers below the service boundary, you manage above it.
  • Confusing capital cost with operating cost; capex is bought once, opex is consumed.
  • Forgetting that data location is a compliance risk, not only a latency issue.

1.5 Cloud reference model and architecture

A cloud service looks like a website until you take it apart. Taken apart, it is a stack of four layers: physical hardware at the bottom, virtualisation above it, a platform above that, and the application on top. Three bands cut across all four: security, management and governance. This stack is the structure half of the cloud reference model. It says where each part lives and who controls it — provider at the bottom, consumer at the top.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for the five essential characteristics and Chapter 1.4 for the cost-and-control trade-off.

Four layers, bottom up

Read the layers from the bottom, because each one is built on the one below it.

Physical layer. Data centres: buildings with power, cooling and racks. Inside are servers (CPU and RAM), storage devices (hard disk, SSD) and network equipment (switches, routers, links to the internet). These are fixed, owned, physical assets. Whoever owns them owns the bottom of the model.

Virtualisation layer. A hypervisor runs on a physical server and carves it into virtual machines (VMs). Each VM gets a virtual CPU, memory and disk, and cannot see the other VMs. Software-defined networking does the same to switches. This layer is what makes pooling possible: a spare slice of one server becomes a VM for a different customer.

Platform layer. Operating system, runtime (Java, Python, Node.js), middleware and databases. A VM with an OS installed sits at the top of virtualisation and the bottom of the platform. A managed database service belongs here: the provider patches it and takes backups.

Application layer. The software a person actually opens — a college portal, a UPI app, a video player.

Control shifts as you move up. The provider controls the physical and virtualisation layers and very little of the application layer. The consumer controls the application and, when buying bare infrastructure, the platform too. Chapter 1.6 shows that IaaS, PaaS and SaaS are this same stack cut at three heights.

Three bands that cut across every layer

Security, management and governance are not layers. They apply at every layer, from the server room to the web page.

Security means confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication and isolation. Physical layer: locked racks, guards, safe disk disposal. Virtualisation layer: hypervisor hardening, so that one VM cannot read another VM's memory. Platform layer: patching and key management. Application layer: login and access control, plus denial-of-service protection so the service stays available. A gap at any one layer is a gap in the whole system; Unit 6 covers this fully.

Management is the day-to-day running of the stack:

  • Resource management: provisioning VMs, allocating CPU and memory, scheduling jobs.
  • Service management: deployment, monitoring, scaling, load balancing, fault recovery.
  • SLA management: measuring uptime and response time against the agreement, and turning the meter into a bill.

Governance is the set of rules written down before any of that starts: where data may be stored, who may read it, which standards apply, how long it is kept, who audits. Governance decides what management is allowed to do.

Management and security both need a live view of the same resources, which is why they are drawn as vertical bands through the stack and not as boxes on top of it. Draw all four layers and all three bands. Four layers alone is half the answer.

Two halves of one model

The reference model has two halves. The first is the layered architecture above. The second is the actor view: the named roles — consumer, provider, broker, auditor, carrier — and the boundaries between them. That is Chapter 1.8. Keep the halves separate. If a question asks for the architecture, draw the layers and the bands. If it asks for roles, draw the actors.

The layered half works as a placement test. Take any item in a cloud design and ask two questions: which layer does it live in, and who manages it?

  1. Is it metal, disk or cable? Physical layer, provider-managed.
  2. Is it a VM, virtual disk or virtual network cut from a shared pool? Virtualisation layer; the provider runs the hypervisor, the consumer runs what is inside.
  3. Is it an OS, runtime, middleware or managed database? Platform layer.
  4. Is it what a user clicks? Application layer.
  5. Does security, management or governance touch it? For any real item, yes.

Some syllabi call this stack the 'cloud computing architectural framework'. Some call it the 'cloud computing reference model'. The diagram is the same either way.

Worked example

Question. A college runs its student portal on cloud infrastructure bought as infrastructure as a service. The design has 4 virtual machines, each with 4 vCPU and 8 GB RAM, behind 1 load balancer. It also has a managed database of 200 GB and 500 GB of object storage for lecture videos. (a) Count the items in the design. (b) Find total vCPU, total RAM and total storage. (c) Place every item on the four-layer model and state who manages it. (d) Name the bands that apply.

  1. Count the items: 4 VMs + 1 load balancer + 1 managed database + 1 object store = 7 items.
  2. Total vCPU = 4 VMs × 4 vCPU per VM = 16 vCPU.
  3. Total RAM = 4 VMs × 8 GB per VM = 32 GB.
  4. Total storage = 200 GB database + 500 GB object storage = 700 GB.
  5. Virtualisation layer: the 4 VMs sit at the virtualisation layer and the college installs and patches their guest operating systems; the load balancer is also a virtualisation-layer service, but the provider runs and patches it.
  6. Platform layer: the managed database. The provider handles patching, backup and failover; the college owns the schema and the data itself.
  7. Virtualisation layer: the 500 GB object store. The provider's raw disks are physical-layer hardware, but the bucket the college buys is a pooled, virtualised storage resource that the provider manages; the college never touches the disks.
  8. Application layer: the portal software the student opens in a browser.
  9. Bands: security, management and governance apply to all 7 items, not to one layer only.

Answer. 7 items, holding 16 vCPU, 32 GB RAM and 700 GB storage; VMs, load balancer and object storage at the virtualisation layer, database at the platform layer, with security, management and governance across all of them.

Exam traps

  • Drawing only the four layers and leaving out the security and management bands. Half the marks go with them.
  • Confusing the layered architecture with the actor view. Consumer, provider, broker, auditor and carrier belong to the reference model's role half, not to the layer diagram.
  • Putting the hypervisor in the platform layer. It belongs to virtualisation, below the operating system.
  • Thinking a managed database moves data responsibility to the provider. Patching moves; the data, the schema and the access rules stay with the consumer.
  • Treating object storage as an application feature. It is a virtualisation-layer resource: the provider pools physical disks and presents them to you as buckets, so you never touch the disks.

1.6 Service models: IaaS, PaaS and SaaS

A cloud provider sells computing at three heights of one stack. Rent the virtual machine and you run the operating system yourself: infrastructure as a service. Rent the runtime and you supply only code and settings: platform as a service. Rent the finished application in a browser: software as a service. The cut point decides three things at once — what you must manage, what you pay for, and who holds the pager when the service fails at 2 a.m. Most real systems mix all three.

You need: the layered stack from chapter 1.5, so that 'layer' and 'cut point' already mean something.

One stack, three cut points

A service model is a cut through the stack from chapter 1.5. The layers, bottom to top: hardware (servers, disks, switches in the provider's data centre), the virtual machine the hypervisor presents, the guest OS and its patches, the runtime (language runtime, web server, libraries, database engine), and the application with its data.

The provider owns and operates every layer below the cut: capacity, patching, replacing failed parts, metering. You own every layer above it. Move the cut up and you manage less, control less, and pay in a different shape — per machine-hour, per instance-month, or per user.

The layers themselves do not change between models. Only the boundary does. The same web application can run on a rented VM, on a platform, or as somebody's finished product, and moving between them is mostly a question of who patches what.

'As a service' carries one promise: the provider does the operational work at that layer and below, and bills by a meter instead of a contract.

IaaS: rent the machine

Infrastructure as a service rents you virtual machines, block and object storage, virtual networks, load balancers and public IP addresses. You pick the OS image, install packages, patch, harden, and decide when to add or remove machines.

Billing follows the resource: compute per second or per hour while the machine runs, storage per GB-month whether or not it runs, and data leaving the provider per GB. Chapter 1.8 covers the meter.

cost = hours × rate<sub>hour</sub> + GB-months × rate<sub>GB</sub> + GB<sub>out</sub> × rate<sub>out</sub>

hours is running time in hours; ratehour is the price per machine-hour; GB-months is stored volume in GB-months; rateGB is the storage price; GBout is the data that leaves the provider; rateout is the egress price per GB.

A rented virtual server is the standard picture: 4 vCPU, 16 GB RAM, a 100 GB disk, an Ubuntu image you patch yourself. Common uses: lifting an existing server into the cloud unchanged, test machines that live for a week, software whose licence is tied to an OS you must control. Virtual machines themselves are Unit 3.

PaaS: rent the runtime

Platform as a service takes code, a dependency list, environment variables and data from you. It supplies the OS, the runtime, the web server, a managed database engine if you want one, log collection, and the scaling machinery.

You push with git push or a command-line tool and get a URL. No server appears in your account. Scaling is a setting: minimum 2 instances, maximum 20, add one when average CPU passes 65 percent. The provider restarts crashed processes and rolls out OS patches beneath you.

You still manage the application: code, configuration, secrets, schema, migrations and data. 'PaaS means you manage nothing' is false. You manage everything the provider cannot know about your business.

Typical uses: a small team shipping a Python or Node web app with no operations staff, an API backend, a nightly batch job. Pricing is usually per instance-hour or per request. The constraint is that your stack must fit the runtime on offer.

SaaS: rent the application

Software as a service delivers the whole application, in a browser or over an API. You do not choose the OS, runtime or machine. You create accounts, set roles, import data, configure workflows and connect other systems.

Billing is per user per month or per seat per year, often tiered by features or storage. Webmail, online invoicing, payroll and customer relationship software are the familiar cases.

You still own the data and who may see it. Access control, retention and audit stay with you even though the code does not; Unit 6 covers identity. One running copy serves many customers, which is what makes a per-seat price possible.

The XaaS tail applies the same idea to one layer at a time: database as a service, storage as a service, desktop as a service, functions as a service (FaaS). FaaS runs one function per event, scales to zero when idle, and bills per invocation plus per 100 ms. It is XaaS sitting on top of PaaS, not a fourth classical model.

Worked example

Question. A college must give 300 students email. Option A: a rented mail service at ₹120 per student per year. Option B: run its own mail server on one virtual machine at ₹2,400 per month, plus 4 hours of administrator time per month at ₹600 per hour. Compare the annual cost of both options and state which wins and by how much.

  1. Option A, rented: 300 students × ₹120 per student per year = ₹36,000 per year.
  2. Option B, virtual machine: 12 months × ₹2,400 per month = ₹28,800 per year.
  3. Option B, administrator time: 12 months × 4 hours per month = 48 hours per year; 48 hours × ₹600 per hour = ₹28,800 per year.
  4. Option B, total: ₹28,800 (machine) + ₹28,800 (administrator) = ₹57,600 per year.
  5. Difference: ₹57,600 − ₹36,000 = ₹21,600 per year in favour of the rented service.

Answer. The rented service costs ₹36,000 per year against ₹57,600 to self-run, so software as a service wins by ₹21,600 per year, before counting the patching work the college stops doing.

  • Calling serverless a fourth classical model. It is a form of XaaS on top of platform as a service.
  • Saying that in IaaS you manage the hardware. You manage from the guest OS upward; hardware, hypervisor and network fabric stay with the provider.
  • Treating any downloaded app or plain website as SaaS. SaaS is a multitenant subscription service reached over a network.
  • Comparing a monthly machine rate with an annual per-user rate without multiplying by 12 and without counting administrator hours, which flips the answer.

1.7 Deployment models: public, private, hybrid and community

A deployment model says who owns the hardware under your workload and who else shares it. It is a separate question from the service model: infrastructure, platform and software as a service say where you cut the stack, while public, private, hybrid and community say who holds the machines and who else stands on them. Every organisation picks one whenever it decides where a workload should live: a hospital's patient records, a group of colleges' examination portal, a shop's festive-season sale traffic.

You need: the five essential characteristics from chapter 1.3 and the service-model cuts from chapter 1.6.

Public and private: who owns the pool

A public cloud is owned by a provider that sells capacity to anyone who signs up. Many customers, called tenants, share the same physical servers, and virtualization keeps them apart. You rent by the hour or by the gigabyte and pay only for what you use. The sharing is the whole point: one provider's spare capacity becomes every customer's cheap capacity.

A private cloud is a pool owned or leased by one organisation and used by that organisation alone. It can sit on the company's own premises or be hosted by a vendor on dedicated hardware. Ownership brings control: you choose the hardware, the network paths, the patch schedule and the exact building where data is stored. It also brings the bill. The machines are capital cost, paid whether they are busy or idle, and someone on your payroll must run them.

A private cloud still has to be a cloud. It needs the essential characteristics from chapter 1.3: pooling, self-service, elasticity and metering. Without them, a room of servers is traditional hosting with a newer name.

Hybrid: a private core with public capacity on tap

A hybrid cloud joins two or more distinct clouds, usually a private core and a public provider, with a secure link such as a VPN or a dedicated interconnect. The link is not decoration. The workload itself must be able to move from one side to the other.

The usual pattern is cloud bursting. Base load runs on private hardware you already own. When demand crosses a threshold, extra instances start in the public cloud, take the overflow, and shut down when the spike ends. The private side keeps the sensitive data; the public side supplies only capacity.

Bursting needs preparation: the same virtual machine image or container on both sides, the data the burst needs reachable from both sides, and a threshold that triggers scale-out. The service models of chapter 1.6 do not change. Only the owner of the hardware changes.

Community, virtual private and multi-cloud

A community cloud is shared by several organisations with a common concern: a group of colleges pooling an examination portal, or hospitals under one regulator pooling a records exchange. Members split the cost and the governance. It is cheaper than each member building a private cloud and more controlled than a public one, but the hard part is human: who operates it, who pays, and whose service level agreement wins.

A virtual private cloud (VPC) is a private slice carved out of public hardware: your own subnets, routing and firewalls inside a provider's network. It gives isolation, not ownership. The hardware is still shared and still the provider's, so a VPC is not a private cloud.

Multi-cloud means using two or more public providers, for lock-in cover or for a region the first provider does not serve. Federated and intercloud arrangements, where separate clouds trade resources across providers, appear in only a few syllabi; see Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. A hospital chain runs a private data centre that serves 300 requests per second. Its appointment site normally handles 200 requests per second, but during a 3-day health camp it must handle 2,000 requests per second. Buying 10 extra servers to cover that peak costs ₹80,000 each. Renting 10 servers in a public cloud costs ₹30 per server-hour. Find (a) the capital cost of covering the peak in-house, (b) the cost of bursting for the 3 days, (c) the saving, and (d) the bursting cost over 3 years if the camp happens once a year.

  1. Peak shortfall = 2,000 − 300 = 1,700 requests per second. Ten servers carry this, so each server carries 1,700 ÷ 10 = 170 requests per second.
  2. Capital cost of 10 servers = 10 × ₹80,000 = ₹8,00,000. These servers would sit idle for 362 days a year.
  3. Burst duration = 3 days × 24 hours per day = 72 hours.
  4. Burst cost = 10 servers × 72 hours × ₹30 per server-hour = ₹21,600.
  5. Saving = ₹8,00,000 − ₹21,600 = ₹7,78,400.
  6. Over 3 years the camp bursts 3 times: 3 × ₹21,600 = ₹64,800, still far below the one-time ₹8,00,000.

Answer. Bursting costs ₹21,600 against ₹8,00,000 of capital, a saving of ₹7,78,400; three years of bursting costs ₹64,800.

Exam traps

  • Calling any server room a private cloud. It still needs pooling, self-service, elasticity and metering; without them it is traditional hosting.
  • Mixing the deployment model with the service model. 'We buy SaaS, so we are on a public cloud' does not follow, because a SaaS product can run in a private or community cloud.
  • Treating hybrid as any two clouds. The parts must be joined by a secure link and a real workload must move between them.
  • Believing a VPC is a private cloud. Isolation is not ownership; the hardware stays shared and provider-owned.
  • Forgetting that a community cloud has closed membership and shared governance. It is not a public cloud with a discount.

1.8 Roles, boundaries and cloud economics

Cloud work is done by named actors, not by an unnamed 'cloud'. A consumer buys a service; a provider owns the shared pool; a broker aggregates and resells; an auditor checks logs and compliance independently; a carrier supplies the network link. Around them sit boundaries: who legally owns the data, who can see or change it, and who manages which layer. Under them sits a meter that turns usage into money. These roles, boundaries and the bill decide what a cloud service really is.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for cost risks, Chapter 1.5 for the actor view, and Chapter 1.6 for the responsibility boundary.

Five roles around one pool

Every cloud transaction has at least two actors. The consumer uses the service and pays for it. The provider owns and operates the physical pool: servers, disks, switches and the software that divides them. A broker sits between many providers and one consumer; it aggregates catalogues and resells, often adding a portal or a billing layer. An auditor checks the provider independently: logs, controls and compliance evidence, without running the service. A carrier supplies the connection from the consumer's network to the provider's edge; it does not own the compute pool. The broker and auditor are the two roles most often left out of answers. The figure in this chapter leaves the roles aside and follows the meter: it builds one month of the worked example’s bill, line by line. The same person can be consumer and provider in a private cloud, but the roles stay separate in an answer because each carries different duties.

Three boundaries

An organisational boundary marks who legally owns the data and who is accountable for it. Moving data to a provider does not transfer ownership; a regulator or a customer still looks to the consumer. A trust boundary marks what one party can see and change. The provider's staff may operate the hypervisor but should not read the consumer's files; encryption and access control draw this line. The responsibility boundary is the cut from chapter 1.6: below it the provider patches and monitors, above it the consumer patches and monitors. In IaaS the cut is low; in SaaS it is high. These boundaries are different questions. A data set can be legally owned by the consumer, inside the provider's trust boundary, while the provider manages the storage layer. An answer that says 'the cloud provider is responsible for security' has collapsed all three boundaries into one.

The meter and the contract

Cloud spending changes shape. Capital expense (capex) is money spent before use: buying servers, racks and cooling. Operating expense (opex) is money spent as the service runs: hourly compute, storage and requests. Cloud moves capex to opex, which helps cash flow but can raise the total if use is steady for years. Economies of scale mean a large provider buys hardware, power and staff more cheaply per unit than a small team can; part of that saving is passed to the consumer. Metering makes pay-per-use possible. Common meters are per second or per hour of a virtual machine, per GB-month of storage, and per million requests or API calls. On-demand pricing has no commitment and follows the meter. Reserved pricing asks for a one- or three-year commitment in return for a discount; it suits a base load that will not disappear. Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds everything: the bill, staff time, migration, training, network egress and the cost of leaving. Price is only the provider's line on the bill; cost is the whole owner's ledger. An SLA is the contract that names the service, its measurable targets and the penalties. An SLO is one target inside it. For example, 99.9% availability in a 30-day month allows at most 43 minutes of downtime.

D = (1 − A) × T

D is allowed downtime, A is the availability target as a fraction, and T is the period in the same time unit. With A = 0.999 and T = 43,200 minutes (30 days), D = 43.2 minutes.

  • Compute: virtual machines or containers, billed by time.
  • Storage: object buckets or block volumes, billed by GB-month.
  • Managed database: the provider runs the engine, billed by instance and storage.
  • OpenStack: open-source private-pool stack with compute, object storage and networking.

See Unit 5 for product names.

Worked example

Question. A team runs 4 virtual machines at ₹6 per VM-hour, 24 hours a day, through a 30-day month. It keeps 500 GB of storage at ₹2 per GB per month and serves 20 million requests at ₹0.5 per million requests. (a) Find the on-demand monthly bill. (b) A one-year reserved contract takes 40% off compute; find the new bill and the monthly and yearly saving.

  1. Compute hours = 4 VMs × 24 hours/day × 30 days = 2,880 VM-hours.
  2. On-demand compute = 2,880 VM-hours × ₹6/VM-hour = ₹17,280.
  3. Storage = 500 GB × ₹2/GB-month = ₹1,000.
  4. Requests = 20 million × ₹0.5/million = ₹10.
  5. On-demand total = ₹17,280 + ₹1,000 + ₹10 = ₹18,290 per month.
  6. Reserved compute = ₹17,280 × 0.60 = ₹10,368.
  7. Reserved total = ₹10,368 + ₹1,000 + ₹10 = ₹11,378 per month.
  8. Monthly saving = ₹18,290 − ₹11,378 = ₹6,912; yearly saving = ₹6,912 × 12 = ₹82,944.

1.9 New paradigms: edge computing and mobile cloud computing

Cloud computing pools machines in a few large data centres. Some jobs cannot wait for that distance: a toll-gate camera, a phone running a speech model, a factory sensor. Edge computing moves computation to a small node beside the data; mobile cloud computing moves it off the phone to a nearby or remote server. Both give up some of the cloud's capacity in return for lower delay and less bandwidth used. Both appear in video surveillance, number-plate reading, augmented reality and factory control. The data centre still runs long jobs, training and archives.

You need: the layered architecture of Chapter 1.5 and the deployment models of Chapter 1.7.

Edge computing: the node beside the data

Edge computing places computation close to where data is produced instead of a distant data centre. The reason is delay. Light in glass travels at about 2 × 108 m/s, so 1,000 km of fibre costs at least 5 ms one way and 10 ms round trip before any device works. Routing, queues and a mobile radio add more. A toll-gate camera talking to a cloud region 1,000 km away commonly sees 60 ms round trip, while the service may allow 50 ms.

An edge node is a small server in the same street, factory or building as the sensors. Typical: a few CPU cores, 8–16 GB memory, sometimes a small accelerator, 30–100 W, no air-conditioned room, no technician on site. Size does not make it edge. It is edge when it sits next to the data source and is managed from the cloud as one fleet: same software image pushed to thousands of nodes, health and error reports flowing back, bad update rolled back from a central console.

That second condition is what exams test. A server in your basement, patched by hand, is a local server, not an edge node.

Fog: the layer in between

Between sensors and data centre there is usually a middle layer. Fog computing is the name for nodes at regional level: a telecom aggregation site or ISP point of presence, tens of kilometres away, bigger than an edge node and smaller than a cloud region. Only a few syllabi use the word fog; two lines are enough.

Each tier trades delay for capacity. A camera sees about 5 ms to an edge node in the same street, 25 ms to a regional fog node in the same state, and 60 ms to a cloud data centre. Run the job at the lowest tier whose total time fits the deadline and whose operators may see the data.

T_total = T_net + T_comp

T_total is the time from the sensor producing a frame to the answer arriving, in ms; T_net is the round-trip network delay between sensor and tier, in ms; T_comp is the model's compute time on that tier, in ms.

Mobile cloud computing: offloading from the phone

Mobile cloud computing (MCC) is the phone, radio network and cloud working as one system: heavy work leaves the phone and only the result comes back. A phone has a small battery and a processor that must stay cool, so a large model run locally for two minutes drains charge and heats the case. The same work on a server uses a fraction of the phone's energy.

The offloading decision compares three quantities: energy, delay and bandwidth. Local execution costs energy = CPU power × time. Offloading costs the radio energy for bytes sent up and result sent down, plus energy of staying awake while waiting. Offload when radio cost plus waiting is less than local cost and the round trip fits what the user will tolerate.

A cloudlet is a small data centre one wireless hop from the phone — same campus, mall or office — so the round trip is a few milliseconds instead of tens. It makes offloading worthwhile indoors.

Four things make MCC hard. Links drop, and a train entering a tunnel kills an offload in flight. Batteries vary. Phones differ in chip and operating system. Mobile data is sold per gigabyte, so an offload that saves battery can cost money on a metered plan; on Wi-Fi the decision may flip.

Where each paradigm wins

  • Edge wins when deadline is a few milliseconds, data is bulky (video), backhaul is expensive or absent, or data must stay on site for privacy.
  • Cloud wins for long jobs, model training, archives and bursty load, because it can hold thousands of machines and cheap storage and is paid by the hour.
  • Usual pattern: train the model in the cloud, push it to the edge fleet, send alerts and logs back to the cloud.

The edge is a thin layer with limited power and no technician. The cloud does not disappear. Its role shifts to the later stages — collecting logs, retraining the model, keeping archives — while the first stage of the job runs on the edge.

Worked example

Question. A city runs 1,000 toll-gate cameras, each producing a 2 MB frame twice a second. The number-plate model needs 20 ms of compute. It can run on the camera (90 ms end to end), in a cloud region with 60 ms round trip, or on an edge node 5 ms away. The service level requires an answer within 50 ms. (a) Which placement meets the deadline, and by how much? (b) What backhaul does sending every frame to the cloud need? (c) If only one 50 KB alert per camera every 10 seconds leaves the edge node, what backhaul is needed, and by what factor does it fall?

  1. Deadline = 50 ms.
  2. On camera: 90 ms. 90 > 50, fails.
  3. Cloud: T_total = 60 + 20 = 80 ms > 50, fails.
  4. Edge: T_total = 5 + 20 = 25 ms ≤ 50, passes with 25 ms spare.
  5. Every frame: 1,000 × 2 = 2,000 frames/s. 2,000 × 2 MB = 4,000 MB/s = 4 GB/s. In bits: 4,000 × 8 = 32,000 Mbit/s = 32 Gbit/s.
  6. Only alerts: 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100 alerts/s. 100 × 50 KB = 5,000 KB/s = 5 MB/s = 40 Mbit/s.
  7. Factor: 32,000 ÷ 40 = 800.

Answer. Only the edge node meets 50 ms, at 25 ms with 25 ms spare; every frame needs 32 Gbit/s, only alerts need 40 Mbit/s, 800 times less.

Exam traps

  • Calling any nearby server 'edge'. A node is edge only if it is close to the data source and managed from the cloud as one fleet. Being small proves nothing.
  • Treating MCC as a website opened on a phone. MCC is offloading of computation; a responsive web page is not offloading.
  • Unit slips in backhaul: MB/s to Mbit/s is × 8, Mbit to Gbit is ÷ 1,000. So 4 GB/s is 32 Gbit/s, not 4 Gbit/s.
  • Forgetting compute time, or counting the round trip twice. Use T_total = T_net + T_comp, where T_net is already a round trip.
  • Claiming edge replaces the cloud. Edge nodes have 30–100 W and no technician; training, archives and long jobs stay in the data centre.

Takeaways

  1. Cloud computing is the commercial form of a long habit: pool many computers, share them, and charge each user by the meter.
  2. Clusters, grids and utility computing each solved one piece; the cloud added self-service and billing to make the pool sellable.
  3. Parallel computing shares data cheaply but is limited by one machine; distributed computing spans machines but pays a network cost for every message.
  4. The NIST definition is a test, not a slogan: all five essential characteristics must hold at once.
  5. Every cloud benefit has a matching risk on the other side of the ledger: elasticity brings bill shock, pooling brings shared-fate risk, self-service brings sprawl.
  6. The reference model is the same stack cut at three heights, and the cut point decides what you rent, manage and pay for.
  7. Deployment model answers who owns and who shares the hardware; service model answers what is being sold.
  8. A meter turns usage into money, and the SLA is the contract that says how honestly the meter must behave.
  9. When delay or connectivity is the binding limit, computation walks to the edge or onto a nearby cloudlet instead of a distant data centre.

Cloud infrastructure used by a single organisation, operated by it or by a third party.
Public cloud
Cloud infrastructure owned by a provider and shared by many unrelated tenants over the internet.
SaaS
A service that delivers a complete application to users, usually through a browser, with the provider managing the whole stack.
SLA
A service level agreement, the contract that fixes the expected service levels, their measurement and the penalties for missing them.
SLO
A service level objective, a measurable target such as availability or response time that the provider aims to meet.
Total cost of ownership
The complete lifetime cost of an option, including purchase, power, staff, maintenance and exit.
Utility computing
Supplying computing as a metered service paid per use, in the way electricity is billed.
Virtualisation
Using software to create many virtual machines, networks or storages over fewer physical resources.
