2.1 Logic gates: basic, universal and exclusive-OR gates

A logic gate is a small electronic circuit with one or more binary inputs and one binary output, and the output depends only on the present inputs. Three gates, AND, OR and NOT, are enough to build any digital function. NAND and NOR are called universal because each of them alone can replace all three, which is why real chips are built mostly from them. Exclusive-OR (XOR) is a fourth family that answers the question "are these inputs different?", and it sits inside every adder, comparator and parity circuit you will meet in Unit 3.

The three basic gates

Every gate is defined by its truth table: the list of outputs for every combination of inputs. For a 2-input gate that is four rows. Inputs are written A and B, the output F, and 1 and 0 stand for the two voltage levels (in positive logic, the higher voltage is 1).

  • AND: F = A·B (written AB). F is 1 only when every input is 1. Think of two switches in series: the lamp lights only if both are closed.
  • OR: F = A + B. F is 1 when at least one input is 1. Two switches in parallel.
  • NOT (inverter): F = A′ (also written Ā). The single input is flipped: 0 becomes 1, 1 becomes 0. The small circle on the symbol, called a bubble, always means inversion.

AND and OR gates come with 2, 3, 4 or 8 inputs; the definition does not change. The symbols in Figure 2.1.1 are the ANSI/IEEE "distinctive shape" symbols used in Indian exam papers; the rectangular IEC symbols (a box with &, ≥1 or =1 inside) mean the same thing.

Universal gates: NAND and NOR

NAND is NOT-AND: F = (AB)′, so the output is 0 only when all inputs are 1. NOR is NOT-OR: F = (A + B)′, so the output is 1 only when all inputs are 0. A gate is called universal if the three basic operations can be built from it alone. Both qualify:

  • NOT from NAND: tie both inputs together. (A·A)′ = A′. One gate.
  • AND from NAND: a NAND followed by that inverter. ((AB)′)′ = AB. Two gates.
  • OR from NAND: invert each input, then NAND. (A′·B′)′ = A + B by De Morgan's law (Chapter 2). Three gates.
  • NOT, OR and AND from NOR follow the same pattern with the roles of AND and OR swapped: (A + A)′ = A′; ((A + B)′)′ = A + B; (A′ + B′)′ = AB.

Manufacturers love NAND and NOR because in CMOS each is a single compact stage (a NAND2 is four transistors), while a CMOS AND gate is a NAND plus an inverter. The classic TTL parts are the 7400 (four 2-input NANDs in one 14-pin package), 7402 (NOR), 7404 (six inverters), 7408 (AND), 7432 (OR) and 7486 (XOR). Some syllabi list "Implementation of logic gates" and "Integrated circuits" here; the transistor-level view is in Unit 6, Logic families.

Exclusive-OR and exclusive-NOR

XOR gives 1 when the inputs are different: A ⊕ B = A′B + AB′. XNOR (also called the equivalence gate) is its complement, (A ⊕ B)′ = AB + A′B′, giving 1 when the inputs are the same. Three facts are examined again and again:

  • With more than two inputs, XOR is an odd function: the output is 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1. That is exactly a parity generator (Unit 3). XNOR of many inputs is the even function.
  • XOR is controlled inversion: A ⊕ 0 = A and A ⊕ 1 = A′. An XOR with one input tied to a control line passes or inverts the other input, which is how an adder is turned into a subtractor.
  • Useful identities: A ⊕ A = 0, A ⊕ A′ = 1, A ⊕ B = B ⊕ A, and A ⊕ B′ = A′ ⊕ B = (A ⊕ B)′.

XOR is also the gate behind Gray-code conversion (Unit 1) and the half adder's sum output (Unit 3).

Exam traps

  • NAND is not "NOT applied to each input then AND". (AB)′ = A′ + B′, not A′B′. Students who draw the bubble on the inputs instead of the output get the wrong truth table.
  • A 3-input NAND gives 0 only for input 111; it is not two 2-input NANDs chained. ((AB)′C)′ ≠ (ABC)′. Check with A=B=0, C=1: chained gives 0, true NAND gives 1.
  • XOR of three inputs is 1 for 001, 010, 100 and 111 (odd count), not "exactly one input 1".
  • "Universal" means the basic gates can be built from it, not that it is the fastest or the most common. The usual 2-mark question: name the universal gates and justify with one construction each.
  • Count gates carefully in "minimum number of NAND gates" questions: XOR needs 4, XNOR needs 5 (NAND) or 4 (NOR), an inverter needs 1.

Understand How does a 4-input XOR behave, and what circuit does that make it? 3 marks

Its output is 1 when an odd number of the four inputs are 1 (the odd function), because each ⊕ flips the running result for every 1. Apply Find the output of a 3-input NAND gate and a 3-input XOR gate for the input ABC = 101. 3 marks

NAND: (1·0·1)′ = 0′ = 1. XOR: 1 ⊕ 0 ⊕ 1 = (1 ⊕ 0) ⊕ 1 = 1 ⊕ 1 = 0 (two 1s is an even count). So NAND = 1, XOR = 0.

Analyze A student builds AND from NOR as "NOR followed by NOR-inverter". Is the circuit correct? If not, fix it with the fewest gates. 5 marks

No. NOR then inverter gives ((A + B)′)′ = A + B, which is OR. For AND you must invert the inputs first: AB = (A′ + B′)′. So use two NOR-inverters (one per input) feeding a third NOR: 3 gates in total. Check A=1, B=0: inverters give 0 and 1; NOR(0,1) = 0 = 1·0.

2.2 Boolean algebra: postulates, theorems and De Morgan's laws

Boolean algebra is ordinary algebra on the set {0, 1} with two operations, AND (·) and OR (+), and one unary operation, complement (′). George Boole published the idea in 1854 as a way of calculating with logic; Claude Shannon showed in 1937 that it describes switching circuits exactly. Its value for you is practical: an expression with fewer literals is a circuit with fewer gates, and the postulates and theorems are the legal moves that get you there. De Morgan's laws are the moves that let you swap AND for OR, which is what converts a circuit to NAND or NOR form.

You need: the gate definitions of Chapter 2.1.

Huntington's postulates

The algebra is defined by a short list of postulates given by E. V. Huntington in 1904; everything else is a theorem proved from them. For variables x, y, z in {0, 1}:

  1. Closure: x + y and x·y are again in {0, 1}.
  2. Identity elements: x + 0 = x and x·1 = x.
  3. Commutative: x + y = y + x and x·y = y·x.
  4. Distributive: x·(y + z) = x·y + x·z and also x + y·z = (x + y)·(x + z). The second one has no counterpart in school algebra.
  5. Complement: for every x there is x′ with x + x′ = 1 and x·x′ = 0.
  6. There are at least two distinct elements, 0 ≠ 1.

Duality. Every postulate comes in a pair: swap + with · and 0 with 1 and you get the other member. So any theorem you prove has a dual that is automatically true. This halves the memorising: learn x + 1 = 1 and you get x·0 = 0 free.

Two proofs show the style. Absorption: x + xy = x·1 + xy = x(1 + y) = x·1 = x (identity, distributive, null, identity). Simplification: x + x′y = (x + x′)(x + y) = 1·(x + y) = x + y, using the second distributive law. When a proof by postulates is hard, a truth table of all 2ⁿ rows is always an accepted proof, and for the consensus theorem it is the faster one.

Order of operations: complement first, then AND, then OR, with brackets overriding. So AB + C′D means (A·B) + ((C)′·D). A bar over a group, written here as (AB)′, is a bracket as well as a complement.

De Morgan's laws

The two laws say that the complement of an OR is the AND of the complements, and the complement of an AND is the OR of the complements. They hold for any number of variables and for whole sub-expressions:

(A + B + C)′ = A′·B′·C′
(A·B·C)′     = A′ + B′ + C′
(AB + C′D)′  = (AB)′·(C′D)′ = (A′ + B′)(C + D′)

The practical reading is the bubble rule in Figure 2.2.1: a NAND gate is the same hardware as an OR gate with inverted inputs, and a NOR gate is an AND gate with inverted inputs. Chapter 8 uses this to push bubbles through a circuit until only NAND or only NOR gates remain.

To complement a whole function, there is a shortcut that follows from De Morgan and duality: take the dual of the expression and complement every literal. For F = x′yz′ + x′y′z the dual is (x′ + y + z′)(x′ + y′ + z); complementing literals gives F′ = (x + y′ + z)(x + y + z′).

Worked example

Question. (a) Simplify F = AB + A′C + BC to the minimum number of literals using Boolean theorems, naming each step. (b) Find the complement of G = A(B′C + BC′) and write it as a sum of products. (8 marks)

Solution (a). The term BC is the consensus of AB and A′C (one term contains A, the other A′, and BC is what is left when A is removed). Prove it rather than just quote it:

F = AB + A′C + BC
  = AB + A′C + BC·1                 identity
  = AB + A′C + BC(A + A′)           complement: A + A′ = 1
  = AB + A′C + ABC + A′BC           distributive
  = AB(1 + C) + A′C(1 + B)          group and factor
  = AB + A′C                        null: 1 + C = 1, 1 + B = 1

Six literals became four. F = AB + A′C. Check one row: A=0, B=1, C=1 gives the original 0 + 1 + 1 = 1 and the simplified 0 + 1 = 1.

Solution (b). Apply De Morgan from the outside in:

G′ = [A(B′C + BC′)]′
   = A′ + (B′C + BC′)′                  De Morgan on the AND
   = A′ + (B′C)′·(BC′)′                 De Morgan on the OR
   = A′ + (B + C′)(B′ + C)              De Morgan on each product
   = A′ + BB′ + BC + C′B′ + C′C         multiply out
   = A′ + BC + B′C′                     BB′ = 0, CC′ = 0

G′ = A′ + BC + B′C′. Sanity check with A=1, B=1, C=0: G = 1·(0 + 1) = 1, so G′ should be 0; the formula gives 0 + 0 + 0 = 0. (The inner bracket B′C + BC′ is B ⊕ C, so G′ = A′ + (B ⊕ C)′ = A′ + B ⊙ C, the XNOR, which is the same thing.)

Exam traps

  • (A + B)′ is not A′ + B′. De Morgan changes the operator every time it removes a bar: (A + B)′ = A′B′.
  • Breaking a long bar: (AB′ + C)′ = (AB′)′·C′ = (A′ + B)·C′. Students forget the inner bar and write (A′ + B′)C′.
  • The second distributive law A + BC = (A + B)(A + C) is true in Boolean algebra and false in ordinary algebra. Use it; examiners set "prove x + x′y = x + y" to test it.
  • Dual ≠ complement. The dual of x + y′ is x·y′ (no change to literals); the complement is x′·y. The complement is the dual with every literal complemented.
  • "Simplify using theorems" needs the theorem named at each step; an answer that jumps straight to the K-map result loses the method marks.

Analyze A friend simplifies AB + A′C + BC to AB + A′C and claims the BC term was "wrong". Correct the claim. 5 marks

BC was not wrong, it was redundant: by the consensus theorem BC is already covered. Whenever B=C=1, either A=1 (so AB=1) or A=0 (so A′C=1), so BC never adds a 1 that the other terms lack. Both expressions are the same function; the shorter one costs one 2-input AND gate less. In a real circuit the "redundant" BC term is sometimes kept on purpose to remove a static hazard (Unit 5).

2.3 Boolean functions and truth tables

A Boolean function takes n binary inputs and gives one binary output. It can be written as an algebraic expression, listed as a truth table, or drawn as a gate circuit, and the three are the same object seen three ways. The expression is convenient to manipulate, the circuit is what you build, but only the truth table is unique: two expressions that look different are the same function exactly when their truth tables match. This chapter teaches the conversions between the three forms, which is the skill every later chapter leans on.

You need: gates (2.1) and the theorems of 2.2.

Functions, literals and cost

A literal is a variable or its complement: x and x′ are two different literals of one variable. A term is a product (or sum) of literals, and an expression is a sum of product terms or a product of sum terms. The usual measure of the cost of an expression is its number of literals and terms, because in a two-level circuit each term is one gate and each literal is one gate input. F = x′y + xz has 4 literals and 2 terms: two AND gates with two inputs each, one 2-input OR, and one inverter for x′.

How many different functions of n variables exist? A truth table has 2ⁿ rows and each row can hold 0 or 1 independently, so there are 2 to the power 2ⁿ functions: 16 for two variables, 256 for three, 65 536 for four. Each of those functions has infinitely many expressions (add a redundant term and you have another), which is why minimisation is a real problem.

Building the truth table from an expression

  1. List the input variables in a fixed order and write all 2ⁿ combinations as a binary count from 0 to 2ⁿ − 1. For three variables: 000, 001, …, 111. Row number = the decimal value of the row.
  2. Add one column per sub-expression (each product term, each complement) and fill them row by row.
  3. Combine the columns with the top-level operator. For a sum of products, F is 1 in a row if any product column is 1.

Figure 2.3.1 does this for F = x′y + xz and then draws the circuit straight from the expression: one gate per operator, one inverter per complemented literal, inputs fanning out to every gate that needs them. The reverse direction, from a circuit to a table, is the same thing done gate by gate from the inputs toward the output.

Reading a function back from the table, and its complement

Each row where F = 1 can be written as a product term that is 1 only in that row: write the variable if the bit is 1 and its complement if the bit is 0. Row 2 (010) gives x′yz′, row 5 (101) gives xy′z. OR-ing those terms gives an expression for F, and Chapter 4 names it the canonical sum of minterms. The rows where F = 0 give F′ in the same way. So the truth table is also the quickest route to a complement: F′ for the function above is 1 in rows 0, 1, 4, 6, so F′ = x′y′z′ + x′y′z + xy′z′ + xyz′ = x′y′ + xz′ after grouping.

The same table proves equivalence. If a colleague claims G = (x′ + z)(x + y) is the same function, you do not argue: fill its column and compare. Row 1 (001): (1 + 1)(0 + 0) = 0; row 2 (010): (1 + 0)(0 + 1) = 1; and so on. All eight rows agree, so G = F, and the algebra confirms it: (x′ + z)(x + y) = x′y + xz + yz = x′y + xz by consensus.

Exam traps

  • Keep the variable order fixed (A B C, or x y z) and count rows in binary. A table with rows in a random order is marked wrong even if every row is right, because minterm numbers depend on it.
  • A product term for a 1-row uses the complement for each 0 bit: row 101 is AB′C, not A′BA′. Writing the row as the literal pattern "1 0 1" without complements is the commonest slip.
  • "Show that two expressions are equal" by truth table needs all 2ⁿ rows, not a few sample rows.
  • The number of functions of n variables is 2 to the power 2ⁿ, not 2ⁿ. For n = 3 that is 256, not 8.
  • Count literals, not variables, when a question asks for the cost: xy′ + x′y has 4 literals and 2 variables.

Understand Why is the truth table called the unique representation of a function while the expression is not? 3 marks

The table lists the output for every input combination, so two functions with the same table behave identically on every input and are therefore the same function. An expression is one of infinitely many ways to compute that table (x′y + xz, (x′ + z)(x + y) and x′y + xz + yz all give the same table), so an expression identifies a function but a function does not fix the expression.

Understand Explain how the complement of a function is read from its truth table. 3 marks

F′ is 1 exactly in the rows where F is 0. Take each 0-row of F, write its product term (variable for a 1 bit, complement for a 0 bit) and OR them; that sum is F′. For F = x′y + xz, the 0-rows 0, 1, 4, 6 give F′ = x′y′ + xz′.

Apply Draw the truth table of F = (A + B′)C and write F as a sum of product terms from the table. 4 marks

F is 1 when C = 1 and (A = 1 or B = 0): rows 001 (A′B′C), 101 (AB′C), 111 (ABC). All other rows 0. F = A′B′C + AB′C + ABC = B′C + AC.

Apply Determine whether F₁ = xy + x′z and F₂ = (x + z)(x′ + y) are the same function. 4 marks

Expand F₂ = xx′ + xy + x′z + yz = xy + x′z + yz = xy + x′z (consensus). So F₂ = F₁. Alternatively compare truth tables: both are 1 in rows 1, 3, 6, 7 of xyz.

Analyze A circuit has 3 inputs and is said to output 1 in "exactly four" rows. A second designer says their circuit for the same spec outputs 1 in five rows. Can both be correct implementations of one function? What single test settles it? 4 marks

No. The number of 1-rows is a property of the function, not of the circuit, so two correct circuits must have identical 8-row truth tables. Build the table for each circuit (or simulate all 8 inputs); the first row that differs proves at least one circuit is wrong against the specification.

2.4 Canonical forms: SOP, POS, minterms and maxterms

A canonical form is a standard way of writing a function so that every function has exactly one such expression. There are two: the sum of minterms (canonical SOP) and the product of maxterms (canonical POS). They are long, never minimal, but they are the bridge between a truth table and algebra, and the shorthand F = Σm(1, 4, 5, 6, 7) is how exam questions hand you a function for a K-map or a Quine-McCluskey table. Learn to move in all directions: table ↔ Σm ↔ ΠM ↔ expression.

You need: truth tables and row numbering from Chapter 2.3.

Minterms and maxterms

For n variables, a minterm mᵢ is a product of all n literals that is 1 in exactly one row of the truth table, row i. Write a variable un-complemented where the row has a 1 and complemented where it has a 0: for A B C = 1 0 1, m₅ = AB′C. A maxterm Mᵢ is a sum of all n literals that is 0 in exactly one row, row i, built with the opposite rule: a variable complemented where the row has a 1. For row 5, M₅ = A′ + B + C′. The two are complements of each other, mᵢ′ = Mᵢ, which is De Morgan's law applied to the product. There are 2ⁿ minterms and 2ⁿ maxterms; Figure 2.4.1 lists all eight of each for three variables.

The two canonical forms

Sum of minterms. Since mᵢ is 1 only in row i, OR-ing the minterms of all rows where F = 1 gives a function that is 1 exactly in those rows: that is F. Shorthand: F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 4, 5, 6, 7) means F = m₁ + m₄ + m₅ + m₆ + m₇. Syllabi word it as "canonical and standard forms" or "SOP, POS canonical logic forms".

Product of maxterms. Since Mᵢ is 0 only in row i, AND-ing the maxterms of all rows where F = 0 gives a function that is 0 exactly there: again F. Shorthand: F = ΠM(0, 2, 3) means F = M₀·M₂·M₃.

The two lists are complementary: every row index is in exactly one of them. Converting between the forms is therefore trivial: swap the symbol and take the missing indices. Σm(1, 4, 5, 6, 7) ↔ ΠM(0, 2, 3) for a 3-variable function. The complement F′ uses the other list with the same symbol: F′ = Σm(0, 2, 3) = ΠM(1, 4, 5, 6, 7).

From any expression to canonical form

Two routes. The truth table route: fill the table, read the 1-rows for Σm and the 0-rows for ΠM. The algebraic route, which examiners like because it shows the theorems at work:

  1. For SOP: write the expression as a sum of products. For each product that is missing a variable x, multiply it by (x + x′) = 1 and expand. Repeat until every term has all n literals. Remove duplicates (x + x = x).
  2. For POS: write the expression as a product of sums (use the distributive law x + yz = (x + y)(x + z) to get there). For each sum missing a variable x, add x·x′ = 0 and expand with the same law. Remove duplicates.

Both routes give the same answer; use the table when the expression is messy and the algebra when the expression is short.

Standard forms are the practical cousins: a sum of products (SOP) or product of sums (POS) in which terms may have fewer than n literals. x′y + xz is a standard SOP; it is not canonical. Any standard SOP is a two-level AND-OR circuit and any standard POS is a two-level OR-AND circuit; these are what minimisation produces and what Chapter 8 converts to NAND or NOR.

Worked example

Question. Express F = A + B′C (a) as a sum of minterms and (b) as a product of maxterms, both by algebra. Then (c) express G = (A + B)(B′ + C) as a product of maxterms and a sum of minterms. (8 marks)

Solution (a). The term A lacks B and C; B′C lacks A.

A    = A(B + B′)(C + C′) = ABC + ABC′ + AB′C + AB′C′     → m₇, m₆, m₅, m₄
B′C  = (A + A′)B′C       = AB′C + A′B′C                   → m₅, m₁
F    = m₁ + m₄ + m₅ + m₆ + m₇   (m₅ appeared twice; keep once)
F    = Σm(1, 4, 5, 6, 7)

Solution (b). First make a product of sums with the second distributive law: A + B′C = (A + B′)(A + C). Each sum lacks one variable:

A + B′ = A + B′ + CC′ = (A + B′ + C)(A + B′ + C′)   → M₂, M₃
A + C  = A + C + BB′  = (A + B + C)(A + B′ + C)     → M₀, M₂
F      = M₀ · M₂ · M₃ = ΠM(0, 2, 3)

Check: {1, 4, 5, 6, 7} and {0, 2, 3} together are all eight indices. F = Σm(1,4,5,6,7) = ΠM(0,2,3).

Solution (c). G is already a product of sums. A + B lacks C: (A + B + C)(A + B + C′) = M₀·M₁. B′ + C lacks A: (A + B′ + C)(A′ + B′ + C) = M₂·M₆. So G = ΠM(0, 1, 2, 6), and the remaining indices give G = Σm(3, 4, 5, 7). Verify one: m₄ = AB′C′ means A=1, B=0, C=0, and G = (1 + 0)(1 + 0) = 1. Correct.

Exam traps

  • Minterm rule: 1 → variable, 0 → complement. Maxterm rule is the opposite: 1 → complement, 0 → variable. M₅ for 101 is A′ + B + C′, not A + B′ + C.
  • Σm uses the rows where F = 1; ΠM uses the rows where F = 0. Writing ΠM with the 1-rows gives F′, not F.
  • The index depends on variable order. F(A, B, C) = Σm(1) is A′B′C; the same function with variables named (C, B, A) has a different index. State the order.
  • "Canonical" means every term has all the variables. xy + z is standard SOP, not canonical SOP; a question that asks for the canonical form expects the expansion.
  • When expanding with (x + x′), a term can appear twice (m₅ above). Count it once; the idempotent law is the justification.

2.5 Karnaugh map simplification up to five variables

A Karnaugh map (K-map, Maurice Karnaugh, 1953, refining Veitch's chart of 1952) is the truth table redrawn as a grid in which cells that differ in exactly one variable sit next to each other. Two adjacent 1s merge into one term with that variable removed, four into a term with two removed, and so on, so minimisation becomes a drawing exercise: circle the largest possible blocks of 1s, write a term per block. It is the standard exam method for 2 to 4 variables, and two stacked maps handle 5. Every syllabus examines it; some go to five variables, others stop at four.

You need: minterm numbering from Chapter 2.4; Gray code from Unit 1, Binary codes.

The map and its adjacency

A map for n variables has 2ⁿ cells, one per minterm. Rows and columns are labelled in Gray code order (00, 01, 11, 10), not binary order, so that one step in any direction changes exactly one variable. Because 10 is also one step from 00, the left and right edges are adjacent, and so are the top and bottom: the map is a torus, and the four corners of a 4-variable map form a block. The 2-variable map is 2 × 2; the 3-variable map is 2 rows (A) by 4 columns (BC); the 4-variable map is 4 × 4 with AB on rows and CD on columns. Learn the cell numbering of Figure 2.5.1 by heart.

A rectangular block of 2ᵏ adjacent 1s (1 × 2, 2 × 2, 1 × 4, 2 × 4, 4 × 4, wrap-around allowed) is an implicant; its term keeps the variables that stay constant over the block, and k variables are eliminated. A block that cannot be enlarged is a prime implicant (PI). A PI that covers a 1 no other PI covers is essential and must appear in the answer.

The procedure

  1. Draw the map and enter a 1 in every minterm of the function.
  2. Find every prime implicant, largest blocks first (16, 8, 4, 2). Blocks may overlap and may wrap around edges.
  3. Select the essential PIs: a 1 covered by only one PI makes that PI essential.
  4. Cover the remaining 1s with as few further PIs as possible, preferring larger ones.
  5. Write one product term per block: a variable that is 1 throughout appears plain, 0 throughout appears complemented, one that changes is dropped. OR the terms.

For a product of sums, group the 0s instead: their blocks give F′ as an SOP, and De Morgan turns it into F as a POS. The exam may ask for either; sometimes the POS is the cheaper circuit.

Five variables: two maps, one above the other

For F(A, B, C, D, E), draw two 4-variable maps of B, C, D, E: one for A = 0 holding minterms 0 to 15, one for A = 1 holding 16 to 31 in the same pattern. Think of the second map as lying on top of the first: a cell is adjacent to its usual four neighbours and to the cell in the same position of the other map, because those minterms differ only in A. So a block is either a group in one map (A fixed) or the same group in both (A eliminated). Some Indian textbooks (A. Anand Kumar, R. P. Jain) draw one 4 × 8 map with columns CDE = 000 001 011 010 110 111 101 100 instead; there the extra adjacency is a mirror about the centre line. Same map, different drawing.

Worked example

Question. Simplify F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 10) (a) as a sum of products and (b) as a product of sums, using a K-map. (8 marks)

Solution (a). Fill the map (Figure 2.5.1). Prime implicants: the four corners 0, 2, 8, 10 (B = 0 and D = 0 throughout, A and C vary) give B′D′; the block 0, 1, 8, 9 (left two columns of the top and bottom rows, wrapping) gives B′C′; the pair 1, 5 (column CD = 01, rows 00 and 01) gives A′C′D. The pairs 0, 1 and 8, 9 lie inside the B′C′ block, so they are not prime. Essential check: cell 2 is covered only by B′D′, cell 9 only by B′C′, cell 5 only by A′C′D. All three are essential and together cover every 1. F = B′D′ + B′C′ + A′C′D (8 literals, down from 28 in the canonical form).

Solution (b). Group the 0s: cells 3, 4, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15. Blocks: 12, 13, 14, 15 (row AB = 11) → AB; 3, 7, 11, 15 (column CD = 11) → CD; 4, 6, 12, 14 (B = 1, D = 0) → BD′. So F′ = AB + CD + BD′ and, by De Morgan, F = (A′ + B′)(C′ + D′)(B′ + D). Both answers are correct; the POS has 6 literals against 8, so it is the cheaper circuit.

Worked example 2 (five variables)

Question. Minimise F(A, B, C, D, E) = Σm(0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 13, 21, 23, 25, 29, 31). (8 marks)

Solution. Minterms 0 to 15 go in the A = 0 map; 16 to 31 go in the A = 1 map at position minterm − 16 (21 → 5, 23 → 7, 25 → 9, 29 → 13, 31 → 15), as in Figure 2.5.2.

  1. A = 0 map: cells 0, 2, 4, 6 are the two outer columns of the top two rows (left–right wrap). Constant A = 0, B = 0, E = 0 → A′B′E′. The same block in the A = 1 map (16, 18, 20, 22) is not all 1s, so A stays.
  2. Cells 9, 13 (column DE = 01, rows 10 and 11) are a pair in the A = 0 map and 25, 29 the same pair in the A = 1 map: one block of four across both maps with B = 1, D = 0, E = 1 constant → BD′E.
  3. A = 1 map: cells 21, 23, 29, 31 form a 2 × 2 block (rows 01, 11; columns 01, 11) with A = 1, C = 1, E = 1 → ACE.
  4. Every 1 is covered and each block is essential (0 only by the first, 9 only by the second, 23 only by the third).

F = A′B′E′ + BD′E + ACE. Check minterm 29 = 11101: BD′E = 1·1·1 = 1. Good.

Exam traps

  • Column order is 00 01 11 10, never 00 01 10 11. A map in binary order gives wrong groups that look right.
  • Block sizes are powers of two only. Three 1s in a row are two overlapping pairs, never a "group of three".
  • Wrap-around: the four corners, the left and right columns, the top and bottom rows are adjacent. Missing the corner block is the most common lost mark in 4-variable questions.
  • Never add a block whose 1s are all already covered: a redundant term is marked "not minimal".
  • In the two-map layout, a block is adjacent to the same block in the other map, not to a mirror image (the mirror rule belongs to the 4 × 8 layout).
  • For POS, complement every literal of each 0-block term: F′ = AB gives the factor (A′ + B′), not (A + B).

2.6 Don't care conditions and incompletely specified functions

Some input combinations never happen, or when they happen nobody looks at the output. A BCD digit uses only the codes 0000 to 1001; the six codes 1010 to 1111 never arrive, so a circuit may output anything for them. Such a combination is a don't care, written X (or d, or Φ) in the truth table, and a function with don't cares is incompletely specified. The point for minimisation is simple and valuable: you may treat each X as a 1 or a 0, whichever makes a block bigger, and the result is often a much cheaper circuit than the fully specified one.

You need: K-map grouping from Chapter 2.5; BCD from Unit 1, Binary codes.

Where don't cares come from

  • Impossible inputs. BCD codes 10 to 15; a 3-bit input that only ever counts 0 to 5; a one-hot control word where two lines are never high together.
  • Unobserved outputs. A seven-segment decoder output when the display is blanked; a data line while an enable is low.
  • Free choices in a specification. "If both buttons are pressed the system may do either."

The specification is written F(w, x, y, z) = Σm(1, 3, 7, 11, 15) + Σd(0, 2, 5): minterms where F must be 1, plus don't-care minterms. All other minterms must be 0. In the POS version, F = ΠM(…)·Πd(…), with the same meaning.

Using X in a K-map

  1. Enter 1s for the required minterms and X for each don't care; leave the rest 0.
  2. Form blocks of 1s, treating any X as a 1 whenever that lets a block grow (a pair becomes a quad, a quad an octet).
  3. Do not form a block that contains only Xs: it would add a term that covers no required minterm and only costs gates.
  4. Xs that no chosen block uses are automatically 0 in the final circuit. Write out which Xs became 1 if the question asks what the circuit does for the unused codes.

The same freedom works for POS: when grouping the 0s, treat an X as 0 wherever that enlarges a block. Because the choices can differ, the SOP and POS answers from one map can describe different (equally valid) completions of the function. That is not an error; the specification allowed it.

Once a block is chosen, the circuit's behaviour on the don't cares is fixed. In Figure 2.6.1 the term w makes the output 1 for every unused BCD code 10 to 15. If a faulty input of 1100 ever appeared, the circuit would say "≥ 5". Designers who need a defined response for illegal codes (for safety, or for testing) give those rows real values instead of X.

Question. Simplify F(w, x, y, z) = Σm(1, 3, 7, 11, 15) + Σd(0, 2, 5). Give two different minimal expressions and state, for each, the value the circuit gives to the don't-care minterms. (8 marks)

  • An X is not a 1. Never count it as a required minterm, and never form a block made of Xs only.
  • Two correct answers may differ. If the examiner's key says yz + w′z and you wrote yz + w′x′, you are still right; state which Xs you took as 1 to make that clear.
  • Don't cares work for POS too: treat X as 0 when grouping 0s. Students forget and leave the Xs unused in the POS half of a question.
  • The notation Σd(…) or Σφ(…) lists don't cares; ΣX in some papers. Σm lists the required 1s. Do not merge the two lists in the K-map without marking them differently.
  • Check the required minterms after simplification, not the don't cares. A term that covers all the Xs but misses minterm 1 is wrong.

2.7 Quine-McCluskey tabulation method

The Quine-McCluskey (QM) method, published by W. V. Quine in 1952 and made mechanical by E. J. McCluskey in 1956, does exactly what a K-map does, finding all prime implicants and then choosing a minimal cover, but as a table procedure that works for any number of variables and that a program can run. You use it in the exam for six or more variables, when a question says "tabulation method", and as the proof that your K-map answer really is minimal. Some syllabi list it by name; others examine it under "minimisation".

You need: minterm numbering (2.4) and the ideas of prime and essential implicants (2.5).

The idea

Two minterms combine when their binary codes differ in exactly one bit: 0001 and 0011 give 00−1, where the dash means "this variable dropped out" (x′y′z + x′yz = x′z). Two such combined terms combine again if their dashes are in the same positions and they differ in one more bit: 00−1 and 01−1 give 0−−1. A term that cannot combine with anything is a prime implicant. Because two codes that differ in one bit always have numbers of 1s that differ by one, we only ever need to compare adjacent groups, which is what makes the method fast enough by hand.

The procedure

  1. Group. Write every minterm (and every don't-care minterm, if any) in binary and sort them into groups by the number of 1s: group 0, group 1, group 2, …
  2. Combine. Compare each term of group k with each term of group k + 1. If they differ in exactly one bit, write the combined term in the next column with a dash in that position and tick (✓) both originals. A term may be ticked many times. Keep combined terms grouped by their number of 1s too.
  3. Repeat on the new column, combining only terms whose dashes are in the same positions. Write each new term once even if it arises twice. Stop when no combinations remain.
  4. Prime implicants are all the unticked terms in all columns. Translate each to a product: 1 → variable, 0 → complement, dash → absent.
  5. Prime implicant chart. Rows are PIs, columns are the required minterms (not the don't cares). Mark × where a PI covers a minterm.
  6. Essential PIs. A column with a single × makes that row essential. Select it and strike out every column it covers.
  7. Cover the rest. Choose the fewest remaining PIs (prefer those with fewer literals) that cover all remaining columns. For hard cases Petrick's method writes the covering condition as a Boolean product of sums and expands it; in the exam the remaining columns are few and inspection is enough.

Worked example

Question. Minimise F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(0, 1, 2, 8, 10, 11, 14, 15) by the Quine-McCluskey method. (10 marks)

Don't cares and multiple outputs

Don't-care minterms go into the grouping and combining steps (they help terms combine, exactly like Xs in a map) but are left out of the chart's columns, because they need not be covered. A PI made only of don't cares will then show an empty row and is discarded. For several output functions sharing inputs (some syllabi list "minimisation of multiple output functions"), the method is run on the products of the outputs' minterm sets so that terms usable by two outputs can be shared between them; the exam version is to minimise each output separately and then spot common terms.

Exam traps

  • Compare only adjacent groups (k with k + 1). Comparing group 1 with group 3 wastes time and, worse, invites combining codes that differ in two bits.
  • Two terms combine only if their dashes are in the same positions. 00−0 and 000− do not combine even though they look close.
  • A ticked term is not a PI, however many times it was ticked. Collect PIs from the unticked terms of every column, not only the last column.
  • Write a combined quad once. It appears from two different pairs; listing it twice produces a duplicate row in the chart.
  • Don't cares appear in the tables, never as chart columns. Putting them in the chart forces you to cover minterms that need not be covered.
  • After choosing the essential PIs, strike every column they cover before choosing more; students pick an extra PI for a column that was already covered.

Check yourself

Remember What does a dash mean in the term 1−01, and what product term is it? 2 marks

The dash marks a variable eliminated by combining two minterms that differed there. 1−01 with variables ABCD is AC′D.

Remember Why are minterms grouped by their number of 1s? 2 marks

Two codes that differ in exactly one bit have 1-counts differing by exactly one, so candidates for combination lie only in adjacent groups. Grouping cuts the number of comparisons from every pair to adjacent pairs.

Understand Compare the K-map and the Quine-McCluskey method: when is each preferred? 3 marks

Both find prime implicants and then a minimal cover. The K-map is faster by hand for 2 to 5 variables because adjacency is visible, but it relies on the eye and becomes error-prone beyond five. QM is a fixed procedure that handles any number of variables, finds all PIs exhaustively and can be programmed, at the cost of more writing. Use the map in the exam for ≤ 5 variables, QM when told to or for more variables.

Understand How is an essential prime implicant found in the PI chart? 2 marks

Look for a column (minterm) that has only one ×. The PI in that row is the only one covering that minterm, so it is essential and must be in the cover.

Apply Find the prime implicants of F(A, B, C) = Σm(1, 3, 5, 7) by tabulation and write the minimal F. 4 marks

Groups: 1 (001); 3 (011), 5 (101); 7 (111). Pairs: 1,3 = 0−1; 1,5 = −01; 3,7 = −11; 5,7 = 1−1. Quads: (0−1)+(1−1) = −−1; (−01)+(−11) = −−1, same term. Only −−1 is unticked: one PI, C. F = C.

Apply For F(A, B, C, D) = Σm(1, 5, 6, 12, 13, 14) the PIs are A′C′D (1,5), BC′D (5,13), BCD′ (6,14), ABC′ (12,13) and ABD′ (12,14). Use a chart to find the minimal cover. 4 marks

Column 1: only A′C′D → essential (covers 1, 5). Column 6: only BCD′ → essential (covers 6, 14). Remaining columns 12 and 13: ABC′ covers both in one term (ABD′ would cover only 12, BC′D only 13). F = A′C′D + BCD′ + ABC′.

Analyze A student's chart has no column with a single ×, and every PI covers two of four remaining minterms. How should the cover be chosen, and why is this the hard case? 5 marks

With no essential PI, inspection or Petrick's method is needed: for each column write the sum of the PIs that cover it, AND the sums, expand to a sum of products, and each product is a valid cover; pick the one with the fewest PIs, then fewest literals. This is a set-cover problem, which is NP-hard in general, so no shortcut guarantees the minimum; it is the step where the method stops being mechanical and where heuristics such as espresso make approximations for large functions. Such functions typically have two different minimal answers (as in Chapter 2.5's 3-variable example), and both earn full marks if the cover is complete.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Exact QM is exponential: the number of prime implicants of an n-variable function can grow roughly as 3ⁿ/n, and the cover step is NP-hard, so real tools use heuristics. Espresso (1980s; later espresso-exact and the ABC system) works on cubes and iterates expand, reduce and irredundant steps to approach a minimal cover without enumerating every PI; it is still what synthesis tools run on two-level blocks and PLA-style structures. Writing a QM minimiser in Python is a classic second-year project and a good way to own the method: search for "Quine-McCluskey Python implementation", "Petrick's method" and "espresso logic minimizer".

2.8 Multilevel NAND and NOR implementations

A minimised expression is an AND-OR drawing, but the chips on the bench and the cells in a CMOS library are NAND and NOR. Converting is mechanical once you trust De Morgan: a two-level sum of products becomes NAND-NAND by inserting pairs of bubbles, and a product of sums becomes NOR-NOR the same way. Multilevel expressions, which are factored to save gates (A(CD + B) + BC′ instead of ACD + AB + BC′), convert by the same bubble rules with one extra check: every bubble that is not cancelled by another on the same wire costs an inverter. Some syllabi name this topic directly; others examine it as "implementation using universal gates".

You need: De Morgan's bubble rule (2.2) and minimised SOP/POS forms (2.5).

Two-level: SOP → NAND-NAND, POS → NOR-NOR

Take F = AB + CD, an AND-OR circuit. Put two bubbles on each wire between an AND output and the OR input (Figure 2.8.1); two inversions cancel, so F is unchanged. Now read the bubbles with the gates they touch: an AND with an output bubble is a NAND, and an OR with bubbles on all its inputs is, by De Morgan, also a NAND. So AND-OR = NAND-NAND, and algebraically F = ((AB)′·(CD)′)′. Any literal that enters the OR level directly (a single-literal term such as F = AB + C) must be complemented first, because it meets an input bubble with nothing to cancel it.

The dual works for a product of sums: F = (A + B)(C + D) is OR-AND; double bubbles between the ORs and the AND give OR-with-output-bubble (NOR) feeding AND-with-input-bubbles (NOR). So OR-AND = NOR-NOR, F = ((A + B)′ + (C + D)′)′. Single literals entering the AND level are complemented. The rule of thumb: SOP → NAND, POS → NOR; if the question insists on the other gate, add an inverter at the output of the natural form (NAND-NAND then NOT = AND-OR-invert, which is F′ in NOR-friendly form) or convert the expression to the other canonical shape first.

Multilevel circuits: the general procedure

  1. Draw the AND-OR (or OR-AND) circuit of the expression exactly as it is factored, with as many levels as the brackets need.
  2. For a NAND implementation, convert every AND to a NAND drawn as AND-invert (bubble on the output) and every OR to a NAND drawn as invert-OR (bubbles on all inputs). For NOR, convert every OR to OR-invert and every AND to invert-AND.
  3. Walk every wire. A wire with two bubbles (one at each end) is fine, they cancel. A wire with one bubble changes the signal: if it is an input literal, use the complemented literal (an inverter, or the complement if it is available); if it is an internal wire, insert an inverter (one more NAND or NOR with tied inputs).
  4. Count gates: NANDs (or NORs) plus inverters. Redraw with the standard symbol if asked.

A faster algebraic route for exams: apply De Morgan to the expression until every operation is of the form (…)′ around a product (for NAND) or around a sum (for NOR). For NAND, F = A(CD + B) + BC′ becomes F = [ (A·(CD + B))′ · (BC′)′ ]′ and CD + B = ((CD)′·B′)′; each (·)′ is one NAND gate and each primed input literal is an inverter.

Worked example

Question. Implement F = A(CD + B) + BC′ using NAND gates only, and count the gates. (8 marks)

Solution. The AND-OR circuit has three levels: G1 = CD (AND); G2 = G1 + B (OR); G3 = A·G2 (AND); G4 = B·C′ (AND); F = G3 + G4 (OR). Convert:

  1. G1 → NAND: N1 = (CD)′, output bubble.
  2. G2 → invert-OR: bubbles on both inputs. The bubble on the G1 wire cancels N1's output bubble. The bubble on the B wire is alone, so feed B′: N2 = (N1·B′)′ = N1′ + B = CD + B. Correct.
  3. G3 → NAND: N3 = (A·N2)′. G4 → NAND: N4 = (B·C′)′ (C′ needs an inverter; B enters an AND-invert input, no bubble, so plain B).
  4. F → invert-OR with input bubbles that cancel N3's and N4's output bubbles: F = (N3·N4)′ = N3′ + N4′ = A·N2 + BC′ = A(CD + B) + BC′. Correct.

Gate count: five 2-input NAND gates (N1 to N4 and F) plus two inverters for B′ and C′, each a NAND with its inputs tied. 7 NAND gates (5 if the complemented inputs B′ and C′ are available). Dry run for A = 1, B = 0, C = 1, D = 1: N1 = (1·1)′ = 0; N2 = (0·1)′ = 1; N3 = (1·1)′ = 0; N4 = (0·0)′ = 1; F = (0·1)′ = 1. Direct: A(CD + B) + BC′ = 1·(1 + 0) + 0 = 1. Match.

Worked example 2 (NOR)

Question. Implement F = (AB′ + A′B)(C + D′) with NOR gates only. (8 marks)

Solution. OR-AND structure: P = AB′ (AND), Q = A′B (AND), X = P + Q (OR), Y = C + D′ (OR), F = X·Y (AND). For NOR: ORs become OR-invert, ANDs become invert-AND (bubbles on inputs).

  1. P → invert-AND: inputs A and B′ get bubbles, so feed A′ and B: N1 = (A′ + B)′ = AB′. Q likewise: N2 = (A + B′)′ = A′B.
  2. X → NOR: N3 = (N1 + N2)′ = (AB′ + A′B)′. N1 and N2 have no output bubble and N3 has no input bubbles: nothing to fix.
  3. Y → NOR: N4 = (C + D′)′, with D′ from an inverter.
  4. F → invert-AND: its input bubbles cancel the output bubbles of N3 and N4. F = (N3 + N4)′ = N3′·N4′ = (AB′ + A′B)(C + D′). Correct.

5 NOR gates plus 3 inverters (A′, B′, D′), 8 NOR gates in all. The inner bracket AB′ + A′B is A ⊕ B, so a 7486 XOR chip would replace N1, N2, N3 and two inverters in practice.

Exam traps

  • SOP goes to NAND-NAND, POS goes to NOR-NOR. Forcing an SOP into NOR gates without first converting the expression doubles the gate count.
  • A single literal feeding the second level must be complemented: F = AB + C in NAND-NAND is ((AB)′·C′)′, so C enters through an inverter (or as C′). Forgetting it is the most common error.
  • In multilevel conversion, check every wire for uncancelled bubbles, including the input literals of invert-OR gates. One missed bubble gives F′ of a sub-expression and a wrong truth table.
  • "Minimum number of gates" questions count inverters. Say whether complemented inputs are assumed available; examiners usually expect you to count the inverters.
  • The invert-OR symbol (OR with input bubbles) is a NAND gate; do not add a NAND after it. Likewise invert-AND is a NOR.

Check yourself

Remember Which gate implements a sum of products directly in two levels, and which implements a product of sums? 2 marks

NAND-NAND implements SOP (AND-OR); NOR-NOR implements POS (OR-AND).

Remember Draw (describe) the two symbols for a NAND gate. 2 marks

AND-invert: an AND shape with a bubble on the output. Invert-OR: an OR shape with a bubble on every input. Both represent (AB)′ = A′ + B′.

Understand Why does inserting two bubbles on a wire not change the function, and why is that the key to NAND conversion? 3 marks

Two bubbles are two inversions, (x′)′ = x, so the signal arriving is unchanged. Attaching one bubble to the driving AND gate makes it a NAND, and attaching the other to the receiving OR gate's input (with the same done on its other inputs) makes that OR an invert-OR, which is also a NAND. The whole circuit becomes NAND gates without altering F.

Understand Explain why F = AB + C needs an inverter in NAND-NAND form while F = AB + CD does not. 3 marks

In NAND-NAND the second-level NAND inverts each input. The terms AB and CD arrive already inverted by their first-level NANDs, so the two inversions cancel. The lone literal C arrives uninverted, so it must be complemented first: F = ((AB)′·C′)′. One inverter (or the available C′).

Apply Implement F = (A + B)(C′ + D) using NOR gates only and count them. 4 marks

POS, so NOR-NOR: N1 = (A + B)′, N2 = (C′ + D)′, F = (N1 + N2)′ = (A + B)(C′ + D). Three NOR gates plus one inverter for C′: 4 NOR gates.

Apply Implement F = x′y + xz with NAND gates and write the NAND expression. 4 marks

N1 = (x′·y)′, N2 = (x·z)′, F = (N1·N2)′ = x′y + xz. Three NAND gates plus one inverter for x′: 4 NAND gates. (Figure 2.3.1's AND-OR circuit with the two bubble pairs added.)

Analyze A student converts F = A(B + C) to NAND gates as N1 = (B·C)′, F = (A·N1)′ and says "two gates". Find the error and give the correct circuit. 5 marks

The OR gate B + C was replaced by a NAND of B and C, which computes B′ + C′, not B + C. The OR must become an invert-OR, whose input bubbles are uncancelled on the literal wires, so it needs B′ and C′: N1 = (B′·C′)′ = B + C. Then F = (A·N1)′ gives (A(B + C))′, which is F′; a final inverter is needed, or restructure: F = AB + AC = ((AB)′·(AC)′)′ with three NANDs. Correct count: 3 NAND gates with no inverters. Proof the student is wrong: their circuit computes (A·(BC)′)′ = A′ + BC; for A = 1, B = 1, C = 0 it gives 0 + 0 = 0, while F = 1·(1 + 0) = 1.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Industrial technology mapping is this chapter at scale: the synthesis tool breaks a design into 2-input NANDs and inverters (a "subject graph"), then covers it with library cells, where an AOI21 cell (AND-OR-invert) implements (AB + C)′ in one compact stage, so a factored multilevel form is often cheaper than NAND-NAND. In CMOS the inverting gates (NAND, NOR, AOI, OAI) are the natural ones and a non-inverting AND is always a NAND plus an inverter, which is why bubble-pushing matters to delay as well as to gate count. Search for "technology mapping DAG covering", "AOI OAI standard cells" and "logical effort NAND vs NOR" to see why designers prefer NAND in CMOS.

Takeaways

  1. NAND and NOR are universal; XOR is the odd function and a controlled inverter.
  2. Every Boolean theorem has a dual; De Morgan swaps AND for OR when a bar is broken, and the bubble rule is De Morgan drawn as gates.
  3. The truth table is the unique description of a function; expressions and circuits are many.
  4. Minterm rule: 1 → variable, 0 → complement. Maxterm rule is the opposite. Σm and ΠM of one function use complementary index sets.
  5. A K-map block of 2ᵏ cells drops k variables; find all prime implicants, take the essential ones, cover the rest with as few as possible.
  6. Edges wrap: the four corners are a block. Columns go 00 01 11 10.
  7. Don't cares are free 1s (or 0s); use them to enlarge blocks, never to make a block of their own.
  8. Quine-McCluskey = group by 1-count, combine adjacent groups, unticked terms are PIs, chart, essentials, cover.
  9. SOP → NAND-NAND, POS → NOR-NOR; a lone literal at the second level needs an inverter; in multilevel circuits every uncancelled bubble costs one.
  10. Count literals and gates, including inverters, when a question says "minimum".

Outline: non-essential PIs can be chosen in different ways to cover the remaining 1s (e.g. Σm(0,2,3,4,5,7): A′C′ + AB′ + BC or B′C′ + AC + A′B). In the QM chart no column has a single ×, so no PI is essential and two covers of equal size exist; Petrick's method lists both.

13. A design uses the don't cares of BCD to minimise a seven-segment decoder. Evaluate the risks when the input can come from a faulty counter, and propose how to keep most of the saving while making illegal codes safe. (8 marks)

Outline: illegal codes 10–15 produce arbitrary segment patterns; risk of misleading display. Fix: detect illegal codes with w(x + y) (one AND, one OR) and gate the segments, or blank the display; or keep don't cares only for segments where safety does not matter. Cost comparison in gates.

14. Compare the costs of implementing F = AB + CD + E as AND-OR, NAND-NAND and NOR-NOR, counting gates and inverters, and recommend one for a CMOS library. (8 marks)

Outline: AND-OR: 2 ANDs + 1 three-input OR = 3 gates; NAND-NAND: 2 NANDs + 1 three-input NAND + inverter for E = 4; NOR-NOR requires POS first: F = (A + C + E)(A + D + E)(B + C + E)(B + D + E) → 4 NORs + 1 four-input NOR = 5 gates. Recommend NAND-NAND (CMOS NAND is compact and fast; AND is NAND + inverter anyway).

Glossary

Literal
A variable or its complement (A or A′).
Truth table
List of the output for all 2ⁿ input combinations; the unique description of a function.
Universal gate
A gate from which NOT, AND and OR can all be built: NAND or NOR.
Bubble
The small circle on a gate symbol meaning inversion; two on one wire cancel.
Exclusive-OR
A ⊕ B = A′B + AB′; 1 when inputs differ; the odd function for many inputs.
Exclusive-NOR
Complement of XOR, also called equivalence; 1 when inputs are equal.
Postulate
An axiom of Boolean algebra (Huntington's list); theorems are proved from them.
Duality
Interchanging + with · and 0 with 1 in a valid identity gives another valid identity.
De Morgan's laws
(x + y)′ = x′y′ and (xy)′ = x′ + y′; the complement of an OR is the AND of complements.
Consensus
The term yz in xy + x′z + yz, which is redundant.
Minterm
Product of all n literals that is 1 in exactly one row; written mᵢ.
Maxterm
Sum of all n literals that is 0 in exactly one row; written Mᵢ.
Canonical form
Sum of minterms or product of maxterms; one expression per function.
Standard form
SOP or POS whose terms may omit variables; what minimisation produces.
Karnaugh map
Grid of minterms in Gray-code order in which adjacent cells differ in one variable.
Gray code
Ordering of codes in which neighbours differ in one bit: 00 01 11 10.
Implicant
A product term (block of 1s) that implies the function: wherever it is 1, F is 1.
Prime implicant
An implicant that cannot be enlarged (no literal can be removed).
Essential prime implicant
A prime implicant that is the only one covering some minterm.
Cover
A set of implicants that together contain every 1 of the function.
Don't care
An input combination whose output is unspecified; X in the table; Σd(…) in the list.
Incompletely specified function
A function with at least one don't-care combination.
Tabulation method
Quine-McCluskey: binary grouping and combination to find all prime implicants, then a chart.
Prime implicant chart
Table of PIs against minterms used to select essential PIs and a minimal cover.
Petrick's method
Algebraic way of listing all minimal covers when essential PIs do not cover everything.
AND-invert / invert-OR
The two symbols of a NAND gate; invert-AND and OR-invert are the two symbols of NOR.
Two-level implementation
A circuit with one level of AND then one of OR (or vice versa), e.g. NAND-NAND.
Multilevel circuit
A circuit with more than two gate levels, from a factored expression.
