5.1 Acting under uncertainty and review of probability

An agent acts under uncertainty when it cannot know the exact state of the world or the exact result of its actions. It gives each fact a degree of belief between 0 and 1, using probability theory, and picks the action whose outcomes it expects to like most. Spam filters, diagnosis aids, a maps app's arrival time and a self-driving car all work this way.

You need: propositional logic (Unit 4, Propositional logic: syntax, semantics and inference) and rational agents (Unit 1, Rational agents and performance measures).

Why logic alone fails

Take the rule Toothache ⇒ Cavity. It is wrong: a toothache can also come from gum disease or an abscess, and the list of causes never ends. Russell and Norvig give three reasons such rules fail: laziness (listing every condition is too much work), theoretical ignorance (science has no complete theory of the domain) and practical ignorance (not every test has been run on this patient).

Plans have the same problem, the qualification problem. "Leave 60 minutes before the train leaves Chennai Central and I will catch it" holds only if there is no waterlogging, no accident, no flat tyre and so on, without end. A probabilistic agent says instead "I will catch it with probability 0.95".

Probability gives a degree of belief about a fact that is itself simply true or false (the train is caught or it is not). A degree of truth, as in "the room is fairly hot", is a different idea, handled by fuzzy sets in Chapter 5.7.

From beliefs to actions: maximum expected utility

Beliefs alone do not choose an action; the agent also needs preferences. A utility U(s) is a number saying how much the agent likes outcome s. The expected utility of an action a is the average utility of its outcomes, weighted by how likely each is:

EU(a) = Σₛ P(s | a) × U(s)

Here P(s | a) is the probability of outcome s if the agent does a. The principle of maximum expected utility (MEU): a rational agent chooses the action with the largest EU. So decision theory = probability theory + utility theory. A decision-theoretic agent updates its belief state from each percept, computes the outcome probabilities of each action and does the one with the highest EU (Figure 5.1.1).

Review of probability

The sample space Ω is the set of possible worlds ω; each has 0 ≤ P(ω) ≤ 1 and all of them add to 1. An event (proposition) φ is a set of worlds, with P(φ) = Σ P(ω) over its worlds. A random variable such as Rain has a domain: Boolean, discrete or continuous. P(rain) means P(Rain = true), and bold P(Weather) = ⟨0.6, 0.3, 0.1⟩ is the whole distribution, one number per value.

  • Prior (unconditional) probability: the belief before any evidence, such as P(rain) = 0.3.
  • Conditional probability: P(a | b) = P(a ∧ b) / P(b), defined when P(b) > 0.
  • Product rule: P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a).
  • Complement P(¬a) = 1 − P(a) and inclusion–exclusion P(a ∨ b) = P(a) + P(b) − P(a ∧ b). Both follow from Kolmogorov's axioms.
  • Independence: a and b are independent when P(a | b) = P(a), that is, P(a ∧ b) = P(a) P(b). Conditional independence of X and Y given Z: P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) P(Y | Z). This is the property that makes Chapters 5.3 and 5.4 possible.

The full joint distribution gives the probability of every world, and any query is answered by inference by enumeration: add the worlds where it is true. Marginalisation (summing out) removes a variable: P(Y) = Σ_z P(Y, z). Normalisation writes P(X | e) = α P(X, e), with α = 1 / P(e). The catch: n Boolean variables need 2ⁿ entries. Question 1. Use the joint table above. Find (a) P(late), (b) P(rain | late), (c) P(rain ∨ jam), (d) P(Rain | late) by normalisation. (6 marks)

  1. (a) Add the four worlds with L true: P(late) = 0.144 + 0.024 + 0.084 + 0.042 = 0.294.
  2. (b) P(rain ∧ late) = 0.144 + 0.024 = 0.168. So P(rain | late) = 0.168 / 0.294 = 0.571. Being late raises the belief in rain from the prior 0.3 to 0.571.
  3. (c) P(rain) = 0.300, P(jam) = 0.144 + 0.036 + 0.084 + 0.056 = 0.320, P(rain ∧ jam) = 0.144 + 0.036 = 0.180. P(rain ∨ jam) = 0.300 + 0.320 − 0.180 = 0.440. Check: 1 − P(¬rain ∧ ¬jam) = 1 − (0.042 + 0.518) = 0.440.

Question 2. For the exam in Figure 5.1.1, utilities are 100 for reaching on time and 0 for being late; leaving at 8:00 costs 10 (lost revision time) and the ₹150 auto costs 15. On a dry day P(on time) is 0.70 for the 8:30 bus, 0.95 for the 8:00 bus and 0.98 for the auto. On a rainy day these fall to 0.50, 0.80 and 0.90. Which action should a MEU agent take on each day? (6 marks)

  1. Dry, 8:30 bus: EU = 0.70 × 100 + 0.30 × 0 = 70.
  2. Dry, 8:00 bus: EU = 0.95 × 90 + 0.05 × (−10) = 85.5 − 0.5 = 85.
  3. Dry, auto: EU = 0.98 × 85 + 0.02 × (−15) = 83.3 − 0.3 = 83. Choose the 8:00 bus (85).
  4. Rain, 8:30 bus: 0.50 × 100 = 50. 8:00 bus: 0.80 × 90 + 0.20 × (−10) = 72 − 2 = 70. Auto: 0.90 × 85 + 0.10 × (−15) = 76.5 − 1.5 = 75. Choose the auto (75).

Exam traps

  • P(a | b) is not P(b | a). P(late | rain) = 0.168 / 0.3 = 0.56, but P(rain | late) = 0.571; the two are close here only by chance, and in Chapter 5.2 they differ by a factor of six.
  • P(a ∨ b) = P(a) + P(b) only when a and b cannot both be true. Otherwise subtract P(a ∧ b).
  • Probability is a degree of belief, not a degree of truth. "The water is fairly hot" is fuzzy (5.7), not probabilistic.
  • The MEU action is not the one with the most likely good outcome; it is the one with the largest weighted average utility, costs included.

5.2 Bayes' rule and Bayesian inference

Bayes' rule turns a probability we know in the causal direction, P(effect | cause), into the one we need for diagnosis, P(cause | effect). It is one line obtained from the product rule, yet it is how a doctor should read a lab report, how a bank scores a suspicious UPI payment and how every Bayesian learning method updates its beliefs. Bayesian inference is the habit of using it again and again: start with a prior belief, see evidence, get a posterior belief.

You need: conditional probability, the product rule and normalisation (Chapter 5.1).

The rule and its four terms

Write the product rule both ways: P(a ∧ b) = P(a | b) P(b) = P(b | a) P(a). Divide by P(a) and rename b as the hypothesis H and a as the evidence E:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) / P(E)

P(E) = P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | ¬H) P(¬H)          (total probability)
In general, for hypotheses H₁ … Hₙ that cover all cases:
P(Hᵢ | E) = P(E | Hᵢ) P(Hᵢ) / Σⱼ P(E | Hⱼ) P(Hⱼ)
Normalised form:  P(H | e) = α P(e | H) P(H)
  • P(H), the prior: belief in H before the evidence.
  • P(E | H), the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if H is true.
  • P(E), the evidence or marginal likelihood: how probable the evidence is overall. It only rescales.
  • P(H | E), the posterior: belief in H after the evidence.

Why not store P(cause | effect) directly? Because the causal number is stable. A dengue test detects 95% of infected people in a quiet month and during an outbreak alike. P(dengue | positive test) changes as soon as the number of cases in the city changes. Store the stable causal knowledge and the current prior, and Bayes' rule gives the diagnosis for today. This family of methods is called statistical reasoning.

Bayesian inference: updating with more evidence

With several pieces of evidence, P(H | e₁, e₂) = α P(e₁, e₂ | H) P(H). Estimating P(e₁, e₂ | H) for every combination is as hard as the full joint. If the pieces of evidence are conditionally independent given H, the likelihood factors:

P(H | e₁, e₂) = α P(e₁ | H) P(e₂ | H) P(H)

This can be done one step at a time: the posterior after e₁ becomes the prior for e₂. With n pieces of evidence the agent needs only n likelihoods per hypothesis, not 2ⁿ. This is exactly the naive Bayes model of Chapter 5.3.

When there are many hypotheses, two choices are common. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) hypothesis is the one with the largest P(Hᵢ | e). The maximum likelihood (ML) hypothesis is the one with the largest P(e | Hᵢ); it ignores the prior and equals MAP only when all priors are equal. The same idea in odds form: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds, where the likelihood ratio is P(e | H) / P(e | ¬H).

Worked example

Question 1. During a season, 2% of fever patients in a Chennai clinic have dengue. A rapid test is positive for 95% of dengue patients and for 10% of the others. (a) A patient tests positive; find P(dengue | positive). (b) A second, independent test is also positive; find the new posterior. (8 marks)

  1. Write the knowns: P(D) = 0.02, P(¬D) = 0.98, P(+ | D) = 0.95, P(+ | ¬D) = 0.10.
  2. Evidence: P(+) = 0.95 × 0.02 + 0.10 × 0.98 = 0.019 + 0.098 = 0.117.
  3. Posterior: P(D | +) = 0.019 / 0.117 = 0.162. Only about 1 in 6 positive patients has dengue, matching Figure 5.2.1.
  4. Second test: use 0.162 as the new prior. Numerator 0.95 × 0.1624 = 0.1543. Other term 0.10 × 0.8376 = 0.0838. P(D | +, +) = 0.1543 / (0.1543 + 0.0838) = 0.1543 / 0.2381 = 0.648.
  5. Check in one step, using conditional independence: α × 0.95² × 0.02 = α × 0.01805 and α × 0.10² × 0.98 = α × 0.0098, so P = 0.01805 / 0.02785 = 0.648. Same answer.

Question 2. A fruit seller in Koyambedu buys mangoes from farms A, B and C in the ratio 50 : 30 : 20. Their spoilage rates are 2%, 4% and 5%. A mango picked at random is spoiled. Find the posterior for each farm, the MAP farm and the ML farm. (6 marks)

  1. Priors 0.5, 0.3, 0.2; likelihoods P(S | A) = 0.02, P(S | B) = 0.04, P(S | C) = 0.05.
  2. Products: 0.5 × 0.02 = 0.010; 0.3 × 0.04 = 0.012; 0.2 × 0.05 = 0.010. Their sum P(S) = 0.032.
  3. Posteriors: P(A | S) = 0.010 / 0.032 = 0.3125; P(B | S) = 0.012 / 0.032 = 0.375; P(C | S) = 0.3125. They add to 1.
  4. MAP farm = B (0.375). ML farm = C, which has the largest likelihood 0.05 but a small share of supply. The prior makes the difference.

Exam traps

  • Base-rate neglect: P(+ | D) = 0.95 does not mean P(D | +) = 0.95. With a rare disease the posterior can be small.
  • The denominator must include every hypothesis: P(E) = P(E | H) P(H) + P(E | ¬H) P(¬H). Dropping the second term gives a "probability" above the true value, sometimes above 1.
  • Sensitivity is P(+ | D); the false-positive rate is P(+ | ¬D) = 1 − specificity. Read which one the question gives.
  • Multiplying likelihoods of two tests assumes they are conditionally independent given the disease. Say so in your answer.
  • MAP uses the prior; ML ignores it. They agree only when priors are equal.

5.3 Naive Bayes models

A naive Bayes model assumes that once the class is known, every feature is independent of every other feature. The posterior of each class is then just its prior times one likelihood per feature. The assumption is usually false, yet the model is fast, needs little data and classifies SMS spam, news topics and product reviews surprisingly well.

You need: Bayes' rule and the update with conditionally independent evidence (Chapter 5.2).

The model

Let C be the class (for example Spam or Ham, meaning not spam) and X₁ … Xₙ the features (for example "the message contains the word free"). Naive Bayes assumes the Xᵢ are conditionally independent given C. Bayes' rule then gives

P(C | x₁, …, xₙ) = α P(C) × P(x₁ | C) × P(x₂ | C) × … × P(xₙ | C)

Predicted class:  c* = argmax over c of  P(c) × Πᵢ P(xᵢ | c)

Here α = 1 / P(x₁, …, xₙ) is the normalising constant; it is the same for every class, so it can be ignored when we only want the winner. Π means "multiply over all i". As a Bayesian network (Chapter 5.4) this is the simplest shape: the class is the only parent, and every feature is its child (Figure 5.3.1).

The saving is large. With a Boolean class and n Boolean features, naive Bayes needs 1 + 2n numbers: one prior, and P(xᵢ | c) for each feature and class. The full joint needs 2ⁿ⁺¹ − 1. For n = 20 that is 41 numbers against 2,097,151. Its blunt assumption has earned it the nickname "idiot Bayes".

Learning by counting, and the zero problem

Training is counting. With N training examples, N_c of them in class c:

P(c)            = N_c / N
P(xᵢ = v | c)   = N(xᵢ = v, c) / N_c                      (raw count)
P(xᵢ = v | c)   = (N(xᵢ = v, c) + 1) / (N_c + k)          (Laplace, add-one)

Here N(xᵢ = v, c) is the number of class-c examples in which feature i has value v, and k is the number of values the feature can take (k = 2 for present or absent). The raw count has a zero-frequency problem: if no spam message in training contained "meeting", P(meeting | spam) = 0, and any new message with "meeting" gets spam probability 0 whatever its other words say. Laplace smoothing adds one imaginary example of every value, so no likelihood is ever 0.

A second practical point: multiplying hundreds of small likelihoods underflows to 0.0 on a computer. Implementations add logarithms instead, log P(c) + Σ log P(xᵢ | c), and compare the sums.

Three variants are common. Bernoulli naive Bayes uses present-or-absent features and multiplies P(¬xᵢ | c) for absent words too. Multinomial naive Bayes uses word counts and is the usual choice for long text. Gaussian naive Bayes models a continuous feature, such as a sensor reading, with a normal curve whose mean and variance are estimated per class.

Why a wrong assumption still works

Words in an email are clearly not independent ("credit" and "card" travel together). But classification only needs the right class to get the largest score. Naive Bayes often gets the ranking right even when its probabilities are pushed too close to 0 or 1. It fails when strongly linked features count the same evidence twice, and its probabilities should not be trusted as calibrated values.

The same calculation in Python, with logarithms as a real filter would do it:

from math import log, exp
prior = {"spam": 4/10, "ham": 6/10}
N     = {"spam": 4, "ham": 6}
count = {"spam": {"free": 3, "win": 2, "meeting": 0},
         "ham":  {"free": 1, "win": 1, "meeting": 3}}

def score(c, present):            # log P(c) + sum of log P(x_i | c)
    s = log(prior[c])
    for w, n in count[c].items():
        p = (n + 1) / (N[c] + 2)  # Laplace smoothing, k = 2
        s += log(p if w in present else 1 - p)
    return s

msg = {"free", "win"}
s = {c: score(c, msg) for c in prior}
z = sum(exp(v) for v in s.values())
for c in s:
    print(c, round(exp(s[c]) / z, 3))

Output:

spam 0.856
ham 0.144

Exam traps

  • The assumption is conditional independence given the class, not plain independence of the features.
  • In Bernoulli naive Bayes, absent words count too: multiply P(¬xᵢ | c), not just the words present.
  • In Laplace smoothing the denominator grows by k, the number of values (2 for a Boolean feature), not by 1.
  • Comparing unnormalised scores picks the class; to report a probability, divide each score by their sum.
  • One raw zero count wipes out a class completely. If a question gives a zero count, smoothing is what the examiner wants to see.

5.4 Bayesian networks: structure and semantics

A Bayesian network (also called a belief network) is a directed graph with no cycles whose nodes are random variables and whose arrows mean "directly influences". Each node stores a small table of its probabilities given its parents, and the product of these tables is the full joint distribution. So a network answers any probability question about its variables while storing a tiny fraction of the numbers. Judea Pearl developed the idea in the 1980s; it is used for medical and machine-fault diagnosis and risk analysis.

You need: conditional independence and the full joint distribution (Chapter 5.1), and the naive Bayes model, which is the simplest Bayesian network (Chapter 5.3).

Structure: nodes, arrows and CPTs

  1. Each node is a random variable, discrete or continuous.
  2. Directed arrows join pairs of nodes, and the graph has no directed cycle: it is a DAG (directed acyclic graph). An arrow X → Y makes X a parent of Y.
  3. Each node Xᵢ has a conditional probability table (CPT) giving P(Xᵢ | Parents(Xᵢ)), one row per combination of parent values. A node with no parents stores just its prior.

Each CPT row adds to 1, so for a Boolean node we store only P(true) per row. Figure 5.4.1 is a network for a Chennai household. Heavy rain (R) and a transformer fault (F) can each cause a power cut (C). A power cut makes the inverter beep (I) and makes the neighbour send a WhatsApp message (W). The topology says what influences what; the CPTs say how strongly.

Semantics: the network is the joint distribution

The meaning of the network is one formula: the probability of any complete assignment is the product of each node's CPT entry given its parents.

P(x₁, …, xₙ) = Πᵢ P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ))

Why is this true? The chain rule, which holds for any distribution, writes P(x₁, …, xₙ) = Πᵢ P(xᵢ | xᵢ₋₁, …, x₁). If the variables are numbered so that parents come before children, and each node is conditionally independent of its other predecessors given its parents, then P(xᵢ | xᵢ₋₁, …, x₁) = P(xᵢ | parents(Xᵢ)), and the formula follows. That condition is exactly what the builder checks while drawing the arrows.

Compactness. If each of n Boolean nodes has at most k parents, the network stores at most n × 2ᵏ numbers, against 2ⁿ − 1 for the full joint. For n = 30 and k = 5 that is 960 numbers instead of about a billion. This is the reason the method scales.

Building a network. (1) Order the variables, causes before effects. (2) For each variable in turn, choose as parents the smallest set of earlier variables that directly influence it. (3) Fill its CPT from data or experts. A bad order, effects first (I, W, C, R, F), still gives a correct network, but it needs more arrows and asks for unnatural numbers such as P(rain | power cut, inverter beeps).

Reading independence from the graph

Local semantics: every node is conditionally independent of its non-descendants given its parents. In Figure 5.4.1, once C is known, I tells us nothing more about R, F or W. A node's Markov blanket is its parents, its children and its children's other parents; given its blanket, a node is independent of everything else. The blanket of R is {C, F}; the blanket of C is {R, F, I, W}.

Three connection patterns decide whether information flows between two nodes. Checking every path this way is called d-separation.

The collider is the surprise. Rain and transformer faults are independent. But once we know there is a power cut, learning that the transformer failed makes rain less likely, because the fault already explains the cut. This is explaining away.

Causal networks

Causal networks are Bayesian networks whose arrows are real cause and effect. They also answer questions about actions. Observing a power cut raises the belief in rain from 0.2 to P(r | c) = 0.0855 / 0.1367 = 0.63. But if the electricity board switches the power off for planned maintenance, written do(C = true) in Pearl's notation, the arrows into C are cut, and P(r | do(c)) stays 0.2. Seeing is not doing.

Exam traps

  • An arrow means direct influence, not "always causes". The CPT can make the influence weak.
  • The joint is a product over all nodes, each given its own parents. Leaving out a root's prior, such as P(¬f) = 0.95, is the most common slip.
  • Two causes of a common effect are independent until the effect (or any descendant of it) is observed; then they become dependent. Chains and forks behave the opposite way.
  • Count CPT numbers per node as 2^(number of parents) for Boolean nodes; a root node needs 1.
  • The Markov blanket includes the children's other parents ("co-parents"), not only parents and children.

5.5 Exact and approximate inference in Bayesian networks

Inference in a Bayesian network means computing the posterior P(X | e) of a query variable X after observing evidence e. Exact methods, enumeration and variable elimination, give the true value but can take exponential time on large, densely linked networks. Approximate methods draw random samples from the network and give an estimate that improves as the number of samples grows.

You need: the network semantics and the power-cut network of Figure 5.4.1 (Chapter 5.4), and marginalisation and normalisation (Chapter 5.1).

The task

Split the variables into the query X, the evidence variables E with observed values e, and the hidden variables Y (everything else). Then

P(X | e) = α P(X, e) = α Σ_y P(X, e, y)

Each term P(X, e, y) is a full assignment, so it is a product of CPT entries (Chapter 5.4). Our running query: the inverter beeps (I = true); how likely is it that it rained? The hidden variables are F, C and W.

Exact inference: enumeration and variable elimination

Enumeration writes the sum and moves each factor as far left as it can go:

P(R, i) = P(R) Σ_f P(f) Σ_c P(c | R, f) P(i | c) Σ_w P(w | c)

The last sum is 1, so W drops out. In general, any variable that is not an ancestor of the query or the evidence is irrelevant and can be deleted first. Enumeration then evaluates the expression as a tree, depth first (Figure 5.5.1). It needs only linear space, but O(dⁿ) time for n variables with d values each, and it recomputes the same subexpressions again and again: P(i | c) and P(i | ¬c) appear under every value of F.

Variable elimination (VE) evaluates the same expression right to left and stores every intermediate result as a factor, a table indexed by its free variables. Two operations are enough:

  1. Pointwise product of factors: multiply matching rows. f(A, B) × g(B, C) gives a factor h(A, B, C).
  2. Summing out a variable: add the rows that differ only in that variable. Σ_b h(A, b, C) gives a factor over A and C.
  3. Eliminate the hidden variables one at a time in some order, then multiply what is left and normalise.

The answer does not depend on the order, but the cost does: it is set by the largest factor created. On a polytree (at most one undirected path between any two nodes, as in Figure 5.4.1) VE runs in time linear in the size of the network. On general multiply connected networks exact inference is NP-hard; in fact it is as hard as counting the solutions of a SAT formula. Systems that answer many queries use the junction tree (clustering) algorithm, which merges nodes into a tree of clusters once.

Approximate inference by sampling

Sampling replaces sums with counting. Its estimates are consistent: the error shrinks roughly as 1/√N for N samples, so four times the samples halve the error.

  • Direct (prior) sampling. Visit nodes in topological order (parents first). Draw each value from its CPT row given the parents already drawn: a Boolean variable is true when a random number u in [0, 1) is below P(true | parents). The fraction of samples with X = x estimates P(x).
  • Rejection sampling. Draw prior samples and throw away those that disagree with the evidence; count among the rest. Here P(i) = 0.166, so about 83% of samples are wasted, and with several pieces of evidence almost all are.
  • Likelihood weighting. Fix the evidence variables at their observed values and sample only the others. Give each sample a weight equal to the product of P(eⱼ | parents) over the evidence variables. Estimate P(x | e) as the total weight of samples with X = x divided by the total weight. Nothing is rejected.
  • Gibbs sampling, a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method. Start from any state that agrees with the evidence. Repeatedly pick a non-evidence variable and redraw it from its distribution given its Markov blanket. In the long run, the fraction of time spent in each state approaches the posterior.

5.6 Dempster-Shafer theory

Dempster-Shafer theory (DST), from Arthur Dempster (1967) and Glenn Shafer (1976), lets evidence support a set of hypotheses without saying how the support splits inside the set. Each hypothesis then gets a belief interval, [Belief, Plausibility], instead of one probability, and the width of the interval shows how much is still unknown.

You need: Bayes' rule and its use of priors (Chapter 5.2).

Mass, belief and plausibility

The frame of discernment Θ is the set of mutually exclusive hypotheses, for example Θ = {Dengue, Malaria, Typhoid} = {D, M, T}. A mass function (basic probability assignment) m gives each subset A of Θ a number; m(∅) = 0 and the masses add to 1. m(A) is the belief committed exactly to A and to no smaller subset; m(Θ) is the mass we cannot place, our ignorance.

Bel(A) = Σ m(B) over all B ⊆ A              (support that surely points into A)
Pl(A)  = Σ m(B) over all B with B ∩ A ≠ ∅   (support that does not rule A out)
Pl(A)  = 1 − Bel(¬A),   and   Bel(A) ≤ P(A) ≤ Pl(A)

Total ignorance is m(Θ) = 1, giving [0, 1] for every hypothesis, where a Bayesian would have to pick a prior such as 1/3 each. With all mass on single hypotheses, Bel = Pl and DST becomes ordinary probability.

Dempster's rule of combination

Two independent sources m₁ and m₂ are combined by multiplying masses and giving the product to the intersection of the two sets:

m₁₂(A) = [ Σ m₁(B) m₂(C) over B ∩ C = A ] / (1 − K)       for A ≠ ∅
K      =   Σ m₁(B) m₂(C) over B ∩ C = ∅                  (the conflict)

Dividing by 1 − K shares out the mass that fell on the empty set. When K is near 1 the rule misleads (Lotfi Zadeh's criticism), and n hypotheses have 2ⁿ subsets, so large frames are costly.

Worked example

  1. Products (Figure 5.6.1): {D}: 0.30 + 0.12 = 0.42; {M}: 0.12; {D, M}: 0.20; Θ: 0.08; ∅: 0.18. So K = 0.18 and 1 − K = 0.82.
  2. Normalise: m({D}) = 0.42 / 0.82 = 0.512; m({M}) = 0.146; m({D, M}) = 0.244; m(Θ) = 0.098.
  3. {D}: Bel = 0.512; Pl = 0.512 + 0.244 + 0.098 = 0.854. Interval [0.512, 0.854].
  4. {D, M}: Bel = 0.512 + 0.146 + 0.244 = 0.902; Pl = 1. Interval [0.902, 1].
  5. {T}: no mass on any subset of {T}, so Bel = 0; Pl = m(Θ) = 0.098 = 1 − Bel({D, M}). Interval [0, 0.098]: typhoid is nearly ruled out.

Heuristic methods. Some syllabi also list heuristic methods, such as MYCIN's certainty factors: CF = MB − MD (belief minus disbelief), between −1 and +1. Two positive factors for one conclusion combine as CF = CF₁ + CF₂(1 − CF₁), so 0.6 and 0.5 give 0.8 (MYCIN is in Unit 6).

Exam traps

  • m(A) is not Bel(A): Bel adds the masses of all subsets of A.
  • Divide by 1 − K, or the masses add to less than 1.
  • In Pl(A) = 1 − Bel(¬A), ¬A is the complement within Θ: ¬{T} = {D, M}.

5.7 Fuzzy sets and fuzzy reasoning

A fuzzy set lets an element belong to it partly, with a membership degree between 0 and 1, so vague words such as "hot", "tall" or "heavy traffic" can be computed with. Fuzzy reasoning applies IF–THEN rules to such sets and turns the result into one crisp number, such as how fast a fan should spin. Lotfi Zadeh introduced fuzzy sets in 1965; the Namboku Line of the Sendai subway in Japan has used fuzzy control for acceleration and braking since it opened in 1987, and washing machines and air conditioners use it today.

You need: basic set operations, and the difference between a degree of belief and a degree of truth (Chapter 5.1).

From crisp sets to fuzzy sets

In a crisp set an element is in (1) or out (0). Is 29.9 °C "hot" if hot means 30 °C and above? A crisp rule says no, and one tenth of a degree flips the answer. A fuzzy set A on a universe X has a membership function μ_A : X → [0, 1]. On a small discrete universe we list pairs, element/degree: Hot = {25/0, 28/0.2, 31/0.5, 34/0.8, 37/1}.

Common shapes: the triangle (a, b, c), rising as (x − a)/(b − a) from a to the peak b and falling as (c − x)/(c − b) to c; the trapezoid (a, b, c, d), which has a flat top from b to c; and the Gaussian bell. Useful terms:

  • Support: elements with μ > 0. Core: elements with μ = 1. Height: the largest μ; the set is normal if the height is 1.
  • α-cut A_α = {x : μ_A(x) ≥ α}, an ordinary crisp set.
  • Linguistic variable: a variable whose values are words, such as Temperature ∈ {Cool, Warm, Hot}, each word a fuzzy set (Figure 5.7.1).
  • Hedges: "very A" has μ², which concentrates the set; "somewhat A" has √μ, which dilates it.

Fuzziness is not probability. μ_Hot(31) = 0.5 says 31 °C is hot to degree 0.5, with nothing unknown. P(rain) = 0.5 is uncertainty about an event that will fully happen or fully not happen.

Operations and relations

Zadeh's standard operations work element by element:

Union          μ_A∪B(x) = max(μ_A(x), μ_B(x))
Intersection   μ_A∩B(x) = min(μ_A(x), μ_B(x))
Complement     μ_A'(x)  = 1 − μ_A(x)

Commutative, associative and distributive laws and De Morgan's laws still hold. Two laws fail: the law of excluded middle (A ∪ A' need not be X) and the law of contradiction (A ∩ A' need not be empty). A fuzzy relation R between X and Y is a matrix of membership degrees, such as "x is similar to y". Two relations chain by max-min composition: (R ∘ S)(x, z) = max over y of min(R(x, y), S(y, z)), which looks like matrix multiplication with min for × and max for +.

Fuzzy reasoning: the Mamdani controller

  1. Fuzzification. Read each crisp input against its membership functions (Figure 5.7.1).
  2. Rule evaluation. For a rule IF x is A AND y is B THEN z is C, the firing strength is min(μ_A(x), μ_B(y)). OR uses max, NOT uses 1 − μ.
  3. Implication. Clip the output set C at the firing strength (the min rule).
  4. Aggregation. Join the clipped output sets of all rules with max.
  5. Defuzzification. Turn the aggregated set into one number. The centroid is z* = Σ μ(z) z / Σ μ(z) (an integral for continuous z). A quick exam method is the weighted average of the rule output centres, weighted by firing strength; mean of maxima is a third.

In a Sugeno (Takagi–Sugeno–Kang) system each rule outputs a crisp value or a linear formula of the inputs, so the final output is simply the weighted average of the rule outputs, with no output fuzzy sets at all.

  1. Fuzzify: μ_Warm(32) = (36 − 32)/(36 − 30) = 0.667; μ_Hot(32) = (32 − 30)/(38 − 30) = 0.25; μ_High(70) = (70 − 50)/(90 − 50) = 0.5; μ_NOT High = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5.
  2. Firing strengths: R1 = min(0.667, 0.5) = 0.5; R2 = 0.25; R3 = min(0.667, 0.5) = 0.5.
  3. Weighted average: (0.5 × 60 + 0.25 × 90 + 0.5 × 30) / (0.5 + 0.25 + 0.5) = (30 + 22.5 + 15) / 1.25 = 67.5 / 1.25 = 54% speed.
  4. At 34 °C the same rules give 64%. The speed rises smoothly with temperature instead of jumping at a threshold, the main practical gain of fuzzy control.

Exam traps

  • Membership is not probability. The degrees of one element in different sets need not add to 1 (here 0.667 + 0.25).
  • Standard AND is min and OR is max. Product AND is a valid alternative, but say which one you use.
  • "Which classical laws fail for fuzzy sets?" Excluded middle and contradiction. De Morgan's laws still hold.
  • An α-cut uses μ ≥ α; the strong α-cut uses μ > α.
  • Name the defuzzification method. Centroid and weighted average of centres usually give different numbers.

5.8 Non-monotonic and temporal reasoning

Classical logic is monotonic: adding a fact never removes a conclusion. Everyday reasoning is not. We assume the shop is open on a weekday, and withdraw that the moment we hear it is Diwali. Non-monotonic reasoning lets an agent jump to default conclusions and retract them when new facts arrive. Temporal reasoning handles facts that hold only for a while and the order of events.

You need: first-order logic and Prolog (Unit 4, First-order predicate logic: syntax and semantics; Logic programming and natural deduction).

Non-monotonic reasoning

Monotonicity says: if KB ⊨ α then KB ∧ β ⊨ α, for any new sentence β. Four standard ways to drop it:

  • Default logic (Reiter, 1980). The rule Bird(x) : Flies(x) / Flies(x) reads "if x is a bird and it is consistent to assume x flies, conclude that x flies". Learning Penguin(Tweety) with Penguin(x) ⇒ ¬Flies(x) blocks the default.
  • Circumscription (McCarthy, 1980). Write Bird(x) ∧ ¬Abnormal(x) ⇒ Flies(x) and prefer models in which as few things as possible are Abnormal.
  • Closed-world assumption and negation as failure. What cannot be proved is taken as false. If a train is not in the IRCTC timetable, assume there is no such train. Prolog's \+ works this way.
  • Truth maintenance systems (TMS) (Doyle, 1979). A justification-based TMS stores, for each belief, the beliefs that support it (IN-list) and those whose absence it assumes (OUT-list). A belief is IN when every IN-list belief is IN and every OUT-list belief is OUT. When a belief changes, the TMS relabels everything that depends on it. An assumption-based TMS (ATMS) instead keeps, for each belief, the sets of assumptions under which it holds.

Temporal reasoning

Facts that change over time are fluents. The situation calculus adds a situation argument, At(Ravi, Hostel, s), with Result(a, s) for the situation after action a; its difficulty is the frame problem, stating everything an action does not change. The event calculus uses time points: Happens(e, t), Initiates(e, f, t), Terminates(e, f, t), and HoldsAt(f, t) is true if some earlier event initiated f and no event since has terminated it.

Allen's interval algebra (1983) relates time intervals rather than points. It has 13 relations: the 7 of Figure 5.8.1 and the inverses of the first six. A composition table chains them, for example meets followed by before gives before.

Worked example

Question. (a) An agent's KB has Weekday(Tue), the default Weekday(d) : Open(d) / Open(d), the rule Holiday(d) ⇒ ¬Open(d) and the plan "buy sweets at the shop on Tuesday", justified by Open(Tue). Trace a JTMS when Holiday(Tue) is added. (b) A timetable has Lecture 9:00–10:00, Lab 10:00–12:00, Seminar 11:00–13:00 and Lunch 12:30–13:30. Give the Allen relations Lecture–Lab, Lab–Seminar, Seminar–Lunch and Lab–Lunch, and infer Lecture–Lunch. (8 marks)

  1. (a) Open(Tue) has IN-list {Weekday(Tue)} and OUT-list {¬Open(Tue)}. Weekday(Tue) is IN and ¬Open(Tue) is OUT, so Open(Tue) is IN, and so is the plan.
  2. Add Holiday(Tue). The rule makes ¬Open(Tue) IN, so Open(Tue)'s justification fails and it goes OUT; the plan, justified only by Open(Tue), goes OUT too. A new fact removed two conclusions: non-monotonic.
  3. (b) Lecture meets Lab (10:00 = 10:00). Lab overlaps Seminar (Lab starts first and ends inside Seminar). Seminar overlaps Lunch. Lab is before Lunch (12:00 < 12:30).
  4. Compose: Lecture meets Lab and Lab before Lunch, so Lecture is before Lunch, found without reading the clock times again.

Exam traps

  • Non-monotonic does not mean inconsistent: conclusions are withdrawn, never left contradicting each other.
  • In a JTMS a belief needs its OUT-list beliefs to be OUT, not false.
  • "Meets" leaves no gap; "before" needs one. Allen has 13 relations, not 7.

Takeaways

  1. Logical rules fail in real domains because of laziness, theoretical ignorance and practical ignorance; probability summarises what is not known, and the MEU principle turns beliefs and utilities into an action.
  2. Every probability question can be answered from the full joint distribution by summing worlds, but the table grows as 2ⁿ; independence and conditional independence are what make reasoning practical.
  3. Bayes' rule turns causal knowledge into diagnosis: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Ignoring the prior (the base rate) is the most common error.
  4. Naive Bayes assumes features are independent given the class; it learns by counting, needs smoothing for zero counts and logarithms against underflow, and ranks classes well even when its probabilities are off.
  5. A Bayesian network is a DAG plus one CPT per node; the joint is the product of the CPT entries, and chains, forks and colliders tell which variables are independent.
  6. Variable elimination computes exact posteriors, in linear time on polytrees but NP-hard in general; rejection sampling, likelihood weighting and Gibbs sampling give estimates whose error falls as 1/√N.
  7. Dempster-Shafer theory spreads mass over sets of hypotheses, reports [Bel, Pl] intervals and combines sources by Dempster's rule, which misleads when conflict is high.
  8. Fuzzy sets give degrees of truth; with min, max and 1 − μ, a Mamdani controller fuzzifies, fires rules, aggregates and defuzzifies to a smooth crisp output.
  9. Non-monotonic reasoning (defaults, circumscription, negation as failure, TMS) withdraws conclusions when facts arrive; temporal reasoning uses the situation calculus, the event calculus and Allen's 13 interval relations.

Glossary

α-cut
The crisp set of elements whose membership in a fuzzy set is at least α.
Bayesian network
A directed acyclic graph of random variables, each with a table of its probabilities given its parents; also called a belief network.
Belief (Bel)
In Dempster-Shafer theory, the total mass of all subsets of a set A: the support that surely points into A.
Certainty factor
MYCIN's heuristic confidence, belief minus disbelief, from −1 to +1.
Circumscription
Non-monotonic reasoning that prefers models with as few abnormal objects as possible.
Collider
A node with two incoming arrows on a path; it blocks the path until it or a descendant is observed.
Conditional independence
X and Y are independent once Z is known: P(X, Y | Z) = P(X | Z) P(Y | Z).
CPT
Conditional probability table: P(X | parents of X) for every combination of parent values.
Default logic
Reiter's logic of rules that apply unless their conclusion is inconsistent with what is known.
Defuzzification
Turning an output fuzzy set into one crisp number, for example by the centroid.
Dempster's rule
Combines two mass functions by multiplying masses onto intersections and dividing by 1 − K.
Expected utility
The probability-weighted average utility of an action's outcomes.
Explaining away
Learning one cause of an observed effect lowers the probability of the other causes.
Frame of discernment
The set Θ of mutually exclusive hypotheses in Dempster-Shafer theory.
Full joint distribution
The probability of every complete assignment of values to all variables.
Gibbs sampling
An MCMC method that redraws one variable at a time given its Markov blanket.
Laplace smoothing
Adding one to every count so that no estimated probability is zero.
Likelihood
P(evidence | hypothesis): how well a hypothesis predicts the evidence.
Likelihood weighting
Sampling that fixes the evidence and weights each sample by the evidence's probability.
Markov blanket
A node's parents, children and children's other parents.
Membership function
μ_A(x) ∈ [0, 1]: the degree to which x belongs to fuzzy set A.
MEU
Maximum expected utility: choose the action with the largest expected utility.
Non-monotonic reasoning
Reasoning in which new facts can remove earlier conclusions.
Plausibility (Pl)
The mass of all sets that intersect A; Pl(A) = 1 − Bel(¬A).
Polytree
A network with at most one undirected path between any two nodes; exact inference is linear on it.
Posterior
Probability of a hypothesis after the evidence is taken into account.
Prior
Probability of a hypothesis before the evidence.
Truth maintenance system
A program that records why each belief is held and retracts beliefs whose support disappears.
Variable elimination
Exact inference that sums out hidden variables one at a time, storing results as factors.
