1.1 What AI is: history, foundations and sub-areas

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the part of computer science that builds machines which perceive, reason, learn and act in ways we would call intelligent if a person did them. It exists because tasks such as reading a chest X-ray, translating Tamil into Hindi or planning a delivery route have no simple formula: the program must search, use knowledge and learn from data.

Four ways to define AI

Definitions of AI differ on two questions. Do we judge how the machine thinks or how it acts? Do we compare it with humans or with an ideal standard, rationality? The answers give Figure 1.1.1.

  • Acting humanly: the Turing test. In 1950 Alan Turing proposed the imitation game: a judge chats by text with a hidden person and a hidden machine; if the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine passes. It needs natural language processing, knowledge representation, automated reasoning and machine learning. The total Turing test adds video and objects to handle, so it also needs computer vision and robotics.
  • Thinking humanly: cognitive modelling. Build a program whose steps match how people think, and test it against experiments on people, as Newell and Simon did with their General Problem Solver (GPS). This grew into cognitive science.
  • Thinking rationally: the laws of thought. Aristotle's syllogisms ("all men are mortal; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is mortal") were the first rules of correct reasoning; logic-based programs follow them (Unit 4). Limits: everyday knowledge is hard to state in logic, and proofs can be too slow.
  • Acting rationally: the rational agent. An agent perceives its environment and acts on it. A rational agent acts to achieve the best outcome or, under uncertainty, the best expected outcome. This is the approach of Russell and Norvig and of this book: it includes correct reasoning as one tool, also covers reflexes such as pulling a hand off a hot stove, and can be defined exactly with mathematics.

Rich and Knight, a textbook many Indian colleges use, define AI as the study of how to make computers do things which, at the moment, people do better.

A short history

AI's history is a cycle of big promises, disappointment and comeback. Learn it as eras with a cause for each turn (Figure 1.1.2).

  • Gestation and birth (1943–1973). McCulloch and Pitts (1943) modelled neurons as on–off logic units. McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester and Shannon ran the 1956 Dartmouth workshop; McCarthy's 1955 proposal for it coined the term "artificial intelligence". Early programs proved theorems, played checkers and solved puzzles.
  • First AI winter (mid-1970s). Programs that worked on toy problems failed on real ones: possibilities exploded (combinatorial explosion), the programs knew little about the world, and Minsky and Papert (1969) showed what a single-layer perceptron cannot learn.
  • Expert systems, then the second winter (1980s). Rule-based programs such as MYCIN (blood infections) and R1/XCON (configuring DEC computers) brought AI into industry (Unit 6). Rule bases proved costly and brittle, and funding fell again in the late 1980s.
  • Learning from data (1986 to today). Backpropagation, probability and machine learning made AI measurable. Deep learning took off in 2012, when large labelled datasets met GPUs, and the transformer (2017) led to today's large language models.

Worked example

Question (6 marks). Chess has an average branching factor b = 35 (legal moves per position), and a typical game lasts 50 moves by each player, so depth d = 100 plies. (a) Estimate the number of move sequences bd. (b) A computer checks 109 sequences per second. How long would it take, compared with the age of the universe (about 13.8 billion years)? (c) What does this say about how AI must work?

  1. Take logarithms to base 10: log10(35100) = 100 × log10 35 = 100 × 1.5441 = 154.41.
  2. So 35100 = 10154.41 = 100.41 × 10154 ≈ 2.6 × 10154 sequences.
  3. Time = 2.6 × 10154 ÷ 109 per second = 2.6 × 10145 seconds.
  4. Age of the universe = 13.8 × 109 years × 3.156 × 107 s per year ≈ 4.4 × 1017 s.
  5. Ratio = 2.6 × 10145 ÷ 4.4 × 1017 ≈ 6 × 10127 ages of the universe.

Answer. (a) About 2.6 × 10154 sequences. (b) About 2.6 × 10145 s, roughly 10128 ages of the universe. (c) Brute force is hopeless even for a board game; this combinatorial explosion is what stopped early AI. A program must use knowledge to judge positions, heuristics to try promising moves first (Unit 2) and pruning to skip moves that cannot matter (Unit 3).

Checking whether an application works

Many applications answer a yes/no question: is this payment fraud, does this eye have disease? Compare the system's answers with the truth and each case falls into one of four boxes:

  • True positive (TP): says yes, truth is yes. False negative (FN): says no, truth is yes (a missed case).
  • False positive (FP): says yes, truth is no (a false alarm). True negative (TN): says no, truth is no.

From these come four measures:

Sensitivity (recall)  = TP / (TP + FN)    share of real cases caught
Specificity           = TN / (TN + FP)    share of healthy cases cleared
Precision (PPV)       = TP / (TP + FP)    share of alarms that are real
Accuracy              = (TP + TN) / N     share of all answers that are right

Here N is the total number of cases and PPV means positive predictive value. When the condition is rare, accuracy hides the truth. A useless system that always says "no disease" is right about every healthy person, so its accuracy equals the share of healthy people. Always report sensitivity and precision with accuracy.

Worked example

Question (8 marks). An AI tool screens retina photos at an eye camp in Madurai. It has sensitivity 90% and specificity 95%. Of the 1,000 people screened, 8% have diabetic eye disease. (a) Fill in TP, FN, FP and TN. (b) Find the accuracy and the precision. (c) A lazy tool marks everyone "healthy". Find its accuracy and sensitivity. (d) Which tool would you deploy, and how?

  1. People with disease = 8% × 1,000 = 80; healthy = 1,000 − 80 = 920.
  2. TP = 90% × 80 = 72; FN = 80 − 72 = 8.
  3. TN = 95% × 920 = 874; FP = 920 − 874 = 46.
  4. Accuracy = (72 + 874) / 1,000 = 946 / 1,000 = 94.6%.
  5. Precision = 72 / (72 + 46) = 72 / 118 = 0.610 = 61.0%.
  6. Lazy tool: TP = 0, FN = 80, TN = 920, FP = 0. Accuracy = 920 / 1,000 = 92.0%; sensitivity = 0 / 80 = 0%.

Answer. (a) TP 72, FN 8, FP 46, TN 874. (b) Accuracy 94.6%, precision 61.0%. (c) The lazy tool scores 92.0% accuracy yet catches nobody (sensitivity 0%), so accuracy alone is a poor guide. (d) Deploy the AI tool as a first filter: refer its 118 positives to an eye doctor, who confirms the 72 real cases, and keep a doctor's check for the decision. The 8 missed cases are why screening is repeated each year.

The future of AI

Narrow and general AI. Most deployed systems are narrow AI: each does one job. Artificial general intelligence (AGI) would match people across most tasks; whether and when it will arrive is debated. Do not mix this pair with weak and strong AI, a philosophical pair: the weak-AI claim is that machines can act as if intelligent; the strong-AI claim is that a suitably programmed machine would really have a mind.

Where the field is heading. Foundation models, one large model trained on huge data and adapted to many tasks; AI agents that plan and use tools such as search and code; AI running on the phone itself; and AI for science, from new materials to drug discovery.

What can go wrong. Bias: a loan model trained on past decisions copies past unfairness. Privacy: training data and face recognition can expose people. Misinformation: deepfake audio and video. Jobs: tasks are automated, so workers need reskilling. Safety: a system pursuing a badly chosen objective can do harm, which is why Chapter 1.4 insists on a careful performance measure. Energy: training large models uses much electricity.

Rules and safeguards. The EU AI Act (2024) sorts AI systems by risk and bans some uses. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) governs personal data, and the IndiaAI Mission (2024) funds shared computing and Indian-language models. Responsible-AI principles ask for fairness, accountability, transparency, privacy and safety, with a human in the loop for high-stakes decisions.

  • For "applications of AI", name the AI technique and what the agent senses and does, not just a list of fields.
  • For "future of AI", give benefits and risks, each with an example and a safeguard; a one-sided answer loses marks.

1.3 Intelligent agents: structure and types of agents

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. A person has eyes and hands; a robot has cameras and motors; a spam filter receives emails and acts by moving them to a folder. Describing every AI system as an agent gives one language for the whole subject, and the five agent types in this chapter, from the simple reflex agent to the learning agent, answer the most-asked question of this unit.

Percepts, the agent function and the agent program

  • A percept is what the agent senses at one instant. The percept sequence is the complete history of everything it has sensed.
  • The agent function f maps every possible percept sequence to an action: f : P* → A, where P* is the set of percept sequences and A the set of actions. It is an abstract, mathematical description of behaviour.
  • The agent program is the code that implements the agent function. It runs on the architecture: the computing device with its sensors and actuators. So agent = architecture + program.

The running example is the vacuum world: two squares, A and B, each Clean or Dirty. The percept is [location, status], such as [A, Dirty], and the actions are Left, Right, Suck and NoOp (do nothing).

The simplest program is a table-driven agent: store the action for every possible percept sequence and look it up. With |P| possible percepts and a lifetime of T steps the table needs Σt=1T |P|t entries, which explodes (see the worked example). The aim of AI is to write small programs that produce the same rational behaviour without the giant table.

The five types of agent program

  1. Simple reflex agent. Chooses an action from the current percept only, using condition–action rules such as "if the car in front is braking, then start braking". It works only when the environment is fully observable. A vacuum agent with no location sensor that always moves Left from a clean square loops for ever at the left wall; choosing a random move can break the loop.
  2. Model-based reflex agent. Keeps an internal state that tracks the parts of the world it cannot see now. It updates the state with a transition model (how the world changes by itself, and what its own actions do) and a sensor model (how the world shows up in percepts). A driver remembers the car that went into the blind spot.
  3. Goal-based agent. Also knows a goal, a description of desired situations, and asks "what will happen if I do action A?". Reaching the goal may need a long sequence of actions, found by search (Unit 2) or planning (Unit 6). At a junction a taxi turns left or right depending on where the passenger wants to go. To change its behaviour you change the goal, not dozens of rules.
  4. Utility-based agent. A goal only says happy or unhappy. A utility function gives each state a number that measures how desirable it is, so the agent can trade off conflicting goals (speed against safety) and, under uncertainty, choose the action with the highest expected utility. Two routes may both reach the airport; utility prefers the quicker, safer, cheaper one.
  5. Learning agent. Any of the four above, plus the machinery to improve from experience (Figure 1.3.2). The performance element is the agent proper and chooses actions. The critic compares results with a fixed performance standard and sends feedback. The learning element uses that feedback to change the performance element. The problem generator suggests new, exploratory actions that may teach it something.

  • In the learning agent, the performance standard is outside the agent and fixed. The critic does not invent its own standard.

1.4 Rational agents and performance measures

A rational agent is one that does the right thing, and "right" is fixed by a performance measure: a score the designer puts on the sequence of environment states that the agent's actions cause. Rationality is not perfection. An agent is rational if, for every percept sequence, it picks the action expected to give the best score, given what it has perceived and what it knows. It is the yardstick for every agent in the rest of the subject.

You need: agents, the vacuum world and agent types from Chapter 1.3.

The definition

For each possible percept sequence, a rational agent selects an action that is expected to maximise its performance measure, given the evidence of the percept sequence and whatever built-in knowledge it has. So what is rational at any moment depends on four things:

  1. the performance measure that defines success;
  2. the agent's prior knowledge of the environment;
  3. the actions the agent can perform;
  4. the agent's percept sequence to date.

When outcomes are uncertain, the agent compares actions by their expected performance: E[a] = Σi pi × si, where outcome i of action a happens with probability pi and earns score si.

Designing the performance measure

The measure judges consequences, the states of the environment, not the agent's opinion of itself. The golden rule: design it for what you actually want in the environment, not for how you think the agent should behave.

  • "Amount of dirt sucked up" sounds right, but it rewards a vacuum that sucks up dirt, dumps it and sucks it again. "One point for each clean square at each time step" rewards what we want: a clean floor.
  • "Chats closed per hour" for a customer-service bot rewards closing chats unsolved. "Problems solved, as confirmed by the customer" does not.
  • Real measures add costs: electricity used, noise made, moves wasted. Each choice changes which behaviour is rational, as the worked example shows.

Rational is not omniscient, and not always successful

  • Omniscience means knowing the actual outcome of every action, which is impossible. You look both ways, the road is clear, you cross, and a coconut falls on you. Crossing was still rational. Rationality maximises expected performance; perfection would maximise actual performance.
  • Information gathering is part of rationality: looking before crossing, or a doctor ordering a test, changes future percepts so later choices are better. Exploring an unknown place is the same idea.
  • Learning. Prior knowledge can be wrong, so a rational agent learns from what it perceives. A dung beetle carries on plugging its nest with a ball of dung even after the ball is taken away: fixed built-in behaviour with no learning fails when the world changes.
  • Autonomy. An agent that relies only on its designer's knowledge lacks autonomy. A rational agent starts with some built-in knowledge and becomes more autonomous as it learns from its own percepts.

Worked example 2: expected performance

Question (6 marks). A cab agent takes a passenger from Chennai Central to the airport. Route 1 (through the city) takes 40 min with probability 0.6 and 70 min with probability 0.4. Route 2 (ring road) takes 55 min with probability 0.9 and 65 min with probability 0.1. (a) Which route has the lower expected time? (b) The measure is −1 per minute and a further −100 if the trip takes over 60 min (missed check-in). Which route is rational? (c) The cab took the rational route but broke down and took 90 min. Was the choice irrational?

  1. E[time, route 1] = 0.6 × 40 + 0.4 × 70 = 24 + 28 = 52 min. E[time, route 2] = 0.9 × 55 + 0.1 × 65 = 49.5 + 6.5 = 56 min.
  2. E[score, route 1] = 0.6 × (−40) + 0.4 × (−70 − 100) = −24 − 68 = −92.
  3. E[score, route 2] = 0.9 × (−55) + 0.1 × (−65 − 100) = −49.5 − 16.5 = −66.

Answer. (a) Route 1, 52 min against 56 min. (b) Route 2, because −66 > −92: the deadline penalty makes the risky route worse even though it is faster on average. (c) No. Rationality is judged on expected performance with the information available; a breakdown the agent could not foresee does not make the choice irrational.

The task environment is the problem to which a rational agent is the solution. Before you design an agent, you describe its task environment with PEAS (Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors) and then classify it on seven properties: observable, agents, deterministic, episodic, static, discrete and known. That classification tells you which agent type from Chapter 1.3 you need and how hard the job is.

The seven properties

  1. Fully or partially observable. Do the sensors give the complete relevant state at each moment? Chess: fully. Poker (hidden cards) and driving (blind spots): partially. With no sensors at all it is unobservable.
  2. Single-agent or multi-agent. Is there another agent whose performance depends on what ours does? A crossword is single-agent; chess is competitive multi-agent; taxi driving is partly cooperative (avoid collisions) and partly competitive (one parking space). A falling coconut is not an agent.
  3. Deterministic or stochastic. Is the next state completely fixed by the current state and the action? A crossword is deterministic; driving is not (a tyre can burst). Stochastic means the outcomes have probabilities; nondeterministic means several outcomes are possible with no probabilities given.
  4. Episodic or sequential. Does the current decision affect later ones? Rejecting a faulty part on an assembly line, or labelling one photo, is episodic: each episode stands alone. Chess and driving are sequential.
  5. Static or dynamic. Can the world change while the agent is thinking? A crossword is static; driving is dynamic. Chess with a clock is semi-dynamic: the board waits, but the clock and so the score do not.
  6. Discrete or continuous. Do states, time, percepts and actions take a finite set of distinct values? Chess is discrete; driving is continuous (speed, position, steering angle).
  7. Known or unknown. Does the agent (or its designer) know the rules, that is, the outcomes of its actions? A new video game played without the manual is unknown. Known is not the same as observable: card solitaire is known but partially observable.

1.6 Problem-solving agents and problem formulation

A problem-solving agent is a goal-based agent that, before it acts, works out a whole sequence of actions that reaches its goal. It does this in four phases: formulate a goal, formulate a problem, search for a solution, then execute it. The skill this chapter teaches is problem formulation: writing a real task as five precise components at the right level of detail, so that a search algorithm (Unit 2) can solve it.

You need: goal-based agents (Chapter 1.3) and environment properties (Chapter 1.5).

The four phases

  1. Goal formulation. From the current situation and the performance measure, choose a goal: "be in Madurai tonight". A goal limits what the agent has to think about.
  2. Problem formulation. Decide which states and actions to consider: "drive from town to town", not "turn the steering wheel one degree".
  3. Search. Try sequences of actions in the model, without acting, until one reaches the goal (Unit 2).
  4. Execution. Carry out the actions. In a fully observable, deterministic, known world the agent can act with its "eyes closed" (an open-loop system); otherwise it must keep checking its percepts (closed-loop).

The five components of a problem

  • Initial state: where the agent starts, e.g. In(Chennai).
  • Actions(s): the actions applicable in state s, e.g. Actions(In(Chennai)) = {Go(Vellore), Go(Villupuram), Go(Puducherry)}.
  • Transition model Result(s, a): the state that action a leads to from s, e.g. Result(In(Chennai), Go(Vellore)) = In(Vellore). Any state reachable in one action is a successor.
  • Goal test: decides whether a state is a goal, either from a list (In(Madurai)) or by a property (checkmate).
  • Path cost: the sum of step costs c(s, a, s′) along a path, where s′ is the state reached; step costs are never negative. Here a step cost is road distance in km.

Together the initial state, actions and transition model define the state space: a graph whose nodes are states and whose arcs are actions. A solution is an action sequence from the initial state to a goal state; an optimal solution has the lowest path cost among all solutions.

Abstraction and a good formulation

Abstraction removes detail that does not matter to the goal. It is valid if every abstract solution can be expanded into a real one, and useful if each abstract action is easy to carry out. The same task can be formulated well or badly. In the 8-queens problem (place 8 queens on a chessboard so none attacks another), "add a queen to any empty square" gives 64 × 63 × … × 57 ≈ 1.8 × 1014 sequences to examine. "Add a queen to the leftmost empty column, on a square no queen attacks" gives only 2,057 states: the same problem, about 1011 times smaller.

Problem types and problem characteristics

The environment decides the problem type. Single-state: fully observable and deterministic, so the agent always knows its state and the solution is a fixed sequence. Sensorless (conformant): no sensors, so the agent reasons over a belief state, the set of states it might be in; in the vacuum world [Right, Suck, Left, Suck] cleans both squares from any of the 8 start states. Contingency: partially observable or nondeterministic, so the plan has branches ("if dirty, suck") and sensing happens during execution. Exploration: the state space is unknown and must be discovered by acting.

1.7 State-space search, control strategies and production systems

Once a problem is formulated, solving it means searching its state space for a path from the start to a goal. Classic AI organises that search as a production system: a set of if–then rules, a working memory holding the current state, and a control strategy that decides which rule to fire next.

You need: the five components of a problem from Chapter 1.6.

Searching a state space

Searching the state space builds a search tree: the root is the start state, the branches are paths tried so far. The same state can appear on many branches, so a careful search remembers visited states and skips repeats (graph search). Two numbers measure the size: the branching factor b, the number of successors of a state, and the depth d of the nearest goal. A tree explored to depth d has about bd nodes at its deepest level.

Data-driven and goal-driven search

  • Data-driven (forward) search starts from the given facts and applies rules to produce new states until it reaches a goal. Use it when all the data is given at the start, there are many possible goals, or a goal is hard to state in advance (configuring a computer, as R1/XCON did).
  • Goal-driven (backward) search starts from the goal, finds rules that could produce it, makes their conditions subgoals, and works back to known facts. Use it when the goal is given (proving a theorem, checking "is it dengue?"), when forward branching is large, or when the goal tells you which data to gather.

The branching factors decide. To check whether you descend from an ancestor 10 generations back, searching forward through 3 children per family visits 310 = 59,049 people; searching backward through 2 parents per person visits 210 = 1,024, about 58 times fewer. Search in the direction with the smaller branching factor.

Control strategies

Rich and Knight set two requirements. A control strategy must cause motion: always firing the first rule in the list can loop for ever (fill a jug, empty it, fill it). It must be systematic: miss no part of the space and search none twice without need. Breadth-first search (level by level) and depth-first search (one path deep, then back up) are the basic systematic strategies (Unit 2).

Strategies are also irrevocable or tentative. An irrevocable strategy never reconsiders a rule once fired (hill climbing). A tentative one keeps the chance to return: backtracking remembers choice points and goes back to the last one on failure; graph search keeps many paths open at once. A heuristic uses knowledge of the domain to try the more promising rule first.

Production systems

A production system has three parts: a rule base of productions, each "condition → action"; a working memory (global database) holding the current state; and a control system (rule interpreter) that runs the recognise–act cycle of Figure 1.7.1 until the goal test succeeds or no rule applies. When several rules match, the set of matching rules is the conflict set, and conflict resolution picks one: by rule order, refraction (do not fire the same rule on the same data twice), recency (prefer rules matching the newest facts) or specificity (prefer the rule with the more detailed condition).

Rich and Knight classify production systems by two properties. Monotonic: firing a rule never stops another rule that was also applicable at that time from being fired later. Partially commutative: if a sequence of rules turns state x into state y, every allowable reordering of those rules also turns x into y. A system with both is commutative.

1.8 Toy problems: tic-tac-toe, missionaries and cannibals, TSP

A toy problem is a small, exactly stated puzzle used to explain and compare problem-solving methods; a real-world problem is one people need solved, such as routing delivery vans. This short chapter formulates each in the form of Chapter 1.6.

Tic-tac-toe

State: a 3 × 3 board, each cell X, O or empty, plus whose turn it is. Initial state: empty board, X to move. Actions: put your mark in an empty cell. Terminal test: three in a row, or a full board. Utility: +1 win, 0 draw, −1 loss. There are 39 = 19,683 ways to fill the board, but only 5,478 are legal positions, and 255,168 different games can be played. Rich and Knight's first program stored a reply for each of the 19,683 boards: fast but rigid. Better programs look ahead with minimax (Unit 3).

Missionaries and cannibals

Three missionaries and three cannibals must cross a river in a boat holding one or two. On neither bank may cannibals outnumber missionaries who are present. State (m, c, b): missionaries and cannibals on the left bank, and the boat's side. Start (3, 3, L); goal (0, 0, R). Actions: carry 1M, 2M, 1C, 2C or 1M+1C. A state is safe when (m = 0 or m ≥ c) and (3 − m = 0 or 3 − m ≥ 3 − c). The shortest solution takes 11 crossings.

Travelling salesman problem (TSP)

Visit each of n cities exactly once and return to the start with the least total distance. State: the cities visited so far and the current city. Actions: go to an unvisited city. Goal test: all visited and back at the start. Path cost: total distance. With symmetric distances there are (n − 1)!/2 different tours: 12 for 5 cities, 181,440 for 10, about 6.1 × 1016 for 20. TSP is NP-hard, so large cases use heuristics such as nearest neighbour: always go to the closest unvisited city. Russell and Norvig count TSP as a real-world problem (circuit-board drilling, delivery routes).

  1. (a) n = 4 places: (4 − 1)!/2 = 6/2 = 3 tours.
  2. (b) R-A-B-C-R = 2 + 4 + 7 + 6 = 19 km. R-A-C-B-R = 2 + 5 + 7 + 3 = 17 km. R-B-A-C-R = 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 18 km.
  3. (c) From R the nearest is A (2 < 3 < 6). From A the nearest unvisited is B (4 < 5). Then C (7), then back to R (6): 2 + 4 + 7 + 6 = 19 km.

Answer. (a) 3 tours. (b) R → A → C → B → R, 17 km. (c) Nearest neighbour gives 19 km, the worst tour, 12% longer (19/17 ≈ 1.12): each greedy step looked cheap but left the long B–C road for later.

Exam traps

  • 39 = 19,683 counts board fillings, not legal positions (5,478) or games.
  • The missionaries-and-cannibals state must include the boat's side; check safety on both banks.
  • Use (n − 1)!/2 for symmetric TSP; (n − 1)! counts each tour twice, once in each direction.

Analyze Compare the three problems by number of agents and by what a solution is. 4 marks

Takeaways

  1. AI builds agents that act rationally: they choose the action with the best expected outcome under a performance measure. The Turing test, cognitive modelling and the laws of thought are the other three approaches.
  2. AI draws on eight older disciplines and has lived through two winters; learn the cause behind each turn of its history.
  3. Judge an AI application with numbers: on rare conditions, accuracy hides failure, so give sensitivity and precision. Every future benefit comes with a risk that needs a safeguard.
  4. Agent = architecture + program. The five agent programs (simple reflex, model-based, goal-based, utility-based, learning) each add one capability to the one before.
  5. Rationality depends on the performance measure, prior knowledge, available actions and the percept sequence. It is not omniscience and does not promise success.
  6. PEAS plus seven properties describe any task environment, and the properties decide the agent design.
  7. A problem has five components; a good formulation can shrink the state space enormously (8-queens: 1.8 × 1014 to 2,057).
  8. A production system is rules + working memory + control strategy. The strategy must cause motion and be systematic, and it decides the quality of the answer (8 water-jug steps instead of 6).
  9. Toy problems show both the formulation and the combinatorial explosion that the search algorithms of Unit 2 are built to tame.

Q15. Using the seven problem characteristics, explain why tic-tac-toe, the 8-puzzle and TSP need different solution methods. Give each formulation and the control strategy you would use. (12 marks; spans Chapters 1.6–1.8)

Outline: tic-tac-toe: irrecoverable, unpredictable opponent, solution is a strategy, game search (Unit 3). 8-puzzle: recoverable, predictable, solution is a path, systematic search with a heuristic, 181,440 reachable states. Q16. A vendor offers a retina-screening AI with "94.6% accuracy". As the hospital's AI officer, evaluate whether to deploy it, using the screening numbers and the idea of a performance measure. (8 marks; spans Chapters 1.2 and 1.4)

Outline: rebuild the table for 8% prevalence (TP 72, FN 8, FP 46, TN 874); precision 61%, sensitivity 90%; a do-nothing tool scores 92% accuracy; accuracy is a poor performance measure here; deploy as a first filter with doctor confirmation and yearly re-screening; monitor performance on local patients.

Glossary

Agent
Anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators.
Agent function
The mapping from every percept sequence to an action.
Agent program
The code that implements the agent function on the architecture.
AI winter
A period when AI results fell short of promises and funding was cut (mid-1970s, late 1980s).
Autonomy
The extent to which an agent's behaviour depends on its own experience rather than only on built-in knowledge.
Belief state
The set of physical states an agent might be in when it cannot observe fully.
Branching factor
The number of successors of a state; with depth d it sets the search size, about bd.
Combinatorial explosion
The very fast growth in the number of possibilities as a problem gets bigger.
Conflict set
All rules whose conditions match working memory in one cycle.
Control strategy
The method that decides which rule or state to try next; it must cause motion and be systematic.
Critic
The part of a learning agent that judges results against a fixed performance standard.
Data-driven search
Forward search from the given facts towards a goal.
Episodic environment
One in which each decision is independent of earlier ones.
Expected utility
The probability-weighted average of the utilities of an action's outcomes.
Goal-driven search
Backward search from the goal through subgoals to known facts.
Heuristic
Domain knowledge used to choose the more promising option first, with no guarantee.
Monotonic production system
One where firing a rule never prevents a rule that was also applicable from firing later.
Omniscience
Knowing the actual outcome of every action; not required for rationality.
Path cost
The sum of the step costs along a path.
PEAS
Performance measure, Environment, Actuators, Sensors: the specification of a task environment.
Percept sequence
The complete history of everything an agent has perceived.
Performance measure
The designer's score for the sequence of environment states an agent causes.
Precision
TP/(TP + FP): the share of positive alarms that are real.
Production system
A rule base, a working memory and a control strategy running the recognise–act cycle.
Rational agent
An agent that chooses the action expected to maximise its performance measure.
Sensitivity
TP/(TP + FN): the share of real cases that are caught; also called recall.
State space
All states reachable from the initial state, as a graph of states and actions.
Transition model
Result(s, a): the state that results from doing action a in state s.
Turing test
Turing's 1950 test: a machine passes if a judge chatting by text cannot tell it from a person.
Utility function
A function that gives each state a number measuring how desirable it is.
