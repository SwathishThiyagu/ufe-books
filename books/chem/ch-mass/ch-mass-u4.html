4.1 Vapour-liquid equilibrium, Raoult's law and relative volatility

At equilibrium, a liquid mixture and the vapour above it have fixed compositions at a given temperature and pressure. For an ideal binary mixture these compositions follow from the two pure-component vapour pressures and the total pressure. Distillation depends on this link: the vapour is richer in the more volatile component, so repeated vapour-liquid contacts separate the mixture. You use the same equilibrium relation to find whether a proposed separation is possible, to size flash drums, and to step stages on a y-x diagram.

You need: basic mole fraction and Dalton's law from chemistry; nothing from Unit 1 is needed, and 4.2 uses this equilibrium directly.

Liquid and vapour in equilibrium

Take a binary mixture of A and B. Mole fraction xA is moles of A in the liquid divided by total moles in the liquid; yA is the same in the vapour. P is total pressure. P°A and P°B are the vapour pressures of pure A and pure B at the same temperature. Some syllabi call this vapour-liquid equilibrium (VLE).

pA = xA P°A;  P = pA + pB

pA is the partial pressure of A (kPa); xA is liquid mole fraction; P°A is pure-A vapour pressure at the same temperature (kPa); P is total pressure (kPa).

Raoult's law gives pA from the liquid composition. Dalton's law adds the two partial pressures. Combine them for B as pB = (1 − xA)P°B, then solve for xA. The result is the bubble-point equation: a liquid of composition xA starts to boil at pressure P when the temperature makes the two P° values satisfy it. The vapour composition follows from yA = pA/P.

xA = (P − P°B)/(P°A − P°B);  yA = xA P°A/P

xA is liquid mole fraction of A; yA is vapour mole fraction of A; P is total pressure (kPa); P°A and P°B are pure-component vapour pressures (kPa).

For a two-phase mixture, P must lie between P°B and P°A when A is the more volatile component. If P is greater than P°A, the mixture is all liquid. If P is less than P°B, it is all vapour. In design you use the liquid x to get the equilibrium y. Raoult's law uses x, not y.

Relative volatility and the y-x curve

Relative volatility αAB measures how easily A leaves B. It is the ratio of the vapour-to-liquid ratios of the two components. For an ideal mixture obeying Raoult's law, the pressure ratios simplify to the ratio of pure-component vapour pressures.

αAB = (yA/xA)/(yB/xB) = P°A/P°B

αAB is relative volatility of A with respect to B (dimensionless); yA, yB are vapour mole fractions; xA, xB are liquid mole fractions; P°A, P°B are pure-component vapour pressures at the same temperature (kPa).

If α > 1, A is more volatile and the vapour is richer in A. Rearranging the definition gives yA = α xA/[1 + (α − 1)xA]. For α = 3, the equilibrium curve starts at (0,0), passes through (0.5,0.75), and ends at (1,1). It lies above the diagonal y = x. The gap between the curve and the diagonal is the driving force for separation. On a y-x diagram, horizontal and vertical steps between this curve and an operating line give theoretical stages (see 4.4). On a T-x-y diagram at fixed P, the lower curve is the bubble-point line and the upper curve is the dew-point line; the area between them is two-phase. A more volatile component has a lower boiling point and a higher y.

Real systems and azeotropes

Real mixtures depart from Raoult's law because liquid interactions are not ideal. A simple correction is pA = γA xA P°A, where γA is the activity coefficient; γA = 1 for an ideal solution. Predicting γA from models belongs to thermodynamics, not to this chapter.

A real y-x curve can cross the diagonal. At the crossing point xA = yA and α = 1. That composition is an azeotrope. At an azeotrope the vapour and liquid have the same composition, so ordinary distillation cannot pass it; a separating agent is needed (see 4.9). Common examples are the minimum-boiling ethanol-water azeotrope and the maximum-boiling nitric acid-water azeotrope.

For real systems, α changes with x, especially near an azeotrope. Use measured VLE for design when the mixture is non-ideal; treat Raoult's law as a starting estimate for similar components. These same equations support flash calculations in 4.2, where a feed is partly vaporised and the equilibrium gives the vapour and liquid products.

Worked example

Question. A binary ideal mixture has P°A = 120 kPa and P°B = 40 kPa at the column pressure P = 101.3 kPa. Find the equilibrium liquid mole fraction xA, vapour mole fraction yA, and relative volatility α.

  1. Write the data: P = 101.3 kPa, P°A = 120 kPa, P°B = 40 kPa.
  2. Use the bubble-point equation: xA = (101.3 − 40)/(120 − 40) = 61.3/80 = 0.76625, which rounds to 0.766.
  3. Find the vapour composition: yA = xA P°A/P = 0.76625 × 120/101.3 = 91.95/101.3 = 0.9077, which rounds to 0.908.
  4. Find relative volatility: α = P°A/P°B = 120/40 = 3.0.

Answer. At P = 101.3 kPa, xA = 0.766, yA = 0.908, and α = 3.0.

Exam traps

  • Mixing x and y: Raoult's law uses liquid x; vapour y comes out of yA = xA P°A/P.
  • Using P° values at a different temperature: both pure-component vapour pressures must be at the equilibrium temperature.
  • Calling α constant for real mixtures: α changes with x, and α = 1 at an azeotrope.
  • Forgetting the pressure condition: the bubble-point equation needs P between P°B and P°A for a two-phase mixture when A is more volatile.
  • Using mass fraction in place of mole fraction: all VLE equations here use mole fractions.

4.2 Flash distillation (equilibrium vaporization)

Flash distillation splits a feed into vapour and liquid in one equilibrium stage. The feed is heated, or its pressure dropped, until it partly vaporises. A drum lets vapour leave the top and liquid settle at the bottom. Both products leave in equilibrium. No trays and no reflux: the split is coarse but cheap. Refineries use it before crude distillation, gas plants remove heavy ends, and desalination plants run multi-stage flash.

You need 4.1 Vapour-liquid equilibrium, Raoult's law and relative volatility for K values and mole fractions.

One drum, one stage

A flash drum does in one step what a column does in many. It contacts liquid and vapour once, lets them reach equilibrium, and removes them separately. Vapour is richer in the light component; liquid is richer in the heavy component.

  1. Feed at F kmol/h and mole fraction z enters a heater.
  2. A valve drops the pressure, taking the feed into the two-phase region.
  3. The mixture enters the drum; vapour disengages and rises, liquid collects at the bottom.
  4. A mist eliminator stops droplets leaving with vapour.
  5. Vapour and liquid leave at drum T and P.
  • Isothermal flash: heat added so both phases leave at one T.
  • Adiabatic flash: no heat added; vapour takes latent heat from liquid, so temperature falls through the drum.
  • Some syllabi call this equilibrium vaporization.

Balances and the K value

F is feed flow (kmol/h), z its mole fraction, V and L vapour and liquid flows (kmol/h), and y and x their mole fractions. V/F is the vapour fraction: the share of feed leaving as vapour.

F z = V y + L x and F = V + L

F feed flow (kmol/h); z feed mole fraction; V, L vapour and liquid flows (kmol/h); y, x their mole fractions.

At equilibrium y_i = K_i x_i. K_i = y_i/x_i is dimensionless and depends on temperature, pressure and composition. For an ideal mixture K_i = Pisat/P (4.1).

  1. Divide by F: z = (V/F)y + (1 − V/F)x.
  2. Put y = Kx: z = x[(V/F)K + 1 − V/F].
  3. Solve for liquid: x_i = z_i/[1 + (V/F)(K_i − 1)].

Degrees of freedom decide the drum. Fix T and P: the K values are fixed, so V/F follows — the isothermal flash. Fix the feed enthalpy and the drum pressure instead: no heat is added or removed, and the drum temperature and V/F come out together from the energy balance and Rachford-Rice — the adiabatic flash. At a given P you cannot pin both T and V/F.

Rachford-Rice: finding the vapour fraction

The unknown is usually V/F. Both sets of mole fractions must sum to one; combining the sums gives one equation in one unknown.

Σ z_i (K_i − 1) / [1 + (V/F)(K_i − 1)] = 0

sum over every component i; z_i feed mole fraction; K_i K value at drum T and P; V/F vapour fraction, the unknown.

  1. Σ x_i = 1 gives Σ z_i/[1 + ψ(K_i − 1)] = 1, with ψ = V/F.
  2. Σ y_i = 1 gives Σ z_i K_i/[1 + ψ(K_i − 1)] = 1.
  3. Subtract the first from the second: Σ z_i(K_i − 1)/[1 + ψ(K_i − 1)] = 0.

The left side falls steadily as ψ rises, so when two phases exist there is a single root with 0 < ψ < 1; at the bubble point the root is ψ = 0 and at the dew point ψ = 1. Find the root by trial or Newton iteration (numerical detail in 4.8). For two components the equation is linear in ψ, because z_B = 1 − z_A:

V/F = −[z_A(K_A − 1) + z_B(K_B − 1)] / [(K_A − 1)(K_B − 1)]

z_A, z_B are feed mole fractions summing to 1; K_A, K_B are the two K values at the drum T and P.

Test for two phases first. Both exist only if Σ z_i K_i > 1 and Σ z_i/K_i > 1. If Σ z_i K_i ≤ 1 the feed is below its bubble point and only liquid leaves; if Σ z_i/K_i ≤ 1 it is above its dew point and only vapour leaves.

Worked example

Question. A flash drum receives F = 100 kmol/h of a binary feed with z_A = 0.50, at a temperature and pressure where K_A = 2.0 and K_B = 0.5. The vapour fraction is V/F = 0.50. Find V, L, x_A and y_A, and show that the vapour fraction satisfies the Rachford-Rice equation.

  1. V = (V/F)F = 0.50 × 100 = 50 kmol/h. L = F − V = 100 − 50 = 50 kmol/h.
  2. Two-phase test: Σ z K = 0.50(2.0) + 0.50(0.5) = 1.25 > 1; Σ z/K = 0.50/2.0 + 0.50/0.5 = 1.25 > 1. Two phases exist.
  3. x_A = z_A/[1 + (V/F)(K_A − 1)] = 0.50/[1 + 0.50(2.0 − 1)] = 0.333.
  4. y_A = K_A x_A = 2.0 × 0.333 = 0.667. Check with B: x_B = 1 − 0.333 = 0.667, y_B = 0.5 × 0.667 = 0.333; x_A + x_B = 1.000 and y_A + y_B = 1.000.
  5. Rachford-Rice with ψ = 0.50: 0.50(2.0 − 1)/[1 + 0.50(2.0 − 1)] = 0.50/1.50 = 0.3333; second term 0.50(0.5 − 1)/[1 + 0.50(0.5 − 1)] = −0.25/0.75 = −0.3333; sum = 0.
  6. Overall balance: V y_A + L x_A = 50(0.667) + 50(0.333) = 50.0 kmol/h, and F z_A = 100 × 0.50 = 50.0 kmol/h.

Answer. V = 50 kmol/h and L = 50 kmol/h, with x_A = 0.333 and y_A = 0.667; the Rachford-Rice sum is zero at V/F = 0.50.

Exam traps

  • Using x = z. The feed composition z is not liquid x; x_i = z_i/[1 + (V/F)(K_i − 1)].
  • Treating K as constant. K depends on T and P.
  • Skipping the two-phase test. Solve only when Σ z_i K_i > 1 and Σ z_i/K_i > 1; otherwise one phase and no root between 0 and 1.
  • Mixing mass and moles. z, x, y, K and V/F are molar; convert kg/h to kmol/h first.
  • Confusing flash with simple distillation. In flash vapour stays in contact until equilibrium; in 4.3 vapour is drawn off as it forms.

Analyze At a proposed drum condition Σ z_i K_i works out to 0.85. What happens in the drum and what do you change? 4 marks

Σ z_i K_i = 0.85 < 1 means the drum is below the bubble point of the feed: it stays all liquid, V = 0, no vapour leaves, and there is no Rachford-Rice root between 0 and 1. Analyze The drum pressure is raised at constant temperature. Explain the effect on V/F and on the product compositions. 4 marks

Raising P lowers every K, since K_i = Pisat/P. Lower K means less vapour, so V/F falls. Both products become closer in composition to each other and to the feed: the vapour gets heavier, the liquid gets lighter, and the separation weakens.

4.3 Simple (differential) and steam distillation

Simple distillation is the oldest batch separation: heat a liquid charge, remove the vapour as it forms, and condense it. Because the vapour is richer in the more volatile component, the pot liquid changes during the run. A differential balance gives the Rayleigh equation, which connects the starting and ending liquid amounts and compositions. Steam distillation uses a second immiscible liquid, usually water, to lower the boiling temperature for heat-sensitive organics such as essential oils. Both methods are common in small pharmaceutical, perfume and solvent-recovery units.

You need: 4.1 VLE and relative volatility and basic integration.

The batch still and the Rayleigh equation

Simple distillation is a batch operation. A known liquid charge sits in a still pot. Heat boils it. The vapour leaves at once, is condensed, and is collected as distillate. No liquid reflux returns from the condenser. Because the more volatile component leaves first, the liquid in the pot becomes richer in the less volatile component. The vapour composition therefore changes with time. The old name is differential distillation: the change is followed over infinitesimal steps, not in one flash stage (compare 4.2).

At some instant the pot holds L mol of liquid with mole fraction x of the more volatile component. Remove a small amount dD = −dL of vapour. That vapour has mole fraction y, in equilibrium with x at that instant. A balance on the more volatile component gives L dx = (y − x) dL. Rearrange to dL/L = dx/(y − x). Integrate from the initial charge (L₁, x₁) to the final residue (L₂, x₂):

ln(L₁/L₂) = ∫x₂x₁ dx/(y − x)

L₁ initial liquid moles; L₂ final residue moles; x₁ initial liquid mole fraction of the more volatile component; x₂ final liquid mole fraction; y equilibrium vapour mole fraction at the local x.

The distillate collected is D = L₁ − L₂. To use the equation, y must come from the VLE curve at the same x (see 4.1). The integral is evaluated graphically or numerically when α is not constant.

Constant relative volatility

For an ideal binary pair, α = (y/(1 − y))/(x/(1 − x)). It is the ratio of volatility of the more volatile component to the less volatile one. Rearranged: y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x]. Substitute this y in the Rayleigh integral. For constant α, the integral has a closed form:

ln(L₁/L₂) = [ln(x₁/x₂) − α ln((1 − x₁)/(1 − x₂))]/(α − 1)

α relative volatility (dimensionless); L₁, L₂, x₁, x₂ as in the Rayleigh equation. The formula needs α ≠ 1.

The equation gives L₂ for a chosen x₂; D = L₁ − L₂. It is useful for quick batch still sizing and for checking a simulation. Average distillate composition, if needed, comes from the overall balance: xD,avg = (L₁x₁ − L₂x₂)/D. For a batch column with reflux, the simple Rayleigh picture no longer applies; trays and reflux change the relation between vapour and liquid. That case is named here and treated with column methods in 4.7.

Steam distillation

Some organic liquids are heat sensitive: a high boiling point damages them before they distil. Steam distillation solves this by adding water as a second liquid phase. Water and the organic are immiscible. Each liquid keeps its own vapour pressure, p°water and p°organic, almost independent of the other. The mixture boils when the sum reaches the total pressure:

P = p°water + p°organic

P total pressure in kPa or bar; p°water vapour pressure of pure water at the boiling temperature; p°organic vapour pressure of pure organic at the same temperature.

At 1 atm, boiling occurs at a temperature below the normal boiling point of each pure liquid. Steam bubbles through the organic, carries organic vapour, and the mixed vapour condenses. The condensate separates into two layers, so the organic is recovered by decantation.

Steam consumption follows from the vapour ratio. If the vapour is saturated and ideal, moles steam per mole organic ≈ p°water/p°organic. Practical units add heat losses and incomplete saturation. The method suits essential oils, perfumes and some edible-oil deodorization. It needs immiscibility: a soluble solvent changes the VLE and does not give the simple sum of vapour pressures (compare 4.9).

Worked example

Question. A batch still is charged with L₁ = 100 mol of an ideal binary liquid at x₁ = 0.50 mole fraction of the more volatile component. The relative volatility is constant at α = 2.0. Distillation stops when the residue liquid reaches x₂ = 0.20. Find L₁/L₂, the final residue L₂, and the distillate D in moles.

  1. Use the constant-α Rayleigh form: ln(L₁/L₂) = [ln(x₁/x₂) − α ln((1 − x₁)/(1 − x₂))]/(α − 1).
  2. Evaluate the first logarithm: ln(0.50/0.20) = ln(2.50) = 0.9163.
  3. Evaluate the second logarithm: ln((1 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.20)) = ln(0.50/0.80) = ln(0.625) = −0.4700.
  4. Put α = 2.0: numerator = 0.9163 − 2.0(−0.4700) = 1.8563. Divide by α − 1 = 1.0, so ln(L₁/L₂) = 1.8563.
  5. Exponentiate: L₁/L₂ = e^1.8563 = 6.40.
  6. Final residue: L₂ = L₁/6.40 = 100/6.40 = 15.6 mol.
  7. Distillate: D = L₁ − L₂ = 100 − 15.6 = 84.4 mol.

Answer. L₁/L₂ = 6.40, final residue L₂ ≈ 15.6 mol, and distillate D ≈ 84.4 mol.

Exam traps

  • Using the average distillate composition in place of the instantaneous equilibrium y. The Rayleigh integral needs y at each local x.
  • Reversing the limits x₁ and x₂ in the integral, or writing L₂/L₁ when the equation is L₁/L₂.
  • Forgetting that the constant-α formula fails at α = 1; there y = x and no separation occurs.
  • Treating steam distillation as ordinary distillation of a water–organic solution. It needs two immiscible liquid phases; a soluble solvent changes the vapour pressures.
  • Unit slip: α and mole fractions are dimensionless; L₁, L₂, D are moles, not mass, unless the same mass basis is used consistently.

4.4 McCabe-Thiele method for binary distillation

The McCabe-Thiele method counts the theoretical stages of a binary distillation column on a y-x diagram. It replaces a column by a staircase between the equilibrium curve and two straight operating lines. The equilibrium curve comes from VLE; the operating lines come from reflux ratio and material balance. A feed line fixes where the two sections meet. Engineers use it for a quick tray count before rigorous simulation. It assumes constant molar overflow, so the operating lines are straight.

You need VLE and relative volatility, flash balances and simple distillation balances.

Rectifying line, stripping line and q-line

In the rectifying section above the feed, liquid L flows down and vapour V rises. The reflux ratio is R = L/D, where D is the distillate molar flow. Constant molar overflow keeps L and V constant in this section. A balance around the top gives the rectifying operating line.

y = (R/(R+1))x + x_D/(R+1)

y is vapour mole fraction of the more volatile component; x is liquid mole fraction; R = L/D is the reflux ratio; x_D is distillate mole fraction. The line passes through (x_D, x_D).

Below the feed, the stripping section has its own operating line. It passes through (x_B, x_B), where x_B is the bottoms mole fraction, and through the point where the rectifying line meets the feed line. A balance around the bottom gives it.

y = (L′/V′)x − (B/V′)x_B

L′ and V′ are stripping-section liquid and vapour molar flows; B is bottoms molar flow; x_B is bottoms mole fraction.

q measures the thermal state of the feed. It is the heat needed to turn 1 mol of feed into saturated vapour, divided by the molar latent heat. For a feed between its bubble point and its dew point, q also equals the moles of liquid formed per mole of feed. The feed line, or q-line, is:

y = [q/(q−1)]x − x_F/(q−1)

x_F is feed mole fraction; q is the feed thermal state defined above; the slope of the line is q/(q−1). The q-line passes through (x_F, x_F).

  • q = 1: saturated liquid, vertical line x = x_F.
  • q = 0: saturated vapour, horizontal line y = x_F.
  • 0 < q < 1: two-phase feed, line slopes down from (x_F, x_F).
  • q > 1: subcooled liquid, slope q/(q−1) is positive and greater than 1.
  • q < 0: superheated vapour, slope q/(q−1) is positive and less than 1.

The q-line is not an operating line. It only locates the intersection P of the two operating lines.

Staircase between equilibrium and operating lines

Use a y-x diagram. The equilibrium curve for constant relative volatility α is:

y* = α x / [1 + (α−1)x]

y* is vapour mole fraction in equilibrium with liquid x; α is relative volatility; x is liquid mole fraction. Solved for x, this gives x* = y / [α − (α−1)y], the liquid in equilibrium with a vapour of composition y.

Draw the diagonal y = x. Mark x_D, x_F and x_B on it. Draw the rectifying line from (x_D, x_D) to the q-line. Draw the q-line through (x_F, x_F) with slope q/(q−1); for a saturated liquid feed this is the vertical line x = x_F. Its intersection with the rectifying line is P. Draw the stripping line from (x_B, x_B) through P.

  1. Start at (x_D, x_D).
  2. Move horizontally left to the equilibrium curve. This gives x*, the liquid mole fraction in equilibrium with the vapour of composition y at that height.
  3. Move vertically down to the operating line in use. Keep the rectifying line while x is above x_P, the x-coordinate of P. When a horizontal step takes x below x_P, switch to the stripping line. Only for a saturated liquid feed (q = 1) does x_P equal x_F.
  4. Repeat horizontal to equilibrium, vertical to operating line.
  5. Stop when x ≤ x_B. Each horizontal step to the equilibrium curve is one equilibrium contact; the vertical move only sets up the next step, and the final step may be horizontal alone.

The final step that crosses x_B includes the partial reboiler. Do not add the reboiler again. The number of real trays needed is greater than the theoretical stage count, because tray efficiency is below one (see tray efficiency).

Why the lines are straight; use and limits

Constant molar overflow assumes equal molar latent heats, negligible heat of mixing, and negligible sensible heat. One mole of vapour condensing then supplies one mole of liquid vaporising. L and V stay constant in each section, so the operating lines are straight.

The method gives the theoretical stage count at a chosen R. A lower R moves the operating lines closer to the equilibrium curve, so more stages are needed. At minimum reflux the steps never reach x_B (see reflux ratio). If CMO fails, use the enthalpy method (see Ponchon-Savarit).

The construction is used for binary columns, for checking rigorous simulation results, and for seeing how feed condition shifts the operating lines.

4.5 Reflux ratio: minimum, total and optimum

Reflux is the liquid returned from the condenser to the column. The reflux ratio R = L/D compares returned liquid L to distillate D. It sets the trade-off: more reflux means fewer stages but more steam and cooling water. At minimum reflux the stage count is infinite; at total reflux it is minimum but no product leaves. The optimum is near 1.2 to 1.5 times minimum reflux, where total annual cost is lowest. Plants use it to set column height and steam use.

You need: 4.1 for α and equilibrium, and 4.4 for McCabe-Thiele operating lines.

Reflux ratio and the operating line

Liquid L flows down the column and vapour flows up. Some top liquid returns from the condenser as reflux L; the rest leaves as distillate D. The reflux ratio is R = L/D, a pure number. Here D is distillate molar flow and L is reflux molar flow. On the y-x diagram the rectifying operating line has slope R/(R+1) and y-intercept x_D/(R+1). y is vapour mole fraction and x is liquid mole fraction of the more volatile component. Higher R raises the slope toward 1, so the line moves toward the diagonal y = x. Each stage then gives a larger composition change and fewer stages are needed. The cost is more reboiler steam and more condenser cooling water. R is therefore an economic choice, not only a graphical one. McCabe-Thiele uses this line. A low R saves energy but needs a taller, costlier column. The choice balances both. That balance matters.

Minimum reflux and the pinch

Lower R moves the operating line away from the diagonal, toward the equilibrium curve. At minimum reflux R_min the operating line first touches the equilibrium curve. The touch point is the pinch. There the driving force y* - y is zero, so a stage cannot change composition. Stepping stages from x_D to x_B stalls, and the required number of stages becomes infinite. R_min is a lower bound, never a working reflux.

For a saturated-liquid feed (q = 1): R_min = (x_D - y_F*)/(y_F* - x_F). x_D is distillate mole fraction, x_F is feed liquid mole fraction, and y_F* is the equilibrium vapour mole fraction in contact with liquid of composition x_F. Get y_F* from the equilibrium curve or from α (4.1). The formula assumes constant molar overflow and saturated-liquid feed. Other feed conditions shift the pinch and need the q-line. Use the q-line from 4.4 for other feeds. The pinch is where the operating line and equilibrium curve meet.

Total reflux, Fenske, and the optimum

At total reflux no distillate is taken: all condensed vapour returns as liquid, R → ∞. The operating lines coincide with the diagonal y = x. This gives the minimum stage count N_min for a required separation. For constant relative volatility α, Fenske gives N_min = log[(x_D/(1-x_D))((1-x_B)/x_B)] / log α. x_D and x_B are distillate and bottoms mole fractions of the more volatile component; α is relative volatility (4.1). N_min is valid only at total reflux, not at the chosen R.

Real operation needs product, so R is finite. Capital cost falls as R rises because fewer stages are needed. Energy cost rises because more vapour is boiled and condensed. Total annual cost is U-shaped. The usual optimum is R_opt ≈ 1.2 to 1.5 R_min. The figure shows an illustrative curve with its minimum near 1.3 R_min. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail. Gilliland shortcuts use N_min and R_min for stages at R_opt (see 4.8).

Worked example

Question. A binary column has α = 2.5, saturated-liquid feed x_F = 0.40, distillate x_D = 0.95, bottoms x_B = 0.05. Find R_min, R_opt = 1.3 R_min, and N_min by Fenske.

  1. Equilibrium vapour at x_F = 0.40: y_F* = αx_F/[1+(α-1)x_F] = 2.5×0.40/[1+1.5×0.40] = 1.00/1.60 = 0.625.
  2. R_min = (x_D - y_F*)/(y_F* - x_F) = (0.95 - 0.625)/(0.625 - 0.40) = 0.325/0.225 = 1.444 ≈ 1.44.
  3. R_opt = 1.3 × 1.44 = 1.872 ≈ 1.88.
  4. Fenske ratio = (0.95/0.05)(0.95/0.05) = 19×19 = 361.
  5. N_min = log(361)/log(2.5) = 2.5575/0.3979 = 6.43 ≈ 6.4 theoretical stages.

Answer. y_F* = 0.625; R_min = 1.44; R_opt = 1.88; N_min = 6.4 theoretical stages at total reflux.

Exam traps

  • Using R_min as the working reflux: stages become infinite at R_min.
  • Reporting Fenske N_min as the stage count at R_opt; Fenske applies only at total reflux.
  • Forgetting feed condition: the given R_min formula is for saturated liquid (q = 1).
  • Mixing x_F and y_F*: y_F* is equilibrium vapour at x_F.
  • Using α upside down or a mole fraction of the wrong component.

4.6 Ponchon-Savarit enthalpy-concentration method

Ponchon-Savarit designs binary distillation with an enthalpy-composition diagram instead of a y-x diagram. It drops the constant molar overflow assumption, so it handles unequal latent heats, concentrated solutions and large heat effects. Two curves, saturated-vapour enthalpy H against y and saturated-liquid enthalpy h against x, carry both material and energy balances. A fixed difference point for each section replaces the operating line. Chemical and petrochemical columns use this check when McCabe-Thiele is too rough.

You need: 4.1 VLE for equilibrium tie lines, 4.4 operating lines, and basic enthalpy balances from thermodynamics.

Two enthalpy curves and a fixed point

McCabe-Thiele assumes constant molar overflow: every mole of vapour condensing sends one mole of liquid down. That holds when the two components have equal latent heats and the column loses no heat. Real mixtures break this rule. Ponchon-Savarit keeps the energy balance honestly.

The diagram has two enthalpy curves. H is the enthalpy of saturated vapour in kJ per mole of mixture, plotted against vapour mole fraction y. h is the enthalpy of saturated liquid in kJ per mole of mixture, plotted against liquid mole fraction x. Both x and y refer to the more volatile component. The vertical gap H − h at equilibrium is the latent heat of vaporisation; it need not be constant.

A difference point replaces the operating line. For the rectifying section the point is ΔD. Its abscissa is the distillate composition xD. Its ordinate is an enthalpy HΔD, found from the reflux ratio and the two enthalpy curves. All rectifying operating lines pass through ΔD. This single fixed point carries the section's material and energy balances together.

Rectifying difference point ΔD

For the rectifying section, take an envelope from the top tray through the condenser. The vapour rate is V, the liquid reflux rate is L, and the distillate rate is D. The reflux ratio is R = L/D. When HV and hL do not change from tray to tray, the balance gives the ordinate of ΔD.

HΔD = HV + R(HV − hL)

HΔD is the ordinate of the rectifying difference point in kJ/mol; HV is the saturated-vapour enthalpy in kJ/mol; hL is the saturated-liquid reflux enthalpy in kJ/mol; R = L/D is the reflux ratio, with L the liquid returned to the column and D the distillate, both in mol/s.

The difference point is (xD, HΔD). It is a construction point, not a tray. It usually sits above the saturated-vapour line because the condenser removes heat. A larger R raises HΔD: more liquid reflux returns to the column, so more vapour must be condensed and the enthalpy difference grows.

  1. Plot H against y and h against x on the same axes.
  2. Mark ΔD at (xD, HΔD).
  3. Start at the reflux liquid (xD, hL) on the h curve.
  4. Join ΔD to that liquid point and extend the line to cut the H curve. The cut is the vapour leaving the top tray.
  5. Draw the equilibrium tie line from that vapour point to the h curve. This gives the liquid leaving the tray, x1. Repeat with the new liquid point.

Each line from ΔD to a saturated-liquid point is the operating line for that tray. Its cut with the H curve gives the vapour entering that tray. The equilibrium tie line then moves to the liquid leaving the tray.

Stripping point and the McCabe-Thiele limit

The stripping section has its own difference point ΔB at xB, the bottoms composition. With constant HV and hL, its ordinate is HΔB = hL − (V′/B)(HV − hL). Here V′ is the boil-up vapour rate and B is the bottoms rate, both mol/s. V′/B is the boil-up ratio. This point lies below the h curve. Stripping operating lines are straight lines through ΔB.

McCabe-Thiele is the special case of equal latent heats. Put HV and hL horizontal with constant gap λ = HV − hL. For a liquid point (x, hL), the line from ΔD cuts the H line at the y-x operating line.

y = (R/(R+1))x + xD/(R+1)

y is the vapour mole fraction on the McCabe-Thiele operating line; x is the liquid mole fraction; R is the reflux ratio; xD is the distillate mole fraction. This is the rectifying line from 4.4.

So the y-x operating line returns. Real columns with sloped H or h curves need Ponchon-Savarit; columns with nearly constant enthalpies can use McCabe-Thiele.

Worked example

Question. A binary distillation column has a total condenser. The saturated-vapour enthalpy is HV = 50 kJ/mol and the saturated-liquid enthalpy is hL = 20 kJ/mol. Both stay constant over the working range. The distillate composition is xD = 0.95 mole fraction of the more volatile component. The reflux ratio is R = 2.0. Find the ordinate HΔD of the rectifying difference point ΔD. State its coordinates on the enthalpy-composition diagram.

  1. Write the formula: HΔD = HV + R(HV − hL).
  2. Subtract the liquid enthalpy from the vapour enthalpy: HV − hL = 50 − 20 = 30 kJ/mol.
  3. Multiply by the reflux ratio: R(HV − hL) = 2.0 × 30 = 60 kJ/mol.
  4. Add the vapour enthalpy: HΔD = 50 + 60 = 110 kJ/mol.
  5. The coordinates are (xD, HΔD) = (0.95, 110 kJ/mol). The point lies above the vapour line because 110 kJ/mol > 50 kJ/mol.

Answer. The rectifying difference point is at x = 0.95 and enthalpy 110 kJ/mol.

Exam traps

  • Putting ΔD on the equilibrium curve. ΔD is fixed by xD and R; it is usually above the H curve.
  • Using H and h per mole of one component. They are per mole of mixture; a mole of mixture changes composition.
  • Writing R = L/V. The reflux ratio is R = L/D, and V = L + D.
  • Treating HΔD as only condenser duty. HΔD = HV + R(HV − hL) is an enthalpy difference per mole of distillate; it includes reflux enthalpy.
  • Placing ΔB above the h curve. It normally lies below because the reboiler adds heat.

4.7 Tray and packed columns; tray efficiency

A real distillation column is not a chain of perfect equilibrium stages. It is a vessel full of trays or packing where vapour and liquid meet for a short time. The vapour leaves a tray without reaching equilibrium with the liquid. Tray efficiency measures this gap. It converts the theoretical stages from McCabe-Thiele or Ponchon-Savarit into the actual trays a fabricator must supply. Packed columns use HETP for the same purpose. Every column in a refinery, a chemical plant or an ethanol unit depends on this step between the drawing board and the steel.

You need: 4.4 theoretical stages; 4.5 reflux ratio; and Unit 3 packed tower HTU/NTU for packed columns.

Trays: how vapour meets liquid

A tray column is a vertical shell with horizontal plates. Liquid flows across each plate and down a downcomer to the plate below. Vapour rises through openings in the plate and bubbles through the liquid. The plate holds a frothy mixture, so the two phases touch over a large area. The ideal stage assumption says the vapour leaving a plate is in equilibrium with the liquid leaving it. A real plate falls short of that.

  • Sieve tray: plain holes in the plate. Cheap, low pressure drop, but a narrow range of vapour rates before liquid weeps through the holes.
  • Bubble-cap tray: a cap sits over each riser and traps a liquid seal. It works at low vapour rates and gives good turn-down, at higher cost and pressure drop.
  • Valve tray: a flap over each hole lifts as vapour flow rises. It opens with load, so it balances capacity and turn-down at moderate cost.

Turn-down is the ratio of maximum to minimum vapour rate at which the tray still works. Trays suit columns with large liquid loads, dirty service that would block packing, and cases where you want side withdrawals. The number of real trays is larger than the theoretical count. The next sections give two measures of that penalty.

Packed columns and HETP

Packing is a bed of solid shapes that gives a wetted surface. Liquid runs down the surface as a film; vapour rises through the voids. There are no plates and no downcomers. The column has a liquid distributor at the top and a support grid at the bottom.

  • Random packing: small hollow or saddle-shaped pieces dumped into the column. Raschig rings, Pall rings and Berl saddles are common. Cheap, but the pieces settle and can channel liquid.
  • Structured packing: corrugated metal or plastic sheets stacked in a regular pattern. Low pressure drop per stage, so it suits vacuum columns and heat-sensitive products. It costs more and needs a good distributor.

Packed columns are rated by HETP, the height equivalent to a theoretical plate. HETP has units of length, usually metres. A packing with HETP = 0.5 m needs 0.5 m of bed to do the work of one ideal stage. Then packed height Z = Nt × HETP. The stage idea is replaced by transfer units in Unit 3: Z = HTU × NTU. Use HTU/NTU when you have mass transfer coefficients; use HETP when the vendor supplies test data.

Packing wins when pressure drop must be low, the liquid foams, or the column is small. Trays win when the liquid rate is high, the service is fouling, or the column needs side withdrawals. Both need careful hydraulic design; a packed tower also needs a distributor that wets the whole cross-section.

Overall and Murphree efficiency

The overall tray efficiency Eo compares the ideal stage count with the real tray count. Let Nt be the theoretical stages from McCabe-Thiele and Na the actual trays installed. Some syllabi call Eo the column efficiency.

Eo = Nt / Na

Eo is the overall tray efficiency (dimensionless); Nt is the number of theoretical stages; Na is the number of actual trays. Rearranged: Na = Nt / Eo.

Eo is a whole-column number. It hides the local contact on one tray. The Murphree vapour efficiency EM looks at a single tray n. Vapour enters tray n from tray n+1 below with mole fraction yn+1. Vapour leaves tray n upward with yn. The liquid leaves tray n with composition xn. The vapour that would be in equilibrium with that leaving liquid is yn*.

EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1)

EM is the Murphree vapour efficiency (dimensionless); yn is the vapour mole fraction leaving tray n upward; yn+1 is the vapour mole fraction entering tray n from below; yn* is the vapour mole fraction in equilibrium with the liquid leaving tray n.

Rearrange to find the leaving vapour: yn = yn+1 + EM(yn* − yn+1). If EM = 1, the tray reaches equilibrium. If EM = 0, the vapour passes unchanged. EM is not the same as Eo. Eo includes many trays, liquid mixing, entrainment and the slope of the equilibrium curve. Use EM per tray; use Eo to count trays.

Three hydraulic limits set the useful operating range. Weeping: at low vapour rate, liquid drips through the holes instead of bubbling, so contact falls. Flooding: at high vapour rate, the vapour holds liquid on the tray and the downcomer cannot carry it away; the column chokes. Entrainment: high vapour speed throws liquid droplets upward into the tray above, carrying heavy components to the top. A tray must run between weeping and flooding. The vapour rate at flooding sets the column diameter.

Worked example

Question. A binary distillation column needs Nt = 10 theoretical stages. The overall tray efficiency is Eo = 0.60. On one tray, vapour entering from below has yn+1 = 0.30. Exam traps

  • Dividing the wrong way: actual trays = theoretical / Eo. An efficiency below one must increase the count, never decrease it.
  • yn* is equilibrium with the liquid leaving tray n, not with the liquid entering the tray. Using the incoming liquid gives a false driving force.
  • Eo and EM are not interchangeable. Eo counts whole-column trays; EM is a local vapour ratio on one tray.
  • HETP has units of length. Multiplying Nt by HETP gives packed height in metres, not a number of trays.
  • Weeping, flooding and entrainment are hydraulic limits, not efficiency values. A high EM does not excuse a column that floods.

4.8 Multicomponent distillation: Fenske, Underwood and Gilliland

Multicomponent distillation separates three or more volatile components. Binary McCabe-Thiele no longer works because every component distributes between distillate and bottoms. Shortcut methods give a quick design: choose two key components, use Fenske for minimum stages, Underwood for minimum reflux, and Gilliland for stages at a chosen reflux. These estimates size a column before rigorous computer calculation. Refineries and petrochemical plants use them for LPG recovery, crude fractions and solvent recovery, where a fast first design matters.

You need: relative volatility, McCabe-Thiele, and reflux definitions.

Keys turn a multicomponent problem into a key pair

A multicomponent feed has components with different volatilities. The light key (LK) is the more volatile component whose recovery in the distillate you specify. The heavy key (HK) is the less volatile component whose recovery in the bottoms you specify. Components lighter than LK are light non-keys; components heavier than HK are heavy non-keys.

Non-key distribution is the amount of each non-key component that leaves in distillate or bottoms. The shortcut equations below use only the two keys. Estimate non-key distribution separately if needed; it is not part of the key equations.

All shortcut methods assume constant relative volatility α and constant molar overflow (CMO). αi is the volatility of component i relative to the heavy key: αi = Ki/KHK, where Ki = yi/xi at equilibrium. CMO means liquid and vapour molar flows are constant within each column section. These assumptions make the algebra simple and give a first design, not a rigorous tray-by-tray result.

Fenske: minimum stages at total reflux

At total reflux no product is removed, so the column needs the fewest theoretical stages for a required separation. Fenske gives this minimum stage count Nmin from the key split.

Nmin = log10[(dLK / bLK)(bHK / dHK)] / log10 αLK-HK

Nmin is the minimum number of theoretical stages; dLK and dHK are molar flow rates of LK and HK in the distillate; bLK and bHK are molar flow rates of LK and HK in the bottoms; αLK-HK is the relative volatility of LK to HK, taken as a geometric mean if it varies.

Use molar flow rates, not mole fractions, in d and b. If total distillate D and bottoms B are known, dLK = D xD,LK and bHK = B xB,HK. A large αLK-HK gives a small Nmin. If αLK-HK is close to 1, Nmin becomes very large because the keys are hard to separate.

Nmin is not the design stage count. It is the limit at total reflux; the real column runs at a lower reflux and needs more stages.

Underwood: minimum reflux

At minimum reflux the column has a pinch and needs infinite stages. Underwood finds Rmin without stepping trays. First solve the feed equation for θ.

Σ (αi zi)/(αi − θ) = 1 − q

αi is the relative volatility of component i to the heavy key; zi is the feed mole fraction; q is the feed thermal condition (q = 1 for saturated liquid, q = 0 for saturated vapour); θ is a root lying between αLK and αHK.

Then use the same θ in the reflux equation.

Rmin + 1 = Σ (αi xDi)/(αi − θ)

Rmin is the minimum reflux ratio; xDi is the distillate mole fraction of component i; the sum runs over all components.

Use each component's αi in both sums. Using only αLK-HK for every term is a common error. For a binary key mixture, the equations reduce to the pinch calculation on the y-x diagram.

Gilliland: stages at the chosen reflux

Choose a reflux ratio R above Rmin, often 1.1 to 1.5 times Rmin. Gilliland relates R, Rmin, N and Nmin through two dimensionless groups.

X = (R − Rmin)/(R + 1),   Y = (N − Nmin)/(N + 1)

X is the reflux group; Y is the stage group; R is the chosen reflux ratio; N is the theoretical stage count at R.

Read Y from the Gilliland chart or an algebraic fit at the calculated X. Rearrange the definition of Y to get N = (Nmin + Y)/(1 − Y). Then round N up to the next whole number for a stage count.

The result is theoretical stages. N counts the reboiler as one theoretical stage, so the number of trays is about (N − 1)/E, where E is the overall tray efficiency; see 4.7. If R is close to Rmin, X is small and N becomes very large; if R is large, N approaches Nmin.

Worked example

Question. A binary key mixture has αLK-HK = 2.0, distillate LK mole fraction xD,LK = 0.98, bottoms LK mole fraction xB,LK = 0.02, and saturated-liquid feed with xF,LK = 0.50. Find Nmin by Fenske and Rmin by Underwood.

  1. For the binary pair, xD,HK = 1 − 0.98 = 0.02 and xB,HK = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98. Overall balance gives D/B = (xF,LK − xB,LK)/(xD,LK − xF,LK) = (0.50 − 0.02)/(0.98 − 0.50) = 1.00.
  2. Key flow ratios: dLK/bLK = (D/B)(xD,LK/xB,LK) = 1.00 × (0.98/0.02) = 49.0. bHK/dHK = (B/D)(xB,HK/xD,HK) = (1/1.00) × (0.98/0.02) = 49.0.
  3. Fenske: Nmin = log10(49.0 × 49.0)/log10(2.0) = log10(2401)/0.3010 = 3.3804/0.3010 = 11.23. Report Nmin ≈ 11.2 stages.
  4. At the feed, LK equilibrium vapour mole fraction y* = α xF/[1 + (α − 1)xF] = 2.0 × 0.50/[1 + (2.0 − 1)0.50] = 0.6667.
  5. Saturated-liquid feed means q = 1. The binary pinch equation gives Rmin = (xD,LK − y*)/(y* − xF,LK) = (0.98 − 0.6667)/(0.6667 − 0.50) = 0.3133/0.1667 = 1.88.

Answer. Nmin ≈ 11.2 theoretical stages and Rmin ≈ 1.88.

Exam traps

  • Underwood uses each component's αi. Fenske needs only αLK-HK, which appears in its denominator.
  • Nmin is the stage count at total reflux, not at the design reflux. Rmin needs infinite stages, not Nmin stages.
  • The Underwood θ must lie between αLK and αHK for the key root. A root outside this interval gives a meaningless Rmin.
  • q is the feed thermal condition, not the reflux ratio. Saturated liquid gives q = 1; saturated vapour gives q = 0.
  • d and b are molar flow rates, but D and B cancel inside the product (dLK/bLK)(bHK/dHK), so Fenske can always be written with mole fractions: Nmin = log10[(xD,LK xB,HK)/(xB,LK xD,HK)] / log10 αLK-HK. Just do not mix a flow in one factor with a mole fraction in the other.

4.9 Azeotropic and extractive distillation

Some liquid mixtures cannot be separated into pure components by ordinary distillation. At an azeotrope the vapour and liquid have the same composition, so the relative volatility is 1 and extra stages achieve nothing. Azeotropic and extractive distillation add a separating agent that changes the vapour-liquid equilibrium and moves the azeotrope or removes it. The agent is an entrainer in azeotropic distillation and a high-boiling solvent in extractive distillation. These methods are used to dehydrate ethanol, purify alcohols and separate close-boiling or azeotrope-forming mixtures.

You need: 4.1 VLE and relative volatility and 4.4 McCabe-Thiele.

Azeotrope: equal vapour and liquid

An azeotrope is a composition at which the vapour and liquid in equilibrium have the same mole fractions. For component i, yi = xi. For a pair i and j, relative volatility is αij = (yi/xi) ÷ (yj/xj). At the azeotrope both ratios are equal, so αij = 1. No distillation stage can enrich the vapour beyond this point: the vapour leaving a stage has the same composition as the liquid it came from. A minimum-boiling azeotrope boils lower than both pure components. Ethanol-water is the standard example; at 1 atm it contains about 95.6 mass % ethanol and boils near 78.2 °C. A maximum-boiling azeotrope boils higher than both pure components. On a y-x diagram the equilibrium curve crosses the diagonal at the azeotrope. A water-rich ethanol feed gives water as bottoms and an overhead near the azeotrope. More trays cannot cross the crossing because the driving force is zero.

yi = xi, so αij = 1

yi and xi are mole fractions of i in vapour and liquid; αij is the relative volatility of i to j.

Azeotropic distillation with an entrainer

Azeotropic distillation adds an entrainer, a third component that forms a new azeotrope with one or more feed components. The new azeotrope is chosen to leave overhead. It carries one component away while the other leaves at the bottom. The overhead is condensed; if it splits into two liquid layers, a decanter separates the entrainer-rich phase and returns it to the column. The usual scheme has an azeotropic column plus an entrainer-recovery column. The entrainer is recycled, so only small make-up is needed. For ethanol-water, the entrainer forms a low-boiling azeotrope with the component that must be removed overhead — for ethanol dehydration this is normally water, so the overhead is a water-rich entrainer azeotrope that splits in a decanter, and ethanol leaves at the bottom. The entrainer must be selective, form an azeotrope that is easy to break, be thermally stable, and be safe and cheap. It leaves with the overhead, unlike an extractive solvent.

Extractive distillation with a solvent

Extractive distillation feeds a high-boiling, relatively nonvolatile solvent near the top of the column. The solvent does not form a new azeotrope in the usual case. It changes the activity coefficients of the feed components, so their relative volatility changes. One component becomes more volatile and leaves overhead; the other travels down with the solvent. The bottom product is solvent plus the less volatile component. A second column strips or distils the solvent from that mixture. The lean solvent is cooled and recycled to the top of the extractive column. For ethanol-water, a high-boiling solvent such as ethylene glycol makes ethanol the overhead product and sends water down with the solvent.

  1. Feed enters below the top of the extractive column.
  2. Solvent enters above the feed and flows down the column.
  3. The more volatile product leaves overhead.
  4. The bottoms, solvent plus the less volatile component, goes to solvent recovery.
  5. Recovered solvent returns to the top of the extractive column.

Pressure-swing and the choice

Pressure-swing distillation uses two columns at different pressures. The azeotrope composition shifts with pressure, so the overhead from one column can be fed to a second column where it is no longer at the azeotrope. No added solvent is needed, but the pressure change costs energy and capital. It works only when the shift is large enough. Choose azeotropic distillation when a suitable entrainer is available and a new azeotrope can be broken easily. Choose extractive distillation when a selective high-boiling solvent is available and the solvent can be recovered simply. In both cases the agent changes equilibrium; it does not violate it. The agent is recycled, so solvent losses and energy for recovery set the operating cost.

Worked example

Question. An ethanol-water feed of 100 kg contains 50 mass % ethanol. At 1 atm the minimum-boiling azeotrope is about 95.6 mass % ethanol. Find the maximum overhead mass if the overhead is at 95.6 mass % ethanol and the ethanol is taken overhead.

  1. Basis: feed F = 100 kg and ethanol mass fraction in feed xF = 0.50.

  3. The overhead is at the azeotrope: xD = 0.956 ethanol mass fraction.
  4. Ethanol balance with all ethanol taken overhead: D × 0.956 = 50 kg.

  6. Total balance: B = F − D = 100 − 52.301 = 47.699 kg.

Exam traps

  • A longer column can cross an azeotrope. At α = 1 the stages do nothing; the equilibrium curve meets the diagonal.
  • Entrainer and extractive solvent both leave at the bottom. The entrainer forms a new azeotrope and leaves overhead; the extractive solvent leaves at the bottom and is recycled.
  • The azeotrope composition is the same at every pressure. It shifts with pressure, which is the basis of pressure-swing distillation.
  • Mass % and mole % are interchangeable in the ethanol-water azeotrope value. 95.6 mass % ethanol is not 95.6 mol % ethanol.
  • Any added liquid will break the azeotrope. The agent must change relative volatility and must be recoverable economically.

Takeaways

  1. Vapour and liquid compositions in equilibrium are fixed by component vapour pressures and total pressure; for an ideal pair the single number α tells you how easy the separation is.
  2. A flash gives only one equilibrium stage, so it can only make a rough split; the vapour fraction is set by the feed enthalpy.
  3. Simple distillation is batch without reflux and its performance is given by the Rayleigh equation; steam distillation lowers the boiling temperature by sharing the total pressure.
  4. The McCabe-Thiele method replaces the column by stepping between the equilibrium curve and the operating lines, with the q-line fixing where the feed enters.
  5. Lower reflux means fewer stages but the stage count explodes as R falls to R_min; total reflux gives N_min.
  6. Ponchon-Savarit does not assume constant molar overflow, so it stays valid for unequal latent heats and concentrated solutions.
  7. Real trays give less separation than an ideal stage, so the theoretical stages are divided by the tray efficiency to get actual trays; packing is rated by HETP.
  8. For multicomponent mixtures use shortcuts: Fenske for minimum stages, Underwood for minimum reflux, Gilliland to get stages at the chosen reflux.
  9. An azeotrope cannot be crossed by ordinary distillation; a separating agent (pressure change, azeotropic entrainer or solvent) shifts the equilibrium.

Plot saturated liquid and saturated vapour lines and tie lines; Locate Δ_D above x_D using Q' = h_D + R(h_D − h_reflected) style enthalpy balance; Locate Δ_B below x_B; Feed point F on the h-z line; Δ_D, F, Δ_B are collinear; Operating lines are drawn from Δ points through points on the saturated lines; Count stages by alternating tie lines and operating lines.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the McCabe-Thiele and Ponchon-Savarit methods. When does the constant molar overflow assumption fail, and why does this matter? 13 marks

McCabe-Thiele: y-x diagram only, needs constant molar overflow and constant latent heat; Ponchon-Savarit: uses enthalpy balance and enthalpy-concentration diagram, no constant molar overflow requirement; Constant molar overflow fails for wide boiling ranges, unequal latent heats, concentrated solutions, heat of mixing; Ponchon-Savarit also handles non-ideal equilibrium through the saturated curves; Cost: Ponchon-Savarit needs enthalpy data, more work per construction.

Evaluate Ethanol and water form a minimum-boiling azeotrope. Explain why ordinary distillation cannot give pure ethanol and evaluate ways to break the azeotrope. 13 marks

At the azeotrope y = x, the equilibrium curve crosses the diagonal; The pinch point lies on the diagonal, so no finite number of stages crosses it; Options: change pressure (azeotrope composition moves), azeotropic distillation with an entrainer, extractive distillation with a heavy solvent, molecular sieve drying; Compare energy use, solvent recovery, capital cost; Choose based on scale and product purity needed.

Evaluate Explain the economic trade-off in choosing the reflux ratio, and show why the optimum lies near a small multiple of the minimum reflux. 13 marks

Operating cost rises with R because reboiler and condenser duties rise roughly with R; Capital cost falls with R because fewer stages are needed; Total cost curve has a minimum, typically at R = 1.2 to 1.5 R_min; Above the optimum, extra reflux is wasted; below it, stage count rises steeply; Practical limits: tray count, column height, pressure drop.

Glossary

Bottoms
The liquid product withdrawn from the bottom of the column, richer in the less volatile component.
Bubble point
The temperature at which the first bubble of vapour appears from a liquid mixture at a stated pressure.
Constant molar overflow
Assumption that the liquid and vapour molar flows are the same in each section of the column, giving straight operating lines.
Dew point
The temperature at which the first drop of liquid condenses from a vapour mixture at a stated pressure.
Distillate
The overhead product withdrawn from the top of the column.
Distillation
A separation method that exploits different volatilities, producing a vapour richer in the more volatile component than the liquid it came from.
Entrainer
A third component added in azeotropic distillation to carry the mixture past the azeotrope.
Equilibrium stage
An ideal contact stage in which the leaving vapour and liquid are in equilibrium.
HETP
Height of packed bed giving one theoretical stage; smaller HETP means more efficient packing.
Ideal solution
A mixture obeying Raoult's law at every composition, with no heat of mixing.
Key components
The light and heavy components that define the required split in multicomponent distillation; the others are distributed.
Minimum-boiling azeotrope
A maximum-pressure, minimum-temperature azeotrope, such as the hydrated ethanol pair.
Murphree efficiency
The actual change in vapour composition across a real tray divided by the change an ideal tray would give.
Non-ideal system
A mixture whose equilibrium deviates from Raoult's law because of interactions or association; can show a maximum or minimum azeotrope.
Packed column
A column filled with rings, saddles or structured packing to give a large contact area for vapour and liquid.
Reboiler
A heater at the base that boils part of the bottoms to supply vapour up the column; counted as one equilibrium stage.
Rectification
Multi-stage distillation in which the rising vapour contacts reflux liquid flowing down the column.
Rectifying section
The part of the column above the feed, enriched by reflux, where the operating line lies above the diagonal.
Relative volatility
The ratio of the vapour-liquid distribution coefficients of two components; values near 1 mean a hard separation.
Solvent (extractive distillation)
A high-boiling liquid that alters the relative volatility of the components without forming a new azeotrope.
Stripping section
The part of the column below the feed, where the operating line lies below the diagonal and x decreases downwards.
Theoretical stage
Same as an equilibrium stage; the ideal plate or packing height that achieves one equilibrium contact.
Total condenser
A condenser that turns all the overhead vapour into liquid; part returns as reflux and the rest leaves as distillate.
Tray column
A vertical shell with horizontal plates (sieve, valve or bubble-cap) on which vapour bubbles through liquid.
