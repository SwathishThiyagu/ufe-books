4.1 Vapour-liquid equilibrium, Raoult's law and relative volatility

At equilibrium, a liquid mixture and the vapour above it have fixed compositions at a given temperature and pressure. For an ideal binary mixture these compositions follow from the two pure-component vapour pressures and the total pressure. Distillation depends on this link: the vapour is richer in the more volatile component, so repeated vapour-liquid contacts separate the mixture. You use the same equilibrium relation to find whether a proposed separation is possible, to size flash drums, and to step stages on a y-x diagram.

You need: mole fractions and Dalton's law of partial pressures from chemistry. Flash distillation and every later chapter use this equilibrium directly.

Liquid and vapour in equilibrium

Take a binary mixture of A and B. Mole fraction xA is moles of A in the liquid divided by total moles in the liquid; yA is the same in the vapour. P is total pressure. P°A and P°B are the vapour pressures of pure A and pure B at the same temperature. Some syllabi call this vapour-liquid equilibrium (VLE).

pA = xA P°A;  P = pA + pB

pA, pB are the partial pressures of A and B (kPa); xA is the liquid mole fraction of A and xB = 1 − xA; P°A, P°B are the pure-component vapour pressures at the same temperature (kPa); P is the total pressure (kPa). For B the same law reads pB = xBP°B.

Raoult's law gives pA from the liquid composition. Dalton's law adds the two partial pressures. Combine them for B as pB = (1 − xA)P°B, then solve for xA. The result is the bubble-point equation: a liquid of composition xA starts to boil at pressure P when the temperature makes the two P° values satisfy it. The vapour composition follows from yA = pA/P.

xA = (P − P°B)/(P°A − P°B);  yA = xA P°A/P

xA is liquid mole fraction of A; yA is vapour mole fraction of A; P is total pressure (kPa); P°A and P°B are pure-component vapour pressures (kPa).

At a fixed temperature, liquid and vapour can coexist only when P lies between P°B and P°A (A the more volatile). Above P°A no liquid of any composition can boil at that temperature, so the mixture is all liquid; below P°B it is all vapour. The condition is necessary, not sufficient: the overall composition must also lie between the x and y it gives. In design you use the liquid x to get the equilibrium y; Raoult's law uses x, not y.

Relative volatility and the y-x curve

Relative volatility αAB measures how easily A leaves B. It is the ratio of the vapour-to-liquid ratios of the two components. For an ideal mixture obeying Raoult's law, the pressure ratios simplify to the ratio of pure-component vapour pressures.

αAB = (yA/xA)/(yB/xB) = P°A/P°B

αAB is relative volatility of A with respect to B (dimensionless); yA, yB are vapour mole fractions; xA, xB are liquid mole fractions; P°A, P°B are pure-component vapour pressures at the same temperature (kPa).

If α > 1, A is more volatile and the vapour is richer in A. Rearranging the definition gives yA = α xA/[1 + (α − 1)xA]. For α = 3, the equilibrium curve starts at (0,0), passes through (0.5,0.75), and ends at (1,1). It lies above the diagonal y = x. The gap between the curve and the diagonal is the driving force for separation. On a y-x diagram, horizontal and vertical steps between this curve and an operating line give theoretical stages (see 4.4). On a T-x-y diagram at fixed P, the lower curve is the bubble-point line and the upper curve is the dew-point line; the area between them is two-phase. A more volatile component has a lower boiling point and a higher y.

Real systems and azeotropes

Real mixtures depart from Raoult's law because unlike molecules attract or repel each other. The correction is pA = γAxAP°A, where γA is the activity coefficient; γA = 1 for an ideal solution. With γ > 1 (positive deviation, as in ethanol-water) the partial pressures are higher than Raoult predicts, and a strong deviation gives a minimum-boiling azeotrope. With γ < 1 (negative deviation, as in acetone-chloroform or nitric acid-water) they are lower, and a strong deviation gives a maximum-boiling azeotrope. Ideal behaviour is close for similar molecules such as benzene-toluene. Predicting γ from models such as Wilson or NRTL belongs to thermodynamics.

A real y-x curve can cross the diagonal. At the crossing point xA = yA and α = 1. That composition is an azeotrope. At an azeotrope the vapour and liquid have the same composition, so ordinary distillation cannot pass it; a separating agent is needed (see 4.9). Common examples are the minimum-boiling ethanol-water azeotrope and the maximum-boiling nitric acid-water azeotrope.

For real systems, α changes with x, especially near an azeotrope. Use measured VLE for design when the mixture is non-ideal; treat Raoult's law as a starting estimate for similar components. These same equations support flash calculations in 4.2, where a feed is partly vaporised and the equilibrium gives the vapour and liquid products.

Worked example

Question. A binary ideal mixture has P°A = 120 kPa and P°B = 40 kPa at the column pressure P = 101.3 kPa. Find the equilibrium liquid mole fraction xA, vapour mole fraction yA, and relative volatility α.

  1. Write the data: P = 101.3 kPa, P°A = 120 kPa, P°B = 40 kPa.
  2. Use the bubble-point equation: xA = (101.3 − 40)/(120 − 40) = 61.3/80 = 0.76625, which rounds to 0.766.
  3. Find the vapour composition: yA = xA P°A/P = 0.76625 × 120/101.3 = 91.95/101.3 = 0.9077, which rounds to 0.908.
  4. Find relative volatility: α = P°A/P°B = 120/40 = 3.0.
  5. Check with B: yB = (1 − 0.76625)(40)/101.3 = 0.0923, and yA + yB = 0.9077 + 0.0923 = 1.000. Figure 4.1.1 marks this pair on both diagrams.

Answer. At P = 101.3 kPa, xA = 0.766, yA = 0.908 and α = 3.0; the vapour is richer in A, as α > 1 requires.

Exam traps

  • Mixing x and y: Raoult's law uses liquid x; vapour y comes out of yA = xA P°A/P.
  • Using P° values at a different temperature: both pure-component vapour pressures must be at the equilibrium temperature.
  • Calling α constant for real mixtures: α changes with x, and α = 1 at an azeotrope.
  • Forgetting the pressure condition: at a given temperature two phases need P between P°B and P°A; outside that range the bubble-point equation returns x below 0 or above 1.
  • Using mass fraction in place of mole fraction: all VLE equations here use mole fractions.

4.2 Flash distillation (equilibrium vaporization)

Flash distillation splits a feed into vapour and liquid in one equilibrium stage. The feed is heated, or its pressure dropped, until it partly vaporises. A drum lets vapour leave the top and liquid settle at the bottom. Both products leave in equilibrium. No trays and no reflux: the split is coarse but cheap. Refineries use it before crude distillation, gas plants remove heavy ends, and desalination plants run multi-stage flash.

You need 4.1 Vapour-liquid equilibrium, Raoult's law and relative volatility for K values and mole fractions.

One drum, one stage

A flash drum does in one step what a column does in many. It contacts liquid and vapour once, lets them reach equilibrium, and removes them separately. Vapour is richer in the light component; liquid is richer in the heavy component.

  1. Feed at F kmol/h and mole fraction z enters a heater.
  2. A valve drops the pressure, taking the feed into the two-phase region.
  3. The mixture enters the drum; vapour disengages and rises, liquid collects at the bottom.
  4. A mist eliminator stops droplets leaving with vapour.
  5. Vapour and liquid leave at drum T and P.
  • Isothermal flash: heat added so both phases leave at one T.
  • Adiabatic flash: no heat is added at the drum; the latent heat of the vapour formed comes out of the hot feed's own sensible heat, so the drum runs colder than the feed entering the valve.
  • Some syllabi call this equilibrium vaporization.

Balances and the K value

F is feed flow (kmol/h), z its mole fraction, V and L vapour and liquid flows (kmol/h), and y and x their mole fractions. V/F is the vapour fraction: the share of feed leaving as vapour.

F z = V y + L x and F = V + L

F feed flow (kmol/h); z feed mole fraction; V, L vapour and liquid flows (kmol/h); y, x their mole fractions.

At equilibrium yi = Ki xi. Ki = yi/xi is dimensionless and depends on temperature, pressure and composition. For an ideal mixture Ki = Pisat/P (4.1).

  1. Divide by F: z = (V/F)y + (1 − V/F)x.
  2. Put y = Kx: z = x[(V/F)K + 1 − V/F].
  3. Solve for liquid: xi = zi/[1 + (V/F)(Ki − 1)].

Degrees of freedom decide the drum. Fix T and P: the K values are fixed, so V/F follows — the isothermal flash. Fix the feed enthalpy and the drum pressure instead: no heat is added or removed, and the drum temperature and V/F come out together from the energy balance and Rachford-Rice — the adiabatic flash. At a given P you cannot pin both T and V/F.

Rachford-Rice: finding the vapour fraction

The unknown is usually V/F. Both sets of mole fractions must sum to one; combining the sums gives one equation in one unknown.

Σ zi (Ki − 1) / [1 + (V/F)(Ki − 1)] = 0

sum over every component i; zi feed mole fraction; Ki K value at drum T and P; V/F vapour fraction, the unknown.

  1. Σ xi = 1 gives Σ zi/[1 + ψ(Ki − 1)] = 1, with ψ = V/F.
  2. Σ yi = 1 gives Σ zi Ki/[1 + ψ(Ki − 1)] = 1.
  3. Subtract the first from the second: Σ zi(Ki − 1)/[1 + ψ(Ki − 1)] = 0.

The left side falls steadily as ψ rises, so when two phases exist there is a single root with 0 < ψ < 1; at the bubble point the root is ψ = 0 and at the dew point ψ = 1. Find the root by trial, bisection or Newton iteration. For two components the equation is linear in ψ, because zB = 1 − zA:

V/F = −[zA(KA − 1) + zB(KB − 1)] / [(KA − 1)(KB − 1)]

zA, zB are feed mole fractions summing to 1; KA, KB are the two K values at the drum T and P.

Test for two phases first. Both exist only if Σ zi Ki > 1 and Σ zi/Ki > 1. If Σ zi Ki ≤ 1 the feed is below its bubble point and only liquid leaves; if Σ zi/Ki ≤ 1 it is above its dew point and only vapour leaves.

Worked example

Question. A flash drum receives F = 100 kmol/h of a binary feed with zA = 0.50, at a temperature and pressure where KA = 2.0 and KB = 0.5. Check that two phases form, find V/F from the two-component Rachford-Rice formula, then find V, L, xA and yA.

  1. Two-phase test: Σ zK = 0.50(2.0) + 0.50(0.5) = 1.25 > 1 and Σ z/K = 0.50/2.0 + 0.50/0.5 = 1.25 > 1. Two phases exist.
  2. Vapour fraction: V/F = −[zA(KA − 1) + zB(KB − 1)]/[(KA − 1)(KB − 1)] = −[0.50(1.0) + 0.50(−0.5)]/[(1.0)(−0.5)] = −0.25/(−0.50) = 0.50.
  3. Flows: V = 0.50 × 100 = 50 kmol/h and L = 100 − 50 = 50 kmol/h.
  4. Liquid: xA = zA/[1 + (V/F)(KA − 1)] = 0.50/[1 + 0.50(1.0)] = 0.50/1.50 = 0.333.
  5. Vapour: yA = KAxA = 2.0 × 0.333 = 0.667. For B: xB = 0.667 and yB = 0.5 × 0.667 = 0.333, so both phases sum to 1.000.
  6. Rachford-Rice check at ψ = 0.50: 0.50(1.0)/1.50 + 0.50(−0.5)/0.75 = 0.3333 − 0.3333 = 0.
  7. Component balance: V yA + L xA = 50(0.667) + 50(0.333) = 50.0 kmol/h = F zA.

Answer. V/F = 0.50, so V = L = 50 kmol/h, with xA = 0.333 in the liquid and yA = 0.667 in the vapour.

Exam traps

  • Using x = z. The feed composition z is not liquid x; xi = zi/[1 + (V/F)(Ki − 1)].
  • Treating K as constant. K depends on T and P.
  • Skipping the two-phase test. Solve only when Σ zi Ki > 1 and Σ zi/Ki > 1; otherwise one phase and no root between 0 and 1.
  • Mixing mass and moles. z, x, y, K and V/F are molar; convert kg/h to kmol/h first.
  • Confusing flash with simple distillation. In flash vapour stays in contact until equilibrium; in 4.3 vapour is drawn off as it forms.
  • Taking the vapour fraction as a free choice in an isothermal flash. Once T and P are fixed, V/F follows from Rachford-Rice; to change V/F you change T or P.

At a true bubble point V/F = 0: the liquid is the feed (xi = zi) and only an infinitesimal bubble of vapour forms, with yi = Ki zi and Σ zi Ki = 1. Analyze At a proposed drum condition Σ zi Ki works out to 0.85. What happens in the drum and what do you change? 4 marks

Σ zi Ki = 0.85 < 1 means the drum is below the bubble point of the feed: it stays all liquid, V = 0, no vapour leaves, and there is no Rachford-Rice root between 0 and 1. Analyze The drum pressure is raised at constant temperature. Explain the effect on V/F and on the product compositions. 4 marks

Raising P lowers every K, since Ki = Pisat/P. Lower K means less vapour, so V/F falls. Both products become closer in composition to each other and to the feed: the vapour gets heavier, the liquid gets lighter, and the separation weakens.

4.3 Simple (differential) and steam distillation

Simple distillation is the oldest batch separation: heat a liquid charge, remove the vapour as it forms, and condense it. Because the vapour is richer in the more volatile component, the pot liquid changes during the run. A differential balance gives the Rayleigh equation, which connects the starting and ending liquid amounts and compositions. Steam distillation uses a second immiscible liquid, usually water, to lower the boiling temperature for heat-sensitive organics such as essential oils. Both methods are common in small pharmaceutical, perfume and solvent-recovery units.

You need: 4.1 VLE and relative volatility and basic integration.

The batch still and the Rayleigh equation

Simple distillation is a batch operation. A known liquid charge sits in a still pot. Heat boils it. The vapour leaves at once, is condensed, and is collected as distillate. No liquid reflux returns from the condenser. Because the more volatile component leaves first, the liquid in the pot becomes richer in the less volatile component. The vapour composition therefore changes with time. The old name is differential distillation: the change is followed over infinitesimal steps, not in one flash stage (compare 4.2).

At some instant the pot holds L mol of liquid with mole fraction x of the more volatile component. Remove a small amount dD = −dL of vapour. That vapour has mole fraction y, in equilibrium with x at that instant. A balance on the more volatile component gives L dx = (y − x) dL. Rearrange to dL/L = dx/(y − x). Integrate from the initial charge (L₁, x₁) to the final residue (L₂, x₂):

ln(L₁/L₂) = ∫x₂x₁ dx/(y − x)

L₁ initial liquid moles; L₂ final residue moles; x₁ initial liquid mole fraction of the more volatile component; x₂ final liquid mole fraction; y equilibrium vapour mole fraction at the local x.

The distillate collected is D = L₁ − L₂. To use the equation, y must come from the VLE curve at the same x (see 4.1). The integral is evaluated graphically or numerically when α is not constant.

Constant relative volatility

For an ideal binary pair, α = (y/(1 − y))/(x/(1 − x)). It is the ratio of volatility of the more volatile component to the less volatile one. Rearranged: y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x]. Substitute this y in the Rayleigh integral. For constant α, the integral has a closed form:

ln(L₁/L₂) = [ln(x₁/x₂) − α ln((1 − x₁)/(1 − x₂))]/(α − 1)

α relative volatility (dimensionless); L₁, L₂, x₁, x₂ as in the Rayleigh equation. The formula needs α ≠ 1.

The equation gives L₂ for a chosen x₂, and D = L₁ − L₂. Average distillate composition comes from the overall balance on the more volatile component: xD,avg = (L₁x₁ − L₂x₂)/D. The equation is used for quick batch-still sizing and to check a simulation. A batch column with trays and reflux needs the stage methods of McCabe-Thiele instead of the single-stage Rayleigh picture.

Steam distillation

Some organic liquids are heat sensitive: a high boiling point damages them before they distil. Steam distillation solves this by adding water as a second liquid phase. Water and the organic are immiscible. Each liquid keeps its own vapour pressure, p°water and p°organic, almost independent of the other. The mixture boils when the sum reaches the total pressure:

P = p°water + p°organic

P total pressure in kPa or bar; p°water vapour pressure of pure water at the boiling temperature; p°organic vapour pressure of pure organic at the same temperature.

The sum reaches the applied pressure at a temperature below the normal boiling point of each pure liquid, and below 100 °C at 1 atm. Live steam is blown through the organic liquid; the vapour leaving carries water and organic in the ratio of their vapour pressures. The condensate separates into two liquid layers, so the organic is recovered by decantation.

Steam consumption follows from the vapour ratio. If the vapour is saturated and ideal, moles steam per mole organic ≈ p°water/p°organic. Practical units add heat losses and incomplete saturation. The method suits essential oils, perfumes and some edible-oil deodorization. It needs immiscibility: a soluble solvent changes the VLE and does not give the simple sum of vapour pressures (compare 4.9).

Worked example

Question. A batch still is charged with L₁ = 100 mol of an ideal binary liquid at x₁ = 0.50 mole fraction of the more volatile component. The relative volatility is constant at α = 2.0. Distillation stops when the residue liquid reaches x₂ = 0.20. Find L₁/L₂, the final residue L₂, the distillate D in moles and its average composition.

  1. Use the constant-α Rayleigh form: ln(L₁/L₂) = [ln(x₁/x₂) − α ln((1 − x₁)/(1 − x₂))]/(α − 1).
  2. Evaluate the first logarithm: ln(0.50/0.20) = ln(2.50) = 0.9163.
  3. Evaluate the second logarithm: ln((1 − 0.50)/(1 − 0.20)) = ln(0.50/0.80) = ln(0.625) = −0.4700.
  4. Put α = 2.0: numerator = 0.9163 − 2.0(−0.4700) = 1.8563. Divide by α − 1 = 1.0, so ln(L₁/L₂) = 1.8563.
  5. Exponentiate: L₁/L₂ = e1.8563 = 6.40.
  6. Final residue: L₂ = L₁/6.40 = 100/6.40 = 15.6 mol.
  7. Distillate: D = L₁ − L₂ = 100 − 15.6 = 84.4 mol.
  8. Average distillate composition: xD,avg = (L₁x₁ − L₂x₂)/D = (100 × 0.50 − 15.625 × 0.20)/84.375 = (50 − 3.125)/84.375 = 0.556.

Answer. L₁/L₂ = 6.40, final residue L₂ ≈ 15.6 mol, distillate D ≈ 84.4 mol with average composition 0.556: only slightly richer than the 0.50 charge, because most of the charge was boiled off.

Worked example 2

Question. An organic liquid that is immiscible with water (molar mass 154 kg/kmol) is steam-distilled at 101.3 kPa. At 95 °C pure water has a vapour pressure of 84.5 kPa and the organic 16.8 kPa. Show that the mixture boils near 95 °C, and find the moles and kilograms of steam carried over per kilogram of organic.

  1. Boiling condition: p°water + p°organic = 84.5 + 16.8 = 101.3 kPa, equal to the applied pressure, so the two liquids boil together at 95 °C, below 100 °C and far below the organic's own boiling point.
  2. Vapour composition: the organic is 16.8/101.3 = 0.166 of the vapour moles and water 84.5/101.3 = 0.834.
  3. Moles of steam per mole of organic = p°water/p°organic = 84.5/16.8 = 5.03.
  4. Mass ratio: (5.03 × 18.02)/(1 × 154) = 90.6/154 = 0.588 kg steam per kg organic.
  5. Per kilogram of organic, 1/154 = 0.00649 kmol organic travels with 5.03 × 0.00649 = 0.0327 kmol (0.588 kg) of steam.

Answer. The mixture boils at about 95 °C; 5.03 mol of steam carry each mole of organic, which is only 0.588 kg of steam per kg of organic because the organic's molar mass is high.

Exam traps

  • Using the average distillate composition in place of the instantaneous equilibrium y. The Rayleigh integral needs y at each local x.
  • Reversing the limits x₁ and x₂ in the integral, or writing L₂/L₁ when the equation is L₁/L₂.
  • Forgetting that the constant-α formula fails at α = 1; there y = x and no separation occurs.
  • Treating steam distillation as ordinary distillation of a water–organic solution. It needs two immiscible liquid phases; a soluble solvent changes the vapour pressures.
  • Unit slip: α and mole fractions are dimensionless; L₁, L₂, D are moles, not mass, unless the same mass basis is used consistently.
  • Writing the steam ratio upside down. Moles of steam per mole of organic = p°water/p°organic; multiply by 18/Morganic for a mass ratio.

4.4 McCabe-Thiele method for binary distillation

The McCabe-Thiele method counts the theoretical stages of a binary column on a y-x diagram, as a staircase between the equilibrium curve and two straight operating lines; a feed line fixes where the two sections meet. It assumes constant molar overflow, which keeps the lines straight, and it is the quick tray count engineers make before a rigorous simulation.

You need VLE and relative volatility, flash balances and simple distillation balances.

Rectifying line, stripping line and q-line

In the rectifying section above the feed, liquid L flows down and vapour V rises. The reflux ratio is R = L/D, where D is the distillate molar flow. Constant molar overflow keeps L and V constant in this section. A balance around the top gives the rectifying operating line.

y = (R/(R+1))x + xD/(R+1)

y is vapour mole fraction of the more volatile component; x is liquid mole fraction; R = L/D is the reflux ratio; xD is distillate mole fraction. The line passes through (xD, xD).

Below the feed, the stripping section has its own operating line. It passes through (xB, xB), where xB is the bottoms mole fraction, and through the point where the rectifying line meets the feed line. A balance around the bottom gives it.

y = (L′/V′)x − (B/V′)xB

L′ and V′ are stripping-section liquid and vapour molar flows; B is bottoms molar flow; xB is bottoms mole fraction.

q measures the thermal state of the feed. It is the heat needed to turn 1 mol of feed into saturated vapour, divided by the molar latent heat. For a feed between its bubble point and its dew point, q also equals the moles of liquid formed per mole of feed. The feed line, or q-line, is:

y = [q/(q−1)]x − xF/(q−1)

xF is feed mole fraction; q is the feed thermal state defined above; the slope of the line is q/(q−1). The q-line passes through (xF, xF).

  • q = 1: saturated liquid, vertical line x = xF.
  • q = 0: saturated vapour, horizontal line y = xF.
  • 0 < q < 1: two-phase feed, line slopes down from (xF, xF).
  • q > 1: subcooled liquid, slope q/(q−1) is positive and greater than 1.
  • q < 0: superheated vapour, slope q/(q−1) is positive and less than 1.

The q-line is not an operating line. It only locates the intersection P of the two operating lines.

Staircase between equilibrium and operating lines

Use a y-x diagram. The equilibrium curve for constant relative volatility α is:

y* = α x / [1 + (α−1)x]

y* is vapour mole fraction in equilibrium with liquid x; α is relative volatility; x is liquid mole fraction. Solved for x, this gives x* = y / [α − (α−1)y], the liquid in equilibrium with a vapour of composition y.

Draw the diagonal y = x. Mark xD, xF and xB on it. Draw the rectifying line from (xD, xD) to the q-line. Draw the q-line through (xF, xF) with slope q/(q−1); for a saturated liquid feed this is the vertical line x = xF. Its intersection with the rectifying line is P. Draw the stripping line from (xB, xB) through P.

  1. Start at (xD, xD).
  2. Move horizontally left to the equilibrium curve. This gives x*, the liquid mole fraction in equilibrium with the vapour of composition y at that height.
  3. Move vertically down to the operating line in use. Keep the rectifying line while x is above xP, the x-coordinate of P. When a horizontal step takes x below xP, switch to the stripping line. Only for a saturated liquid feed (q = 1) does xP equal xF.
  4. Repeat horizontal to equilibrium, vertical to operating line.
  5. Stop when x ≤ xB. Each horizontal step to the equilibrium curve is one equilibrium contact; the vertical move only sets up the next step, and the final step may be horizontal alone.

The final step that crosses xB is the partial reboiler, which is itself an equilibrium stage; do not add it again. Real trays outnumber the theoretical stages because tray efficiency is below one (see tray efficiency).

Why the lines are straight; use and limits

Constant molar overflow assumes equal molar latent heats, negligible heat of mixing, and negligible sensible heat. One mole of vapour condensing then supplies one mole of liquid vaporising. L and V stay constant in each section, so the operating lines are straight.

The method gives the theoretical stage count at a chosen R. A lower R moves the operating lines closer to the equilibrium curve, so more stages are needed. At minimum reflux the steps never reach xB (see reflux ratio). If CMO fails, use the enthalpy method (see Ponchon-Savarit).

The construction is used for binary columns, for checking rigorous simulation results, and for seeing how feed condition shifts the operating lines.

4.5 Reflux ratio: minimum, total and optimum

Reflux is the liquid returned from the condenser to the top of the column, and the reflux ratio R = L/D compares it with the distillate D. R sets the main trade-off of distillation: more reflux needs fewer stages but more reboiler steam and condenser water. At minimum reflux the stage count is infinite; at total reflux it is the smallest possible but no product leaves. Designers work between the two, usually at 1.2 to 1.5 times the minimum, where the total annual cost is lowest.

You need: 4.1 for α and equilibrium, and 4.4 for McCabe-Thiele operating lines.

Reflux ratio and the operating line

Vapour from the top tray is condensed. Part of the condensate, L, returns to the column as reflux; the rest leaves as distillate D. The reflux ratio is a pure number:

R = L/D, V = L + D = (R + 1)D

R is the reflux ratio; L is the reflux flow (kmol/h); D is the distillate flow (kmol/h); V is the vapour flow to the condenser (kmol/h).

On the y-x diagram the rectifying operating line has slope R/(R + 1) and intercept xD/(R + 1) (see McCabe-Thiele). Raising R steepens the line toward the diagonal and pulls it away from the equilibrium curve, so each stage makes a larger change in composition and fewer stages are needed. The price is vapour: every extra mole of reflux must be boiled in the reboiler and condensed again, so both duties grow as (R + 1)D.

  • R → ∞ (total reflux): fewest stages, no product.
  • R = Rmin: infinite stages, least energy.
  • Rmin < R < ∞: a finite column; the choice is economic.

Minimum reflux and the pinch

Lowering R moves the operating line toward the equilibrium curve. At the minimum reflux Rmin the two first touch. At the touch point, the pinch, the driving force y* − y is zero: a stage there changes nothing, the staircase crowds into the corner and never passes it, and the stage count becomes infinite. Rmin is a limit, never an operating reflux.

For an ideal-shaped curve the pinch sits where the q-line meets the equilibrium curve, at (x′, y′). The rectifying line through (xD, xD) and the pinch gives:

Rmin/(Rmin + 1) = (xD − y′)/(xD − x′)

x′, y′ are the pinch coordinates on the equilibrium curve (mole fractions); xD is the distillate mole fraction. For a saturated-liquid feed x′ = xF and y′ = yF*, which rearranges to Rmin = (xD − yF*)/(yF* − xF).

For a saturated-vapour feed the q-line is horizontal at y = xF, so y′ = xF and x′ is the liquid in equilibrium with it. A non-ideal curve with an inflection, such as ethanol-water, can touch the operating line above the feed first: the tangent pinch. Then Rmin comes from the tangent drawn from (xD, xD), and the feed-point formula gives too low a value.

Total reflux, Fenske, and the optimum

At total reflux no distillate is drawn and all condensate returns, so R → ∞ and both operating lines lie on the diagonal y = x. The staircase then runs between the curve and the diagonal and gives the fewest stages, Nmin. For constant α, Fenske gives it directly:

Nmin = log[(xD/(1 − xD))((1 − xB)/xB)] / log α

Nmin is the minimum number of theoretical stages, including the reboiler; xD, xB are distillate and bottoms mole fractions of the more volatile component; α is the relative volatility.

Real columns need product, so R is finite. The annual cost has two parts that move in opposite directions. The capital charge falls as R rises, because the column gets shorter, but it rises again at high R because the larger vapour flow needs a wider column and bigger exchangers. The energy cost rises in proportion to (R + 1)D. Their sum is U-shaped with a flat bottom, usually between 1.2 and 1.5 Rmin. Below that the stage count climbs steeply toward the pinch; above it, extra reflux buys few stages. 4.6 Ponchon-Savarit enthalpy-concentration method

Ponchon-Savarit steps binary stages on an enthalpy-composition diagram instead of a y-x diagram. It drops constant molar overflow, so it handles unequal latent heats and heats of mixing: two enthalpy curves and one difference point per section carry the material and energy balances together.

You need: 4.1 VLE for equilibrium tie lines, 4.4 operating lines, and basic enthalpy balances from thermodynamics.

Two enthalpy curves and a fixed point

McCabe-Thiele assumes that each mole of vapour condensed sends one mole of liquid down, which needs equal latent heats and no heat losses. Ponchon-Savarit drops that assumption and keeps the energy balance.

The diagram has two enthalpy curves. H is the enthalpy of saturated vapour in kJ per mole of mixture, plotted against vapour mole fraction y. h is the enthalpy of saturated liquid in kJ per mole of mixture, plotted against liquid mole fraction x. Both x and y refer to the more volatile component. The vertical gap H − h at equilibrium is the latent heat of vaporisation; it need not be constant.

A difference point replaces the operating line. For the rectifying section it is ΔD, at abscissa xD and an ordinate HΔD fixed by the reflux ratio; every rectifying operating line passes through it.

Rectifying difference point ΔD

Take an envelope round the condenser and the top of the column. Vapour V₁ from the top tray, with y₁ = xD for a total condenser and enthalpy H₁, is condensed; L₀ = RD returns as reflux and D leaves, both as liquid with enthalpy hD. The condenser removes QC = V₁(H₁ − hD). Only these top values are needed to place the difference point:

HΔD = hD + QC/D = H₁ + R(H₁ − hD)

HΔD is the ordinate of the rectifying difference point (kJ/mol); H₁ is the enthalpy of the saturated vapour leaving the top tray at y₁ = xD (kJ/mol); hD is the enthalpy of the saturated reflux and distillate liquid (kJ/mol); QC is the condenser duty (kJ/h); R = L₀/D.

ΔD = (xD, HΔD) is a construction point, not a tray. It sits above the H curve, higher for a larger R. Every rectifying operating line passes through it: the passing streams xn (liquid down from tray n) and yn+1 (vapour up from tray n + 1) lie on one straight line through ΔD.

  1. Plot H against y and h against x on the same axes, with tie lines from the equilibrium data.
  2. Mark ΔD at (xD, HΔD), directly above the distillate composition.
  3. Top tray: with a total condenser y₁ = xD, the point (xD, H₁) on the H curve.
  4. Follow the tie line from y₁ down to the h curve: this is x₁, the liquid leaving tray 1.
  5. Draw the line from ΔD through (x₁, h₁) and extend it to cut the H curve: the cut is y₂, the vapour rising to tray 1.
  6. Repeat tie line and operating line until x passes the feed composition, then switch to ΔB.

The lever rule on each operating line gives the internal reflux ratio: Ln/Vn+1 = (HΔD − Hn+1)/(HΔD − hn). When the H and h curves slope, this ratio changes from tray to tray, which is exactly what McCabe-Thiele cannot show.

Stripping point and the McCabe-Thiele limit

The stripping section has its own difference point ΔB at xB, with ordinate HΔB = hB − QB/B, where QB is the reboiler duty and B the bottoms flow. It lies below the h curve. A balance round the whole column puts ΔD, the feed point F = (zF, hF) and ΔB on one straight line: once ΔD is placed, ΔB is where the line from ΔD through F crosses the vertical x = xB. Stripping operating lines are drawn through ΔB.

McCabe-Thiele is the special case of horizontal curves, H = HV and h = hL with constant gap λ = HV − hL. Then HΔD = HV + Rλ, and the lever rule gives L/V = (HΔD − HV)/(HΔD − hL) = Rλ/[(R + 1)λ] = R/(R + 1) on every tray: constant molar overflow. The operating line returns as With HV = 50, hL = 20 kJ/mol and R = 2, for example, HΔD = 50 + 2(30) = 110 kJ/mol and L/V = 60/90 = 0.667.

y = (R/(R+1))x + xD/(R+1)

y is the vapour mole fraction on the McCabe-Thiele operating line; x is the liquid mole fraction; R is the reflux ratio; xD is the distillate mole fraction. This is the rectifying line of McCabe-Thiele.

Columns whose curves are nearly flat and parallel can use McCabe-Thiele. Large or unequal latent heats, heats of mixing and wide boiling ranges (ammonia-water, concentrated acids) need Ponchon-Savarit.

4.7 Tray and packed columns; tray efficiency

A real distillation column is not a chain of perfect equilibrium stages. It is a vessel full of trays or packing where vapour and liquid meet for a short time. The vapour leaves a tray without reaching equilibrium with the liquid. Tray efficiency measures this gap. It converts the theoretical stages from McCabe-Thiele or Ponchon-Savarit into the actual trays a fabricator must supply. Packed columns use HETP for the same purpose. Every column in a refinery, a chemical plant or an ethanol unit depends on this step between the drawing board and the steel.

You need: theoretical stages from McCabe-Thiele, the reflux ratio, and for packed columns the HTU-NTU height method from gas absorption.

Trays: how vapour meets liquid

A tray column is a vertical shell with horizontal plates. Liquid flows across each plate and down a downcomer to the plate below. Vapour rises through openings in the plate and bubbles through the liquid. The plate holds a frothy mixture, so the two phases touch over a large area. The ideal stage assumption says the vapour leaving a plate is in equilibrium with the liquid leaving it. A real plate falls short of that.

  • Sieve tray: plain holes in the plate. Cheap, low pressure drop, but a narrow range of vapour rates before liquid weeps through the holes.
  • Bubble-cap tray: a cap sits over each riser and traps a liquid seal. It works at low vapour rates and gives good turn-down, at higher cost and pressure drop.
  • Valve tray: a flap over each hole lifts as vapour flow rises. It opens with load, so it balances capacity and turn-down at moderate cost.

Turn-down is the ratio of maximum to minimum vapour rate at which the tray still works. Trays suit columns with large liquid loads, dirty service that would block packing, and cases where you want side withdrawals. The number of real trays is larger than the theoretical count. The next sections give two measures of that penalty.

Packed columns and HETP

Packing is a bed of solid shapes that gives a wetted surface. Liquid runs down the surface as a film; vapour rises through the voids. There are no plates and no downcomers. The column has a liquid distributor at the top and a support grid at the bottom.

  • Random packing: small hollow or saddle-shaped pieces dumped into the column. Raschig rings, Pall rings and Berl saddles are common. Cheap, but the pieces settle and can channel liquid.
  • Structured packing: corrugated metal or plastic sheets stacked in a regular pattern. Low pressure drop per stage, so it suits vacuum columns and heat-sensitive products. It costs more and needs a good distributor.

Packed columns are rated by HETP, the height equivalent to a theoretical plate. HETP has units of length, usually metres. A packing with HETP = 0.5 m needs 0.5 m of bed to do the work of one ideal stage, so the packed height is Z = Nt × HETP. Gas absorption uses transfer units instead, Z = HOG × NOG; use HTU-NTU when you have mass-transfer coefficients and HETP when the vendor supplies test data.

Packing wins when pressure drop must be low, the liquid foams, or the column is small. Trays win when the liquid rate is high, the service is fouling, or the column needs side withdrawals. Both need careful hydraulic design; a packed tower also needs a distributor that wets the whole cross-section.

Overall and Murphree efficiency

The overall tray efficiency Eo compares the ideal stage count with the real tray count. Let Nt be the theoretical stages from McCabe-Thiele and Na the actual trays installed. Some syllabi call Eo the column efficiency.

Eo = Nt / Na

Eo is the overall tray efficiency (dimensionless); Nt is the number of theoretical stages; Na is the number of actual trays. Rearranged: Na = Nt / Eo.

Eo is a whole-column number. It hides the local contact on one tray. The Murphree vapour efficiency EM looks at a single tray n. Vapour enters tray n from tray n+1 below with mole fraction yn+1. Vapour leaves tray n upward with yn. The liquid leaves tray n with composition xn. The vapour that would be in equilibrium with that leaving liquid is yn*.

EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1)

EM is the Murphree vapour efficiency (dimensionless); yn is the vapour mole fraction leaving tray n upward; yn+1 is the vapour mole fraction entering tray n from below; yn* is the vapour mole fraction in equilibrium with the liquid leaving tray n.

Rearrange to find the leaving vapour: yn = yn+1 + EM(yn* − yn+1). If EM = 1 the tray reaches equilibrium; if EM = 0 the vapour passes unchanged. EM is not Eo: Eo also carries liquid mixing, entrainment and the slope of the equilibrium curve. When both lines are straight, Lewis's relation links them: Eo = ln[1 + EM(λ − 1)]/ln λ, with λ = mV/L the ratio of the equilibrium slope to the operating slope. Count Nt and Na on the same basis: if Nt includes the partial reboiler, the trays are (Nt − 1)/Eo.

Three hydraulic limits set the useful operating range. Weeping: at low vapour rate, liquid drips through the holes instead of bubbling, so contact falls. Flooding: at high vapour rate, the vapour holds liquid on the tray and the downcomer cannot carry it away; the column chokes. Entrainment: high vapour speed throws liquid droplets upward into the tray above, carrying heavy components to the top. A tray must run between weeping and flooding. The vapour rate at flooding sets the column diameter.

Worked example

Question. A binary distillation column needs Nt = 10 theoretical stages. The overall tray efficiency is Eo = 0.60. On one tray, vapour entering from below has yn+1 = 0.30. The vapour in equilibrium with the liquid leaving that tray is yn* = 0.50. The Murphree vapour efficiency is EM = 0.70. (a) Find the number of actual trays. (b) Find the leaving vapour mole fraction yn.

  1. Actual trays: Na = Nt / Eo = 10 / 0.60 = 16.7.
  2. Trays are counted in whole numbers, so install 17 actual trays.
  3. Leaving vapour: yn = yn+1 + EM(yn* − yn+1) = 0.30 + 0.70(0.50 − 0.30).
  4. 0.50 − 0.30 = 0.20; 0.70 × 0.20 = 0.14; yn = 0.30 + 0.14 = 0.44.

Answer. 17 actual trays are needed, and the leaving vapour mole fraction is yn = 0.44.

Exam traps

  • Dividing the wrong way: actual trays = theoretical / Eo. An efficiency below one must increase the count, never decrease it.
  • yn* is equilibrium with the liquid leaving tray n, not with the liquid entering the tray. Using the incoming liquid gives a false driving force.
  • Eo and EM are not interchangeable. Eo counts whole-column trays; EM is a local vapour ratio on one tray.
  • HETP has units of length. Multiplying Nt by HETP gives packed height in metres, not a number of trays.
  • Weeping, flooding and entrainment are hydraulic limits, not efficiency values. A high EM does not excuse a column that floods.
  • Dividing a stage count that includes the reboiler by Eo. The reboiler is one ideal stage already; take it off first.

4.8 Multicomponent distillation: Fenske, Underwood and Gilliland

Multicomponent distillation separates three or more volatile components, and binary McCabe-Thiele no longer fits because every component distributes between the products. Shortcut methods give a fast first design: pick two key components, then Fenske for the minimum stages, Underwood for the minimum reflux and Gilliland for the stages at the chosen reflux.

You need: relative volatility, McCabe-Thiele, and reflux definitions.

Keys turn a multicomponent problem into a key pair

A multicomponent feed has components with different volatilities. The light key (LK) is the more volatile component whose recovery in the distillate you specify. The heavy key (HK) is the less volatile component whose recovery in the bottoms you specify. Components lighter than LK are light non-keys; components heavier than HK are heavy non-keys.

Non-key distribution is the amount of each non-key that leaves in distillate and in bottoms. The Fenske equation estimates it once Nmin is known: (d/b)i = (d/b)HK αiNmin. Light non-keys go almost wholly overhead and heavy non-keys almost wholly to the bottoms.

All shortcut methods assume constant relative volatility α and constant molar overflow (CMO). αi is the volatility of component i relative to the heavy key: αi = Ki/KHK, where Ki = yi/xi at equilibrium. CMO means liquid and vapour molar flows are constant within each column section. These assumptions make the algebra simple and give a first design, not a rigorous tray-by-tray result.

Fenske: minimum stages at total reflux

At total reflux no product is removed, so the column needs the fewest theoretical stages for a required separation. Fenske gives this minimum stage count Nmin from the key split.

Nmin = log10[(dLK / bLK)(bHK / dHK)] / log10 αLK-HK

Nmin is the minimum number of theoretical stages; dLK and dHK are molar flow rates of LK and HK in the distillate; bLK and bHK are molar flow rates of LK and HK in the bottoms; αLK-HK is the relative volatility of LK to HK, taken as a geometric mean if it varies.

Use molar flow rates, not mole fractions, in d and b. If total distillate D and bottoms B are known, dLK = D xD,LK and bHK = B xB,HK. A large αLK-HK gives a small Nmin. If αLK-HK is close to 1, Nmin becomes very large because the keys are hard to separate.

Nmin is not the design stage count. It is the limit at total reflux; the real column runs at a lower reflux and needs more stages.

Underwood: minimum reflux

At minimum reflux the column has a pinch and needs infinite stages. Underwood finds Rmin without stepping trays. First solve the feed equation for θ.

Σ (αi zi)/(αi − θ) = 1 − q

αi is the relative volatility of component i to the heavy key; zi is the feed mole fraction; q is the feed thermal condition (q = 1 for saturated liquid, q = 0 for saturated vapour); θ is a root lying between αLK and αHK.

Then use the same θ in the reflux equation.

Rmin + 1 = Σ (αi xDi)/(αi − θ)

Rmin is the minimum reflux ratio; xDi is the distillate mole fraction of component i; the sum runs over all components.

Use each component's αi in both sums. Using only αLK-HK for every term is a common error. For a binary key mixture, the equations reduce to the pinch calculation on the y-x diagram.

Gilliland: stages at the chosen reflux

Choose a reflux ratio R above Rmin, often 1.1 to 1.5 times Rmin. Gilliland relates R, Rmin, N and Nmin through two dimensionless groups.

X = (R − Rmin)/(R + 1),   Y = (N − Nmin)/(N + 1)

X is the reflux group; Y is the stage group; R is the chosen reflux ratio; N is the theoretical stage count at R.

Read Y from the Gilliland chart or from the Molokanov fit, Y = 1 − exp{[(1 + 54.4X)/(11 + 117.2X)][(X − 1)/√X]}. Rearrange the definition of Y to get N = (Nmin + Y)/(1 − Y), then round N up to a whole number of stages.

The result is theoretical stages. N counts the reboiler as one theoretical stage, so the number of trays is about (N − 1)/E, where E is the overall tray efficiency; see 4.7. If R is close to Rmin, X is small and N becomes very large; if R is large, N approaches Nmin.

4.9 Azeotropic and extractive distillation

Some liquid mixtures cannot be separated into pure components by ordinary distillation. At an azeotrope the vapour and liquid have the same composition, so the relative volatility is 1 and extra stages achieve nothing. Azeotropic and extractive distillation add a separating agent that changes the vapour-liquid equilibrium and moves the azeotrope or removes it. The agent is an entrainer in azeotropic distillation and a high-boiling solvent in extractive distillation. These methods are used to dehydrate ethanol, purify alcohols and separate close-boiling or azeotrope-forming mixtures.

You need: 4.1 VLE and relative volatility and 4.4 McCabe-Thiele.

Azeotrope: equal vapour and liquid

An azeotrope is a composition at which the vapour and liquid in equilibrium have the same mole fractions: yi = xi. For a pair i and j the relative volatility αij = (yi/xi)/(yj/xj) is then 1, so no stage can enrich the vapour beyond this point. A minimum-boiling azeotrope boils lower than both pure components; ethanol-water at 1 atm is the standard case, about 95.6 mass % (89.5 mol %) ethanol, boiling at 78.2 °C. A maximum-boiling azeotrope boils higher than both, such as nitric acid-water near 68 mass % acid. On a y-x diagram the equilibrium curve crosses the diagonal at the azeotrope. A water-rich ethanol feed therefore gives water as bottoms and an overhead approaching the azeotrope, and more trays cannot carry it past the crossing because the driving force there is zero.

yi = xi, so αij = 1

yi and xi are mole fractions of i in vapour and liquid; αij is the relative volatility of i to j.

Azeotropic distillation with an entrainer

Azeotropic distillation adds an entrainer, a third component that forms a new, lower-boiling azeotrope with one of the feed components. That azeotrope leaves overhead and carries the component away, while the other component leaves at the bottom. For ethanol-water the entrainer forms a ternary azeotrope that takes the water overhead: cyclohexane today, benzene in older plants. The condensed overhead splits in a decanter into an entrainer-rich layer, returned as reflux, and a water-rich layer sent to a recovery column, while dry ethanol leaves at the bottom. The entrainer is recycled, so only a small make-up is needed. It must be selective, form an azeotrope that splits easily, be thermally stable, and be safe and cheap.

Extractive distillation with a solvent

Extractive distillation feeds a high-boiling, relatively nonvolatile solvent near the top of the column. The solvent does not form a new azeotrope in the usual case. It changes the activity coefficients of the feed components, so their relative volatility changes. One component becomes more volatile and leaves overhead; the other travels down with the solvent. The bottom product is solvent plus the less volatile component. A second column strips or distils the solvent from that mixture. The lean solvent is cooled and recycled to the top of the extractive column. For ethanol-water, a high-boiling solvent such as ethylene glycol makes ethanol the overhead product and sends water down with the solvent.

  1. Feed enters below the top of the extractive column.
  2. Solvent enters above the feed and flows down the column.
  3. The more volatile product leaves overhead.
  4. The bottoms, solvent plus the less volatile component, goes to solvent recovery.
  5. Recovered solvent returns to the top of the extractive column.

Pressure-swing and the choice

Pressure-swing distillation uses two columns at different pressures. The azeotrope composition shifts with pressure, so the overhead from one column can be fed to a second column where it is no longer at the azeotrope. No added solvent is needed, but the pressure change costs energy and capital. It works only when the shift is large enough. Choose azeotropic distillation when a suitable entrainer is available and a new azeotrope can be broken easily. Choose extractive distillation when a selective high-boiling solvent is available and the solvent can be recovered simply. In both cases the agent changes equilibrium; it does not violate it. The agent is recycled, so solvent losses and energy for recovery set the operating cost.

Exam traps

  • Believing a taller column can cross an azeotrope. At α = 1 every stage leaves the composition unchanged; the equilibrium curve meets the diagonal there.
  • Saying the entrainer and the extractive solvent both leave at the bottom. The entrainer leaves overhead in its new azeotrope; the extractive solvent leaves at the bottom and is recycled.
  • Taking the azeotrope composition as fixed at every pressure. It shifts with pressure, which is what pressure-swing distillation uses.
  • Treating 95.6 mass % ethanol as 95.6 mol %. With molar masses 46.07 and 18.02 kg/kmol the azeotrope is about 89.5 mol % ethanol.
  • Assuming any added liquid breaks the azeotrope. The agent must change the relative volatility strongly and be recoverable at low cost.

Analyze Compare where the entrainer and the extractive solvent leave the distillation scheme, and why. 5 marks

An entrainer forms a new azeotrope and leaves with the overhead product; it is separated later and recycled. An extractive solvent is high-boiling, leaves at the bottom with the less volatile component, and is recovered in a second column and recycled to the top.

Understand Why is the extractive solvent fed near the top of the column? 3 marks

Feeding near the top keeps a high solvent concentration throughout the column. The solvent changes activity coefficients and relative volatility along the stages.

Analyze When is pressure-swing distillation preferred over extractive distillation? 5 marks

Pressure-swing is preferred when the azeotrope composition shifts enough with pressure and no added solvent is wanted. Extractive distillation is preferred when a selective high-boiling solvent is available and easy to recover.

Takeaways

  1. Vapour and liquid compositions in equilibrium are fixed by component vapour pressures and total pressure; for an ideal pair the single number α tells you how easy the separation is.
  2. A flash gives only one equilibrium stage, so it can only make a rough split; the vapour fraction is set by the feed enthalpy.
  3. Simple distillation is batch without reflux and its performance is given by the Rayleigh equation; steam distillation lowers the boiling temperature by sharing the total pressure.
  4. The McCabe-Thiele method replaces the column by stepping between the equilibrium curve and the operating lines, with the q-line fixing where the feed enters.
  5. Lower reflux means fewer stages but the stage count explodes as R falls to Rmin; total reflux gives Nmin.
  6. Ponchon-Savarit does not assume constant molar overflow, so it stays valid for unequal latent heats and concentrated solutions.
  7. Real trays give less separation than an ideal stage, so the theoretical stages are divided by the tray efficiency to get actual trays; packing is rated by HETP.
  8. For multicomponent mixtures use shortcuts: Fenske for minimum stages, Underwood for minimum reflux, Gilliland to get stages at the chosen reflux.
  9. An azeotrope cannot be crossed by ordinary distillation; a separating agent (pressure change, azeotropic entrainer or solvent) shifts the equilibrium.

Understand State Raoult's law and Dalton's law and derive the equilibrium relation y = αx / (1 + (α − 1)x) for a binary ideal mixture. 10 marks

Points to cover. Raoult: pA = xA PAsat, pB = xB PBsat; Dalton: P = pA + pB, yA = pA/P, yB = pB/P; Divide yA/yB = (xA PAsat)/(xB PBsat), so α = PAsat/PBsat; Put yB = 1 − yA and xB = 1 − xA and solve for yA; Note α depends on temperature, so use an average α over the column.

Model answer.

  1. Raoult's law. For an ideal liquid mixture the partial pressure of each component above the liquid equals its liquid mole fraction times its pure-component vapour pressure at the same temperature: pA = xA PAsat and pB = xB PBsat. It holds when A and B are chemically similar (benzene-toluene, hexane-heptane), so that A-B attractions equal the A-A and B-B ones; the activity coefficients are then 1.
  2. Dalton's law. In an ideal gas mixture each component exerts its partial pressure independently, and the total pressure is their sum: P = pA + pB. The vapour mole fraction of A is its share of the total: yA = pA/P, and similarly yB = pB/P.
  3. Combining the two. yA = xA PAsat/P and yB = xB PBsat/P. Adding the partial pressures gives the bubble-point condition P = xA PAsat + (1 − xA)PBsat, so xA = (P − PBsat)/(PAsat − PBsat).
  4. Relative volatility. Define α = (yA/xA)/(yB/xB). Substituting the two expressions for y, the P cancels: α = (PAsat/P)/(PBsat/P) = PAsat/PBsat. For an ideal pair α is simply the ratio of the pure vapour pressures; A is the more volatile component when α > 1.
  5. Deriving the y-x relation. From the definition, yA/(1 − yA) = α xA/(1 − xA), since yB = 1 − yA and xB = 1 − xA. Cross-multiply: yA(1 − xA) = α xA(1 − yA). Expand: yA − yA xA = α xA − α xA yA. Collect the yA terms: yA(1 − xA + α xA) = α xA. Hence y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x], dropping the subscript A.
  6. Numerical illustration (PAsat = 120 kPa, PBsat = 40 kPa, P = 101.3 kPa, so α = 3):

  7. Assumptions and limits. Both laws assume ideal behaviour: an ideal liquid (no heat of mixing, no volume change) and an ideal vapour (moderate pressure). PAsat and PBsat both rise with temperature but not in the same proportion, so α changes along a column, which runs hotter at the bottom. Designers use a mean, often the geometric mean of the top and bottom values, αavg = √(αtop αbottom).
  8. Non-ideal mixtures. When the liquid is not ideal, Raoult's law becomes pA = γA xA PAsat with an activity coefficient γA. Then α = γA PAsat/(γB PBsat) varies strongly with composition and can reach 1 at an azeotrope, where the y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x] curve with a constant α no longer describes the data.
  9. Use. The relation y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x] is the equilibrium curve drawn on every McCabe-Thiele diagram, and its inverse x = y/[α − (α − 1)y] is used when stepping stages. Closing check: at x = 0.5 and α = 3, y = 1.5/2 = 0.75, which lies above the diagonal as it must for α > 1.
Understand Explain flash distillation with a neat sketch of the equipment and state what decides the fraction vaporised. 10 marks

Points to cover. Feed heated in a furnace, throttled through a valve into a low-pressure drum; Vapour and liquid separate in the drum and leave in equilibrium; Mass balance F = V + L and component balance Fz = Vy + Lx; Equilibrium relation y = f(x) links the compositions; Enthalpy balance fixes V/F; degree of freedom argument shows V/F is not free.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. Flash (equilibrium) distillation partly vaporises a liquid feed in a single step and lets the vapour and liquid separate while they are in equilibrium. It gives one theoretical stage, so the separation is coarse, but it needs no column, no trays and no reflux.
  2. Equipment, as in the sketch. The pressurised feed is heated in a heater or furnace to a temperature above its bubble point at the drum pressure, but it stays liquid because its pressure is high. It is throttled through a valve into a drum at lower pressure; part of it flashes to vapour. The vapour rises through a mist eliminator and leaves at the top; the liquid collects and leaves at the bottom.
  3. Material balances. Overall: F = V + L. Component: Fz = Vy + Lx, where F, V and L are molar flows and z, y and x the mole fractions of the more volatile component. Dividing by F with f = V/F: z = f y + (1 − f) x, which rearranges to the operating line y = −[(1 − f)/f]x + z/f. It passes through (z, z) on the diagonal with slope −L/V.
  4. Equilibrium. The products leave in equilibrium, y = K x, or for a binary of constant α, y = αx/[1 + (α − 1)x]. The answer is the intersection of the operating line with the equilibrium curve. For many components each one obeys yi = Ki xi, and combining Σxi = 1 with Σyi = 1 gives the Rachford-Rice equation Σ zi(Ki − 1)/[1 + f(Ki − 1)] = 0.
  5. What decides the fraction vaporised. The vapour fraction f is not a free choice once the drum conditions are set. In an isothermal flash, fixing the drum temperature and pressure fixes every Ki, and Rachford-Rice then gives one f. In an adiabatic flash the feed enthalpy and drum pressure are fixed; the enthalpy balance F hF = V HV + L hL, solved together with equilibrium, gives both the drum temperature and f. Raising the feed temperature or lowering the drum pressure raises f; raising the pressure lowers every K and so lowers f.
  6. Degrees of freedom. For a binary feed of known F, z and enthalpy, specifying the drum pressure leaves one more specification: temperature, f, or one product composition. Choose one and the others follow; specifying two of them over-fixes the problem.
  7. Worked number. F = 100 kmol/h, z = 0.50, KA = 2.0, KB = 0.5: two-phase test Σz K = 1.25 > 1 and Σz/K = 1.25 > 1. f = −[0.5(1) + 0.5(−0.5)]/[(1)(−0.5)] = 0.50, so V = L = 50 kmol/h, x = 0.50/1.50 = 0.333 and y = 0.667.
  8. Uses. Pre-flash ahead of crude distillation, removing light gases from a liquid product, flashing hot condensate, and multi-stage flash desalination. Closing check: 50(0.667) + 50(0.333) = 50.0 = 100(0.50), so the component balance closes.
Understand Explain steam distillation. Why does adding steam reduce the boiling temperature of an organic liquid? 8 marks

Points to cover. Organic and water are immiscible, so each exerts its own vapour pressure; Total pressure = porganic + pwater; Boiling starts when the sum equals the applied pressure; Each component boils below its own normal boiling point; Steam needed: moles of steam per mole of organic = pwater/porganic; Used for heat-sensitive, high-boiling organics.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose. Many high-boiling organics (essential oils, aniline, fatty acids) decompose at their normal boiling points. Steam distillation lets them distil well below that temperature, at atmospheric pressure, without a vacuum system.
  2. Immiscible liquids. Water and the organic are practically insoluble in each other, so they form two separate liquid layers. Each layer behaves as a pure liquid and exerts its full pure-component vapour pressure, whatever the amount of the other: pwater = p°water(T) and porganic = p°organic(T). Raoult's law with mole fractions does not apply because there is no common solution.
  3. Boiling condition. The mixture boils when the sum of the two vapour pressures equals the applied pressure: P = p°water(T) + p°organic(T). Because the two pressures add, the sum reaches P at a temperature where each pressure is below P. So the mixture boils below 100 °C (the water alone) and well below the organic's own boiling point.
  4. Steam requirement. In the vapour the mole ratio of the two components equals the ratio of their partial pressures. Moles of steam per mole of organic = p°water/p°organic. The mass of steam per kg of organic = (p°water/p°organic)(18/Morganic). A high molar mass therefore keeps the mass of steam small even when the molar ratio is large.

Understand Define reflux ratio, minimum reflux ratio and total reflux, and explain their effect on the McCabe-Thiele construction. 10 marks

Points to cover. R = L/D; Higher R: operating line further from equilibrium, fewer stages, more liquid and vapour traffic; Rmin: operating line touches equilibrium at the pinch, infinite stages; Total reflux: R → ∞, operating line is the diagonal y = x, minimum stages; Sketch the three constructions.

Model answer.

  1. Reflux ratio. R = L/D, the liquid returned to the top of the column per mole of distillate. The vapour to the condenser is V = (R + 1)D. On the y-x diagram the rectifying operating line is y = [R/(R + 1)]x + xD/(R + 1), through (xD, xD) with slope R/(R + 1).
  2. Effect of R. Raising R steepens the rectifying line toward the diagonal and moves it away from the equilibrium curve. The steps between curve and line then grow and fewer stages reach xB. The cost is more liquid and vapour traffic, so a wider column and larger reboiler and condenser duties, which grow as (R + 1)D.
  3. Minimum reflux. As R falls the operating line approaches the equilibrium curve. At Rmin the two touch at the pinch; there the driving force is zero and the staircase never passes the pinch, so the stage count is infinite. For a saturated-liquid feed and an ideal curve the pinch lies on the q-line at xF: Rmin = (xD − yF*)/(yF* − xF). For a non-ideal curve a tangent pinch above the feed may come first.
  4. Total reflux. No product is drawn: R → ∞, the operating lines lie on the diagonal y = x, and the steps between curve and diagonal give the minimum number of stages, Nmin. For constant α, Fenske gives Nmin = log[(xD/(1 − xD))((1 − xB)/xB)]/log α. It is used at start-up and for efficiency tests.

Apply A McCabe-Thiele construction gives 12 theoretical stages. The column uses sieve trays with an overall efficiency of 60%. Find the number of actual trays and explain the meaning of the efficiency value. 8 marks

Points to cover. Actual trays = theoretical stages / efficiency; 12/0.60 = 20 actual trays; Efficiency less than 1 because contact time is finite and trays are not perfectly mixed; If the 12 stages include a partial reboiler, trays = 11/0.60 = 18.3 → 19; state which basis is used.

Model answer.

  1. Overall efficiency. Eo = Nt/Na, the theoretical stages divided by the actual trays needed for the same separation. Rearranged, Na = Nt/Eo.
  2. Calculation. If the 12 theoretical stages are all column trays (a total reboiler that does no separation, or a stage count quoted for the column alone): Na = 12/0.60 = 20 actual trays.
  3. Reboiler basis. A partial reboiler is itself an equilibrium stage and is counted in a McCabe-Thiele staircase. If the 12 stages include it, only 11 are trays: Na = 11/0.60 = 18.3, rounded up to 19 actual trays plus the reboiler. A partial condenser, when present, is also an equilibrium stage and is removed the same way. State the basis in the answer.
  4. Summary:

  6. Meaning of 60 %. On a real sieve tray the vapour and liquid are in contact for a fraction of a second, the liquid is not perfectly mixed across the tray, and some liquid is carried up as entrainment or weeps down through the holes. The vapour therefore leaves less enriched than an equilibrium stage would make it. An overall efficiency of 0.60 means the column needs 1/0.60 = 1.67 real trays for each ideal stage.
  7. Murphree efficiency. The local measure is EM = (yn − yn+1)/(yn* − yn+1), the real enrichment of the vapour across tray n divided by the enrichment an equilibrium tray would give. With straight lines, Lewis's relation Eo = ln[1 + EM(λ − 1)]/ln λ links the two, λ = mV/L.
  8. What sets the value. Typical Eo for hydrocarbon sieve trays is 0.6 to 0.8; viscous liquids, high relative volatility and poor hydraulics lower it. O'Connell's correlation estimates Eo from the product of α and the liquid viscosity.
  9. Answer: 20 actual trays if the 12 stages are trays only; 19 trays plus the reboiler if one of the 12 is the partial reboiler. Closing check: 19 × 0.60 = 11.4 ≥ 11 ideal trays, and 20 × 0.60 = 12.
Apply Explain the Ponchon-Savarit construction on an enthalpy-concentration diagram and show how the difference points are located for a given feed. 13 marks

Points to cover. Plot saturated liquid and saturated vapour lines and tie lines; Locate ΔD at (xD, HΔD) with HΔD = hD + QC/D = H1 + R(H1 − hD); Locate ΔB below xB; Feed point F on the h-z line; ΔD, F, ΔB are collinear; Operating lines are drawn from Δ points through points on the saturated lines; Count stages by alternating tie lines and operating lines.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose. Ponchon-Savarit designs a binary column without constant molar overflow by carrying the enthalpy balance on an enthalpy-composition diagram. It is needed when latent heats differ, heats of mixing are large or the boiling range is wide.
  2. The diagram. Plot the saturated-vapour enthalpy H against y and the saturated-liquid enthalpy h against x, both per mole of mixture, on the same axes. Equilibrium tie lines join each liquid x to its vapour y. The vertical gap H − h is the latent heat.
  3. Rectifying difference point. Around the condenser: V1 = L0 + D, and the enthalpy balance V1 H1 = L0 hD + D hD + QC. Define HΔD = hD + QC/D. With R = L0/D this becomes HΔD = H1 + R(H1 − hD). ΔD = (xD, HΔD) lies above the H curve.
  4. Why the lines are straight. For any section above the feed, Vn+1 − Ln = D, Vn+1yn+1 − Ln xn = DxD and Vn+1Hn+1 − Ln hn = D HΔD. So (xn, hn), (yn+1, Hn+1) and ΔD lie on one straight line: the operating line. The lever rule on it gives Ln/Vn+1 = (HΔD − Hn+1)/(HΔD − hn).
  5. Stripping difference point. Around the reboiler, Lm − Vm+1 = B and HΔB = hB − QB/B, so ΔB = (xB, HΔB) lies below the h curve.
  6. Locating ΔB for a given feed. An overall balance F = D + B and F hF + QB = D hD + B hB + QC gives F hF = D HΔD + B HΔB, so ΔD, the feed point F = (zF, hF) and ΔB are collinear. Draw the line from ΔD through F and extend it to x = xB.
  7. Stepping. From y1 = xD on the H curve follow the tie line to x1 on the h curve; draw the line from ΔD through x1 to cut the H curve at y2; repeat. When the tie line crosses the line ΔD-F-ΔB, switch to ΔB. Count the tie lines; the last, at x ≤ xB, is the reboiler.

Analyze Compare the McCabe-Thiele and Ponchon-Savarit methods. When does the constant molar overflow assumption fail, and why does this matter? 13 marks

Points to cover. McCabe-Thiele: y-x diagram only, needs constant molar overflow and constant latent heat; Ponchon-Savarit: uses enthalpy balance and enthalpy-concentration diagram, no constant molar overflow requirement; Constant molar overflow fails for wide boiling ranges, unequal latent heats, concentrated solutions, heat of mixing; Ponchon-Savarit also handles non-ideal equilibrium through the saturated curves; Cost: Ponchon-Savarit needs enthalpy data, more work per construction.

Model answer.

  1. What each uses. McCabe-Thiele works on a y-x diagram with the equilibrium curve and straight operating lines from material balances alone. Ponchon-Savarit works on an enthalpy-composition diagram with the saturated H and h curves, tie lines and difference points, so it carries material and energy balances together.
  2. Comparison:
    FeatureMcCabe-ThielePonchon-Savarit
    Diagramy against xenthalpy against x, y
    Data neededVLE onlyVLE plus H and h curves
    Operating linesstraight, slope L/V constantlines through ΔD, ΔB; L/V varies
    Assumptionconstant molar overflownone on the flows
    Heat dutiesestimated separatelyread from Δ points: QC = D(HΔD − hD)
    Effortquick, standard first designmore data and drawing
  3. Constant molar overflow. McCabe-Thiele assumes that each mole of vapour condensing on a tray boils exactly one mole of liquid, so L and V are constant within each section. That needs (1) equal molar latent heats of the two components, (2) negligible heat of mixing, (3) negligible sensible-heat change from tray to tray, and (4) an adiabatic column.
  4. When it fails. Unequal latent heats (ammonia-water, methanol-water), large heats of mixing (acid-water systems such as nitric acid or sulphuric acid with water), a wide boiling range so that sensible heat matters, and columns with heat losses or side heaters. On the enthalpy diagram these show up as H and h curves that slope differently or curve.
  5. Why it matters. If L/V changes from tray to tray, the true operating line is curved. Using a straight line then misplaces every step: the stage count, the feed stage and the reboiler and condenser duties can all be wrong. In the chapter example with H = 50 − 10y and h = 20 − 4x, L/V fell from 0.663 to 0.653 over three trays instead of staying at 0.667, and the vapours drifted from the McCabe-Thiele values (0.7800 against 0.7764 after three trays). Over a long column such drift adds or removes stages.
  6. The link. Ponchon-Savarit contains McCabe-Thiele as a limit. With horizontal H and h lines of constant gap λ, HΔD = HV + Rλ and the lever rule gives L/V = Rλ/[(R + 1)λ] = R/(R + 1) on every tray: the straight McCabe-Thiele line returns.
  7. Practical choice. McCabe-Thiele for nearly ideal pairs with similar latent heats (benzene-toluene) and for quick estimates and teaching. Ponchon-Savarit, or a rigorous simulation with enthalpy balances, for the non-CMO systems above and whenever the heat duties must be known with the stage count.
  8. Closing check: for benzene (30.7 kJ/mol) and toluene (33.2 kJ/mol) the latent heats differ by about 8 %, small enough for McCabe-Thiele; for methanol (35.3) and water (40.7 kJ/mol) the difference is about 15 %, and the error grows with it.
Evaluate Ethanol and water form a minimum-boiling azeotrope. Explain why ordinary distillation cannot give pure ethanol and evaluate ways to break the azeotrope. 13 marks

Points to cover. At the azeotrope y = x, the equilibrium curve crosses the diagonal; The pinch point lies on the diagonal, so no finite number of stages crosses it; Options: change pressure (azeotrope composition moves), azeotropic distillation with an entrainer, extractive distillation with a heavy solvent, molecular sieve drying; Compare energy use, solvent recovery, capital cost; Choose based on scale and product purity needed.

Model answer.

  1. The azeotrope. Ethanol-water shows a strong positive deviation from Raoult's law. At 1 atm the mixture of about 95.6 mass % ethanol (89.5 mol %) boils at 78.2 °C, below pure ethanol (78.4 °C) and pure water (100 °C). At that composition the vapour and liquid have the same composition: y = x and α = 1.
  2. Why ordinary distillation stops there. On the y-x diagram the equilibrium curve lies above the diagonal for x below 0.895 and crosses it at the azeotrope. A column fed with dilute ethanol sends water to the bottoms, but the overhead can only approach 0.895: as the steps near the crossing they shrink to zero, because each stage needs y > x to make progress. Adding stages or reflux cannot move the distillate past it. Pure ethanol is not reachable from the water-rich side.
  3. Mass balance illustration. From 100 kg of 50 mass % ethanol, all the ethanol taken overhead at the azeotrope gives D = 50/0.956 = 52.3 kg and water bottoms of 47.7 kg. The product is still 4.4 % water.
  4. Option 1, pressure-swing distillation. The azeotrope composition shifts with pressure; for ethanol-water it becomes richer in ethanol at low pressure and disappears below roughly 10 kPa. Two columns at different pressures pass the azeotrope back and forth. No added chemical, but the shift for ethanol-water at practical pressures is small, so recycle and energy are large.
  5. Option 2, azeotropic distillation. An entrainer (cyclohexane today, benzene in older plants) forms a lower-boiling ternary azeotrope that carries the water overhead. The overhead splits in a decanter into an entrainer layer (reflux) and a water layer (to a recovery column); dry ethanol leaves at the bottom. Proven, but it needs three columns and careful control, and benzene is toxic.
  6. Option 3, extractive distillation. A high-boiling solvent (ethylene glycol) fed near the top lowers the volatility of water; ethanol goes overhead, water leaves with the solvent, and a second column recovers the glycol for recycle. Simpler and robust; the cost is solvent recovery energy and some solvent loss.
  7. Option 4, molecular sieve adsorption. 3 Å zeolite beads adsorb water but not the larger ethanol molecule, in two beds alternating between adsorption and regeneration (pressure-swing adsorption), fed with azeotropic vapour. Low energy, no third chemical; the beds must be replaced after some years.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Two costs. The reflux ratio R = L/D fixes the vapour flow V = (R + 1)D, which sets the reboiler and condenser duties (energy cost) and the column diameter. It also fixes the stage count, which sets the column height. Annual cost = annualised capital (shell, trays, exchangers) + energy (steam and cooling water).
  2. Energy cost rises with R. Reboiler duty ≈ V λ = (R + 1)Dλ, so steam and cooling water grow linearly with R + 1.
  3. Capital cost falls, then rises. Near Rmin the stage count is very large, so the column is tall and expensive. As R rises the stages fall steeply at first, then level off toward Nmin. Glossary

Bottoms
The liquid product withdrawn from the bottom of the column, richer in the less volatile component.
Bubble point
The temperature at which the first bubble of vapour appears from a liquid mixture at a stated pressure.
Constant molar overflow
Assumption that the liquid and vapour molar flows are the same in each section of the column, giving straight operating lines.
Dew point
The temperature at which the first drop of liquid condenses from a vapour mixture at a stated pressure.
Difference point
The fixed point Δ on a Ponchon-Savarit diagram through which every operating line of one column section passes.
Distillate
The overhead product withdrawn from the top of the column.
Distillation
A separation method that exploits different volatilities, producing a vapour richer in the more volatile component than the liquid it came from.
Entrainer
A third component added in azeotropic distillation to carry the mixture past the azeotrope.
Equilibrium stage
An ideal contact stage in which the leaving vapour and liquid are in equilibrium.
HETP
Height of packed bed giving one theoretical stage; smaller HETP means more efficient packing.
Ideal solution
A mixture obeying Raoult's law at every composition, with no heat of mixing.
Key components
The light and heavy components that define the required split in multicomponent distillation; the others are distributed.
Minimum-boiling azeotrope
An azeotrope that boils below both pure components (positive deviation from Raoult's law), such as ethanol-water at about 89.5 mol % ethanol.
Murphree efficiency
The actual change in vapour composition across a real tray divided by the change an ideal tray would give.
Non-ideal system
A mixture whose equilibrium deviates from Raoult's law because of interactions or association; can show a maximum or minimum azeotrope.
Packed column
A column filled with rings, saddles or structured packing to give a large contact area for vapour and liquid.
q (feed condition)
Heat to turn one mole of feed into saturated vapour divided by the molar latent heat; 1 for saturated liquid, 0 for saturated vapour.
Reboiler
A heater at the base that boils part of the bottoms to supply vapour up the column; counted as one equilibrium stage.
Rectification
Multi-stage distillation in which the rising vapour contacts reflux liquid flowing down the column.
Rectifying section
The part of the column above the feed, enriched by reflux, where the operating line lies above the diagonal.
Relative volatility
The ratio of the vapour-liquid distribution coefficients of two components; values near 1 mean a hard separation.
Solvent (extractive distillation)
A high-boiling liquid that alters the relative volatility of the components without forming a new azeotrope.
Stripping section
The part of the column below the feed, where the operating line lies below the diagonal and x decreases downwards.
Theoretical stage
Same as an equilibrium stage; the ideal plate or packing height that achieves one equilibrium contact.
Total condenser
A condenser that turns all the overhead vapour into liquid; part returns as reflux and the rest leaves as distillate.
Tray column
A vertical shell with horizontal plates (sieve, valve or bubble-cap) on which vapour bubbles through liquid.
