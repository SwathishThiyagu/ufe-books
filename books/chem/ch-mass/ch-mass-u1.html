1.1 Mass transfer operations and their classification

Mass transfer is the movement of one component through a mixture because that component is more concentrated in one place than in another. Refining crude oil, drying a shirt on a balcony, scrubbing CO2 out of flue gas, and your blood picking up oxygen all work the same way: a concentration difference pushes a species from a rich region to a lean one. Almost every separation that works by moving a component between phases is a named way of arranging that push. Mechanical separations such as filtration and centrifugation are a different family and are not part of this subject. This chapter defines the subject, sorts the operations into families, and fixes the vocabulary of concentration that every later formula uses.

You need mole and mass balances, the ideal gas law, and the idea of a phase from the thermodynamics course; no earlier chapter of this unit is assumed.

Mass transfer: one component moving because it is unevenly spread

Mass transfer is the movement of a component inside a mixture, or from one phase to another, driven by a difference in concentration. Concentration here means how much of that component sits in a given volume or in a given number of moles. The component moves from where it is rich to where it is lean. No pump, no stirrer and no heat are needed for the movement itself.

Compare the three transport subjects. Heat transfer moves energy because of a temperature difference. Fluid flow moves the whole mixture together because of a pressure difference. Mass transfer moves one species relative to the others because of a concentration difference. A river carrying salt to the sea moves water and salt together — that is fluid flow. Salt slowly spreading from a salty patch into a fresh patch of the same river is mass transfer.

The subject exists because almost every separation in a chemical plant is built on this one movement. You contact two phases, arrange that one phase prefers the wanted component, and diffusion carries it across. Two examples: CO2 washed out of flue gas by an amine solution, and sugar leached from chopped cane by hot water.

Four families, sorted by how the second phase is made

A single phase cannot be separated by mass transfer alone. Separation needs a second phase — immiscible, or only partly miscible, with the first — that holds the wanted component more strongly or more weakly. The four families below differ only in how that second phase is produced. Knowing the family tells you which unit of this subject you are reading.

  • Phase creation — heat or work makes a new phase out of the feed. Distillation, where heat raises a vapour; evaporation, where solvent is boiled off; crystallization, where cooling or concentrating makes solid appear. See Unit 4 and Unit 6.
  • Phase addition — a foreign liquid or gas is added and it dissolves the wanted component. Absorption (gas into liquid), extraction (solute from one liquid into another), leaching (solute from a solid into a liquid). See Unit 3 and Unit 5.
  • Barrier — a membrane lets one species through faster than another. Reverse osmosis passes water and holds salt back; dialysis passes small molecules and holds large ones. See Unit 6.
  • Solid agent — a solid surface or resin takes up the solute. Adsorption on activated carbon, ion exchange on a resin. See Unit 6.

Most books place drying with hot gas under phase creation, since heat makes the vapour; a few treat the hot gas as an added phase and put it under phase addition. Drying is never a solid-agent operation in the adsorption sense, because the solid gives up water rather than taking up solute. For any operation named in an exam, state which component moves and which phase is new.

Phases in contact, contactors, and the four numbers called concentration

The same operations are also sorted by the phases that touch. Gas-liquid: absorption, distillation. Liquid-liquid: extraction. Gas-solid: adsorption, drying. Liquid-solid: leaching, ion exchange, crystallization. Each pair brings its own equipment, its own equilibrium relation and its own mass transfer coefficient, which is why the later units are organised this way.

A third sorting is by contactor. A stage-wise contactor mixes the two phases, lets them approach equilibrium, then separates them; a tray column is a stack of such stages. A differential contactor keeps the phases in touch continuously along a path, so composition changes smoothly from end to end; a packed tower, a falling film and a spray are examples. Batch and continuous cut across both. Contactor behaviour is developed in Unit 2.

Four different numbers are all called concentration. Learn them now, because every flux formula later needs the right one.

Exam traps

  • Writing 'concentration' without saying which one. Mole fraction and mass fraction are dimensionless and are not concentrations; a flux needs mol/m³ or kg/m³. Converting a molar concentration to a mass concentration multiplies by the molar mass in kg/mol. Always write the symbol and the unit.
  • Using c = P/RT for a liquid. That formula holds for an ideal gas only. For a liquid use the molar density c = ρ/M, with ρ in kg/m³ and M in kg/mol.
  • Swapping y and x. By long convention y is the mole fraction in the vapour or gas and x is the mole fraction in the liquid. An answer that writes x for a gas stream loses marks even when the number is right.
  • Thinking mass transfer needs bulk flow. Diffusion carries a species through a completely still mixture; diffusion and bulk flow are separated properly in 1.2.
  • Classifying by equipment instead of by mechanism. A packed tower is not a separation; it is a contactor that can carry out absorption, stripping or distillation, and the examiner often asks you to say which.

1.2 Molecular and eddy diffusion; diffusion velocities and fluxes

Mass transfer is easier to write once every species has its own velocity. Species A moves with velocity v_A, and c_A v_A is its molar flux N_A. That flux contains two parts: A drifting relative to the mixture, and the whole mixture moving. This chapter fixes the vocabulary of velocities, fluxes and reference frames for binary and multicomponent mixtures. It also separates molecular diffusion, the random walk of molecules, from eddy diffusion, the mixing by turbulent lumps. Every later calculation of D, absorption and distillation uses this language.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for concentration, mole fraction and the idea of a component moving because of a concentration difference.

Species velocity and molar flux

Pick a small window fixed to the laboratory wall. Species i crosses it with an average velocity v_i, in m/s. Its molar concentration is c_i, in mol/m³. The molar flux of i through this fixed window is N_i = c_i v_i, in mol/(m²·s). The total concentration is c = Σc_i, and the mole fraction is y_i = c_i/c. For a binary mixture A + B, c = c_A + c_B and y_A + y_B = 1.

The molar flux N_A is not the mass flux. The mass flux is n_A = ρ_A v_A = M_A N_A, in kg/(m²·s). Here ρ_A is the mass concentration in kg/m³ and M_A is the molar mass in kg/mol. The mass fraction is w_A = ρ_A/ρ. Use the table to keep the objects separate.

Molar average velocity and diffusion flux

The mixture itself has a velocity. The molar average velocity is u = Σc_i v_i/c = ΣN_i/c, in m/s. For a binary, u = (N_A + N_B)/c. The diffusion velocity of A is v_A − u. The diffusion flux of A is J_A = c_A(v_A − u), in mol/(m²·s). This J_A is measured relative to the molar-average frame, not the laboratory wall.

Add and subtract c_A u: N_A = c_A v_A = c_A u + c_A(v_A − u) = y_A(N_A + N_B) + J_A. Rearranged, N_A = J_A + y_A(N_A + N_B). The term y_A(N_A + N_B) is the bulk flow; it is not diffusion. In a binary, J_A = −J_B because c_A(v_A − u) + c_B(v_B − u) = Σc_i v_i − c u = 0. But N_A is not −N_B unless the fluxes are equimolar, N_A + N_B = 0.

N_A = J_A + y_A(N_A + N_B)

N_A and N_B are fixed-frame molar fluxes in mol/(m²·s); J_A is the diffusion flux of A in mol/(m²·s); y_A is the mole fraction of A. The second term is the bulk flow.

Other frames have other names. The mass-average velocity is u_m = Σρ_i v_i/ρ, and the mass-average diffusion flux is j_A = ρ_A(v_A − u_m). The volume-average frame uses volume-fraction weighting; it is common for liquid mixtures. Use N_A in the fixed frame, J_A in the molar-average frame, and j_A in the mass-average frame.

Molecular and eddy diffusion

Molecular diffusion comes from random molecular motion. Molecules of A and B collide and swap places even when the fluid is still. Eddy diffusion comes from turbulence. Eddies carry lumps of fluid, with their A and B, from one place to another. Eddy diffusion is fast in the core of a pipe and zero at a solid wall, where the eddy size falls to zero.

The measured mean flux can contain both parts. Write J_A = J_A(mol) + J_A(eddy), then N_A = J_A + y_A(N_A + N_B). The eddy part is modelled with an eddy diffusivity D_E, in m²/s: J_A(eddy) = −D_E dc_A/dz. Here dc_A/dz is the gradient of the mean concentration, in mol/m⁴. D_E depends on flow speed, geometry and distance from the wall; it is not a property of the A–B pair. At the wall D_E → 0, so only molecular diffusion survives.

The molecular part uses D_AB, the molecular diffusivity of the pair at that temperature and pressure. Chapter 1.3 gives Fick's law for that part. Unit 2 turns the near-wall molecular region into a mass transfer coefficient. For a dilute species, engineers often add D_AB + D_E as an effective diffusivity, but the bulk term stays separate.

Worked example

Question. A binary gas at 300 K and 1 atm has c = 40.6 mol/m³ and y_A = 0.30. The measured molar fluxes are N_A = 3.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s) and N_B = 1.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s). Find the molar average velocity u, the diffusion flux J_A, the velocity of A and its diffusion velocity.

  1. Given c = 40.6 mol/m³, y_A = 0.30, N_A = 3.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), N_B = 1.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s). The total molar flux is N_A + N_B = 4.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).
  2. Molar average velocity: u = (N_A + N_B)/c = 4.0×10⁻³/40.6 = 9.85×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  3. Concentration of A: c_A = y_A c = 0.30 × 40.6 = 12.18 mol/m³.
  4. Diffusion flux: J_A = N_A − y_A(N_A + N_B) = 3.0×10⁻³ − 0.30 × 4.0×10⁻³ = 3.0×10⁻³ − 1.2×10⁻³ = 1.8×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).
  5. Velocity of A: v_A = N_A/c_A = 3.0×10⁻³/12.18 = 2.46×10⁻⁴ m/s.
  6. Diffusion velocity: v_A − u = 2.46×10⁻⁴ − 9.85×10⁻⁵ = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s. Check: J_A/c_A = 1.8×10⁻³/12.18 = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s.

Answer. u = 9.85×10⁻⁵ m/s, J_A = 1.8×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), c_A = 12.18 mol/m³, v_A = 2.46×10⁻⁴ m/s, and the diffusion velocity v_A − u = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Calling N_A the diffusion flux. N_A is the fixed-frame flux; J_A is the diffusion flux. At large y_A the bulk term y_A(N_A + N_B) is a big part of N_A. Setting N_A = J_A gives a wrong D in Chapter 1.3.
  • Assuming N_A = −N_B always. Only equimolar counter-diffusion has that; in general J_A = −J_B while N_A + N_B is not zero.
  • Mixing units of mass and molar flux. n_A = M_A N_A; use kg/(m²·s) for n_A and mol/(m²·s) for N_A.
  • Using the molar average velocity u as the fluid flow velocity. u is a concentration-weighted mean of species velocities; the bulk term is y_A(N_A + N_B), not c_A times a guessed flow.
  • Treating eddy diffusivity D_E as a fluid property. D_E depends on turbulence and position and falls to zero at a wall; D_AB depends on the pair, temperature and pressure.

1.3 Fick's first and second laws of diffusion

Fick's first law says a component diffuses down its concentration gradient, with flux proportional to the gradient. Fick's second law adds conservation of mass, so it predicts how concentration changes with time at a point. Together they turn a qualitative idea—ink spreading in water, oxygen entering a lung, dopant entering silicon—into equations an engineer can solve. This chapter states both laws, derives the second from a slab balance, and lists the conditions that make a diffusion problem well posed.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for JA, NA and diffusion velocities.

First law: flux from a gradient

Fick's first law applies at a point in a mixture. In general, it relates the molar diffusion flux of A to the gradient of mole fraction. The minus sign is physics: A moves from high yA to low yA, so when dyA/dz is negative, JA is positive in the +z direction.

JA = −c DAB dyA/dz

JA is the molar diffusion flux of A relative to the molar average velocity, mol/(m²·s); c is total molar concentration, mol/m³; DAB is the binary diffusivity of A in B, m²/s; yA is mole fraction of A; z is position, m.

By definition, cA = c yA; when c is constant, dcA/dz = c dyA/dz. The general form then becomes the special case JA = −DAB dcA/dz.

JA = −DAB dcA/dz

valid when c is constant; cA is molar concentration of A, mol/m³; other symbols as above.

JA is measured relative to the molar average velocity of the mixture. The molar flux measured relative to a fixed laboratory frame is NA. It contains JA plus the bulk flow carried by the mixture (see Chapter 1.2). For a binary mixture:

NA = −c DAB dyA/dz + yA(NA + NB)

NA and NB are molar fluxes relative to a fixed frame, mol/(m²·s).

For a binary ideal mixture DAB = DBA. The first law is local: it gives the flux at one position from the gradient there. It does not by itself say how the concentration changes with time.

Second law: a shell balance adds time

To get time, add conservation of mass over a small slab. Consider one-dimensional diffusion in z through a slab of cross-sectional area S and thickness Δz. Take no convection, no reaction and constant D first.

  1. A enters at z and leaves at z + Δz.
  2. Moles in minus moles out equals accumulation: JA at z times S − JA at z + Δz times S = S Δz ∂cA/∂t.
  3. Divide by S Δz, then let Δz → 0: −∂JA/∂z = ∂cA/∂t.
  4. Put JA = −DAB ∂cA/∂z: ∂cA/∂t = DAB ∂²cA/∂z².
∂cA/∂t = DAB ∂²cA/∂z²

cA is concentration at position z and time t; DAB is constant; no reaction.

With a reaction that produces A at net rate RA (mol/(m³·s)), and with D depending on concentration, the general one-dimensional form is:

∂cA/∂t = ∂/∂z(DAB ∂cA/∂z) + RA

RA is positive for production and negative for consumption; DAB may depend on cA.

To solve the second law, a problem needs one initial condition and two boundary conditions, because the equation is first order in t and second order in z. Typical conditions: cA(z,0) = f(z); cA(0,t) = c0; a fixed flux −D ∂cA/∂z = NA; an impermeable surface ∂cA/∂z = 0; a convective surface −D ∂cA/∂z = kc(cs − cbulk), where kc is a mass transfer coefficient in m/s. The last condition is used in Chapter 1.9.

Steady state, boundary conditions and limits

At steady state, concentration at each point stops changing: ∂cA/∂t = 0. With constant D and no reaction, the second law becomes d²cA/dz² = 0. Integrate twice: cA = a + bz, where a and b are constants fixed by boundary conditions. A straight-line profile is therefore the steady answer for a slab, not a general property of diffusion. In the worked example the profile is straight and the steady state is consistent.

Limits to check before using the simple laws: dilute solutions so properties are well behaved; no convection beyond the bulk term already included; no reaction unless RA is retained; one-dimensional geometry; constant D. For gases D changes little with concentration. For liquids D can change strongly with concentration, so use the variable-D form. If the slab is not flat, use the geometry in Chapter 1.5. For time-dependent solutions see Chapter 1.9.

Worked example

Question. A gas mixture at 1 atm and 300 K has total concentration c = 40.6 mol/m³ and D_AB = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s. The mole fraction of A is 0.30 at z = 0 and 0.10 at z = 4 mm. (a) Find J_A if the profile is a straight line. (b) Does that profile satisfy Fick's second law at steady state?

  1. Convert thickness: Δz = 4 mm = 0.004 m.
  2. Mole-fraction gradient: dy_A/dz = (0.10 − 0.30)/0.004 = −50 m⁻¹.
  3. Concentration gradient: dc_A/dz = c dy_A/dz = 40.6 × (−50) = −2030 mol/m⁴.
  4. First law: J_A = −D_AB dc_A/dz = −(1.8×10⁻⁵)(−2030) = 3.654×10⁻² mol/(m²·s). Positive, so towards increasing z.
  5. Steady second law: ∂c_A/∂t = 0 gives d²c_A/dz² = 0. A linear c_A(z) has zero second derivative, so yes, it satisfies the steady equation.

Answer. The gradient is −2030 mol/m⁴ and J_A = 3.654×10⁻² mol/(m²·s) towards increasing z; a straight line satisfies steady Fick's second law.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the minus sign. If y_A falls as z increases, J_A is positive in the +z direction; a negative J_A means flux is towards −z.
  • Using J_A when the question asks for N_A through a stagnant gas. Add the bulk term y_A(N_A + N_B); the result contains the factor 1/(y_B)_lm.
  • Treating D as constant for every fluid. Gas D changes little with composition, but liquid D can change strongly with concentration.
  • Assuming a straight-line profile always. It holds only for steady state, constant D, no reaction and no source term.
  • Flipping the reaction sign. A net production of A adds R_A to the right side; a consumption term is negative.

1.4 Steady diffusion in gases and liquids: stagnant and equimolar

Steady one-dimensional diffusion has two standard answers. If A and B swap places in equal amounts, the flux follows the plain mole-fraction difference. If B cannot pass, A still moves through B, and the bulk drift adds a factor 1/(y_B)lm. The same equations describe gases and liquids, with c replaced by liquid molar density and y by x. They appear in evaporation into air, absorption from an insoluble carrier, and the film model used later for mass transfer coefficients.

You need Chapter 1.2 for flux definitions and Chapter 1.3 for Fick's first law and simple integration.

Equimolar counter-diffusion

Take steady, one-dimensional diffusion along z in a binary gas or liquid mixture. D is the diffusivity (m²/s), c is the total molar concentration (mol/m³), yA is the mole fraction of A, and NA is the molar flux of A (mol m⁻² s⁻¹). Constant D and c are assumed. Equimolar counter-diffusion means NA = −NB: every molecule of A moving in one direction is replaced by a molecule of B moving the other way. The net molar bulk flow is zero. Integrating Fick's first law over a path of length L (m) gives the formula below. The mole-fraction profile is a straight line. Distillation of close-boiling components is often treated this way, and so is two-gas diffusion in a straight tube when the counterfluxes are equal.

NA = D c (yA1 − yA2) / L = −NB

yA1 and yA2 are the mole fractions of A at z = 0 and z = L; L is the diffusion path length; NB is the molar flux of B, with the opposite sign.

Diffusion through stagnant B

Now B cannot cross the end planes, so NB = 0. This is diffusion of A through a non-diffusing B: evaporation of a liquid into air, or absorption of a soluble gas from an insoluble carrier. Fick's law for A alone is not enough because the mixture itself drifts. The total flux of A is NA = −D c dyA/dz + yA(NA + NB). Set NB = 0 and rearrange: NA(1 − yA) = −D c dyA/dz. Since yB = 1 − yA, integration from z = 0 to z = L gives the log form below. The same result written with a log mean is shown next to it. The factor 1/(yB)lm is greater than 1, so the flux is larger than in the equimolar case: A must drift through B. A film model treats L as a stagnant gas film next to an interface and uses this equation inside the film.

NA = (D c / L) ln(yB2/yB1) = D c (yA1 − yA2) / [L (yB)lm]

(yB)lm = (yB2 − yB1) / ln(yB2/yB1); yB1 and yB2 are the mole fractions of B at z = 0 and z = L.

Liquids, films and laminar flow

For a liquid solution, replace c by the solution molar density (mol/m³) and y by x, the liquid mole fraction. For water, c is about 55,500 mol/m³. The stagnant-B equation becomes the formula below. Liquid D values are much smaller than gas D values, but c is about a thousand times larger, so liquid fluxes can still be significant. The assumptions are steady state, constant D and c, and one-dimensional transport. Laminar flow here means smooth streamlines and no eddies, so transport across the stream is by molecular diffusion only. Standard apparatus are the wetted-wall column, where liquid runs as a thin film down a tube wall and gas flows in the core, and the laminar jet. A Stefan tube is the gas-phase stagnant-B apparatus. Do not use the gas c for a liquid.

NA = (D c / L) ln(xB2/xB1) = D c (xA1 − xA2) / [L (xB)lm]

xA1, xA2, xB1, xB2 are liquid mole fractions at the two planes; (xB)lm is the log mean of xB, defined like (yB)lm; c is the solution molar density.

Worked example

Question. A gas at 300 K and 1 atm has c = 40.6 mol/m³ and D_AB = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s. The mole fraction of A is 0.20 at one plane and 0.05 at a plane 5 mm away. Find the steady flux of A when (a) A and B diffuse in opposite directions in equimolar amounts, (b) B is stagnant. Check part (b) by the log form.

  1. Convert L = 5 mm = 0.005 m; y_A1 = 0.20, y_A2 = 0.05; c = 40.6 mol/m³; D = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s.

  3. (b) Stagnant B: y_B1 = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80; y_B2 = 1 − 0.05 = 0.95.
  4. (y_B)lm = (0.95 − 0.80)/ln(0.95/0.80) = 0.15/0.1719 = 0.873.
  5. N_A = 0.0219/0.873 = 0.0251 mol m⁻² s⁻¹.

  • Using N_A = D c Δy_A/L when B is stagnant. Evaporation into air and absorption from an insoluble carrier usually need the log-mean form.
  • Using gas c (about 40 mol/m³ at 1 atm) for a liquid. Water is about 55,500 mol/m³; the liquid flux would be wrong by a factor of about a thousand.
  • Mixing y_A and y_B in the log mean, or taking ln(y_B1/y_B2) instead of ln(y_B2/y_B1). The flux sign follows the direction from plane 1 to plane 2.
  • Forgetting to convert L from mm to m, or using arithmetic mean (y_B1 + y_B2)/2 instead of the log mean. The arithmetic mean is close only when the two y_B values are almost equal.

1.5 Diffusion through variable area; pseudo-steady state diffusion

Most diffusion problems assume a tube of fixed cross-section. In real equipment the path often widens or narrows: vapour leaves a droplet, gas enters a spherical pellet, liquid falls down a tube. The total molar flow W stays the same through every slice, but the flux changes because the area A(z) changes. Fick's law is therefore written with A(z) inside and integrated over the geometry. When a boundary such as a subliming surface moves slowly, each instant can be treated as steady; this pseudo-steady result sizes evaporating droplets and the Stefan tube.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for the stagnant-film flux, and the integration of 1/A.

Total rate is constant, flux is not

Pick a coordinate z along the diffusion path. Let W be the total molar flow of A in mol/s. In steady state W is the same through every cross-section: nothing accumulates. The area A(z) in m² changes with z, so the flux N_A = W/A(z) in mol/(m²·s) changes too. Fick's law applies at each slice, not to the whole shape at once.

W = A(z) N_A = −D A(z) dc_A/dz

W is total molar flow of A (mol/s); A(z) is cross-sectional area (m²); N_A is molar flux of A (mol/(m²·s)); D is diffusivity (m²/s); c_A is concentration of A (mol/m³); z is distance along the path (m). This form is for equimolar diffusion or a dilute A.

Rearrange and integrate between two stations 1 and 2: W∫dz/A = −D∫dc_A. The left side depends only on geometry; the right side only on concentrations. For a constant-area tube A cancels, and you recover Chapter 1.4. For a varying area, carry A(z) inside the integral.

For diffusion of A through stagnant B, replace the concentration gradient term by c dy_A/(1−y_A), where c is total concentration (mol/m³) and y_A is mole fraction of A. The same integration then produces a logarithm, as the sphere below shows.

Sphere in full

For a sphere of radius R losing A into a stagnant gas, A(r)=4πr². At distance r from the centre, W = −D(4πr²)c/(1−y_A) dy_A/dr. Separate variables and integrate from the surface (r=R, y_A=y_As) to the bulk (r→∞, y_A=0).

W = 4πR D c ln(1/(1 − y_As))

W is molar flow of A leaving the sphere (mol/s); R is sphere radius (m); D is diffusivity of A in the gas (m²/s); c is total concentration (mol/m³); y_As is mole fraction of A at the surface.

If both sides are equimolar and the two spheres have radii R₁ and R₂, the same integration gives W = 4πD(c_A1 − c_A2)/(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). Here c_A1 and c_A2 are the concentrations of A at the two surfaces in mol/m³. The denominator is positive when R₁ < R₂.

A cylinder uses A=2πrL, so ∫dr/r gives ln(r₂/r₁). A cone uses A=πr(z)² with r(z) linear in z, giving a rational integral. The recipe never changes: write A(z), separate, integrate.

Pseudo-steady state: a boundary that moves

A subliming ball or an evaporating droplet has a surface that moves. The gas film around it holds very little material compared with the amount that crosses during the process. The concentration profile therefore adjusts in seconds, while the radius changes over hours. At any instant the steady formula applies with the current radius. This is the pseudo-steady-state assumption.

For a ball, the moles left in the solid are N = (4/3)πR³ρ/M, where ρ is solid density (kg/m³) and M is molar mass (kg/mol). Differentiating, dN/dt = 4πR²(ρ/M)dR/dt. This loss equals −W. Using the stagnant-sphere W gives R dR/dt = −D c L M/ρ, with L = ln(1/(1−y_As)). Integrate from R₁ at t=0 to R at t: R₁² − R² = 2(D c L M/ρ)t. The time depends on R₁² − R², not on R₁ − R.

The falling liquid level in a tube is the same balance: the moles leaving the liquid surface equal the steady diffusion rate through the gas column above it. This is the working principle of the Stefan tube. The assumption fails at very short times, during very fast pressure or temperature changes, and when the gas dissolves appreciably in the liquid, because then the gas holdup is not small.

Worked example

Question. A naphthalene ball of radius 10 mm sublimes in still air at 300 K and 1 atm. The total concentration is c = 40.6 mol/m³, D = 6.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s, and the mole fraction of naphthalene at the surface is y_As = 0.0100. The bulk air has y_A = 0. The molar mass is M = 128 g/mol and the solid density is ρ = 1140 kg/m³. Find the time for the radius to fall to 5 mm, assuming pseudo-steady diffusion.

  1. Convert the data: R₁ = 10 mm = 0.010 m, R₂ = 5 mm = 0.005 m, M = 128 g/mol = 0.128 kg/mol.
  2. Compute the logarithmic driving factor: L = ln(1/(1 − 0.0100)) = ln(1/0.99) = 0.01005.
  3. Write the stagnant-sphere rate with R in metres: W = 4πR D c L = 4πR(6.0×10⁻⁶)(40.6)(0.01005) = 3.08×10⁻⁵ R mol/s.
  4. The solid contains N = (4/3)πR³ρ/M mol. Differentiate: dN/dt = 4πR²(ρ/M)dR/dt.
  5. Mass balance: dN/dt = −W. So 4πR²(ρ/M)dR/dt = −4πR D c L.
  6. Divide by 4πR and rearrange: R dR/dt = −D c L M/ρ = −k, where k has units m²/s.
  7. Calculate k = (6.0×10⁻⁶)(40.6)(0.01005)(0.128)/1140 = 2.75×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s.
  8. Integrate R dR = −k dt from R₁ to R₂: (R₁² − R₂²)/2 = kt.
  9. Put in the numbers: t = (0.010² − 0.005²)/(2×2.75×10⁻¹⁰) = (1.0×10⁻⁴ − 2.5×10⁻⁵)/(5.50×10⁻¹⁰) = 1.36×10⁵ s.
  10. Convert to hours: 1.36×10⁵/3600 = 37.9 h, about 38 h.

Answer. The naphthalene ball takes about 1.36×10⁵ s, that is 38 hours, for its radius to fall from 10 mm to 5 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using a fixed area and forgetting A(z): W is constant through the shape, but flux N_A = W/A(z) is not.
  • For stagnant B, using the plain dc_A/dz form and missing the 1/(1−y_A) factor; this loses the logarithm ln(1/(1−y_As)).
  • Integrating the sphere as if R were the variable instead of R²; the time contains R₁² − R₂², not R₁ − R₂.
  • A sign slip: W is positive outward, but dR/dt is negative for a shrinking ball. Keep the loss as −W.
  • Applying pseudo-steady state at very short times or when the gas dissolves appreciably, where the gas holdup is not small.

1.6 Diffusivity: measurement and prediction

Every diffusion calculation needs one number: the diffusivity D_AB, the constant in Fick's law that says how quickly A spreads through B. You cannot read it from a table for every pair, so engineers either measure it in a small cell or predict it from simpler properties. Measurement gives the truth for one pair; prediction gives a quick estimate before a pilot plant exists. The same number sizes absorbers, dryers and membrane units. This chapter shows the standard methods for gases and liquids, the units each one uses, and the order of magnitude to expect.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for Fick's laws and Chapter 1.4 for the flux equations used to interpret a measurement; the ideal gas law from Chapter 1.1.

What D is and how big it is

The diffusivity D_AB is the proportionality constant in Fick's first law for A diffusing through B. Its SI unit is m²/s; older tables use cm²/s, and 1 cm²/s = 10⁻⁴ m²/s. For a binary gas pair the number is symmetric: D_AB = D_BA. The value depends on temperature and pressure. Gases diffuse fast, liquids slow, solids far slower; Chapter 1.7 covers solids.

PhaseTypical D at room temperatureCommon route
Gasabout 1×10⁻⁵ m²/stwo-bulb, Fuller
Liquidabout 1×10⁻⁹ m²/sdiaphragm cell, Wilke–Chang

The orders matter in design. A gas-phase value near 10⁻⁵ m²/s means a species crosses a millimetre in about a tenth of a second; a liquid-phase value near 10⁻⁹ m²/s takes about a thousand seconds for the same distance. That is why absorbers and extraction columns use stirring, packing or small droplets: the molecular value alone would need an impractically tall column. See Unit 2 for how flow adds eddy diffusion.

Measuring D in a cell

All measurements put the pair in a known geometry, hold temperature constant, and follow concentration with time. The integrated form of Fick's law turns the slope of a log plot into D.

Two-bulb (Winkelmann) method for gases. Two bulbs of volume V each are joined by a tube of length L and cross-section S. One bulb starts rich in A, the other poor. With equal volumes and equimolar counter-diffusion, the mole-fraction difference Δy falls exponentially:

ln(Δy₀/Δy) = (2 D_AB S / (L V)) t

Δy₀ is the initial mole-fraction difference; Δy is the difference at time t; S is the tube cross-section in m²; L is the tube length in m; V is each bulb volume in m³; t is time in s.

A plot of ln(Δy₀/Δy) against t is a straight line through the origin, and D_AB comes from its slope. The Stefan tube measures a gas pair by the fall of a liquid level; Chapter 1.5 gives the pseudo-steady treatment, and the slope of level against time gives D.

For liquids the diaphragm cell is standard. Two stirred compartments are separated by a porous glass diaphragm. The cell constant β is found once by calibrating with a pair of known D, such as dilute KCl in water. Then ln(Δc₀/Δc) = β D_AB t. A closed cell with a sensor or optical window follows concentration against time in a still sample; fit the same exponential decay form, ln(Δc₀/Δc) = k t (k is the fitted slope), and extract D once the cell's geometric constant is known. Taylor dispersion is a modern flow method: a pulse is injected into laminar flow in a capillary, and the spread of the pulse gives D. Only a few universities teach it in the first course.

Predicting D in gases

Kinetic theory gives the Chapman–Enskog equation. It needs Lennard–Jones parameters σ (collision diameter) and ε/k_B (energy well depth) for each species, plus a collision integral Ω_D that depends on T. The result is right to a few per cent for non-polar gases. The formula is long, so most hand calculations use the Fuller–Schettler–Giddings correlation.

D_AB = 1.00×10⁻³ T1.75 (1/M_A + 1/M_B)0.5 / [P (Σv_A1/3 + Σv_B1/3)²]

D_AB is in cm²/s; T is in K; P is in atm; M_A and M_B are molar masses in g/mol; Σv_A and Σv_B are diffusion volume increments (tabulated numbers, not molar volumes); Σv_A1/3 means the cube root of the tabulated increment Σv_A; the cube roots of A and B are added, and that sum is squared in the denominator. The 0.5 exponent applies only to (1/M_A + 1/M_B).

Read the dependence, not just the formula. D rises as T1.75 and falls as 1/P. Doubling pressure halves D at the same temperature. The correlation gives cm²/s only when P is in atm; if you feed kPa you get a value too small by the conversion factor. Convert to m²/s at the end by multiplying by 10⁻⁴.

Predicting D in liquids

Liquid diffusivities are harder because molecules touch. The Stokes–Einstein relation treats the solute as a sphere of radius r moving through a continuum of viscosity μ:

D_AB = k_B T / (6π μ r)

k_B = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; T is absolute temperature in K; μ is viscosity in Pa·s; r is the solute radius in m; D_AB is in m²/s.

The relation shows the key scaling: D ∝ T/μ. Warm a liquid and D rises; add a thickener and D falls. It is accurate for large spherical solutes in a solvent, less so for small molecules of similar size to the solvent.

Wilke–Chang is the common engineering correlation. It uses the solvent viscosity μ_B, an association factor φ (about 2.6 for water, 1.9 for methanol, 1.0 for non-polar solvents) and the solute molar volume at its normal boiling point V_A. Hayduk–Laudie is an alternative that uses μ_B and V_A without φ. Both return cm²/s when viscosity is in cP and volume in cm³/mol. For a first estimate, check the correlation output lies near 10⁻⁵ cm²/s, that is 10⁻⁹ m²/s. A value near 10⁻¹ cm²/s (10⁻⁵ m²/s) means a gas correlation slipped in.

Worked example

  1. Write the correlation with P in atm: D_AB = 1.00×10⁻³ T1.75 (1/M_A + 1/M_B)0.5 / [P (Σv_A1/3 + Σv_B1/3)²], with D in cm²/s.
  2. Compute the mass term: 1/44 + 1/29 = 0.02273 + 0.03448 = 0.05721. Its square root is 0.2392.
  3. Compute T1.75 = 3001.75 = 2.164×10⁴ K1.75.
  4. Compute the cube roots: 26.9^1/3 = 3.00 and 20.1^1/3 = 2.72. Their sum is 5.72, and the square is 32.7.
  5. Insert with P = 1 atm: D_AB = 1.00×10⁻³ × 2.164×10⁴ × 0.2392 / (1 × 32.7) = 0.1584 cm²/s.

1.7 Diffusion in solids

Diffusion in a solid is the slow hopping of atoms between fixed sites, or the creep of gas molecules through tiny pores. The diffusivity D is far smaller than in a gas, often 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻²⁰ m²/s, and it rises steeply with temperature. Industry uses it to harden steel, dope silicon, dry wood and food, protect packaged food, and judge hydrogen loss from pipelines. Fick's laws still apply, but the concentration is inside the solid, and the surface value is fixed by solubility, not by the gas composition alone.

You need Fick's laws and the steady slab flux.

Mechanisms: how an atom gets past its neighbours

In a metal or ceramic most atoms sit on lattice sites. Diffusion means an atom leaves one site and joins another. The path decides the speed.

  • Interstitial diffusion: small atoms such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen squeeze between host atoms. This is the fast path in steel treatment.
  • Vacancy or substitutional diffusion: an atom swaps places with an empty lattice site. This is slower and controls self-diffusion and alloy mixing.
  • Grain-boundary and surface diffusion: atoms along boundaries and free surfaces move faster than through the grain. These are fast paths in polycrystals, especially at low temperature.
  • Pore or Knudsen diffusion: a gas in a porous solid uses ordinary diffusion in large pores; in pores smaller than the mean free path, molecules hit walls more than each other. Porosity and tortuosity lower the effective D.

Fick's first law still holds: J_A = −D dc_A/dx. Here J_A is the flux in mol/(m²·s), c_A is concentration in mol/m³ of solid, x is position in m, and D is in m²/s. At the surface, equilibrium often gives c_A = S p_A, a Henry-type relation. Here p_A is gas partial pressure in Pa and S is solubility in mol/(m³·Pa). So the gas composition alone does not set the surface concentration.

Temperature: the Arrhenius law

D = D₀ exp(−E/RT)

D is diffusivity (m²/s); D₀ is the pre-exponential factor (m²/s); E is activation energy (J/mol); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature (K).

E is the energy barrier for one hop. Only the fraction exp(−E/RT) of atoms has enough thermal energy to cross it, so D climbs steeply as T rises. Near 700 K, a 100 K rise can multiply D by about 4 for the worked values below. Grain boundaries have a lower E than the lattice, so they dominate at low temperature. The figure plots D against T for D₀ = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and E = 60 kJ/mol; the marked points are the worked values.

Steady diffusion through a sheet, and where it is used

N_A = D (c_A1 − c_A2)/L

N_A is steady flux (mol/(m²·s)); c_A1 and c_A2 are concentrations at the two faces (mol/m³); L is sheet thickness (m).

This is Fick's first law integrated across a flat sheet with constant flux. If one face touches a gas, use c_A = S p_A. Then N_A = D S (p_A1 − p_A2)/L, and the product D S is the permeability.

  • Carburizing and nitriding steel: carbon or nitrogen enters the hot surface and diffuses inward. Case depth grows with time; see unsteady-state diffusion.
  • Doping silicon wafers: phosphorus or boron is driven into silicon at high temperature to set electrical properties.
  • Drying wood, clay and food: moisture diffuses to the surface before evaporation. Drying curves are in Unit 7.
  • Polymer films in packaging: oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour permeate slowly through the film; low D keeps food fresh.
  • Hydrogen through pipeline steel: hydrogen is small and interstitial, so it can enter steel and cause embrittlement.
  • Membrane separation: differences in D and solubility separate gases or solutes; see Unit 6.

Only a few syllabi add the Darken relation and the Kirkendall shift: two species with different D can interdiffuse, and inert markers move.

Worked example

Question. For a gas in a metal take D₀ = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and E = 60 kJ/mol; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). (a) Find D at 400 °C and at 500 °C. (b) A sheet 3 mm thick holds a steady concentration difference of 0.45 mol/m³ across it. Find the flux at 500 °C.

  • Using a gas-phase diffusivity of order 10⁻⁵ m²/s for a solid. Solid D is often 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻²⁰ m²/s.

1.8 Multicomponent diffusion

In a mixture of three or more species, every pair has its own diffusivity, and the flux of one species depends on the gradients of all the others. One effective D is no longer enough. The Stefan–Maxwell equations keep one friction term per pair; Wilke's formula collapses them into a single effective diffusivity for a species present in small amount. This decides the size of columns for multi-component distillation, the modelling of combustion, and the design of membrane and adsorbent pores where reformer gas is separated.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for Fick's first law, and Chapter 1.4 for the equimolar and stagnant-film flux forms.

When two species are not enough

Two species are easy. One number, D_AB, describes everything. Real streams carry more. A reformer gas holds H₂, CO, CO₂, CH₄ and steam together. A multi-component distillation feed may have six hydrocarbons. Flue gas from a coal boiler carries N₂, O₂, CO₂ and H₂O.

An n-component mixture has n species with mole fractions y₁, y₂, …, y_n that add to 1. Every pair (i, j) has its own binary diffusivity D_ij, measured or predicted as in Chapter 1.6. The number of pairs is n(n−1)/2: three for n = 3, ten for n = 5. D_ij = D_ji, so the table is symmetric with blanks on the diagonal.

Treat the mixture with a single effective diffusivity only when one species is dilute, or when the rest move as one block. When three or more species are present in comparable amounts, the motion of A is braked by B and separately by C, and those two brakes act through different pairs. The flux of A then depends on the gradients of B and C as well as its own. That is the multicomponent regime. It appears in multi-component distillation (see Unit 4, Distillation), in combustion where radicals diffuse against burnt gas, and in narrow membrane pores.

The Stefan–Maxwell equations

Stefan and Maxwell wrote the balance as driving force against friction. For one-dimensional diffusion along z:

dy_i/dz = Σ_{j≠i} (y_i N_j − y_j N_i) / (c D_ij)

y_i and y_j are mole fractions; N_i and N_j are molar fluxes relative to a fixed frame, mol/(m²·s); c is the total molar concentration, mol/m³; D_ij is the binary diffusivity of the pair i–j, m²/s; z is distance, m. The sum runs over every j other than i.

Read the right side as friction. Species i rubs against species j, and the rubbing grows with how fast they slip past each other. The slip shows up as (y_i N_j − y_j N_i), which is zero when i and j travel together. There are n such equations, but only n − 1 are independent, because the mole fractions add to 1.

Check the two-species case. For n = 2 the sum has one term: c D₁₂ dy₁/dz = y₁N₂ − (1 − y₁)N₁ = y₁(N₁ + N₂) − N₁. Rearranged:

N₁ = −c D₁₂ dy₁/dz + y₁ (N₁ + N₂)

the first term is the diffusion part and the second is the bulk-flow part carried by the mixture, in mol/(m²·s).

When the two fluxes are equal and opposite, N₁ + N₂ = 0 and plain Fick's law remains. When species 1 moves through stagnant 2, N₂ = 0, so N₁ = −c D₁₂ dy₁/dz / (1 − y₁), the stagnant-film form of Chapter 1.4. The Stefan–Maxwell set therefore contains both textbook cases inside it.

Wilke's approximation for a dilute species

Solving n coupled equations by hand is slow. Wilke's shortcut works when species i is present in small amount and the rest of the mixture is roughly still. The friction sum then collapses into one effective diffusivity:

D_i,m = (1 − y_i) / Σ_{j≠i} (y_j / D_ij)

D_i,m is the effective diffusivity of species i in the mixture, m²/s; y_i is the mole fraction of i; y_j is the mole fraction of any other species j; D_ij is the binary pair diffusivity, m²/s.

The value is then used inside Fick's law with the bulk term kept:

N_i = −c D_i,m dy_i/dz + y_i ΣN

ΣN is the total molar flux of all species, mol/(m²·s). Drop the second term only when ΣN = 0, that is, equimolar counter-diffusion.

  • Notice the structure: the denominator adds y_j/D_ij, so the resistances 1/D add, weighted by mole fraction. A pair with a small D_ij (heavy friction) adds a large resistance.
  • D_i,m is a local value. It depends on the composition at position z, and composition changes along the path. The flux equation must be integrated from z₁ to z₂, not multiplied by Δz. A shortcut using the inlet composition can be off by tens of percent over a long column.
  • In fine pores, wall collisions add a Knudsen diffusivity D_K, and the two resistances combine as 1/D_eff = 1/D_i,m + 1/D_K, the Bosanquet relation, which only a few universities teach.

Worked example

Question. A gas mixture at one atmosphere and 300 K has y_A = 0.10, y_B = 0.30 and y_C = 0.60. The binary diffusivities are D_AB = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s, D_AC = 3.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and D_BC = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Find the effective diffusivity of A in the mixture by Wilke's formula, and state how the answer would be used in Fick's law.

  1. Check the data. y_A + y_B + y_C = 0.10 + 0.30 + 0.60 = 1.00, so the composition is complete.
  2. Write Wilke's formula for the dilute species A: D_A,m = (1 − y_A) / (y_B/D_AB + y_C/D_AC). Only pairs that contain A appear, because the sum runs over j ≠ A. The B–C pair does not enter.
  3. Compute the first resistance term: y_B/D_AB = 0.30 / (1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s) = 3.0×10⁴ s/m².
  4. Compute the second resistance term: y_C/D_AC = 0.60 / (3.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s) = 2.0×10⁴ s/m².
  5. Add the resistances: 3.0×10⁴ + 2.0×10⁴ = 5.0×10⁴ s/m².
  6. Compute the numerator: 1 − y_A = 1 − 0.10 = 0.90, dimensionless.
  7. Divide: D_A,m = 0.90 / (5.0×10⁴ s/m²) = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  8. Use it as N_A = −c D_A,m dy_A/dz + y_A ΣN. Compare with D_AB = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s: A moves through this mixture faster than through pure B alone, because C is the faster partner. If the composition changes along the path, D_A,m changes too, and the flux must be integrated rather than multiplied by the path length.

Answer. The effective diffusivity of A in the mixture is D_A,m = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s.

Exam traps

  • Averaging diffusivities as y_B D_AB + y_C D_AC. Wilke's formula averages resistances, y_j/D_ij, not D itself. The two answers differ in size and in trend.
  • Using N_i = −c D_i,m dy_i/dz and forgetting the bulk term y_i ΣN. That is valid only for equimolar counter-diffusion; in a stagnant mixture it under-predicts the flux, sometimes by a factor of two or more.
  • Carrying one D_i,m from the bottom of a column to the top. It is a local value tied to the local composition, so the flux equation has to be integrated.
  • Putting all n² diffusivities in a table. Only n(n−1)/2 are distinct, and D_ij = D_ji. Examiners like this count.
  • Mixing frames: the Stefan–Maxwell equations use N_i, the flux relative to a fixed frame, not J_i, the diffusion flux relative to the molar average velocity. The two differ by y_i ΣN.

1.9 Unsteady-state diffusion

Concentration inside a solid or a stagnant liquid often changes with time: a wet brick dries, a polymer sheet loses solvent, a membrane lets gas through. Fick's second law describes this change, but it is a partial differential equation, so the answer depends on the shape and on what happens at the surface. The results come as error functions, series, charts or time lags, not one formula. Engineers use them to size dryers, membranes and diffusion cells.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for the second law and Chapter 1.7 for diffusion in solids.

The second law and the three shapes

Fick's second law gives concentration as a function of position and time. For one direction and constant D it is:

∂c/∂t = D ∂²c/∂x²

c is concentration of the diffusing species in mol/m³; t is time in s; x is position in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s.

To solve it, fix the starting state and the surface state. The starting state is usually a uniform concentration c₀. The surface state is either a fixed surface concentration c_s, or a convective condition with an external mass transfer coefficient k_c. Three standard shapes cover most problems: a slab of half-thickness L, a cylinder of radius R, and a sphere of radius R. For a slab dried on both faces, L is half the total thickness.

Fo = D t / L², Bi_m = k_c L / D

Fo is the mass Fourier number (dimensionless); D is diffusivity in m²/s; t is time in s; L is half-thickness of a slab or radius of a cylinder or sphere in m; Bi_m is the mass Biot number; k_c is the external mass transfer coefficient in m/s.

Fo measures how far diffusion has progressed. Bi_m measures surface resistance against internal resistance. Large Bi_m means the surface resistance is small, so the surface concentration is almost fixed. Small Bi_m means the surface resistance controls the rate.

Short times, long times and charts

At very short contact times, or in a thick body, the far side feels nothing. The body behaves as semi-infinite. Hold the surface at c_s and start from c₀. The concentration at depth z is:

(c − c₀)/(c_s − c₀) = erfc(z/(2√(Dt)))

c is concentration at distance z and time t; c₀ is initial uniform concentration; c_s is surface concentration held constant; z is distance from surface in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s; erfc is the complementary error function.

This is the penetration idea: only a layer of order √(Dt) changes. For long times in a slab with zero surface concentration, the centre concentration follows the first term of the series:

C/C₀ = (4/π) exp(−π² Fo/4)

C is centre concentration at time t; C₀ is initial uniform concentration; Fo = D t/L² is the mass Fourier number; L is half-thickness in m. Valid for Fo > 0.2 when the surface concentration is zero.

Use this first-term result only when Fo > 0.2. Below that, higher terms matter and the formula is badly wrong. For the middle range, use charts of C/C₀ against Fo, drawn for each Bi_m. At Fo = 0.379 the first-term slab result gives C/C₀ = 0.5.

Time lag and numerical work

A membrane initially free of penetrant shows a delay before steady permeation starts. The time lag is:

t_lag = L²/(6D)

t_lag is the time lag in s; L is membrane thickness in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s.

Plotting permeation against time and reading the intercept gives D. When D varies with concentration, temperature or moisture, no closed formula applies. Solve Fick's second law numerically, for example by finite differences. Unit 7 drying uses exactly these unsteady curves to estimate drying time.

Worked example

Question. A polymer sheet 20 mm thick is dry on both faces, so the surface concentration is zero. It starts with a uniform moisture concentration of 0.50 mol/m³. D = 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s. Find the time for the centre to fall to 0.25 mol/m³.

  1. Both faces are dry, so use the half-thickness: L = 20/2 = 10 mm = 0.010 m.
  2. The target ratio is C/C₀ = 0.25/0.50 = 0.50, dimensionless.
  3. Assume Fo > 0.2 and use the first-term slab formula: C/C₀ = (4/π) exp(−π² Fo/4).
  4. Put C/C₀ = 0.50: 0.50 = 1.2732 exp(−2.4674 Fo).
  5. Then exp(−2.4674 Fo) = 0.50/1.2732 = 0.3927.
  6. Take natural logs: −2.4674 Fo = ln(0.3927) = −0.9340.
  7. So Fo = 0.9340/2.4674 = 0.379, dimensionless. This is greater than 0.2, so the first-term formula is valid.
  8. Find time: t = Fo L²/D = 0.379 × (0.010)² / (2.0×10⁻¹⁰).
  9. (0.010)² = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m². So t = 0.379 × 1.0×10⁻⁴ / 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ = 1.895×10⁵ s.
  10. In hours: t = 1.895×10⁵ / 3600 = 52.6 h, about 53 h.

Answer. The centre reaches 0.25 mol/m³ after about 1.9×10⁵ s, which is about 53 hours.

  • Using the first-term slab formula below Fo = 0.2. There the higher series terms are not negligible and the answer is badly wrong.
  • Using diameter instead of radius for a cylinder or sphere, and using slab constants for a sphere or cylinder. The first-term constants differ with shape.
  • Writing the semi-infinite argument as z/(2Dt) instead of z/(2√(Dt)). The square root is part of the error-function argument.
  • Using half-thickness in the membrane time lag t_lag = L²/(6D). Here L is the full membrane thickness.

Check yourself

Remember State Fick's second law for one-dimensional diffusion with constant D. Define every symbol. 2 marks

∂c/∂t = D ∂²c/∂x². c is concentration in mol/m³, t is time in s, x is position in m, D is diffusivity in m²/s.

Understand Why does the mass Biot number Bi_m decide whether the surface concentration can be treated as fixed? 3 marks

Bi_m = k_c L/D compares external surface resistance with internal diffusion resistance. Large Bi_m means k_c is large, surface resistance is small, and the surface concentration is almost fixed. Small Bi_m means surface resistance controls.

Apply A slab has half-thickness L = 5 mm, D = 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s, and Fo = 0.379. Find the time. 4 marks

t = Fo L²/D = 0.379 × (0.005)² / 1.0×10⁻¹⁰. (0.005)² = 2.5×10⁻⁵ m². t = 0.379 × 2.5×10⁻⁵ / 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ = 9.475×10⁴ s, about 26.3 h.

Apply A semi-infinite body has c₀ = 0.02 mol/m³, c_s = 0.10 mol/m³, D = 1.0×10⁻⁹ m²/s. Find c at z = 1 mm and t = 1000 s. 4 marks

z/(2√(Dt)) = 0.001/(2√(1.0×10⁻⁶)) = 0.001/(2×0.001) = 0.5. erfc(0.5) ≈ 0.4795. c = c₀ + (c_s − c₀)×0.4795 = 0.02 + 0.08×0.4795 = 0.0584 mol/m³.

Analyze Why is the first-term slab formula invalid below Fo = 0.2? 4 marks

Below Fo = 0.2 the concentration profile is still close to the initial step. The series needs many terms to represent that sharp profile. The first term alone overpredicts the centre concentration badly.

Analyze A slab of total thickness 2L is dried from one face. Another identical slab is dried from both faces. Compare the times to reach the same centre concentration. 4 marks

For one face the diffusion length is 2L. For both faces it is L. Since t ∝ L², the one-face slab needs four times as long, if everything else is unchanged.

Remember What is the membrane time lag, and what does its formula contain? 2 marks

The time lag is t_lag = L²/(6D), where L is the full membrane thickness in m and D is diffusivity in m²/s. It is the delay before steady permeation is established.

Takeaways

  1. Every separation operation is a named way of arranging a concentration gradient and a contacting pattern so one component moves faster.
  2. Fick's first law links diffusion flux to the negative concentration gradient; Fick's second law adds conservation of mass to give concentration versus time.
  3. Equimolar counter-diffusion uses the plain mole-fraction difference, while stagnant-B diffusion is larger by the factor 1/(y_B)lm.
  4. When the diffusion area changes, the total rate stays constant but the flux changes, so Fick's law must be integrated over the geometry.
  5. Pseudo-steady treatment works when a boundary moves slowly enough that each instant can be solved as a steady problem.
  6. D is measured in a known geometry or predicted from kinetic theory and group correlations; solid D values are small and rise steeply with temperature.
  7. With three or more species, each flux depends on all gradients, and Stefan–Maxwell or Wilke relations are needed.
  8. Unsteady diffusion answers come as charts, series or error functions, organized by dimensionless groups such as Fo_m.

Glossary

Activation energy
The energy barrier that makes solid diffusion rise steeply with temperature.
Binary mixture
A mixture containing only two species.
Boundary condition
A stated concentration or flux at the surface of the diffusion path.
Bulk flow
Motion of the mixture as a whole, carrying all species with it.
Carburizing
Adding carbon to the surface of steel by diffusion at high temperature.
Concentration gradient
Change in concentration per unit distance; it drives diffusion.
Convective mass transfer
Mass transfer between a flowing fluid and a surface or another phase.
Diffusion coefficient
The proportionality constant between diffusion flux and concentration gradient.
Diffusive flux
Moles of a species crossing unit area per unit time relative to the mixture velocity.
Doping
Adding a controlled impurity to silicon by diffusion to change its electrical behaviour.
Effective diffusivity
An apparent D for a porous solid that includes porosity and tortuosity effects.
Grain-boundary diffusion
Faster diffusion along boundaries between grains in a solid.
Initial condition
The concentration field at time zero.
Interstitial diffusion
Small atoms hop between lattice sites without displacing host atoms.
Knudsen diffusion
Pore diffusion in which molecule-wall collisions dominate over molecule-molecule collisions.
Mass fraction
Mass of a species divided by total mass of the mixture.
Mole fraction
Moles of a species divided by total moles in the mixture.
Pore diffusion
Diffusion through gas-filled or liquid-filled pores in a solid.
Semi-infinite medium
A medium thick enough that the far boundary has no effect during the observed time.
Steady state
Condition in which concentration at a fixed point does not change with time.
Surface diffusion
Movement of adsorbed molecules along the wall of a pore.
Tortuosity
A factor greater than one that accounts for the longer crooked path through pores.
Total flux
Moles of a species crossing a fixed plane per unit area per unit time.
Unsteady state
Condition in which concentration at a fixed point changes with time.
Vacancy diffusion
An atom moves into an empty lattice site, so the vacancy moves the other way.
