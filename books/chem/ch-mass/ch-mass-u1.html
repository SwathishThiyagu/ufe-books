1.1 Mass transfer operations and their classification

Mass transfer is the movement of one component through a mixture because that component is more concentrated in one place than in another. Refining crude oil, drying a shirt on a balcony, scrubbing CO2 out of flue gas, and your blood picking up oxygen all work the same way: a concentration difference pushes a species from a rich region to a lean one. Almost every separation that works by moving a component between phases is a named way of arranging that push. Mechanical separations such as filtration and centrifugation are a different family and are not part of this subject. This chapter defines the subject, sorts the operations into families, and fixes the vocabulary of concentration that every later formula uses.

You need mole and mass balances, the ideal gas law, and the idea of a phase from the thermodynamics course; no earlier chapter of this unit is assumed.

Mass transfer: one component moving because it is unevenly spread

Mass transfer is the movement of a component inside a mixture, or from one phase to another, driven by a difference in concentration. Concentration here means how much of that component sits in a given volume or in a given number of moles. The component moves from where it is rich to where it is lean. No pump, no stirrer and no heat are needed for the movement itself.

Compare the three transport subjects. Heat transfer moves energy because of a temperature difference. Fluid flow moves the whole mixture together because of a pressure difference. Mass transfer moves one species relative to the others because of a concentration difference. A river carrying salt to the sea moves water and salt together — that is fluid flow. Salt slowly spreading from a salty patch into a fresh patch of the same river is mass transfer.

The subject matters because most separations in a chemical plant, apart from mechanical ones such as filtration, are built on this one movement. You contact two phases, arrange that one phase prefers the wanted component, and diffusion carries it across. Two examples: CO2 washed out of flue gas by an amine solution, and sugar leached from chopped cane by hot water.

Four families, sorted by how the second phase is made

A single phase cannot be separated by mass transfer alone. Separation needs a second phase — immiscible, or only partly miscible, with the first — that holds the wanted component more strongly or more weakly. The four families below differ only in how that second phase is produced. Knowing the family tells you which unit of this subject you are reading.

  • Phase creation — heat or work makes a new phase out of the feed. Distillation, where heat raises a vapour; evaporation, where solvent is boiled off; crystallization, where cooling or concentrating makes solid appear. See the distillation and crystallization units.
  • Phase addition — a foreign liquid or gas is added and it dissolves the wanted component. Absorption (gas into liquid), extraction (solute from one liquid into another), leaching (solute from a solid into a liquid). See gas absorption, and liquid extraction and leaching.
  • Barrier — a membrane lets one species through faster than another. Reverse osmosis passes water and holds salt back; dialysis passes small molecules and holds large ones. See membrane separations.
  • Solid agent — a solid surface or resin takes up the solute. Adsorption on activated carbon, ion exchange on a resin. See adsorption and ion exchange.

Most books place drying with hot gas under phase creation, since heat makes the vapour; a few treat the hot gas as an added phase and put it under phase addition. Drying is never a solid-agent operation in the adsorption sense, because the solid gives up water rather than taking up solute. For any operation named in an exam, state which component moves and which phase is new.

Phases in contact, contactors, and the four numbers called concentration

The same operations are also sorted by the phases that touch. Gas-liquid: absorption, distillation. Liquid-liquid: extraction. Gas-solid: adsorption, drying. Liquid-solid: leaching, ion exchange, crystallization. Each pair brings its own equipment, its own equilibrium relation and its own mass transfer coefficient, which is why the later units are organised this way.

A third sorting is by contactor. A stage-wise contactor mixes the two phases, lets them approach equilibrium, then separates them; a tray column is a stack of such stages. A differential contactor keeps the phases in touch continuously along a path, so composition changes smoothly from end to end; a packed tower, a falling film and a spray are examples. Batch and continuous cut across both. Contactors are sized later with equilibrium stages and mass transfer coefficients.

Five numbers describe composition, and people loosely call all of them concentration. Only the last two are concentrations; learn all five now, because every flux formula later needs the right one.

Worked example

Question. A flue gas at 1 atm and 300 K contains 22 mol% CO2 and 78 mol% air. Take M(air) = 29 g/mol, M(CO2) = 44 g/mol, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and 1 atm = 101325 Pa. Find (a) the mean molar mass, (b) the mass fraction of CO2, (c) the total molar concentration, (d) the mass concentration of CO2 in kg/m³.

  1. Mean molar mass: M̄ = y(CO2)·M(CO2) + y(air)·M(air) = 0.22 × 44 + 0.78 × 29 = 9.68 + 22.62 = 32.3 g/mol.
  2. Mass fraction: w(CO2) = y(CO2)·M(CO2) / M̄ = 9.68 / 32.3 = 0.300, that is 30.0 % by mass. The CO2 is 22 % of the moles but 30 % of the mass, because a CO2 molecule is heavier than the average air molecule.
  3. Total molar concentration from the ideal gas law: c = P / (R T) = 101325 / (8.314 × 300) = 101325 / 2494.2 = 40.6 mol/m³.
  4. CO2 molar concentration: c(CO2) = y(CO2)·c = 0.22 × 40.6 = 8.93 mol/m³.
  5. CO2 mass concentration: ρ(CO2) = c(CO2)·M(CO2) = 8.93 × 0.044 kg/mol = 0.393 kg/m³.

Answer. The gas has a mean molar mass of 32.3 g/mol, a CO2 mass fraction of 0.300, a total concentration of 40.6 mol/m³ and a CO2 mass concentration of 0.393 kg/m³.

Exam traps

  • Writing 'concentration' without saying which one. Mole fraction and mass fraction are dimensionless and are not concentrations; a flux needs mol/m³ or kg/m³. Converting a molar concentration to a mass concentration multiplies by the molar mass in kg/mol. Always write the symbol and the unit.
  • Using c = P/RT for a liquid. That formula holds for an ideal gas only. For a liquid use the molar density c = ρ/M, with ρ in kg/m³ and M in kg/mol.
  • Swapping y and x. By long convention y is the mole fraction in the vapour or gas and x is the mole fraction in the liquid. An answer that writes x for a gas stream loses marks even when the number is right.
  • Thinking mass transfer needs bulk flow. Diffusion carries a species through a completely still mixture; diffusion and bulk flow are separated properly in 1.2.
  • Classifying by equipment instead of by mechanism. A packed tower is not a separation; it is a contactor that can carry out absorption, stripping or distillation, and the examiner often asks you to say which.

1.2 Molecular and eddy diffusion; diffusion velocities and fluxes

Mass transfer is easier to write once every species has its own velocity. Species A moves with velocity vA, and cA vA is its molar flux NA. That flux contains two parts: A drifting relative to the mixture, and the whole mixture moving. This chapter fixes the vocabulary of velocities, fluxes and reference frames for binary and multicomponent mixtures. It also separates molecular diffusion, the random walk of molecules, from eddy diffusion, the mixing by turbulent lumps. Every later calculation of D, absorption and distillation uses this language.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for concentration, mole fraction and the idea of a component moving because of a concentration difference.

Species velocity and molar flux

Pick a small window fixed to the laboratory wall. Species i crosses it with an average velocity vi, in m/s. Its molar concentration is ci, in mol/m³. The molar flux of i through this fixed window is Ni = ci vi, in mol/(m²·s). The total concentration is c = Σci, and the mole fraction is yi = ci/c. For a binary mixture A + B, c = cA + cB and yA + yB = 1.

The molar flux NA is not the mass flux. The mass flux is nA = ρA vA = MA NA, in kg/(m²·s). Here ρA is the mass concentration in kg/m³ and MA is the molar mass in kg/mol. The mass fraction is wA = ρA/ρ.

Molar average velocity and diffusion flux

The mixture itself has a velocity. The molar average velocity is u = Σci vi/c = ΣNi/c, in m/s. For a binary, u = (NA + NB)/c. The diffusion velocity of A is vA − u. The diffusion flux of A is JA = cA(vA − u), in mol/(m²·s). This JA is measured relative to the molar-average frame, not the laboratory wall.

Add and subtract cA u: NA = cA vA = cA u + cA(vA − u) = yA(NA + NB) + JA. Rearranged, NA = JA + yA(NA + NB). The term yA(NA + NB) is the bulk flow; it is not diffusion. In a binary, JA = −JB because cA(vA − u) + cB(vB − u) = Σci vi − c u = 0. But NA is not −NB unless the fluxes are equimolar, NA + NB = 0.

NA = JA + yA(NA + NB)

NA and NB are fixed-frame molar fluxes in mol/(m²·s); JA is the diffusion flux of A in mol/(m²·s); yA is the mole fraction of A. The second term is the bulk flow.

Other frames have other names. The mass-average velocity is um = Σρi vi/ρ, and the mass-average diffusion flux is jA = ρA(vA − um). The volume-average frame uses volume-fraction weighting; it is common for liquid mixtures. Use NA in the fixed frame, JA in the molar-average frame, and jA in the mass-average frame.

Molecular and eddy diffusion

Molecular diffusion comes from random molecular motion. Molecules of A and B collide and swap places even when the fluid is still. Eddy diffusion comes from turbulence. Eddies carry lumps of fluid, with their A and B, from one place to another. Eddy diffusion is fast in the core of a pipe and zero at a solid wall, where the eddy size falls to zero.

The measured mean flux can contain both parts. Write JA = JA(mol) + JA(eddy), then NA = JA + yA(NA + NB). The eddy part is modelled with an eddy diffusivity DE, in m²/s: JA(eddy) = −DE dcA/dz. Here dcA/dz is the gradient of the mean concentration, in mol/m⁴. DE depends on flow speed, geometry and distance from the wall; it is not a property of the A–B pair. At the wall DE → 0, so only molecular diffusion survives.

The molecular part uses DAB, the molecular diffusivity of the pair at that temperature and pressure. Chapter 1.3 gives Fick's law for that part. The topic of mass transfer coefficients turns that near-wall molecular region into a single coefficient. For a dilute species, engineers often add DAB + DE as an effective diffusivity, but the bulk term stays separate.

Worked example

Question. A binary gas at 300 K and 1 atm has c = 40.6 mol/m³ and yA = 0.30. The measured molar fluxes are NA = 3.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s) and NB = 1.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s). Find the molar average velocity u, the diffusion flux JA, the velocity of A and its diffusion velocity.

  1. Given c = 40.6 mol/m³, yA = 0.30, NA = 3.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), NB = 1.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s). The total molar flux is NA + NB = 4.0×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).
  2. Molar average velocity: u = (NA + NB)/c = 4.0×10⁻³/40.6 = 9.85×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  3. Concentration of A: cA = yA c = 0.30 × 40.6 = 12.18 mol/m³.
  4. Diffusion flux: JA = NA − yA(NA + NB) = 3.0×10⁻³ − 0.30 × 4.0×10⁻³ = 3.0×10⁻³ − 1.2×10⁻³ = 1.8×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).
  5. Velocity of A: vA = NA/cA = 3.0×10⁻³/12.18 = 2.46×10⁻⁴ m/s.
  6. Diffusion velocity: vA − u = 2.46×10⁻⁴ − 9.85×10⁻⁵ = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s. Check: JA/cA = 1.8×10⁻³/12.18 = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s.

Answer. u = 9.85×10⁻⁵ m/s, JA = 1.8×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), cA = 12.18 mol/m³, vA = 2.46×10⁻⁴ m/s, and the diffusion velocity vA − u = 1.48×10⁻⁴ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Calling NA the diffusion flux. NA is the fixed-frame flux; JA is the diffusion flux. At large yA the bulk term yA(NA + NB) is a big part of NA. Setting NA = JA gives a wrong D in Chapter 1.3.
  • Assuming NA = −NB always. Only equimolar counter-diffusion has that; in general JA = −JB while NA + NB is not zero.
  • Mixing units of mass and molar flux. nA = MA NA; use kg/(m²·s) for nA and mol/(m²·s) for NA.
  • Using the molar average velocity u as the fluid flow velocity. u is a concentration-weighted mean of species velocities; the bulk term is yA(NA + NB), not cA times a guessed flow.
  • Treating eddy diffusivity DE as a fluid property. DE depends on turbulence and position and falls to zero at a wall; DAB depends on the pair, temperature and pressure.

Analyze A student sets NA = JA for a concentrated binary gas. Explain the error and its effect on the diffusivity found from the next chapter. 4 marks

NA = JA + yA(NA + NB). For A moving through stagnant B (NB = 0) this gives NA = JA/(1 − yA), so NA is larger than JA. The error vanishes only for a dilute A or equimolar counter-diffusion.

Understand Distinguish molecular diffusion from eddy diffusion. Why does eddy diffusivity fall to zero at a solid wall? 3 marks

Molecular diffusion is random molecular motion and exists even in a still fluid. Eddy diffusion is mixing by turbulent eddies carrying fluid lumps. At a wall the turbulent fluctuations are suppressed, so eddy size and DE fall to zero; only molecular diffusion remains.

1.3 Fick's first and second laws of diffusion

Fick's first law says a component diffuses down its concentration gradient, with flux proportional to the gradient. Fick's second law adds conservation of mass, so it predicts how concentration changes with time at a point. Together they turn a qualitative idea—ink spreading in water, oxygen entering a lung, dopant entering silicon—into equations an engineer can solve. This chapter states both laws, derives the second from a slab balance, and lists the conditions that make a diffusion problem well posed.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for JA, NA and diffusion velocities.

First law: flux from a gradient

Fick's first law applies at a point in a mixture. In general, it relates the molar diffusion flux of A to the gradient of mole fraction. The minus sign is physics: A moves from high yA to low yA, so when dyA/dz is negative, JA is positive in the +z direction.

JA = −c DAB dyA/dz

JA is the molar diffusion flux of A relative to the molar average velocity, mol/(m²·s); c is total molar concentration, mol/m³; DAB is the binary diffusivity of A in B, m²/s; yA is mole fraction of A; z is position, m.

By definition, cA = c yA; when c is constant, dcA/dz = c dyA/dz. The general form then becomes the special case JA = −DAB dcA/dz.

JA = −DAB dcA/dz

valid when c is constant; cA is molar concentration of A, mol/m³; other symbols as above.

JA is measured relative to the molar average velocity of the mixture. The molar flux measured relative to a fixed laboratory frame is NA. It contains JA plus the bulk flow carried by the mixture (see Chapter 1.2). For a binary mixture:

NA = −c DAB dyA/dz + yA(NA + NB)

NA and NB are molar fluxes relative to a fixed frame, mol/(m²·s).

For any binary mixture DAB = DBA, because JA = −JB and the two mole-fraction gradients are equal and opposite. The first law is local: it gives the flux at one position from the gradient there. It does not by itself say how the concentration changes with time.

Second law: a shell balance adds time

To get time, add conservation of mass over a small slab. Consider one-dimensional diffusion in z through a slab of cross-sectional area S and thickness Δz. Take no convection, no reaction and constant D first.

  1. A enters at z and leaves at z + Δz.
  2. Moles in minus moles out equals accumulation: JA at z times S − JA at z + Δz times S = S Δz ∂cA/∂t.
  3. Divide by S Δz, then let Δz → 0: −∂JA/∂z = ∂cA/∂t.
  4. Put JA = −DAB ∂cA/∂z: ∂cA/∂t = DAB ∂²cA/∂z².
∂cA/∂t = DAB ∂²cA/∂z²

cA is concentration at position z and time t; DAB is constant; no reaction.

With a reaction that produces A at net rate RA (mol/(m³·s)), and with D depending on concentration, the general one-dimensional form is:

∂cA/∂t = ∂/∂z(DAB ∂cA/∂z) + RA

RA is positive for production and negative for consumption; DAB may depend on cA.

To solve the second law, a problem needs one initial condition and two boundary conditions, because the equation is first order in t and second order in z. Typical conditions: cA(z,0) = f(z); cA(0,t) = c0; a fixed flux −D ∂cA/∂z = NA; an impermeable surface ∂cA/∂z = 0; a convective surface −D ∂cA/∂z = kc(cs − cbulk), where kc is a mass transfer coefficient in m/s. The last condition is used in Chapter 1.9.

Steady state, boundary conditions and limits

At steady state, concentration at each point stops changing: ∂cA/∂t = 0. With constant D and no reaction, the second law becomes d²cA/dz² = 0. Integrate twice: cA = a + bz, where a and b are constants fixed by boundary conditions. A straight-line profile is therefore the steady answer for a slab, not a general property of diffusion. In the worked example the profile is straight and the steady state is consistent.

Limits to check before using the simple forms: constant total concentration c (true for a gas at fixed T and P, roughly true for a dilute liquid); no convection beyond the bulk term already included; no reaction unless RA is kept; a flat, one-dimensional path; constant D. For gases D changes little with concentration. For liquids D can change strongly with concentration, so use the variable-D form. If the slab is not flat, use the geometry in Chapter 1.5. For time-dependent solutions see Chapter 1.9.

Worked example

Question. A gas mixture at 1 atm and 300 K has total concentration c = 40.6 mol/m³ and DAB = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s. The mole fraction of A is 0.30 at z = 0 and 0.10 at z = 4 mm. (a) Find JA if the profile is a straight line. (b) Does that profile satisfy Fick's second law at steady state?

  1. Convert thickness: Δz = 4 mm = 0.004 m.
  2. Mole-fraction gradient: dyA/dz = (0.10 − 0.30)/0.004 = −50 m⁻¹.
  3. Concentration gradient: dcA/dz = c dyA/dz = 40.6 × (−50) = −2030 mol/m⁴.
  4. First law: JA = −DAB dcA/dz = −(1.8×10⁻⁵)(−2030) = 3.654×10⁻² mol/(m²·s). Positive, so towards increasing z.
  5. Steady second law: ∂cA/∂t = 0 gives d²cA/dz² = 0. A linear cA(z) has zero second derivative, so yes, it satisfies the steady equation.

Answer. The gradient is −2030 mol/m⁴ and JA = 3.65×10⁻² mol/(m²·s) towards increasing z. A straight line has zero second derivative, so it satisfies the steady form of Fick's second law.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the minus sign. If yA falls as z increases, JA is positive in the +z direction; a negative JA means flux is towards −z.
  • Using JA when the question asks for NA through a stagnant gas. Add the bulk term yA(NA + NB); the result contains the factor 1/(yB)_lm.
  • Treating D as constant for every fluid. Gas D changes little with composition, but liquid D can change strongly with concentration.
  • Assuming a straight-line profile always. It holds only at steady state with constant D and c, no reaction, no bulk flow and a flat path of constant area.
  • Flipping the reaction sign. A net production of A adds RA to the right side; a consumption term is negative.

It is first order in time, so one initial condition fixes the starting profile. It is second order in z, so two boundary conditions fix the two spatial constants or fluxes at the ends.

In research, the second law is solved with moving boundaries, concentration-dependent D, and coupled Stefan–Maxwell terms for multicomponent mixtures. 1.4 Steady diffusion in gases and liquids: stagnant and equimolar

Steady one-dimensional diffusion has two standard answers. If A and B swap places in equal amounts, the flux follows the plain mole-fraction difference. If B cannot pass, A still moves through B, and the bulk drift adds a factor 1/(yB)lm. The same equations describe gases and liquids, with c replaced by liquid molar density and y by x. They appear in evaporation into air, absorption from an insoluble carrier, and the film model used later for mass transfer coefficients.

You need Chapter 1.2 for flux definitions and Chapter 1.3 for Fick's first law and simple integration.

Equimolar counter-diffusion

Take steady, one-dimensional diffusion along z in a binary gas or liquid mixture. D is the diffusivity (m²/s), c is the total molar concentration (mol/m³), yA is the mole fraction of A, and NA is the molar flux of A (mol m⁻² s⁻¹). Constant D and c are assumed. Equimolar counter-diffusion means NA = −NB: every molecule of A moving in one direction is replaced by a molecule of B moving the other way. The net molar bulk flow is zero. Integrating Fick's first law over a path of length L (m) gives the formula below. The mole-fraction profile is a straight line. Distillation is treated this way when the two components have nearly equal molar latent heats, so each mole that condenses frees one mole of vapour.

NA = D c (yA1 − yA2) / L = −NB

yA1 and yA2 are the mole fractions of A at z = 0 and z = L; L is the diffusion path length; NB is the molar flux of B, with the opposite sign.

Diffusion through stagnant B

Now B cannot cross the end planes, so NB = 0. This is diffusion of A through a non-diffusing B: evaporation of a liquid into air, or absorption of a soluble gas from an insoluble carrier. Fick's law for A alone is not enough because the mixture itself drifts. The total flux of A is NA = −D c dyA/dz + yA(NA + NB). Set NB = 0 and rearrange: NA(1 − yA) = −D c dyA/dz. Since yB = 1 − yA, integration from z = 0 to z = L gives the log form below. The same result written with a log mean is shown next to it. The factor 1/(yB)lm is greater than 1, so the flux is larger than in the equimolar case: A must drift through B. A film model treats L as a stagnant gas film next to an interface and uses this equation inside the film.

NA = (D c / L) ln(yB2/yB1) = D c (yA1 − yA2) / [L (yB)lm]

(yB)lm = (yB2 − yB1) / ln(yB2/yB1); yB1 and yB2 are the mole fractions of B at z = 0 and z = L.

Liquids, films and laminar flow

For a liquid solution, replace c by the solution molar density (mol/m³) and y by x, the liquid mole fraction. For water, c is about 55,500 mol/m³. The stagnant-B equation becomes the formula below. Liquid D values are about 10⁴ times smaller than gas values while c is about 10³ times larger, so for the same mole-fraction difference and path a liquid flux is roughly ten times smaller than a gas flux. The assumptions are steady state, constant D and c, and one-dimensional transport. Laminar flow here means smooth streamlines and no eddies, so transport across the stream is by molecular diffusion only. Standard apparatus are the wetted-wall column, where liquid runs as a thin film down a tube wall and gas flows in the core, and the laminar jet. A Stefan tube is the gas-phase stagnant-B apparatus. Do not use the gas c for a liquid.

NA = (D c / L) ln(xB2/xB1) = D c (xA1 − xA2) / [L (xB)lm]

xA1, xA2, xB1, xB2 are liquid mole fractions at the two planes; (xB)lm is the log mean of xB, defined like (yB)lm; c is the solution molar density.

Worked example

Question. A gas at 300 K and 1 atm has c = 40.6 mol/m³ and DAB = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s. The mole fraction of A is 0.20 at one plane and 0.05 at a plane 5 mm away. Find the steady flux of A when (a) A and B diffuse in opposite directions in equimolar amounts, (b) B is stagnant. Check part (b) by the log form.

  1. Convert L = 5 mm = 0.005 m; yA1 = 0.20, yA2 = 0.05; c = 40.6 mol/m³; D = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  2. (a) Equimolar: NA = D c (yA1 − yA2)/L = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(40.6)(0.15)/0.005 = 0.0219 mol m⁻² s⁻¹. Also NB = −0.0219 mol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  3. (b) Stagnant B: yB1 = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80; yB2 = 1 − 0.05 = 0.95.
  4. (yB)lm = (0.95 − 0.80)/ln(0.95/0.80) = 0.15/0.1719 = 0.873.
  5. NA = 0.0219/0.873 = 0.0251 mol m⁻² s⁻¹.
  6. Log-form check: D c/L = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(40.6)/0.005 = 0.1462 mol m⁻² s⁻¹; NA = 0.1462 ln(0.95/0.80) = 0.1462 × 0.1719 = 0.0251 mol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Answer. NA = 0.0219 mol/(m²·s) for equimolar counter-diffusion and 0.0251 mol/(m²·s) for stagnant B.

Worked example 2

Question. Acetic acid (A) diffuses through a film of water (B), taken as non-diffusing, 1.0 mm thick at 17 °C. The solution is 9.0 % acid by mass on one face (density 1012 kg/m³) and 3.0 % on the other (density 1003 kg/m³). DAB = 0.95×10⁻⁹ m²/s; MA = 60.05 and MB = 18.02 g/mol. Find NA.

  1. Face 1, per kg of solution: 0.09/60.05 = 1.499×10⁻³ kmol acid and 0.91/18.02 = 50.50×10⁻³ kmol water. So xA1 = 1.499/(1.499 + 50.50) = 0.0288 and the mean molar mass is 1/(52.00×10⁻³) = 19.23 kg/kmol.
  2. Face 2: 0.03/60.05 = 0.500×10⁻³ and 0.97/18.02 = 53.83×10⁻³ kmol per kg, so xA2 = 0.0092 and the mean molar mass is 18.41 kg/kmol.
  3. Molar densities: c₁ = 1012/19.23 = 52.62 kmol/m³ and c₂ = 1003/18.41 = 54.49 kmol/m³; the mean is c = 53.56 kmol/m³ = 5.356×10⁴ mol/m³.
  4. Water fractions: xB1 = 0.9712 and xB2 = 0.9908, so (xB)lm = (0.9908 − 0.9712)/ln(0.9908/0.9712) = 0.981.
  5. NA = D c (xA1 − xA2)/[L (xB)lm] = (0.95×10⁻⁹)(5.356×10⁴)(0.0196)/(0.001 × 0.981) = 1.02×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).
  6. Check with the log form: (D c/L) ln(xB2/xB1) = (0.95×10⁻⁹ × 5.356×10⁴/0.001) × 0.0200 = 1.02×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s).

Answer. NA = 1.02×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), that is 1.02×10⁻⁶ kmol/(m²·s). The film is dilute, so (xB)lm is close to 1 and the stagnant-B correction is only 2 %. A gas film of the same thickness with Δy = 0.02 would carry about ten times more.

Exam traps

  • Using NA = D c ΔyA/L when B is stagnant. Evaporation into air and absorption from an insoluble carrier usually need the log-mean form.
  • Using gas c (about 40 mol/m³ at 1 atm) for a liquid. Water is about 55,500 mol/m³; the liquid flux would be wrong by a factor of about a thousand.
  • Mixing yA and yB in the log mean, or taking ln(yB1/yB2) instead of ln(yB2/yB1). The flux sign follows the direction from plane 1 to plane 2.
  • Forgetting to convert L from mm to m, or using arithmetic mean (yB1 + yB2)/2 instead of the log mean. The arithmetic mean is close only when the two yB values are almost equal.

1.5 Diffusion through variable area; pseudo-steady state diffusion

Most diffusion problems assume a tube of fixed cross-section. In real equipment the path often widens or narrows: vapour leaves a droplet, gas enters a spherical pellet, liquid falls down a tube. The total molar flow W stays the same through every slice, but the flux changes because the area A(z) changes. Fick's law is therefore written with A(z) inside and integrated over the geometry. When a boundary such as a subliming surface moves slowly, each instant can be treated as steady; this pseudo-steady result sizes evaporating droplets and the Stefan tube.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for the stagnant-film flux, and the integration of 1/A.

Total rate is constant, flux is not

Pick a coordinate z along the diffusion path. Let W be the total molar flow of A in mol/s. In steady state W is the same through every cross-section: nothing accumulates. The area A(z) in m² changes with z, so the flux NA = W/A(z) in mol/(m²·s) changes too. Fick's law applies at each slice, not to the whole shape at once.

W = A(z) NA = −D A(z) dcA/dz

W is total molar flow of A (mol/s); A(z) is cross-sectional area (m²); NA is molar flux of A (mol/(m²·s)); D is diffusivity (m²/s); cA is concentration of A (mol/m³); z is distance along the path (m). This form is for equimolar diffusion or a dilute A.

Rearrange and integrate between two stations 1 and 2: W∫dz/A = −D∫dcA. The left side depends only on geometry; the right side only on concentrations. For a constant-area tube A cancels, and you recover Chapter 1.4. For a varying area, carry A(z) inside the integral.

For diffusion of A through stagnant B, replace the concentration gradient term by c dyA/(1−yA), where c is total concentration (mol/m³) and yA is mole fraction of A. The same integration then produces a logarithm, as the sphere below shows.

Sphere in full

For a sphere of radius R losing A into a stagnant gas, A(r)=4πr². At distance r from the centre, W = −D(4πr²)c/(1−yA) dyA/dr. Separate variables and integrate from the surface (r=R, yA=yAs) to the bulk (r→∞, yA=0).

W = 4πR D c ln(1/(1 − yAs))

W is molar flow of A leaving the sphere (mol/s); R is sphere radius (m); D is diffusivity of A in the gas (m²/s); c is total concentration (mol/m³); yAs is mole fraction of A at the surface.

If both sides are equimolar and the two spheres have radii R₁ and R₂, the same integration gives W = 4πD(cA1 − cA2)/(1/R₁ − 1/R₂). Here cA1 and cA2 are the concentrations of A at the two surfaces in mol/m³. The denominator is positive when R₁ < R₂.

A cylinder uses A=2πrL, so ∫dr/r gives ln(r₂/r₁). A cone uses A=πr(z)² with r(z) linear in z, giving a rational integral. The recipe never changes: write A(z), separate, integrate.

Pseudo-steady state: a boundary that moves

A subliming ball or an evaporating droplet has a surface that moves. The gas film around it holds very little material compared with the amount that crosses during the process. The concentration profile therefore adjusts in seconds, while the radius changes over hours. At any instant the steady formula applies with the current radius. This is the pseudo-steady-state assumption.

For a ball, the moles left in the solid are N = (4/3)πR³ρ/M, where ρ is solid density (kg/m³) and M is molar mass (kg/mol). Differentiating, dN/dt = 4πR²(ρ/M)dR/dt. This loss equals −W. Using the stagnant-sphere W gives R dR/dt = −D c L M/ρ, with L = ln(1/(1−yAs)). Integrate from R₁ at t=0 to R at t: R₁² − R² = 2(D c L M/ρ)t. The time depends on R₁² − R², not on R₁ − R.

The falling liquid level in a narrow tube is the same balance. With the gas path z above the liquid, ρL/M · dz/dt = NA = D c (yA1 − yA2)/[z (yB)lm], so the level falls from z₀ to z in a time t = ρL (yB)lm (z² − z₀²)/[2 M D c (yA1 − yA2)]. This is the Stefan tube, and read backwards it measures D (Winkelmann's method, in the next chapter). The pseudo-steady assumption fails at very short times, when the profile has not yet formed; during fast changes of temperature or pressure; and when the vapour is not dilute compared with the condensed phase, for example a volatile liquid close to its boiling point, because the gas then stores a large share of the material that crosses.

Worked example

Question. A naphthalene ball (a mothball) of radius 10 mm sublimes in still air at 298 K and 1 atm. Take D = 6.1×10⁻⁶ m²/s for naphthalene in air, a vapour pressure of 11 Pa at the surface (about 0.08 mmHg), and zero naphthalene far away. M = 128 g/mol and the solid density is ρ = 1140 kg/m³. Find the time for the radius to fall to 5 mm, assuming pseudo-steady diffusion.

  1. Data in SI: R₁ = 0.010 m, R₂ = 0.005 m, M = 0.128 kg/mol. Total concentration c = P/(RT) = 101325/(8.314 × 298) = 40.9 mol/m³.
  2. Surface mole fraction: yAs = 11/101325 = 1.086×10⁻⁴. The log factor ln(1/(1 − yAs)) = 1.086×10⁻⁴; at this dilution it equals yAs.
  3. Rate from the ball at radius R: W = 4πR D c ln(1/(1 − yAs)) = 4πR (6.1×10⁻⁶)(40.9)(1.086×10⁻⁴) = 3.40×10⁻⁷ R mol/s. At R = 10 mm, W = 3.40×10⁻⁹ mol/s, about 0.038 g per day.
  4. Moles in the solid: N = (4/3)πR³ρ/M, so dN/dt = 4πR²(ρ/M) dR/dt. The mass balance dN/dt = −W gives 4πR²(ρ/M) dR/dt = −4πR D c ln(1/(1 − yAs)).
  5. Divide by 4πR: R dR/dt = −k with k = D c ln(1/(1 − yAs)) M/ρ = (6.1×10⁻⁶)(40.9)(1.086×10⁻⁴)(0.128)/1140 = 3.04×10⁻¹² m²/s.
  6. Integrate R dR = −k dt from R₁ to R₂: t = (R₁² − R₂²)/(2k) = (1.0×10⁻⁴ − 2.5×10⁻⁵)/(2 × 3.04×10⁻¹²) = 1.23×10⁷ s.
  7. Convert: 1.23×10⁷ s / 3600 = 3425 h = 143 days.

Answer. About 1.23×10⁷ s, roughly 143 days (nearly five months), for the radius to halve. The tiny vapour pressure, not D, makes a mothball last a season. Air movement in a real cupboard adds convection and shortens this time.

Exam traps

  • Using a fixed area and forgetting A(z): W is constant through the shape, but flux NA = W/A(z) is not.
  • For stagnant B, using the plain dcA/dz form and missing the 1/(1−yA) factor; this loses the logarithm ln(1/(1−yAs)).
  • Integrating the sphere as if R were the variable instead of R²; the time contains R₁² − R₂², not R₁ − R₂.
  • A sign slip: W is positive outward, but dR/dt is negative for a shrinking ball. Keep the loss as −W.
  • Applying pseudo-steady state at very short times or when the gas dissolves appreciably, where the gas holdup is not small.

1.6 Diffusivity: measurement and prediction

Every diffusion calculation needs one number: the diffusivity DAB, the constant in Fick's law that says how quickly A spreads through B. You cannot read it from a table for every pair, so engineers either measure it in a small cell or predict it from simpler properties. Measurement gives the truth for one pair; prediction gives a quick estimate before a pilot plant exists. The same number sizes absorbers, dryers and membrane units. This chapter shows the standard methods for gases and liquids, the units each one uses, and the order of magnitude to expect.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for Fick's laws and Chapter 1.4 for the flux equations used to interpret a measurement; the ideal gas law from Chapter 1.1.

What D is and how big it is

The diffusivity DAB is the proportionality constant in Fick's first law for A diffusing through B. Its SI unit is m²/s; older tables use cm²/s, and 1 cm²/s = 10⁻⁴ m²/s. For any binary pair DAB = DBA. Typical values near room temperature are 10⁻⁵ m²/s in gases, 10⁻⁹ m²/s in liquids and far less in solids; D rises with temperature and, in gases, falls with pressure.

The orders matter in design. A gas-phase value near 10⁻⁵ m²/s means a species crosses a millimetre in about a tenth of a second; a liquid-phase value near 10⁻⁹ m²/s takes about a thousand seconds for the same distance. That is why absorbers and extraction columns use stirring, packing or small droplets: the molecular value alone would need an impractically tall column. Flow adds eddy diffusion on top, as the mass transfer coefficient topic shows.

Measuring D in a cell

Two-bulb method for gases. Two bulbs of volume V each are joined by a tube of length L and cross-section S. One bulb starts rich in A, the other poor. With equal volumes and equimolar counter-diffusion, the mole-fraction difference Δy falls exponentially:

ln(Δy₀/Δy) = (2 DAB S / (L V)) t

Δy₀ is the initial mole-fraction difference; Δy is the difference at time t; S is the tube cross-section in m²; L is the tube length in m; V is each bulb volume in m³; t is time in s.

A plot of ln(Δy₀/Δy) against t is a straight line through the origin, and DAB comes from its slope. Winkelmann's method uses the Stefan tube of the previous chapter. A volatile liquid sits in a narrow vertical tube at constant temperature; air sweeps the open top, so yA2 = 0 there and yA1 is the vapour-pressure value at the liquid surface. The level is read as the gas path grows from z₀ to z. The pseudo-steady result, rearranged, is a straight line:

t/(z − z₀) = [ρL (yB)lm / (2 M D c yA1)] (z − z₀) + ρL (yB)lm z₀ / (M D c yA1)

ρL is the liquid density (kg/m³), M its molar mass (kg/mol), c = P/RT the gas concentration (mol/m³), (yB)lm the log-mean air fraction between the surface and the top. Plot t/(z − z₀) against (z − z₀): the slope s gives D = ρL (yB)lm/(2 M c yA1 s). The initial path z₀ need not be read exactly, which is why the method is popular.

For liquids the diaphragm cell is standard. Two stirred compartments are separated by a porous glass diaphragm. The cell constant β is found once by calibrating with a pair of known D, such as dilute KCl in water. Then ln(Δc₀/Δc) = β DAB t.

Predicting D in gases

Kinetic theory gives the Chapman–Enskog equation, right to a few per cent for non-polar gases. It needs the Lennard–Jones collision diameter σAB (in Å) and a collision integral ΩD, read from a table against kB T/εAB:

DAB = 1.858×10⁻³ T3/2 (1/MA + 1/MB)1/2 / (P σAB² ΩD)

DAB in cm²/s, T in K, P in atm, M in g/mol, σAB in Å. Because σ and ΩD are not always at hand, most hand calculations use the Fuller–Schettler–Giddings correlation:

DAB = 1.00×10⁻³ T1.75 (1/MA + 1/MB)0.5 / [P (ΣvA1/3 + ΣvB1/3)²]

DAB in cm²/s; T in K; P in atm; M in g/mol; ΣvA and ΣvB are sums of tabulated diffusion-volume increments (not molar volumes). Take the cube root of each sum, add them and square the result.

Read the dependence, not just the formula. D rises as T1.75 and falls as 1/P. Doubling pressure halves D at the same temperature. The correlation gives cm²/s only when P is in atm; if you feed kPa you get a value too small by the conversion factor. Convert to m²/s at the end by multiplying by 10⁻⁴.

Predicting D in liquids

Liquid diffusivities are harder because molecules touch. The Stokes–Einstein relation treats the solute as a sphere of radius r moving through a continuum of viscosity μ:

DAB = kB T / (6π μ r)

kB = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; T is absolute temperature in K; μ is viscosity in Pa·s; r is the solute radius in m; DAB is in m²/s.

The key scaling is D ∝ T/μ: warm a liquid and D rises; thicken it and D falls. The relation suits large spherical solutes, less so small molecules.

Wilke–Chang is the common engineering correlation for a dilute solute. It uses the solvent viscosity μB, an association factor φ (2.6 for water, 1.9 for methanol, 1.5 for ethanol, 1.0 for non-polar solvents) and the solute molar volume at its normal boiling point VA, often from Le Bas increments:

DAB = 7.4×10⁻⁸ (φ MB)1/2 T / (μB VA0.6)

DAB in cm²/s; T in K; μB in cP; VA in cm³/mol; MB is the solvent molar mass in g/mol. A correct liquid answer lies near 10⁻⁵ cm²/s (10⁻⁹ m²/s); a value near 10⁻¹ cm²/s means a gas formula slipped in. The Hayduk–Laudie form for water drops φ.

Worked example

  1. Correlation with P in atm gives D in cm²/s: DAB = 1.00×10⁻³ T1.75 (1/MA + 1/MB)0.5 / [P (ΣvA1/3 + ΣvB1/3)²].

1.7 Diffusion in solids

Diffusion in a solid is the slow hopping of atoms between fixed sites, or the creep of gas molecules through tiny pores. The diffusivity D is far smaller than in a gas, often 10⁻¹⁰ to 10⁻²⁰ m²/s, and it rises steeply with temperature. Industry uses it to harden steel, dope silicon, dry wood and food, protect packaged food, and judge hydrogen loss from pipelines. Fick's laws still apply, but the concentration is inside the solid, and the surface value is fixed by solubility, not by the gas composition alone.

You need Fick's laws and the steady slab flux.

Mechanisms: how an atom gets past its neighbours

Textbooks split solid diffusion into two kinds. Structure-insensitive diffusion follows Fick's law through the solid itself, treated as a uniform material: a gas dissolving in and crossing a polymer film or a metal wall. Structure-sensitive diffusion runs through the pores and cracks of a porous solid such as a catalyst pellet, a dried clay or a bed of grain, so the pore geometry sets the rate.

In a metal or ceramic most atoms sit on lattice sites. Diffusion means an atom leaves one site and joins another. The path decides the speed.

  • Interstitial diffusion: small atoms such as carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen squeeze between host atoms. This is the fast path in steel treatment.
  • Vacancy or substitutional diffusion: an atom swaps places with an empty lattice site. This is slower and controls self-diffusion and alloy mixing.
  • Grain-boundary and surface diffusion: atoms along boundaries and free surfaces move faster than through the grain. These are fast paths in polycrystals, especially at low temperature.
  • Pore or Knudsen diffusion: a gas in a porous solid uses ordinary diffusion in large pores; in pores smaller than the mean free path, molecules hit walls more than each other. Porosity and tortuosity lower the effective D.

Fick's first law still holds: JA = −D dcA/dx. Here JA is the flux in mol/(m²·s), cA is concentration in mol/m³ of solid, x is position in m, and D is in m²/s. At the surface, equilibrium often gives cA = S pA, a Henry-type relation. Here pA is gas partial pressure in Pa and S is solubility in mol/(m³·Pa). So the gas composition alone does not set the surface concentration.

Temperature: the Arrhenius law

D = D₀ exp(−E/RT)

D is diffusivity (m²/s); D₀ is the pre-exponential factor (m²/s); E is activation energy (J/mol); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature (K).

E is the energy barrier for one hop. Only the fraction exp(−E/RT) of atoms has enough thermal energy to cross it, so D climbs steeply as T rises. Near 700 K, a 100 K rise can multiply D by about 4 for the worked values below. Grain boundaries have a lower E than the lattice, so they dominate at low temperature. The figure plots D against T for D₀ = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and E = 60 kJ/mol; the marked points are the worked values.

Steady diffusion through a sheet, and where it is used

NA = D (cA1 − cA2)/L

NA is steady flux (mol/(m²·s)); cA1 and cA2 are concentrations at the two faces (mol/m³); L is sheet thickness (m).

This is Fick's first law integrated across a flat sheet with constant flux. If one face touches a gas, use cA = S pA. Then NA = D S (pA1 − pA2)/L, and the product D S is the permeability.

  • Carburizing and nitriding steel: carbon or nitrogen enters the hot surface and diffuses inward. Case depth grows with time; see unsteady-state diffusion.
  • Doping silicon wafers: phosphorus or boron is driven into silicon at high temperature to set electrical properties.
  • Drying wood, clay and food: moisture diffuses to the surface before evaporation. The drying chapters build drying-rate curves on this.
  • Polymer films in packaging: oxygen, carbon dioxide and water vapour permeate slowly through the film; low D keeps food fresh.
  • Hydrogen through pipeline steel: hydrogen is small and interstitial, so it can enter steel and cause embrittlement.
  • Membrane separation: differences in D and solubility separate gases or solutes; see membrane separations.

In a porous solid the gas diffuses through the pores, not the solid. Two corrections apply. The path is longer and narrower than the pellet, so Deff = ε D/τ, with porosity ε and tortuosity τ (often 2 to 6). When the pore radius r is smaller than the mean free path, molecules hit the walls more than each other, and the Knudsen diffusivity takes over: DK = 97 r (T/M)1/2, with DK in m²/s, r in m, T in K and M in kg/kmol. In between, the two resistances add: 1/D = 1/DAB + 1/DK.

Only a few syllabi add the Darken relation and the Kirkendall shift: two species with different D can interdiffuse, and inert markers move.

Worked example

Question. For a gas in a metal take D₀ = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and E = 60 kJ/mol; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K). (a) Find D at 400 °C and at 500 °C. (b) A sheet 3 mm thick holds a steady concentration difference of 0.45 mol/m³ across it. Find the flux at 500 °C.

1.8 Multicomponent diffusion

In a mixture of three or more species, every pair has its own diffusivity, and the flux of one species depends on the gradients of all the others. One effective D is no longer enough. The Stefan–Maxwell equations keep one friction term per pair; Wilke's formula collapses them into a single effective diffusivity for a species moving through a stagnant mixture of the others. This decides the size of columns for multi-component distillation, the modelling of combustion, and the design of membrane and adsorbent pores where reformer gas is separated.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for Fick's first law, and Chapter 1.4 for the equimolar and stagnant-film flux forms.

When two species are not enough

Two species need one number, DAB. Real streams carry more: reformer gas holds H₂, CO, CO₂, CH₄ and steam; flue gas carries N₂, O₂, CO₂ and H₂O; a distillation feed may have six hydrocarbons.

An n-component mixture has n species with mole fractions y₁, y₂, …, yn that add to 1. Every pair (i, j) has its own binary diffusivity Dij, measured or predicted as in Chapter 1.6. The number of pairs is n(n−1)/2: three for n = 3, ten for n = 5. Dij = Dji, so the table is symmetric with blanks on the diagonal.

Treat the mixture with a single effective diffusivity only when one species is dilute, or when the rest move as one block. When three or more species are present in comparable amounts, the motion of A is braked by B and separately by C, and those two brakes act through different pairs. The flux of A then depends on the gradients of B and C as well as its own. That is the multicomponent regime. It appears in multi-component distillation (see distillation), in combustion where radicals diffuse against burnt gas, and in narrow membrane pores.

The Stefan–Maxwell equations

Stefan and Maxwell wrote the balance as driving force against friction. For one-dimensional diffusion along z:

dyi/dz = Σj≠i (yi Nj − yj Ni) / (c Dij)

yi and yj are mole fractions; Ni and Nj are molar fluxes relative to a fixed frame, mol/(m²·s); c is the total molar concentration, mol/m³; Dij is the binary diffusivity of the pair i–j, m²/s; z is distance, m. The sum runs over every j other than i.

Read the right side as friction. Species i rubs against species j, and the rubbing grows with how fast they slip past each other. The slip shows up as (yi Nj − yj Ni), which is zero when i and j travel together. There are n such equations, but only n − 1 are independent, because the mole fractions add to 1.

Check the two-species case. For n = 2 the sum has one term: c D₁₂ dy₁/dz = y₁N₂ − (1 − y₁)N₁ = y₁(N₁ + N₂) − N₁. Rearranged:

N₁ = −c D₁₂ dy₁/dz + y₁ (N₁ + N₂)

the first term is the diffusion part and the second is the bulk-flow part carried by the mixture, in mol/(m²·s).

When the two fluxes are equal and opposite, N₁ + N₂ = 0 and plain Fick's law remains. When species 1 moves through stagnant 2, N₂ = 0, so N₁ = −c D₁₂ dy₁/dz / (1 − y₁), the stagnant-film form of Chapter 1.4. The Stefan–Maxwell set therefore contains both textbook cases inside it.

Wilke's approximation for a dilute species

Solving n coupled equations by hand is slow. Wilke's shortcut is derived for species i diffusing through a stagnant mixture of the others (every Nj = 0 for j ≠ i). It is exact when the other species keep their ratios along the path and is a good estimate for any dilute species. The friction sum then collapses into one effective diffusivity:

Di,m = (1 − yi) / Σj≠i (yj / Dij)

Di,m is the effective diffusivity of species i in the mixture, m²/s; yi is the mole fraction of i; yj is the mole fraction of any other species j; Dij is the binary pair diffusivity, m²/s.

The value is then used inside Fick's law with the bulk term kept:

Ni = −c Di,m dyi/dz + yi ΣN

ΣN is the total molar flux of all species, mol/(m²·s). Drop the second term only when ΣN = 0, that is, equimolar counter-diffusion.

  • Notice the structure: the denominator adds yj/Dij, so the resistances 1/D add, weighted by mole fraction. A pair with a small Dij (heavy friction) adds a large resistance.
  • Di,m is a local value. It depends on the composition at position z, and composition changes along the path. The flux equation must be integrated from z₁ to z₂, not multiplied by Δz. A shortcut using the inlet composition can be off by tens of percent over a long column.
  • In fine pores, wall collisions add a Knudsen diffusivity DK, and the two resistances combine as 1/Deff = 1/Di,m + 1/DK, the Bosanquet relation, which only a few universities teach.

Worked example

Question. A gas mixture at one atmosphere and 300 K has yA = 0.10, yB = 0.30 and yC = 0.60. The binary diffusivities are DAB = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s, DAC = 3.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and DBC = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Find the effective diffusivity of A in the mixture by Wilke's formula, and state how the answer would be used in Fick's law.

  1. Check the data. yA + yB + yC = 0.10 + 0.30 + 0.60 = 1.00, so the composition is complete.
  2. Write Wilke's formula for the dilute species A: DA,m = (1 − yA) / (yB/DAB + yC/DAC). Only pairs that contain A appear, because the sum runs over j ≠ A. The B–C pair does not enter.
  3. Compute the first resistance term: yB/DAB = 0.30 / (1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s) = 3.0×10⁴ s/m².
  4. Compute the second resistance term: yC/DAC = 0.60 / (3.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s) = 2.0×10⁴ s/m².
  5. Add the resistances: 3.0×10⁴ + 2.0×10⁴ = 5.0×10⁴ s/m².
  6. Compute the numerator: 1 − yA = 1 − 0.10 = 0.90, dimensionless.
  7. Divide: DA,m = 0.90 / (5.0×10⁴ s/m²) = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  8. Use it as NA = −c DA,m dyA/dz + yA ΣN. Compare with DAB = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s: A moves through this mixture faster than through pure B alone, because C is the faster partner. If the composition changes along the path, DA,m changes too, and the flux must be integrated rather than multiplied by the path length.

Exam traps

  • Averaging diffusivities as yB DAB + yC DAC. Wilke's formula averages resistances, yj/Dij, not D itself. The two answers differ in size and in trend.
  • Using Ni = −c Di,m dyi/dz and forgetting the bulk term yi ΣN. That is valid only when ΣN = 0; for diffusion through a stagnant mixture it under-predicts the flux by the factor (1 − yi): 10 % at yi = 0.1, half at yi = 0.5.
  • Carrying one Di,m from the bottom of a column to the top. It is a local value tied to the local composition, so the flux equation has to be integrated.
  • Putting all n² diffusivities in a table. Only n(n−1)/2 are distinct, and Dij = Dji. Examiners like this count.
  • Mixing frames: the Stefan–Maxwell equations use Ni, the flux relative to a fixed frame, not Ji, the diffusion flux relative to the molar average velocity. The two differ by yi ΣN.

1.9 Unsteady-state diffusion

Concentration inside a solid or a stagnant liquid often changes with time: a wet brick dries, a polymer sheet loses solvent, a membrane lets gas through. Fick's second law describes this change, but it is a partial differential equation, so the answer depends on the shape and on what happens at the surface. The results come as error functions, series, charts or time lags, not one formula. Engineers use them to size dryers, membranes and diffusion cells.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for the second law and Chapter 1.7 for diffusion in solids.

The second law and the three shapes

Fick's second law gives concentration as a function of position and time. For one direction and constant D it is:

∂c/∂t = D ∂²c/∂x²

c is concentration of the diffusing species in mol/m³; t is time in s; x is position in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s.

To solve it, fix the starting state and the surface state. The starting state is usually a uniform concentration c₀. The surface state is either a fixed surface concentration cs, or a convective condition with an external mass transfer coefficient kc. Three standard shapes cover most problems: a slab of half-thickness L, a cylinder of radius R, and a sphere of radius R. For a slab dried on both faces, L is half the total thickness.

Fo = D t / L²,    Bim = kc L / D

Fo is the mass Fourier number (dimensionless); D is diffusivity in m²/s; t is time in s; L is half-thickness of a slab or radius of a cylinder or sphere in m; Bim is the mass Biot number; kc is the external mass transfer coefficient in m/s.

Fo measures how far diffusion has progressed. Bim measures surface resistance against internal resistance. Large Bim means the surface resistance is small, so the surface concentration is almost fixed. Small Bim means the surface resistance controls the rate.

Short times, long times and charts

At very short contact times, or in a thick body, the far side feels nothing. The body behaves as semi-infinite. Hold the surface at cs and start from c₀. The concentration at depth z is:

(c − c₀)/(cs − c₀) = erfc(z/(2√(Dt)))

c is concentration at distance z and time t; c₀ is initial uniform concentration; cs is surface concentration held constant; z is distance from surface in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s; erfc is the complementary error function.

This is the penetration idea: only a layer of order √(Dt) changes. For long times in a slab with zero surface concentration, the centre concentration follows the first term of the series:

C/C₀ = (4/π) exp(−π² Fo/4)

C is centre concentration at time t; C₀ is initial uniform concentration; Fo = D t/L² is the mass Fourier number; L is half-thickness in m. Valid for Fo > 0.2 when the surface concentration is zero.

Use the first-term centre result when Fo > 0.2; there the neglected terms change C/C₀ by less than 1 %. Below about Fo = 0.1 the error grows fast: at Fo = 0.05 the first term gives 1.12, more than the starting value. For short times use the semi-infinite erfc result, and for a surface resistance use the charts of C/C₀ against Fo drawn for each Bim. At Fo = 0.379 the first-term slab result gives C/C₀ = 0.5.

Drying and leaching usually ask for the average concentration of the slab, not the centre value. Its first term is:

Cavg/C₀ = (8/π²) exp(−π² Fo/4)

Cavg is the mean concentration over the slab; Fo = D t/L² with L the half-thickness. The next term is 1/9 as large and decays nine times faster, so this form is good once Cavg/C₀ is below about 0.6. For a sphere of radius R the centre result is C/C₀ = 2 exp(−π² D t/R²) when D t/R² > 0.1.

Time lag and numerical work

A membrane initially free of penetrant shows a delay before steady permeation starts. The time lag is:

tlag = L²/(6D)

tlag is the time lag in s; L is membrane thickness in m; D is diffusivity in m²/s.

Plotting permeation against time and reading the intercept gives D. When D varies with concentration, temperature or moisture, no closed formula applies. Solve Fick's second law numerically, for example by finite differences. Drying calculations for the falling-rate period use exactly these unsteady curves.

Worked example

Question. A polymer sheet 20 mm thick is dry on both faces, so the surface concentration is zero. It starts with a uniform moisture concentration of 0.50 mol/m³. D = 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s. Find the time for the centre to fall to 0.25 mol/m³.

  1. Both faces are dry, so use the half-thickness: L = 20/2 = 10 mm = 0.010 m.
  2. The target ratio is C/C₀ = 0.25/0.50 = 0.50, dimensionless.
  3. Assume Fo > 0.2 and use the first-term slab formula: C/C₀ = (4/π) exp(−π² Fo/4).
  4. Put C/C₀ = 0.50: 0.50 = 1.2732 exp(−2.4674 Fo).
  5. Then exp(−2.4674 Fo) = 0.50/1.2732 = 0.3927.
  6. Take natural logs: −2.4674 Fo = ln(0.3927) = −0.9340.
  7. So Fo = 0.9340/2.4674 = 0.379, dimensionless. This is greater than 0.2, so the first-term formula is valid.
  8. Find time: t = Fo L²/D = 0.379 × (0.010)² / (2.0×10⁻¹⁰).
  9. (0.010)² = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m². So t = 0.379 × 1.0×10⁻⁴ / 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ = 1.895×10⁵ s.
  10. In hours: t = 1.895×10⁵ / 3600 = 52.6 h, about 53 h.

Answer. The centre reaches 0.25 mol/m³ after about 1.9×10⁵ s, which is about 53 hours.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same sheet (20 mm thick, dried on both faces, C₀ = 0.50 mol/m³, D = 2.0×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s), find the time for the average concentration to fall to 0.25 mol/m³.

  1. Cavg/C₀ = 0.50, and L = 0.010 m as before.
  2. 0.50 = (8/π²) exp(−2.4674 Fo) = 0.8106 exp(−2.4674 Fo), so exp(−2.4674 Fo) = 0.6168.
  3. Fo = −ln(0.6168)/2.4674 = 0.4832/2.4674 = 0.196. The next series term is (8/π²)(1/9)exp(−9 × 2.4674 × 0.196) = 0.0011, negligible.
  4. t = Fo L²/D = 0.196 × 1.0×10⁻⁴/2.0×10⁻¹⁰ = 9.79×10⁴ s = 27.2 h.

Answer. About 9.8×10⁴ s, or 27 h. The average halves in about half the 53 h the centre needs, because the layers near the faces dry first.

Exam traps

  • Using the full thickness instead of the half-thickness for a slab dried from both faces. This gives a time four times too large.
  • Using the first-term slab formula below Fo = 0.2. There the higher series terms are not negligible and the answer is badly wrong.
  • Using diameter instead of radius for a cylinder or sphere, and using slab constants for a sphere or cylinder. The first-term constants differ with shape.
  • Writing the semi-infinite argument as z/(2Dt) instead of z/(2√(Dt)). The square root is part of the error-function argument.
  • Using half-thickness in the membrane time lag tlag = L²/(6D). Here L is the full membrane thickness.

Takeaways

  1. Most separations, apart from mechanical ones, work by giving one component a second phase to move into; how that phase is obtained sorts them into phase creation, phase addition, barrier and solid agent.
  2. Fick's first law links diffusion flux to the negative concentration gradient; Fick's second law adds conservation of mass to give concentration versus time.
  3. Equimolar counter-diffusion uses the plain mole-fraction difference, while stagnant-B diffusion is larger by the factor 1/(yB)lm.
  4. When the diffusion area changes, the total rate stays constant but the flux changes, so Fick's law must be integrated over the geometry.
  5. Pseudo-steady treatment works when a boundary moves slowly enough that each instant can be solved as a steady problem.
  6. D is measured in a known geometry or predicted from kinetic theory and group correlations; solid D values are small and rise steeply with temperature.
  7. With three or more species, each flux depends on all gradients, and Stefan–Maxwell or Wilke relations are needed.
  8. Unsteady diffusion answers come as charts, series or error functions, organized by dimensionless groups such as Fom.

Understand State Fick's first law in its general molar form and in the constant-concentration form. Derive Fick's second law from a balance on a thin slab, extend it to a reacting system and state the initial and boundary conditions a solution needs. 12 marks

Model answer.

  1. Fick's first law: JA = −c DAB dyA/dz, where JA is the molar diffusion flux relative to the molar average velocity (mol/(m²·s)), c the total molar concentration (mol/m³), DAB the binary diffusivity (m²/s) and yA the mole fraction. When c is constant (a gas at fixed T and P), c dyA/dz = dcA/dz and JA = −DAB dcA/dz. The minus sign says A moves down its gradient.
  2. Slab balance: take a slab of area S and thickness Δz with no flow and no reaction. In a time Δt, moles in at z minus moles out at z + Δz equal the gain inside: [JA(z) − JA(z + Δz)] S Δt = S Δz ΔcA.
  3. Divide by S Δz Δt and let Δz, Δt → 0:
    ∂cA/∂t = −∂JA/∂z = ∂/∂z (DAB ∂cA/∂z)
constant D:  ∂cA/∂t = DAB ∂²cA/∂z²
  4. With a homogeneous reaction producing A at RA mol/(m³·s) (negative when A is consumed), the balance gains a source term: ∂cA/∂t = ∂/∂z(DAB ∂cA/∂z) + RA. For a first-order consumption RA = −k cA at steady state, D d²cA/dz² = k cA, whose solution is a sum of exponentials, not a straight line.
  5. The equation is first order in t and second order in z, so it needs one initial condition, for example cA(z, 0) = c₀, and two boundary conditions, one at each face. Common ones: a fixed surface value cA(0, t) = cs; a sealed face ∂cA/∂z = 0; a fixed flux; or a convective face −D ∂cA/∂z = kc(cs − cbulk).

  7. Numerical check: for yA = 0.30 at z = 0 and 0.10 at z = 4 mm in a gas with c = 40.6 mol/m³ and DAB = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s, dcA/dz = 40.6 × (−0.20/0.004) = −2030 mol/m⁴ and JA = 1.8×10⁻⁵ × 2030 = 3.65×10⁻² mol/(m²·s).   7. End fluxes: narrow end NA = 3.78×10⁻⁷/(π × 0.010²) = 1.20×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s); wide end NA = 3.78×10⁻⁷/(π × 0.025²) = 1.92×10⁻⁴ mol/(m²·s). The ratio is (25/10)² = 6.25, as the areas require.
  8. Comparison: a straight tube of radius 10 mm would carry 1.51×10⁻⁷ mol/s and one of 25 mm 9.45×10⁻⁷ mol/s. The cone behaves like a straight tube of radius √(r₁ r₂) = 15.8 mm, and the narrow end, where the flux is highest, controls the rate.
  9. If B were stagnant instead, the same separation would give W ∫dz/A = (c DAB) ln[(1 − yA2)/(1 − yA1)], so the geometry factor L/(π r₁ r₂) is unchanged and only the driving term changes: the geometry and the driving force always separate, which is the point of the method.
  10. Check: π r₁ r₂ = π × (15.8 mm)² confirms the equivalent radius, and W lies between the two straight-tube values.
Apply Benzene evaporates from a narrow vertical tube into air that sweeps the open top, at 298 K and 1 atm. Its vapour pressure is 12.7 kPa, liquid density 880 kg/m³, M = 78 g/mol, D = 9.6×10⁻⁶ m²/s. Using pseudo-steady state, derive the time for the level to fall from 20 mm to 50 mm below the top, and evaluate it. 14 marks

Points to cover. Air stagnant, benzene diffuses up; Flux at a given path z; Mass balance on the liquid; Integrate z dz; Numbers and initial rate; Pseudo-steady check.

Model answer.

  1. Air does not dissolve in benzene, so air is stagnant in the tube. At the liquid surface yA1 = 12.7/101.325 = 0.1253; at the top the sweeping air keeps yA2 = 0. c = 101325/(8.314 × 298) = 40.9 mol/m³.
  2. (yB)lm between yB1 = 0.8747 and yB2 = 1: (1 − 0.8747)/ln(1/0.8747) = 0.1253/0.1339 = 0.936.
  3. When the gas path is z, the stagnant-B flux is NA = D c (yA1 − yA2)/[z (yB)lm].
  4. The level falls because this flux removes liquid: (ρL/M) dz/dt = NA, so (ρL/M) z dz = [D c (yA1 − yA2)/(yB)lm] dt.
  5. Integrate from z₀ to z:
    t = ρL (yB)lm (z² − z₀²) / [2 M D c (yA1 − yA2)]
  = 880 × 0.936 × (0.050² − 0.020²) / (2 × 0.078 × 9.6×10⁻⁶ × 40.9 × 0.1253)
  = 880 × 0.936 × 2.10×10⁻³ / (7.67×10⁻⁶)
  = 2.25×10⁵ s = 62.6 h
  6. At the start (z = 20 mm) the flux is 9.6×10⁻⁶ × 40.9 × 0.1253/(0.020 × 0.936) = 2.63×10⁻³ mol/(m²·s), and the level falls at NA M/ρL = 2.63×10⁻³ × 0.078/880 = 2.3×10⁻⁷ m/s, or 0.84 mm/h. The rate falls as the path grows, since NA ∝ 1/z.
  7. Pseudo-steady check: the vapour held in the gas column (about yA c/2 = 2.6 mol/m³ on average) is tiny against the liquid that evaporates (ρL/M = 1.13×10⁴ mol/m³), a ratio near 2×10⁻⁴, so the profile keeps up with the moving surface.
  8. Exam variant: if the question gives the level fall and the time and asks for D, the same equation is solved for D. With 30 mm of fall in 62.6 h it returns D = 9.6×10⁻⁶ m²/s, which is how Winkelmann's method measures diffusivity.
  9. Check: halving D would double the time; the time depends on z² − z₀² = 2.1×10⁻³ m², not on the 30 mm fall alone.
Apply A vapour A at yA = 0.05 diffuses through a stagnant gas mixture of B (y = 0.40), C (0.35) and D (0.20) at 300 K and 1 atm. DAB = 6.0×10⁻⁵, DAC = 1.9×10⁻⁵ and DAD = 2.2×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Find the effective diffusivity of A by Wilke's equation, and the flux across a 1.5 mm film over which yA falls from 0.05 to 0.01. 12 marks

Points to cover. Wilke's equation and when it holds; The three resistance terms; DA,m; the A-free basis gives the same; Stagnant-mixture flux with the bulk term; Compare with a wrong D average.

Model answer.

  1. Wilke's equation, DA,m = (1 − yA)/Σ(yj/DAj), holds for A diffusing through a mixture whose other species are stagnant; here B, C and D do not move, so it applies.
  2. Where the equation comes from: with Nj = 0 for every j ≠ A, each Stefan–Maxwell term for A reduces to −yj NA/(c DAj), so
    dyA/dz = −(NA/c) Σ yj/DAj = −NA (1 − yA)/(c DA,m)
which defines DA,m = (1 − yA)/Σ (yj/DAj)
  3. Resistance terms:
    PairyjDAj (m²/s)yj/DAj (s/m²)
    A–B0.406.0×10⁻⁵6.67×10³
    A–C0.351.9×10⁻⁵1.84×10⁴
    A–D0.202.2×10⁻⁵9.09×10³
    sum3.42×10⁴
  4. DA,m = 0.95/3.42×10⁴ = 2.78×10⁻⁵ m²/s. On an A-free basis (y′_B = 0.421, y′_C = 0.368, y′_D = 0.211) DA,m = 1/Σ(y′_j/DAj) gives the same 2.78×10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  5. C has the smallest pair diffusivity and the largest share of the resistance (54 %), so it pulls DA,m well below DAB even though B is the most abundant partner.
  6. Flux through the stagnant mixture: c = 101325/(8.314 × 300) = 40.6 mol/m³, and NA = (c DA,m/L) ln[(1 − yA2)/(1 − yA1)] = (40.6 × 2.78×10⁻⁵/0.0015) × ln(0.99/0.95) = 0.753 × 0.0412 = 3.10×10⁻² mol/(m²·s).
  7. Because B, C and D keep their ratios through the film, DA,m stays at 2.78×10⁻⁵ m²/s at every point, and the integration with a constant D is exact here.
  8. A common error is to average D itself: (0.40 × 6.0 + 0.35 × 1.9 + 0.20 × 2.2)×10⁻⁵/0.95 = 3.69×10⁻⁵ m²/s, which would give 4.12×10⁻² mol/(m²·s), 33 % too high.
  9. If the film were not stagnant, for example if B were absorbed alongside A, the terms yA NB/(c DAB) would no longer vanish and Wilke's equation would not apply; the full Stefan–Maxwell set would be needed.
  10. Check: DA,m = 2.78×10⁻⁵ lies between the smallest (1.9×10⁻⁵) and largest (6.0×10⁻⁵) pair values, as a weighted resistance sum must.
Apply A steel part 10 mm thick, with 0.20 wt% carbon, is carburized from both faces in a gas that holds the surface at 1.20 wt%. At the carburizing temperature D = 1.5×10⁻¹¹ m²/s. Find the time for the carbon to reach 0.80 wt% at 0.50 mm depth, justify the solution used and say how long a 1.0 mm case would take. Take erfc(0.35) = 0.6206 and erfc(0.40) = 0.5716. 13 marks

Points to cover. Unsteady diffusion; semi-infinite solution; Concentration ratio and erfc; Interpolate for the argument; t = z²/(4Dη²); Check of the semi-infinite assumption; Effect of doubling the depth.

Model answer.

  1. This is unsteady diffusion into a solid with a fixed surface value, Fick's second law with constant D. For thin case depths the far side of the part feels nothing, so the semi-infinite solution applies: (c − c₀)/(cs − c₀) = erfc[z/(2√(Dt))].
  2. Why erfc: Fick's second law ∂c/∂t = D ∂²c/∂z² with c = c₀ everywhere at t = 0, c = cs at the surface for t > 0 and c → c₀ far inside has the similarity variable η = z/(2√(Dt)); its solution is the complementary error function. Carbon is a small interstitial atom in hot austenite, which is why D is as large as 10⁻¹¹ m²/s.
  3. Ratio: (0.80 − 0.20)/(1.20 − 0.20) = 0.600, so erfc(η) = 0.600 with η = z/(2√(Dt)).
  4. Interpolate: η = 0.35 + 0.05 × (0.6206 − 0.600)/(0.6206 − 0.5716) = 0.35 + 0.05 × 0.420 = 0.371.
  5. Solve for t:
    t = z² / (4 D η²) = (0.50×10⁻³)² / (4 × 1.5×10⁻¹¹ × 0.371²)
  = 2.5×10⁻⁷ / 8.26×10⁻¹² = 3.03×10⁴ s = 8.4 h
  6. Semi-infinite check: the carbon front reaches about 4√(Dt) = 4 × √(1.5×10⁻¹¹ × 3.03×10⁴) = 4 × 0.67 mm = 2.7 mm, less than the 5 mm half-thickness, so the two fronts do not meet and the solution is valid.
  7. Doubling the depth: t ∝ z² at a fixed concentration ratio, so a 1.0 mm case to the same 0.80 wt% needs 4 × 8.4 = 33.7 h, and its front (about 5.4 mm) would then touch the centre, so the semi-infinite answer becomes only an estimate.
  8. Practical point: since t ∝ z²/D and D follows the Arrhenius law, plants raise the temperature to cut the cycle time; a higher D at a hotter furnace gives the same case depth in a fraction of the hours, at the cost of grain growth in the steel.
  9. Check: after 10 h, the 0.80 wt% depth is 2 × 0.371 × √(1.5×10⁻¹¹ × 36000) = 0.54 mm, a little beyond 0.50 mm, consistent with 8.4 h.

Evaluate Compare the two-bulb, Winkelmann (Stefan tube) and diaphragm-cell methods for measuring diffusivity, with their assumptions and errors. Then evaluate D from this Winkelmann run for CCl₄ in air at 321 K and 101.3 kPa (vapour pressure 37.6 kPa, ρL = 1540 kg/m³, M = 154 g/mol): level fall z − z₀ = 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 mm after t = 7100, 15780, 26030, 37860, 51270 s. 14 marks

Points to cover. Principle of each method; Assumptions and main error sources; Winkelmann straight-line form; Table of t/(z − z₀); Slope, D and z₀ from the intercept; Choice of method for a gas and for a liquid.

Model answer.

  1. Two-bulb method (gases): two bulbs of volume V joined by a tube of length L and area S start at different compositions; with equimolar counter-diffusion ln(Δy₀/Δy) = (2 D S/(L V)) t, so D comes from the slope. Assumes a pseudo-steady tube and well-mixed bulbs; errors come from temperature drift, leaks and the tube end effects.
  2. Winkelmann method (vapour of a volatile liquid in a gas): the liquid evaporates up a narrow tube into a gas stream that keeps yA2 = 0 at the top; the fall of the level is read with a travelling microscope. Assumes stagnant gas, a pseudo-steady profile and constant temperature; errors come from convection at the mouth, cooling of the surface by evaporation and reading the level.
  3. Diaphragm cell (liquids): two stirred compartments separated by a porous glass disc; ln(Δc₀/Δc) = β D t. The cell constant β is found by calibration with a known pair (dilute KCl in water). Errors come from poor stirring (films at the disc faces), bubbles in the pores and a wrong calibration.
  4. Winkelmann data. With ρL/M · z dz/dt = D c yA1/[(yB)lm] and the level read from an unknown start z₀:
    t/(z − z₀) = [ρL (yB)lm / (2 M D c yA1)] (z − z₀) + ρL (yB)lm z₀/(M D c yA1)
  5. Working table:
    z − z₀ (mm)t (s)t/(z − z₀) (s/m)
    571001.420×10⁶
    10157801.578×10⁶
    15260301.735×10⁶
    20378601.893×10⁶
    25512702.051×10⁶
  6. The points lie on a straight line; least squares gives slope s = 3.153×10⁷ s/m² and intercept 1.262×10⁶ s/m.
  7. Properties: c = 101300/(8.314 × 321) = 37.96 mol/m³; yA1 = 37.6/101.3 = 0.3712; (yB)lm = 0.3712/ln(1/0.6288) = 0.800.
  8. D = ρL (yB)lm/(2 M c yA1 s) = 1540 × 0.800/(2 × 0.154 × 37.96 × 0.3712 × 3.153×10⁷) = 9.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s. The intercept over twice the slope gives the starting path z₀ = 1.262×10⁶/(2 × 3.153×10⁷) = 0.020 m, so the level did start 20 mm below the top.
  9. Evaluation: for a vapour in air the Winkelmann method is the simplest (one tube, a thermostat, a microscope) and z₀ need not be known; the two-bulb cell suits two permanent gases; the diaphragm cell is the standard for liquids, where D is 10⁴ times smaller and a Stefan tube would take years. Whatever the method, quote the temperature, because D rises as about T1.75 in gases.
Analyze Explain why diffusivities in solids are small and strongly temperature dependent, naming the mechanisms. A gas in a metal has D = 4.41×10⁻¹⁰ m²/s at 673 K and 1.76×10⁻⁹ m²/s at 773 K: find E and D₀, the value at 300 K and what it means for room-temperature use. Contrast diffusion through the pores of a solid. 14 marks

Points to cover. Lattice hops: interstitial, vacancy; fast paths; Arrhenius law and its log form; E and D₀ from two points; Extrapolation to 300 K and a penetration depth; Porous solids: ε, τ and Knudsen; Applications.

Model answer.

  1. In a crystalline solid atoms sit on lattice sites; a diffusing atom must jump to a neighbouring site. Small atoms (C, N, H) jump between host atoms (interstitial diffusion); larger ones swap with an empty site (vacancy diffusion). Grain boundaries and surfaces are disordered fast paths with lower barriers. Each jump needs the atom to cross an energy barrier E, and only the fraction exp(−E/RT) of attempts succeed, so D is small and climbs steeply with T.
  2. Arrhenius law and two-point solution:
    D = D₀ exp(−E/RT)
ln(D₂/D₁) = (E/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
E = 8.314 × ln(1.76×10⁻⁹/4.41×10⁻¹⁰) / (1/673 − 1/773)
  = 8.314 × 1.384 / 1.922×10⁻⁴ = 5.99×10⁴ J/mol ≈ 60 kJ/mol
D₀ = 4.41×10⁻¹⁰ × exp(59900/(8.314 × 673)) = 1.95×10⁻⁵ m²/s
  3. At 300 K: D = 1.95×10⁻⁵ exp(−59900/(8.314 × 300)) = 7.4×10⁻¹⁶ m²/s, about six orders of magnitude below the 673 K value.
  4. Meaning: the depth reached in one day is about √(Dt) = √(7.4×10⁻¹⁶ × 86400) = 8×10⁻⁶ m, eight micrometres, against about 6 mm at 673 K. Heat treatment and doping are therefore done hot, and a part keeps its case profile at room temperature.
  5. Steady permeation: at 773 K a 5 mm sheet with a concentration difference of 0.45 mol/m³ passes N = 1.76×10⁻⁹ × 0.45/0.005 = 1.6×10⁻⁷ mol/(m²·s). When a gas sets the surface value, c = S p and the flux is D S Δp/L, so the permeability D S, not D alone, decides leakage.
  6. Porous solids are different: the gas moves through the pores, not the solid. The effective diffusivity is Deff = ε D/τ, and in pores narrower than the mean free path the Knudsen value DK = 97 r (T/M)1/2 replaces the molecular one. For 5 nm pores at 500 K, DK = 2.05×10⁻⁶ m²/s, and with ε = 0.4, τ = 4 the pellet gives about 2×10⁻⁷ m²/s, still far above lattice diffusion. Its temperature dependence is weak (√T), unlike the Arrhenius law.
  7. Check: D₀ exp(−E/(R × 773)) = 1.95×10⁻⁵ × 9.0×10⁻⁵ = 1.76×10⁻⁹ m²/s reproduces the second data point.
Evaluate Evaluate why multicomponent diffusion cannot be handled as independent binary pairs. Present the Stefan–Maxwell equations, show that they reduce to the binary results, derive the condition for Wilke's equation and apply it to A (yA = 0.10 falling to 0.02 across a 2.0 mm film) in stagnant B and C (yB = 0.30, yC = 0.60, B:C fixed at 1:2), with DAB = 1.0×10⁻⁵, DAC = 3.0×10⁻⁵ m²/s and c = 40.6 mol/m³. 14 marks

Points to cover. n(n − 1)/2 pair diffusivities and coupling; Stefan–Maxwell form and its friction meaning; Binary reduction; Wilke: Nj = 0 for j ≠ A; Numbers: DA,m and flux; Limits of Wilke.

Model answer.

  1. In an n-component gas every pair has its own diffusivity, n(n − 1)/2 of them (3 for a ternary). The flux of A is braked separately by B and by C, so it depends on the gradients and fluxes of all species. One Fick constant per species cannot capture this; in extreme cases a species can even diffuse against its own gradient.
  2. Stefan–Maxwell equations (ideal gas, one dimension):
    dyi/dz = Σj≠i (yi Nj − yj Ni)/(c Dij),   i = 1 … n (n − 1 independent)
  3. Each term is a friction between i and j, proportional to how fast they slip past each other and to 1/Dij. For n = 2: c D₁₂ dy₁/dz = y₁N₂ − y₂N₁, which rearranges to N₁ = −c D₁₂ dy₁/dz + y₁(N₁ + N₂), the binary result that contains both the equimolar and stagnant cases.
  4. Wilke: put Nj = 0 for every j ≠ A:
    dyA/dz = −NA Σj≠A yj/(c DAj)
        = −NA (1 − yA)/(c DA,m),   DA,m = (1 − yA)/Σ yj/DAj
  5. So NA = −c DA,m dyA/[(1 − yA) dz], the stagnant-film form with DA,m in place of DAB. If the stagnant species keep their ratios, DA,m does not change along the path and the integration is exact.
  6. Numbers: DA,m = 0.90/(0.30/1.0×10⁻⁵ + 0.60/3.0×10⁻⁵) = 0.90/5.0×10⁴ = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s. At the other face (yA = 0.02, yB = 0.3267, yC = 0.6533) the same formula gives 1.8×10⁻⁵ m²/s again.
  7. Flux: NA = (c DA,m/L) ln[(1 − yA2)/(1 − yA1)] = (40.6 × 1.8×10⁻⁵/0.002) × ln(0.98/0.90) = 0.365 × 0.0852 = 3.11×10⁻² mol/(m²·s). Dropping the bulk term would give 2.92×10⁻² (6 % low); averaging D instead of resistances (2.33×10⁻⁵ m²/s) would give 4.03×10⁻² (30 % high).
  8. Limits: Wilke's equation fails when the other species are not stagnant (for example when B is also absorbed or counter-diffuses), when no species is dilute and the ratios change along the path, and in non-ideal liquids. Then the full Stefan–Maxwell set must be solved, as process simulators do; for a dilute A in a stagnant mixture Wilke is adequate for design.

Glossary

Activation energy
The energy barrier that makes solid diffusion rise steeply with temperature.
Binary mixture
A mixture containing only two species.
Boundary condition
A stated concentration or flux at the surface of the diffusion path.
Bulk flow
Motion of the mixture as a whole, carrying all species with it.
Carburizing
Adding carbon to the surface of steel by diffusion at high temperature.
Concentration gradient
Change in concentration per unit distance; it drives diffusion.
Convective mass transfer
Mass transfer between a flowing fluid and a surface or another phase.
Diffusion coefficient
The proportionality constant between diffusion flux and concentration gradient.
Diffusive flux
Moles of a species crossing unit area per unit time relative to the mixture velocity.
Doping
Adding a controlled impurity to silicon by diffusion to change its electrical behaviour.
Effective diffusivity
An apparent D for a porous solid that includes porosity and tortuosity effects.
Grain-boundary diffusion
Faster diffusion along boundaries between grains in a solid.
Initial condition
The concentration field at time zero.
Interstitial diffusion
Small atoms hop between lattice sites without displacing host atoms.
Knudsen diffusion
Pore diffusion in which molecule-wall collisions dominate over molecule-molecule collisions.
Le Bas volume
Molar volume of a liquid at its normal boiling point, estimated by adding atomic increments; used in Wilke–Chang.
Mass fraction
Mass of a species divided by total mass of the mixture.
Mole fraction
Moles of a species divided by total moles in the mixture.
Pore diffusion
Diffusion through gas-filled or liquid-filled pores in a solid.
Semi-infinite medium
A medium thick enough that the far boundary has no effect during the observed time.
Steady state
Condition in which concentration at a fixed point does not change with time.
Surface diffusion
Movement of adsorbed molecules along the wall of a pore.
Tortuosity
A factor greater than one that accounts for the longer crooked path through pores.
Total flux
Moles of a species crossing a fixed plane per unit area per unit time.
Unsteady state
Condition in which concentration at a fixed point changes with time.
Vacancy diffusion
An atom moves into an empty lattice site, so the vacancy moves the other way.
Winkelmann method
Measuring the diffusivity of a vapour from the slow fall of the liquid level in a narrow tube swept at the top by a gas.
