5.1 Liquid-liquid extraction and ternary (triangular) diagrams

Liquid–liquid extraction removes a solute from a liquid feed by mixing the feed with a second liquid, the solvent, that dissolves the solute but hardly mixes with the feed liquid. After mixing, the liquids settle into two layers that share the solute. Extraction is chosen when distillation is poor: close boiling points, a little high-boiling solute in much water (dilute acetic acid), or a heat-sensitive solute (penicillin). Because three components are present, the compositions are read from a triangular (ternary) diagram.

Names of the streams and what makes a good solvent

Call the solute A, the feed liquid that carries it B (the carrier or diluent) and the added liquid S (the solvent). The solvent-rich layer that leaves is the extract (E); the carrier-rich layer is the raffinate (R). A stage is one mixing followed by one settling. In an ideal (equilibrium) stage the extract and raffinate leave in equilibrium with each other. The solvent is later distilled off the extract and reused.

Syllabi list the solvent characteristics below. Each one has a reason you should be able to state in the exam.

  • Selectivity β = (yA/yB)/(xA/xB), where y is a mass fraction in the extract and x in the raffinate. β must be well above 1, or the extract carries as much carrier as solute and nothing is separated.
  • Distribution coefficient K = yA/xA large, so little solvent is needed.
  • Low mutual solubility of B and S: less solvent lost in the raffinate and a purer extract.
  • Recoverability: a boiling point well away from that of A and no azeotrope with it, so the solvent can be distilled off and recycled.
  • Density difference between the layers large enough to settle quickly; interfacial tension high enough for drops to coalesce but not so high that the liquids cannot be dispersed.
  • Low viscosity, stability, low toxicity and flammability, low cost.

Reading the equilateral triangle

The three mass fractions add to 1, so a mixture is fixed by two numbers and fits on a triangle. Each corner is a pure component: A at the top, B at the bottom left, S at the bottom right. A point on a side is a two-component mixture. For a point inside, the fraction of A is its perpendicular distance from the side BS divided by the height of the triangle; the same rule with the other two sides gives B and S. In practice you read the grid lines drawn parallel to the sides.

The binodal (solubility) curve divides the diagram. Mixtures outside it form one liquid phase. A mixture inside it splits into two layers whose compositions lie on the curve: the raffinate branch near B and the extract branch near S. The straight line joining two layers in equilibrium is a tie line; tie lines are measured, and the overall mixture point always lies on its tie line. Tie lines usually slope, because the solute is not equally distributed. They get shorter as the solute content rises and shrink to a single point, the plait point, where the two layers become identical. Beyond it no separation is possible.

Type I systems have one partly miscible pair (B–S) and a plait point; water–acetic acid–isopropyl ether is the usual textbook case. Type II systems have two partly miscible pairs (A–S and B–S), so the two-phase band runs right across the diagram with no plait point.

Lever rule. When F kg of feed and S kg of solvent are mixed, the mixture point M lies on the straight line FS, with FM/MS = S/F. When M splits into E and R on a tie line, E/M = RM/RE and R/M = ME/RE. The lever rule is a mass balance drawn as a picture, so it can always be checked with the component balances.

Distribution coefficient and solute-free ratios

K = yA / xA      Y = yA/(1 − yA),  X = xA/(1 − xA)

K is the distribution coefficient on mass fractions at the two ends of one tie line. X is kg A per kg of the rest of the raffinate (nearly all B) and Y is kg A per kg of the rest of the extract (nearly all S). The ratio form is used when B and S are almost immiscible, because then the B flow and the S flow stay constant from stage to stage.

K changes along the binodal curve and with temperature, so one K is used only over a range where it is nearly constant. When B and S are almost immiscible the stage calculations become simple algebra on X and Y; that is the route of the next two chapters. When they are partly miscible, the stages are drawn on the triangle.

Worked example

Question. 100 kg of a feed containing 30 mass% acetic acid (A) in water (B) is mixed in one ideal stage with 120 kg of pure solvent S. The tie line through the mixture point joins a raffinate of xA = 0.100, xS = 0.030, xB = 0.870 to an extract of yA = 0.1543, yS = 0.800, yB = 0.0457. Find the mixture point, the masses of extract and raffinate, the acid recovered, K and the selectivity.

  1. Mixture: M = F + S = 100 + 120 = 220 kg. It holds 30 kg A, 70 kg B and 120 kg S, so xA,M = 30/220 = 0.1364, xS,M = 120/220 = 0.5455, xB,M = 70/220 = 0.3182. Lever check on line FS: FM/MS = 120/100 = 1.2.
  2. Total balance: E + R = 220 kg.
  3. Acid balance: 0.1543 E + 0.100 R = 30. Put R = 220 − E: 0.1543 E + 22.0 − 0.100 E = 30, so 0.0543 E = 8.0 and E = 147.3 kg; R = 220 − 147.3 = 72.7 kg.
  4. Check the other components. Solvent: 0.800 × 147.3 + 0.030 × 72.7 = 117.9 + 2.2 = 120.1 kg (fed 120). Water: 0.0457 × 147.3 + 0.870 × 72.7 = 6.7 + 63.2 = 69.9 kg (fed 70). The small differences are rounding of the tie-line ends.
  5. Lever rule: E/M = RM/RE = 0.669, so E = 0.669 × 220 = 147.3 kg, the same answer read as lengths on the diagram.
  6. Acid in extract = 0.1543 × 147.3 = 22.7 kg, so 22.7/30 = 75.8 % of the acid is recovered in one stage. Acid left in raffinate = 0.100 × 72.7 = 7.3 kg.
  7. K = 0.1543/0.100 = 1.54. Selectivity β = (0.1543/0.0457)/(0.100/0.870) = 3.38/0.115 = 29.4, far above 1, so the solvent takes acid much more readily than water.

Answer. M lies at xA = 0.136, xS = 0.545; E = 147.3 kg, R = 72.7 kg; 22.7 kg acid (75.8 %) goes to the extract; K = 1.54 and β = 29.4.

Exam traps

  • Tie lines are measured lines through the two equilibrium layers. A line drawn parallel to a side of the triangle is a line of constant composition of one component, not a tie line.
  • The mixture point lies on the straight line FS and on a tie line, never on the binodal curve itself unless the mixture is just saturated.
  • K and β are different: K = 1.54 here says how much solvent is needed, β = 29.4 says how cleanly A is separated from B. A solvent with large K and β near 1 is useless.
  • In the lever rule E/M = RM/RE, the arm opposite E is RM. Swapping the arms gives E = 72.7 kg instead of 147.3 kg.
  • The plait point is where the layers become identical, not the richest possible extract.

Understand List four properties of a good extraction solvent and give the reason for each. 4 marks

Analyze Two solvents give the same K for the solute. Solvent P dissolves 2 % of the carrier, solvent Q dissolves 12 %. Which do you choose, and what goes wrong with the other? 5 marks

Choose P. With Q the extract carries much more carrier, so β is lower and the recovered solute is impure; more carrier must be separated from the solvent, and more Q is lost in the raffinate, raising make-up cost. Equal K means equal solvent flow, so the decision rests on mutual solubility, recovery cost and density difference.

5.2 Single-stage and multistage cross-current extraction

One equilibrium stage leaves part of the solute in the raffinate. In cross-current extraction the raffinate from one stage is contacted again with a fresh portion of solvent, stage after stage, and each extract is drawn off separately. When the carrier and solvent are immiscible and the distribution ratio is constant, every stage removes the same fraction of what is left, so the solute falls geometrically and one formula gives the result. Cross-current contact is the arithmetic of batch extraction in a laboratory funnel and of small multi-product plants; large continuous plants use counter-current flow, the next chapter.

You need Liquid-liquid extraction and ternary diagrams (lever rule, solute-free ratios X and Y).

One stage on solute-free ratios

Take B and S as immiscible. Then the carrier flow B (kg) leaves entirely in the raffinate and the solvent S (kg) entirely in the extract, and the solute is followed with ratios: X = kg A per kg B, Y = kg A per kg S. Equilibrium is Y = K′X, where K′ is the distribution ratio on this basis (it differs from the mass-fraction K).

A solute balance on one ideal stage fed with pure solvent: B X0 = B X1 + S Y1, with Y1 = K′X1. Solving gives the result below.

X1 = X0 / (1 + ε),   ε = K′S/B

X0 and X1 are kg solute per kg carrier in the feed and the raffinate; S is kg of pure solvent; B is kg of carrier; ε is the extraction factor, the solute leaving in the extract divided by the solute left in the raffinate for one ideal stage (dimensionless).

On a triangular diagram, when B and S partly dissolve each other, the same stage is drawn: join F to S, locate M by the lever rule (FM/MS = S/F), draw the tie line through M (by interpolation between measured tie lines), and read E1 and R1 at its ends.

The cross-current cascade

In stage n the raffinate Rn−1 meets a fresh solvent portion Sn, and the extract En is withdrawn. With equal portions and constant K′, every stage has the same ε and the same balance repeats.

Xn = X0 / (1 + ε)n

Xn is the raffinate ratio after n ideal stages, each fed S kg of pure solvent. Fraction recovered = 1 − 1/(1 + ε)n; fraction left in the raffinate = 1/(1 + ε)n.

  1. Graphically on the triangle, stage 1 is drawn as above.
  2. R1 becomes the feed of stage 2: join R1 to S, place M2 with R1M2/M2S = S2/R1, and draw the tie line through M2.
  3. Repeat until the raffinate composition reaches the target; the number of tie lines drawn is the number of ideal stages.
  4. On an X–Y plot (immiscible case) each stage is a straight line of slope −B/Sn from (Xn−1, 0) to the equilibrium curve; where it meets the curve is (Xn, Yn).

For a fixed total solvent and constant K′, equal portions leave the least solute: the product of the (1 + εn) terms is largest when the portions are equal. More stages always help, but with diminishing return. With a total solvent ST split into very many portions, Xn/X0 approaches e−K′ST/B, a limit that no number of cross-current stages can pass.

Stage efficiency

A real stage that is mixed for too short a time does not reach equilibrium. The stage efficiency η is the actual fall in raffinate ratio divided by the fall in an ideal stage: η = (Xn−1 − Xn)/(Xn−1 − Xn*), where Xn* = Xn−1/(1 + ε) is the ideal value. Rearranged, each real stage leaves Xn = Xn−1[1 − η ε/(1 + ε)].

The overall efficiency is the number of ideal stages divided by the number of real stages needed for the same duty. Keep the two apart: a stage efficiency of 0.7 does not mean that 3 real stages equal 2.1 ideal ones; the worked example shows how to count them.

Worked example

Question. 500 kg of a solution containing 125 kg solute and 375 kg carrier is extracted in three ideal cross-current stages, each with 200 kg of pure solvent. Carrier and solvent are immiscible and Y = 2.0X (both kg solute per kg solute-free liquid). Find the solute left and the recovery. Compare with putting all 600 kg of solvent into one stage.

  1. Basis: B = 375 kg, X0 = 125/375 = 0.3333 kg/kg.
  2. Extraction factor per stage: ε = K′S/B = 2.0 × 200/375 = 1.0667; 1 + ε = 2.0667.
  3. Stage by stage: X1 = 0.3333/2.0667 = 0.1613; X2 = 0.1613/2.0667 = 0.0780; X3 = 0.0780/2.0667 = 0.03776 kg/kg. Equivalently X3 = 0.3333/2.0667³ = 0.3333/8.827.
  4. Solute left = 0.03776 × 375 = 14.16 kg; recovered = 125 − 14.16 = 110.84 kg, that is 88.7 %.
  5. Extract loadings: Y1 = 2 × 0.1613 = 0.3226, Y2 = 0.1560, Y3 = 0.0755 kg/kg, so the three extracts carry 64.5, 31.2 and 15.1 kg (sum 110.8 kg).
  6. One stage with 600 kg: ε = 2.0 × 600/375 = 3.2, X1 = 0.3333/4.2 = 0.0794; solute left 29.76 kg, recovery 76.2 %.

Answer. Three stages of 200 kg leave 14.16 kg (88.7 % recovered); one stage of 600 kg leaves 29.76 kg (76.2 %). The extracts get leaner stage by stage, from 0.323 to 0.076 kg/kg.

Worked example 2

Question. The same stages run at a stage efficiency η = 0.7. Find the recovery in three real stages and the number of real stages needed to match the 14.16 kg of the three ideal stages.

  1. Fraction removed by one ideal stage: ε/(1 + ε) = 1.0667/2.0667 = 0.5161.
  2. One real stage keeps 1 − 0.7 × 0.5161 = 0.6387 of the solute entering it.
  3. Three real stages: 0.6387³ = 0.2606, so 0.2606 × 125 = 32.6 kg is left and the recovery is 73.9 %.
  4. Target: 14.16/125 = 0.1133 left. Real stages n = ln 0.1133/ln 0.6387 = (−2.178)/(−0.4483) = 4.86, so 5 real stages are needed.

Answer. Three real stages recover 73.9 %; 5 real stages are needed to do the work of 3 ideal ones (overall efficiency 3/5 = 0.6).

Exam traps

  • Recovery after n stages is 1 − 1/(1 + ε)n, not n times the one-stage recovery; three stages of 51.6 % each give 88.7 %, not 155 %.
  • B in ε = K′S/B is the carrier alone (375 kg), not the feed (500 kg).
  • K′ on ratios and K on mass fractions are different numbers; use the one the question gives, or convert with X = x/(1 − x).
  • Unequal portions (400, 100, 100 kg) give 1 + ε of 3.133, 1.533, 1.533 with product 7.37 against 8.83 for equal portions, so they leave more solute: 17.0 kg against 14.2 kg.
  • Stage efficiency 0.7 does not turn 3 real stages into 2.1 ideal ones; count the stages from the real-stage factor.

5.3 Multistage counter-current extraction; right-angled triangle method

In counter-current extraction the feed enters stage 1 and the fresh solvent enters the last stage N, so the two streams cross the cascade in opposite directions. The extract leaves from stage 1, where it last meets the richest feed, so it leaves loaded close to equilibrium with the feed; the raffinate leaves from stage N, where it last meets clean solvent. The same recovery needs much less solvent than cross-current flow. Stages are counted with a difference point on a right-angled triangular diagram, or with the Kremser equation for immiscible liquids; only a few universities teach the construction in detail.

You need Single-stage and multistage cross-current extraction (ε = K′S/B, ratios X and Y).

Balances and the difference point

Feed F enters stage 1, solvent S enters stage N; extract E1 leaves stage 1 and raffinate RN leaves stage N. The overall balance F + S = E1 + RN rearranges to F − E1 = RN − S = Δ. The same difference holds between any pair of passing streams: Rn − En+1 = Δ. So Δ is a fixed (fictitious) point, and every line joining a raffinate Rn to the next extract En+1 passes through it. Δ usually lies outside the triangle because one of its component flows is negative.

The right-angled triangular diagram plots the mass fraction of solute A upward and that of solvent S to the right, with pure B at the origin. It is the equilateral triangle sheared to a right angle, so lines, the lever rule and Δ work as before on ordinary graph paper.

Stepping off stages

  1. Plot the binodal curve, tie lines, F, S and the required RN.
  2. Locate M on FS with FM/MS = S/F; E1 lies on the extract branch on the line RNM extended.
  3. Locate Δ where lines E1F and SRN, extended, intersect.
  4. From E1 follow its tie line to R1; draw ΔR1 and extend it to the extract branch at E2; follow the tie line to R2; repeat.
  5. Count tie lines until a raffinate reaches or passes RN: that count is the number of ideal stages.

Less solvent moves Δ and makes the steps smaller. The minimum solvent is the rate at which a line through Δ coincides with a tie line (usually the tie line through F): the stages become infinite there. For immiscible liquids with Y = K′X the pinch is at the feed end, Y1 = K′X0, so Smin = B(X0 − XN)/(K′X0) for pure solvent.

Immiscible liquids: Kremser form

XN/X0 = (ε − 1)/(εN+1 − 1),  ε = K′S/B ≠ 1;   XN/X0 = 1/(N + 1) when ε = 1

Valid for pure entering solvent (YN+1 = 0), constant K′ and immiscible B and S. X0, XN are kg A per kg B in feed and final raffinate; N is the number of ideal stages. Then Y1 = (B/S)(X0 − XN). If the solvent enters loaded at YN+1, replace XN and X0 by XN − YN+1/K′ and X0 − YN+1/K′.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg/h of feed, 300 kg/h solute A in 700 kg/h carrier B, is extracted counter-currently in three ideal stages with 500 kg/h of pure solvent. B and S are immiscible and Y = 1.5X. Find the raffinate, the extract, the recovery, the difference point and the minimum solvent. Compare with the same 500 kg/h split over three cross-current stages.

  1. X0 = 300/700 = 0.4286; ε = 1.5 × 500/700 = 1.0714; ε⁴ = 1.3178.
  2. X3 = 0.4286 × (0.0714/0.3178) = 0.0963 kg/kg; solute left 700 × 0.0963 = 67.4 kg/h; recovery (300 − 67.4)/300 = 77.5 %.
  3. Y1 = (700/500)(0.4286 − 0.0963) = 0.4652 kg/kg, so E1 carries 232.6 kg/h of A with 500 kg/h of S (732.6 kg/h).
  4. Stage check: X1 = Y1/1.5 = 0.3101; Y2 = 1.4(0.3101 − 0.0963) = 0.2993; X2 = 0.1995; Y3 = 0.1445; X3 = 0.0963. Three steps land exactly on the target.
  5. Δ = F − E1: 267.4 kg/h with A = 67.4, S = −500, B = 700 kg/h, so on the right-angled diagram Δ is at xS = −500/267.4 = −1.87, xA = 67.4/267.4 = 0.252, far to the left of the triangle.
  6. Smin = 700 × (0.4286 − 0.0963)/(1.5 × 0.4286) = 362 kg/h; the design uses 500/362 = 1.38 times the minimum.
  7. Cross-current, 166.7 kg/h per stage: ε = 0.357, X3/X0 = 1/1.357³ = 0.400, recovery 60.0 %. Reaching 77.5 % in three cross-current stages needs 301 kg/h per stage, 903 kg/h in all.

Answer. Raffinate 767.4 kg/h at X = 0.0963; extract 732.6 kg/h at Y = 0.4652; recovery 77.5 %; Δ at (−1.87, 0.252); Smin = 362 kg/h. Cross-current with the same solvent recovers 60.0 % and needs 903 kg/h to match.

Exam traps

  • Counter-current does not give every stage a larger driving force: stage 1 meets solvent already at Y2. Its gain is that the extract leaves near equilibrium with the richest liquid, so each kilogram of solvent carries more solute.
  • The Kremser form with (ε − 1)/(εN+1 − 1) assumes pure entering solvent; for recycled solvent shift X by YN+1/K′.
  • Δ is not a real stream and often lies outside the triangle; lines RnEn+1 pass through it, tie lines do not.
  • Minimum solvent gives infinite stages, not zero stages.

5.4 Extraction equipment: mixer-settlers and extraction columns

Every liquid–liquid contactor does two jobs: it disperses one liquid as drops in the other to make interfacial area for mass transfer, and it then lets the drops coalesce so the layers can be separated. Stage-wise equipment (mixer-settlers) does the two jobs in separate vessels, one stage per unit. Differential contactors (spray, packed and mechanically agitated columns) do both continuously in one tall vessel with the liquids in counter-current flow, and are sized by transfer units rather than stages. The choice depends on the number of stages, throughput, floor space, settling behaviour and how much solvent the plant can afford to hold.

You need Multistage counter-current extraction (extraction factor, ideal stages).

Mixer-settlers

A mixer-settler stage is a stirred tank followed by a settling tank. The mixer is sized by residence time t = V/Q (V mixer volume, Q total flow of both liquids); a few minutes usually brings the stage close to equilibrium, giving stage efficiencies of about 0.8 to 1. The settler is a horizontal tank in which the drops must rise or fall across the layer while the liquid flows to the far end. Like a clarifier, its plan area must be large enough that the upward or downward speed of the continuous liquid is smaller than the settling velocity of the drops: A ≥ Qc/ut.

ut = Δρ g d²/(18 μc)

Stokes settling velocity of a small drop (Reynolds number below about 1): Δρ is the density difference (kg/m³), d the drop diameter (m), μc the viscosity of the continuous liquid (Pa·s), g = 9.81 m/s².

Mixer-settlers give reliable, easily scaled stages, tolerate solids and high flows, and a battery can be extended stage by stage. Their costs are floor space, a large inventory of (often flammable) solvent, and pumping or gravity flow between stages.

Column contactors: spray, packed and agitated

  • Spray column: an empty shell with a distributor that sprays the dispersed liquid as drops through the continuous liquid. Cheap and hard to foul, but the continuous phase back-mixes badly, so one spray column rarely gives more than one or two stages.
  • Packed column: random or structured packing breaks and re-forms the drops and limits back-mixing, giving several stages. The packing should be wetted by the continuous phase, so the dispersed liquid stays as drops instead of a film. Capacity is limited by flooding.
  • Sieve-tray column: the dispersed liquid coalesces under (or over) each perforated tray and is re-dispersed through the holes, so each tray acts roughly as a stage.
  • Mechanically agitated columns: the rotating-disc contactor (RDC) has discs on a central shaft between stator rings; the Scheibel and Oldshue–Rushton columns have impellers between calming zones; the Karr column moves a stack of perforated plates up and down. Agitation makes smaller drops and more area, so the height per stage is much less.

Flooding is the condition where the dispersed drops can no longer pass through the continuous phase and one liquid is carried out with the other. Columns are designed at about 50 to 70 % of the flooding throughput, which fixes the cross-sectional area: A = (Qc + Qd)/(allowable total superficial velocity).

Z = HOR NOR;   NOR = ln[ ((x1 − y2/m)/(x2 − y2/m))(1 − 1/ε) + 1/ε ] / (1 − 1/ε)

Height of a column contactor for dilute solutions with a straight equilibrium line y = mx. x1 and x2 are the solute concentrations of the raffinate phase entering and leaving; y2 is that of the entering solvent; ε = mS/R is the extraction factor on the flow basis used for m; HOR is the overall height of a transfer unit based on the raffinate phase (m), from pilot data or correlations.

Packed extraction with reflux, and choosing a contactor

With a simple counter-current column the extract can be no richer than equilibrium with the feed. When the solvent is selective but dissolves some carrier, part of the solvent-free extract product is returned to the extract end of the column as extract reflux. Above the feed point the reflux strips carrier out of the extract, as the rectifying section of a distillation column enriches the vapour. The feed then enters part way up, the column has an enriching and a stripping section, and the extract product can be much purer than with plain counter-current flow.

5.5 Leaching: principles, equipment and stage calculations

Leaching (solid–liquid extraction) dissolves a soluble solute out of a solid with a liquid solvent: oil from soybean flakes with hexane, sugar from beet with hot water, copper or gold from ore with acid or cyanide. The solvent wets the solid, dissolves the solute and carries it out by diffusion. When the solid is separated afterwards it is never dry: it holds back some solution, and that retained solution is where most of the lost solute goes. So leaching balances are written on the insoluble (inert) solid and on the solution it carries.

The leaching step and solid–liquid equilibrium

Leaching has three steps in series: the solvent enters the pores and the solute dissolves; the solution diffuses out through the pores to the surface; it then crosses the liquid film into the bulk liquid. Fine grinding, flakes or cossettes (thin slices) shorten the pore path; higher temperature raises solubility and diffusivity; agitation thins the film. Over-fine solids, though, pack and drain badly.

After a mixture of solid and solvent is left long enough, it separates into an overflow, clear solution free of inert solid, and an underflow, the inert solid plus the solution it retains. In an ideal stage all the solute has dissolved (the solution is not saturated) and the retained solution has the same composition as the overflow. This is the solid–liquid equilibrium of leaching: it is set by the mechanical holding of solution, not by a distribution coefficient.

Equilibrium is drawn on a diagram of N, kg inert per kg of solution, against y, the mass fraction of solute in the solution (solid-free basis). The overflow lies on N = 0. The underflow lies on the underflow line N = 1/r, where r is the kg of solution retained per kg inert; when r changes with concentration, the line curves. Ideal-stage tie lines are vertical (same y in both streams); a sloping tie line means the stage did not reach equilibrium or the solid adsorbs solute.

One stage, repeated stages and efficiency

L = rI;  M = AF + WF + S;  y = (AF + AS)/M;  V = M − L

I is kg inert solid; r is kg solution retained per kg inert; L is kg of solution in the underflow; AF is kg solute in the feed and WF kg solvent (liquid) already in it; S is kg of fresh solvent stream and AS the solute it brings (0 if pure); M is the total solution; y its solute mass fraction, valid while all solute dissolves and the solution is below saturation; V is kg of overflow.

Solute in the overflow is Vy and in the underflow Ly. Recovery of the solid's solute is (Vy − AS)/AF. If the solvent is too little to dissolve all the solute, y is the saturation value and the undissolved solute leaves with the underflow as solid.

Multistage cross-current leaching: the underflow from stage 1 (I kg inert with L kg solution at y1) is mixed with a fresh portion of solvent in stage 2, and so on. With constant r and pure solvent portions W, each later stage keeps yn = yn−1L/(L + W). Every stage needs fresh solvent and every overflow is weaker, so the combined product is dilute; counter-current washing, in the last chapter of this unit, is the usual plant route.

Stage efficiency: a real stage dissolves less solute than an ideal one when the contact time is short or the particles are thick. It is defined on the solute leached: η = (solute actually leached)/(solute leached in an ideal stage). The overall efficiency, ideal stages divided by real stages, is used to turn a stage count into equipment.

Batch and continuous leaching equipment

  • Agitated vessels: a stirred tank for fine solids; the slurry is then filtered or settled. The Pachuca tank, a tall cone-bottomed tank stirred by compressed air rising in a central tube, handles fine ore slurries with no moving parts in the liquid.
  • Fixed-bed (percolation) extractors for coarse solids: solvent percolates through a stationary bed of coffee, tea, herbs or sugar-beet cossettes; batteries of these form the Shanks system.
  • Thickeners: a Dorr thickener is a wide shallow tank with a slow rake; clear overflow leaves at the rim and thick underflow is drawn from the centre. A row of thickeners with solids going one way and wash liquor the other is continuous counter-current decantation (CCD), used for ores and alumina.
  • Moving-bed extractors for flakes and seeds: the Bollman extractor carries perforated baskets on a bucket elevator, with solvent sprayed on them; the Rotocel turns a ring of baskets under solvent sprays; the Kennedy extractor pushes solids through a row of tubs with paddles while solvent flows the other way; screw-conveyor extractors (the Hildebrandt type) move the solid up a U-shaped screw against the solvent.

Question. 1000 kg of seeds holding 20 mass% oil and 80 mass% inert meal is leached in one ideal stage with 500 kg of pure solvent. The underflow retains 0.5 kg of solution per kg of inert. Find the oil in the overflow and the recovery.

Exam traps

  • Write the balances on the inert solid, not on the total solid: the solid loses mass as the solute dissolves, the inert does not.
  • The underflow is wet: the overflow is M − rI, not the whole solvent charged (300 kg, not 500 kg, in the first example).
  • r must be kg solution per kg inert; if the data give kg solution per kg of underflow or kg solvent per kg inert, convert first.
  • The ideal-stage formula y = AF/M assumes all the solute dissolves; if y comes out above the solubility, the solution is saturated and some solute stays undissolved.
  • Recovery counts only solute that came from the solid; solute brought in with an impure solvent is subtracted.

5.6 Pulsed, centrifugal and supercritical extraction

Gravity is a weak force for separating two liquids whose densities differ by only 100 or 200 kg/m³, so plain columns are tall and mixer-settlers are large. Three ways round this are taught together. A pulsed column adds an oscillating motion to the liquid so the drops become small without moving internals. A centrifugal extractor replaces g by a centrifugal field thousands of times stronger, so contact and separation take seconds. Supercritical extraction replaces the liquid solvent by a fluid such as carbon dioxide above its critical point, whose solvent power is set by pressure and which leaves no residue. The stage and leaching balances of this unit still apply to all three.

You need Extraction equipment (Stokes settling, flooding, hold-up).

Pulsed columns

A pulsed column is a sieve-plate or packed column whose liquid contents are moved up and down by a piston, bellows or compressed-air pulser connected to the bottom. The plates do not move; the liquid is forced through the holes in both directions, which shears the drops small and renews their surfaces. The operating variable is the pulse intensity, the product A·f of the stroke A (the total, peak-to-peak displacement of the liquid in the column) and the frequency f. Over one cycle the liquid moves A up and A down, so its mean speed is 2Af.

At low intensity the drops are large and the column behaves like an unpulsed sieve-tray column (the mixer-settler regime, drops coalescing under each plate). As intensity rises the drops become fine and uniform (the dispersion regime), hold-up and area rise and the height of a transfer unit falls. Too much intensity makes an emulsion, and the column floods at a lower throughput. There are no seals or bearings inside the column, so pulsed columns are used where the liquid is radioactive or corrosive, as in nuclear fuel reprocessing with tributyl phosphate.

Centrifugal extractors

uc = Δρ d² ω² r/(18 μc);   uc/ut = ω²r/g

Stokes settling in a centrifugal field: ω is the angular speed (rad/s, = 2πN/60 for N rpm), r the radius of the drop's path (m); the other symbols are as in gravity settling. ω²r/g is the number of g of the field.

The Podbielniak extractor is a horizontal drum spinning at a few thousand rpm, containing a long spiral (or concentric perforated cylinders). The heavy liquid enters near the shaft and is thrown outward; the light liquid enters at the rim and is forced inward; they pass counter-currently through the perforations, giving several stages in one machine. Annular centrifugal contactors mix the liquids in the gap between a spinning rotor and the casing and separate them inside the rotor, one stage per unit. Residence times are seconds and hold-up is very small, which suits penicillin (unstable at the low pH of extraction), other antibiotics and radioactive liquors. The costs are a high price, seals and maintenance. The centrifuge changes the speed of separation, not the equilibrium: stages are still counted with tie lines or the Kremser equation.

Supercritical fluid extraction

Above its critical temperature and pressure a substance is a supercritical fluid: no liquid–vapour boundary exists, the density can be liquid-like while the viscosity and diffusivity stay gas-like. Carbon dioxide is the usual choice: critical point 31.0 °C and 73.8 bar, non-toxic, non-flammable, cheap and easy to remove from the product. Near the critical point a small rise in pressure gives a large rise in density, and solvent power rises with density. At 40 °C and 200 bar CO2 is about 0.84 g/cm³.

  1. Compressed CO2 is heated to the extraction temperature and passed through a vessel packed with the ground solid (coffee beans, hops, spices).
  2. The solute dissolves; the loaded fluid leaves the extractor.
  3. A pressure-reduction valve drops the pressure in a separator; the density and solvent power fall and the extract drops out.
  4. The lean CO2 is recompressed (or condensed and pumped) and recycled.

Pure CO2 is a weak, non-polar solvent: it dissolves oils, essential oils and other low-polarity compounds well. Caffeine is moderately polar, so decaffeination uses CO2 at high density, with moist beans (water helps release the caffeine), and a polar co-solvent such as a few per cent of ethanol is added when more polar solutes are wanted. The solid feed is a leaching problem, so its balances are those of leaching; only the solvent is unusual. Against conventional solvents it gives solvent-free products and low temperatures for flavours, at the price of high-pressure equipment.

5.7 Leaching by percolation, moving bed and the Shanks system; multistage stage calculations

Coarse solids that let liquid drain through them are leached without stirring: solvent percolates through a stationary bed, or the solid is carried slowly against the solvent in a moving bed, or several stationary beds are connected so that the liquor passes through them in turn and the positions are switched as each bed is exhausted, which is the Shanks system. All three aim at counter-current contact, because the leanest solid then meets the cleanest solvent and the product liquor leaves strong. Their stage calculations use the inert-solid basis of leaching, with the first stage treated separately when the feed brings dry solute.

You need Leaching: principles, equipment and stage calculations (inert basis, retained solution L = rI, ideal stage).

Percolation through stationary beds

In percolation the crushed or sliced solid is loaded into a tank with a perforated false bottom. Solvent is sprayed or flooded on top, trickles or flows down through the voids and is drawn off below. Solute near the particle surface comes out first, so the first runnings are strong; later runnings are weak because the remaining solute must diffuse from deep in the particles. A bed can be run as repeated flood–soak–drain cycles, each cycle acting roughly as a stage, or with continuous downward flow. The particles must be coarse and uniform enough for even flow; fines cause channelling and slow drainage. Coffee extraction, tea, tanning materials and the old sugar-beet diffusion batteries are percolation processes.

Moving beds and the Shanks system

In a moving-bed extractor the solid is moved through the machine while the solvent moves the other way: baskets on a chain in the Bollman extractor, a rotating ring of cells in the Rotocel, screws in tower and U-tube extractors. The solid leaves where the fresh solvent enters, and the strong liquor (the miscella, in oil extraction) leaves where the fresh solid enters.

The Shanks system gets counter-current contact without moving the solid. A battery of percolation tanks is connected by pipes and valves. Fresh solvent is fed to the tank with the most nearly exhausted solid; the liquor passes in series through the tanks holding progressively fresher solid and leaves as strong liquor from the tank just charged with fresh solid. When the oldest tank is spent it is cut out, emptied and refilled with fresh solid, and the valves are changed so that this refilled tank becomes the last one the liquor passes through. Fresh solvent then goes to the tank that was second oldest. The inlet and outlet positions advance one tank per cycle round the battery, so over time the solid appears to move against the liquor. The same principle is used in continuous counter-current decantation with thickeners.

Multistage counter-current leaching calculation

Number the stages from the solid feed end: stage 1 takes the fresh solid and delivers the strong overflow V1; stage N takes the fresh solvent W and delivers the spent underflow. Assume ideal stages and constant underflow: every underflow carries L = rB kg of solution with B kg/h of inert. For a dry feed carrying A kg/h of solute, an overall solution balance gives V1 = W + A − L. In stages 2 to N the overflow rate is constant and equal to W, so in that section the washing factor is E = W/L.

yN/y1 = (E − 1)/(EN − 1),  E = W/L ≠ 1;    A = V1y1 + L yN

y1 is the solute fraction of the strong overflow (and of the underflow solution leaving stage 1); yN that of the solution in the spent underflow; the first relation is the Kremser equation for the N − 1 washing stages 2 to N with pure solvent; the second is the overall solute balance. Solve the two together for y1 and yN. If E = 1, yN/y1 = 1/N.

For multistage cross-current washing, each stage gets its own fresh solvent Wn and yn = yn−1L/(L + Wn). Counter-current uses the same solvent in every washing stage, so for the same total solvent it loses far less solute and gives a stronger product. When r changes with concentration, the stages are stepped on the N–y diagram with a difference point, exactly as on the extraction triangle. Real stages are fewer than ideal ones: real stages = ideal stages/overall stage efficiency.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg/h of solid holding 25 mass% soluble solute and 75 mass% inert is leached in a three-stage ideal counter-current battery with 600 kg/h of fresh water. Every underflow retains 0.6 kg of solution per kg of inert. Find the strong-liquor flow and strength, the solute lost and the recovery. How many ideal stages give at least 95 % recovery?

  1. Inert B = 750 kg/h; solute A = 250 kg/h; retained solution L = 0.6 × 750 = 450 kg/h.
  2. Strong overflow V1 = W + A − L = 600 + 250 − 450 = 400 kg/h. In stages 2 and 3 the overflow is 600 kg/h, so E = 600/450 = 1.333.
  3. Washing section (N − 1 = 2 stages): y3/y1 = (1.333 − 1)/(1.333³ − 1) = 0.3333/1.370 = 0.2432.
  4. Solute balance: 250 = 400 y1 + 450 × 0.2432 y1 = 509.5 y1, so y1 = 0.4907 and y3 = 0.1194.
  5. Stage-by-stage check: y2 follows from stage 3, 450 y2 = (450 + 600) y3, so y2 = 0.2785; stage 1: 250 + 600 × 0.2785 = (400 + 450) × 0.4907 gives 417.1 = 417.1.
  6. Solute lost = L y3 = 450 × 0.1194 = 53.7 kg/h; strong liquor carries 400 × 0.4907 = 196.3 kg/h; recovery 196.3/250 = 78.5 %.
  7. Repeating step 3 and 4 for more stages: N = 4 gives 85.2 %, N = 6 gives 92.5 %, N = 7 gives 94.5 %, N = 8 gives 96.0 %. So 8 ideal stages are needed for 95 %.

Answer. Strong liquor 400 kg/h at 49.1 % solute; 53.7 kg/h lost; recovery 78.5 %. Eight ideal stages reach 95 % (96.0 %).

Exam traps

  • The overflow from stage 1 (400 kg/h) is not the overflow in the washing stages (600 kg/h): the dry feed's solute dissolves only in stage 1. Using E = 400/450 for the whole battery gives 70.4 % instead of 78.5 %.
  • Stage 1 is not a washing stage; the Kremser relation covers the N − 1 stages from 2 to N.
  • In the Shanks system the solid does not move; the inlet and outlet of the liquor are switched round the battery.
  • Strong liquor leaves from the tank (or stage) holding the freshest solid, not from the one receiving fresh solvent.
  • Solids and liquor flowing the same way (co-current) give little more than one ideal stage however many tanks are used, because the two streams leave together at the same composition.

Takeaways

  1. Extraction pays when distillation is poor; the solvent needs selectivity, a useful K, low mutual solubility with the carrier and easy recovery.
  2. Every stage calculation is a mass balance: on the triangle the lever rule draws it, on solute-free ratios it becomes algebra.
  3. Cross-current stages each remove the fraction ε/(1 + ε) of what is left, so Xn = X0/(1 + ε)n, with a limit e−K′ST/B however many stages.
  4. Counter-current flow gets more recovery from the same solvent because the extract leaves near equilibrium with the richest feed; stages come from the difference point or the Kremser equation.
  5. A contactor must disperse and then separate; settler area follows the continuous-phase flow and the drop settling velocity, column height follows transfer units.
  6. Leaching balances are written on the inert solid; the retained solution L = rI carries most of the lost solute.
  7. In counter-current leaching of a dry feed, stage 1 has a smaller overflow than the washing stages; treat it separately and apply the Kremser equation to stages 2 to N.
  8. Pulsing, centrifuging and supercritical solvents change how fast phases meet and part, or how strong the solvent is; the equilibrium balances do not change.

Understand Explain the triangular diagram for a type I ternary liquid system: how a composition is read, the binodal curve, tie lines, the plait point and the lever rule. Show how the amounts of extract and raffinate are found for one equilibrium stage. 10 marks

Points to cover. Corners, sides, reading a point; Binodal curve, one- and two-phase regions; Tie lines and plait point; type I and II; Mixing on FS, lever rule; E and R by the lever rule and by balances.

Model answer.

  1. Coordinates. In an equilateral triangle each corner is a pure component: solute A at the top, carrier B at the bottom left, solvent S at the bottom right. A point on a side is a binary mixture. For an inside point, the mass fraction of A is its perpendicular distance from side BS divided by the height; likewise for B (from side AS) and S (from side AB). The three fractions add to 1, so in practice they are read off grid lines parallel to the sides. A right-angled version (xA up, xS across) works the same way.
  2. Binodal curve. For a type I system B and S are partly miscible while A mixes with both. The binodal (solubility) curve starts near B on side BS, rises to a maximum and returns near S. Outside it the mixture is one liquid; inside it splits into two layers, one on the raffinate branch near B and one on the extract branch near S.
  3. Tie lines and plait point. A tie line joins two layers in equilibrium; it is measured experimentally and normally slopes because A favours one layer. Tie lines shorten as A increases and vanish at the plait point, where both layers have the same composition. A type II system (two partly miscible pairs, A–S and B–S) has a two-phase band across the diagram and no plait point.
  4. Mixing. When F kg of feed and S kg of solvent are mixed, the mixture point M lies on the straight line FS with FM/MS = S/F; its composition is the weighted average, xA,M = (F xA,F + S yA,S)/(F + S).
  5. Splitting. If M lies inside the binodal curve, the tie line through it (interpolated between measured tie lines) gives the extract E and raffinate R at its ends. The lever rule gives the amounts: E/M = RM/RE and R/M = ME/RE, where the lengths are measured on the tie line.
  6. Numbers. 100 kg of 30 % acetic acid in water mixed with 120 kg of solvent: M = 220 kg at xA = 0.136, xS = 0.545. With the tie line R (xA = 0.100, xS = 0.030) to E (yA = 0.1543, yS = 0.800):

Remember List the characteristics of a good solvent for liquid–liquid extraction and explain why each matters. 8 marks

Points to cover. Selectivity with its definition; Distribution coefficient; Insolubility in the carrier; Recoverability; Density difference and interfacial tension; Chemical and safety properties, cost.

Model answer.

  1. Selectivity β = (yA/yB)/(xA/xB), the separation factor between solute and carrier. It must be well above 1; at β = 1 the extract has the same solute-to-carrier ratio as the raffinate and no separation results. For the acid–water example in this unit β = 29.4.
  2. Distribution coefficient K = yA/xA. A large K means each kilogram of solvent carries more solute, so the solvent flow, the equipment and the solvent-recovery load are smaller. K need not exceed 1 if the solvent is cheap and easily recovered, but small K means large flows.
  3. Low mutual solubility with the carrier. Solvent dissolved in the raffinate is lost (or must be stripped), and carrier dissolved in the extract dilutes the product and must be separated later.
  4. Recoverability. The solvent is nearly always recovered by distillation from the extract, so its boiling point should be well away from that of the solute, it should form no azeotrope with it, and its latent heat should be low.
  5. Density difference between the saturated layers, so the drops settle quickly in a settler or a column; differences of the order of 100 kg/m³ or more are comfortable.
  6. Interfacial tension in the right range: high enough that drops coalesce and the layers separate cleanly, low enough that the liquids can be dispersed without great power. Very low tension gives emulsions.
  7. Chemical properties: stable, not reacting with the solute or materials of construction, non-corrosive.
  8. Safety and cost: low toxicity, low flammability (a high flash point), low viscosity for easy pumping and fast mass transfer, low freezing point, cheap and available in quantity. In practice no solvent is best on every count, so the choice is a compromise; solvent-selection guides rank candidates on these properties.
  9. How the properties show on the triangle. Selectivity and K are read from the tie lines: steep tie lines rising towards the solvent side mean a large K, and a raffinate end close to the B corner with an extract end close to the S corner means high selectivity. Low mutual solubility shows as a binodal curve that hugs side BS, leaving a wide two-phase region. A plait point at high solute content allows strong extracts before the layers merge.
  10. Priorities in practice. Selectivity and recoverability usually decide, because a solvent that carries carrier or cannot be cheaply distilled off fails however large its K. K then fixes the flows, density difference and interfacial tension fix the contactor, and safety and cost settle a choice between candidates that pass the first tests. The answer is checked against the example of this unit: K = 1.54 and β = 29.4 mean a moderate solvent flow and a clean separation.
Understand With sketches, describe the mixer-settler, the spray, packed and mechanically agitated extraction columns, and the pulsed and centrifugal extractors. State how each disperses and separates the phases and where each is used. 12 marks

Points to cover. Two jobs of a contactor; Mixer-settler; Spray and packed columns; wetting; flooding; RDC, Scheibel, Karr; Pulsed column; Podbielniak and annular centrifugal; Selection.

Model answer.

  1. Two jobs. Every contactor disperses one liquid as drops in the other to make interfacial area, then lets the drops coalesce so the two layers can leave separately. Stage-wise units do the jobs in separate vessels; differential columns do both continuously with counter-current flow.
  2. Mixer-settler. Sketch a stirred tank discharging into a horizontal settler with a light layer, a dispersion band and a heavy layer, each leaving over a weir. The mixer is sized by residence time (0.5 m³ at 12 m³/h gives 2.5 min) and gives a stage efficiency of 0.8 to 1. The settler area is the continuous-phase flow over the drop settling velocity (8 m³/h at 6.62 m/h needs 1.21 m²). Used where many stages, high flows or solids occur, as in copper and uranium extraction; drawbacks are floor space and solvent inventory.
  3. Spray column. An empty tower; the dispersed liquid is sprayed in at one end and rises (or falls) through the continuous liquid. Cheap and hard to foul, but back-mixing limits it to one or two stages.
  4. Packed column. Rings or structured packing re-form the drops and reduce back-mixing. The packing should be wetted by the continuous phase so that the dispersed phase stays as drops. Capacity is limited by flooding; design at 50–70 % of flooding sets the diameter, and Z = HORNOR sets the height (4.16 transfer units × 0.9 m = 3.74 m in this unit's example).
  5. Mechanically agitated columns. The rotating-disc contactor has discs on a central shaft between stator rings; the Scheibel and Oldshue–Rushton columns have impellers between calming or packed zones; the Karr column reciprocates a stack of perforated plates. Agitation makes small drops, so many stages fit in a short column. Used for many-stage duties with clean liquids.
  6. Pulsed column. A sieve-plate or packed column whose liquid contents are pulsed by a piston or air pulser. Pulse intensity A·f (stroke × frequency) controls drop size: too little gives large drops, too much gives emulsions and early flooding. No moving parts inside, so it is used for radioactive liquors in nuclear fuel reprocessing.
  7. Centrifugal extractors. The Podbielniak extractor spins a drum containing a perforated spiral; the heavy liquid is thrown outward and the light liquid driven inward, counter-currently, in seconds. Annular centrifugal contactors mix in the annulus and separate in the rotor. At 3000 rpm and r = 0.2 m the field is 2012 g. Used where contact time must be short (penicillin) or hold-up small.
  8. Selection. Few stages and dirty liquids: mixer-settlers or spray columns. Many stages and clean liquids: agitated or pulsed columns. Short contact, unstable products or small hold-up: centrifugal extractors. Low density difference or emulsifying systems: centrifugal.
Understand Describe the equipment for batch and continuous leaching, including percolation tanks, the Shanks system, agitated vessels, thickeners and moving-bed extractors. Explain how each brings solid and solvent into contact. 13 marks

Points to cover. Mechanism and particle size; Percolation tanks; Shanks system operation; Agitated vessels and Pachuca; Thickeners and CCD; Bollman, Rotocel, Kennedy, screw; Choice.

Model answer.

  1. Mechanism. The solvent enters the solid, dissolves the solute, and the solution diffuses out through the pores and across a liquid film. Small particles shorten the diffusion path but drain badly; coarse particles drain well but leach slowly. The equipment depends mainly on particle size: coarse solids are leached in beds, fine solids in suspension.
  2. Percolation tanks. A tank with a perforated false bottom holds a bed of coarse solid. Solvent is sprayed or flooded on top and percolates down; the liquor is drawn from below. First runnings are strong, later ones weak. Used for coffee, tea, tanning bark, ores in vats.
  3. Shanks system. A battery of percolation tanks connected by pipes. Fresh solvent enters the tank with the most nearly exhausted solid; the liquor flows through the tanks in order of increasing freshness of solid and leaves as strong liquor from the newest tank. When the oldest tank is spent, it is emptied and refilled with fresh solid and becomes the last tank in the liquor path; the inlet and outlet move one tank round the battery each cycle. The solid never moves, but the flow is counter-current.
  4. Agitated vessels. Fine solids are suspended in the solvent in a stirred tank, batch or in series for continuous work, then separated by settling or filtration. The Pachuca tank is a tall cylinder with a conical base, agitated by air rising in a central draft tube, used for fine ore slurries.
  5. Thickeners and CCD. A Dorr thickener is a shallow tank with a slowly rotating rake; clear overflow leaves at the rim, thickened underflow at the centre of the base. Several thickeners in series with solids going one way and wash liquor the other form continuous counter-current decantation, used for alumina, uranium and gold ores. The underflow still holds solution, which the stage calculation tracks.
  6. Moving-bed extractors for seeds and flakes. Bollman: perforated baskets on a vertical chain; on the way down the solid meets half-miscella sprayed co-currently, on the way up fresh solvent counter-currently. Rotocel: a rotating ring of cells passes under a series of solvent sprays and drains, emptying at one point of each turn. Kennedy: paddles push the solid through a row of tubs against the solvent. Screw extractors (the Hildebrandt type) carry the solid up a U-tube against the solvent.
  7. Choice. Coarse, free-draining solids: percolation, Shanks or moving-bed units. Fine solids: agitated vessels with thickeners or filters. Large throughput of oilseed: Rotocel or similar. Ore slurries: Pachuca tanks and CCD trains. In every case the solid leaves wet, so the retained solution sets the final loss.

Every ideal stage on the triangle is the same three moves: locate the mixture point on the line joining the two streams fed (by the lever rule or by component balances), find the tie line through it (interpolating between measured tie lines with a conjugate line if needed), and split M into E and R by the lever rule, checking with the acid balance. A second cross-current stage repeats the moves starting from R1 instead of F.
  10. Meaning of the results. K = 2.41 means each kilogram of solvent leaves with 2.4 times the acid concentration of the raffinate, so the solvent flow is moderate; β = 37.8 means the extract carries very little water for its acid, so the acid can be recovered from it fairly pure. The 12.4 kg of acid left in R1 is the target of the second stage; with 100 kg more solvent most of it can be recovered, though each later stage recovers less because R becomes leaner.

Apply A packed extraction column must reduce the solute in an aqueous feed from x1 to x2 = x1/20 with pure solvent. Pilot data give HOR = 1.2 m. Find the packed height for ε = mS/R = 1.25 and for ε = 2.0, and comment. The feed then goes to a mixer-settler for a polishing stage: 30 m³/h total flow, a 1.0 m³ mixer, 10 m³/h continuous phase, 200 µm drops, Δρ = 120 kg/m³, μc = 0.8 mPa·s. Find the mixer time and the settler area. 12 marks

Points to cover. NOR formula; ε = 1.25; ε = 2.0; Trade-off; Mixer time; Stokes velocity and Re; Settler area.

Model answer.

  1. Transfer units. For dilute solutions, a straight equilibrium line and pure solvent: NOR = ln[(x1/x2)(1 − 1/ε) + 1/ε]/(1 − 1/ε), and Z = HORNOR.
  2. ε = 1.25. 1/ε = 0.800, 1 − 1/ε = 0.200. NOR = ln(20 × 0.2 + 0.8)/0.2 = ln 4.8/0.2 = 1.5686/0.2 = 7.84. Z = 1.2 × 7.84 = 9.41 m.
  3. ε = 2.0. 1/ε = 0.5. NOR = ln(20 × 0.5 + 0.5)/0.5 = ln 10.5/0.5 = 2.351/0.5 = 4.70. Z = 1.2 × 4.70 = 5.64 m.
  4. Comment. Raising the solvent by 60 % (ε 1.25 → 2.0) cuts the height by 40 %. At ε = 1 the formula becomes NOR = x1/x2 − 1 = 19, so low solvent rates make the column very tall. The optimum balances column cost against the cost of recovering more solvent.
  5. Mixer. t = V/Q = 1.0/30 h = 2.0 min.
  6. Stokes velocity. ut = Δρgd²/(18μc) = 120 × 9.81 × (200 × 10⁻⁶)²/(18 × 0.8 × 10⁻³) = 3.27 × 10⁻³ m/s = 11.8 m/h. Re = 1000 × 3.27 × 10⁻³ × 2 × 10⁻⁴/0.8 × 10⁻³ = 0.82, below 1, so Stokes' law holds.
  7. Settler. A = Qc/ut = 10/11.8 = 0.85 m² minimum; a real settler is made larger to hold the dispersion band.
  8. Check. Units: (m³/h)/(m/h) = m². Doubling the drop size would quarter the area, which is why the mixer must not be over-stirred into fine drops.
  9. Lowest solvent case. At ε = 1 the column would need NOR = 19 transfer units, Z = 1.2 × 19 = 22.8 m, more than twice the height at ε = 1.25; below ε = 1 an infinitely tall column removes at most the fraction ε of the solute, so the 95 % removal (x1/x2 = 20) becomes impossible once ε falls below 0.95.
  10. Column diameter and HOR. The diameter is set separately, from the total flow per unit area at about 50–70 % of flooding. HOR depends on the packing, the flows and which phase is dispersed, so the 1.2 m pilot value holds only at similar velocities; a change of packing or a much higher throughput needs new pilot data.
  11. Settler in practice. Between the clear layers a dispersion band forms, whose thickness grows with the flow per unit area; designers add margin above the Stokes minimum (often doubling it) and fit coalescing plates or mesh where drops are small.

Analyze A feed of 600 kg/h carrier with 150 kg/h solute (Y = 2.0X, immiscible liquids) can be extracted with 450 kg/h of pure solvent in three ideal stages, either cross-current (150 kg/h to each stage) or counter-current. Compare the recovery, the extract concentration and the solvent each needs for the same recovery, and explain the difference. 13 marks

Points to cover. Cross-current result; Counter-current result; Extract strength; Solvent to match; Smin; Reason.

Model answer.

  1. Basis. B = 600 kg/h, X0 = 0.25.
  2. Cross-current. ε per stage = 2 × 150/600 = 0.5. X3 = 0.25/1.5³ = 0.0741; solute left 44.4 kg/h; recovery 70.4 %. The combined extract is 450 kg/h of solvent with 105.6 kg/h, an average Y of 0.235.
  3. Counter-current. ε = 2 × 450/600 = 1.5. X3 = 0.25 × 0.5/(1.5⁴ − 1) = 0.125/4.0625 = 0.0308; solute left 18.5 kg/h; recovery 87.7 %. Y1 = (600/450)(0.25 − 0.0308) = 0.292.
  4. Solvent to match 87.7 % by cross-current. Need (1 + ε)³ = 0.25/0.0308 = 8.125, so 1 + ε = 2.010, ε = 1.010 per stage, S = 1.010 × 600/2 = 303 kg/h per stage: 909 kg/h in all, twice the counter-current solvent, and the extract would be correspondingly weaker.
  5. Minimum for counter-current. Smin = 600 × (0.25 − 0.0308)/(2 × 0.25) = 263 kg/h; the 450 kg/h used is 1.71 Smin.
  6. Why. In counter-current flow the extract leaves stage 1 in contact with the fresh feed, so it can approach equilibrium with the richest liquid (Y1* = 0.50); the solvent is used to carry solute from every stage. In cross-current flow each solvent portion leaves in equilibrium with a progressively leaner raffinate, so the later portions carry little. It is not that every counter-current stage has a larger driving force; stage 1 meets partly loaded solvent.
  7. Verdict. For continuous plants counter-current is chosen: higher recovery and a stronger extract from the same solvent, so less solvent to recover. Cross-current suits small batches or where the extracts are wanted separately.
  8. Check. Cross: 105.6 + 44.4 = 150; counter: 0.292 × 450 = 131.5 and 131.5 + 18.5 = 150 kg/h.
  9. Counter-current stage check (B/S = 600/450 = 1.333, Xn = Yn/2, Yn+1 = 1.333(Xn − X3)):

Evaluate Evaluate hexane leaching against supercritical CO2 extraction for 1000 kg of oilseed (20 % oil). For hexane take four ideal counter-current stages, 600 kg of hexane and 0.5 kg of retained solution per kg of meal. For CO2 take a leaving-fluid loading of 1.0 mass% oil and a CO2 flow of 2000 kg/h. Compare recovery, solvent use, product quality, safety and cost. 13 marks

Points to cover. Hexane battery calculation; CO2 requirement; Recovery and solvent per kg oil; Quality and residue; Safety and energy; Verdict.

Model answer.

  1. Hexane basis. Meal B = 800 kg, oil A = 200 kg, L = 400 kg. V1 = 600 + 200 − 400 = 400 kg; washing factor E = 600/400 = 1.5.
  2. Hexane result. y4/y1 = 0.5/(1.5⁴ − 1) = 0.5/4.0625 = 0.1231. 200 = 400y1 + 400 × 0.1231y1, y1 = 0.445, y4 = 0.0548. Oil lost 400 × 0.0548 = 21.9 kg; recovery 89.0 %. The miscella (400 kg, 44.5 % oil) is distilled and the wet meal is desolventised with steam.
  3. CO2 requirement. 200 kg of oil at 1.0 % in the leaving fluid needs 200/0.010 = 20 000 kg of fluid, i.e. 19 800 kg of CO2, 99 kg per kg of oil, about 9.9 h at 2000 kg/h. This is a minimum: towards the end the fluid leaves unsaturated, so real runs use more.
  4. Recovery. CO2 can approach complete removal because it has no retained-solution loss at the end (the CO2 simply vents from the meal), while the hexane battery loses the oil in the 400 kg of retained solution; more stages would raise hexane recovery (95 % needs more stages, as in the battery questions of this bank).
  5. Quality. CO2 leaves no solvent residue and works at 40–60 °C, which suits flavours, spices and nutraceuticals; hexane products need desolventising and carry trace-residue limits. CO2 is selective (low-polarity components), which can be good or bad depending on the product.
  6. Safety and energy. Hexane is flammable and its vapour must be contained; CO2 is non-flammable and non-toxic but needs vessels at 200–300 bar and energy to recompress about 100 kg of CO2 per kg of oil.
  7. Cost. For bulk edible oil at hundreds of tonnes per day, hexane leaching in moving-bed extractors is far cheaper per tonne. For small, high-value products (hops, spice oleoresins, decaffeination) the CO2 process wins on quality and regulation.
  8. Verdict. Hexane for bulk oilseed; supercritical CO2 for high-value, heat-sensitive or residue-free products.
  9. More hexane stages. With five stages y5/y1 = 0.5/(1.55 − 1) = 0.0758, y1 = 0.465 and 14.1 kg of oil is lost (93.0 %); six stages give 95.4 %. The hexane plant reaches high recovery by adding stages at little extra solvent.
  10. Solvent per kilogram of oil. Hexane: 600 kg per 200 kg oil, 3 kg/kg, recovered by distillation at low temperature. CO2: about 99 kg/kg, recovered by depressurising and recompressing; the larger mass is the main operating cost of the supercritical route, partly offset by needing no desolventising.

Glossary

Binodal curve
Boundary between the one-phase and two-phase regions of a ternary diagram.
Carrier (diluent)
The feed liquid in which the solute is dissolved (B).
Counter-current decantation
A series of thickeners with solids and wash liquor flowing in opposite directions.
Counter-current extraction
Feed and solvent enter at opposite ends of a cascade and flow in opposite directions.
Cross-current extraction
Fresh solvent added to every stage, extracts withdrawn separately.
Difference point
Fixed net flow Δ = F − E1 through which all operating lines of a counter-current cascade pass.
Distribution coefficient
Ratio of solute concentration in extract to that in the raffinate at equilibrium.
Extract
The solvent-rich layer leaving a stage.
Extract reflux
Return of solvent-free extract product to the extract end of a column to purify the extract.
Extraction factor
ε = K′S/B, the solute capacity of the solvent relative to the carrier for one stage.
Flooding
Column condition in which the dispersed phase can no longer pass counter-currently and is carried out with the continuous phase.
Kremser equation
Closed-form stage relation for counter-current cascades with constant flows and a straight equilibrium line.
Leaching
Solid–liquid extraction of a soluble component from a solid with a liquid solvent.
Lever rule
Graphical mass balance: the amounts of two streams are in inverse ratio to their distances from the mixture point.
Miscella
The oil-in-solvent solution from oilseed leaching.
Mixer-settler
Stirred mixer followed by a gravity settler; one stage per unit.
Overflow
Clear solution, free of inert solid, leaving a leaching stage.
Percolation
Leaching by passing solvent through a stationary bed of solid.
Plait point
Point on the binodal curve where the two layers become identical.
Podbielniak extractor
Centrifugal extractor with a perforated spiral in a spinning drum, giving counter-current contact in seconds.
Pulse intensity
Stroke times frequency of the liquid pulsation in a pulsed column.
Raffinate
The carrier-rich layer leaving a stage.
Rotating-disc contactor
Agitated extraction column with discs on a central shaft between stator rings.
Selectivity
Separation factor of solute over carrier between the two layers; must exceed 1.
Shanks system
Battery of stationary leaching tanks in which the liquor inlet and outlet are advanced one tank per cycle to give counter-current contact.
Solute
The component transferred from the feed to the solvent (A).
Stage efficiency
Actual composition change of a real stage divided by that of an ideal stage.
Supercritical fluid
Substance above its critical temperature and pressure, with liquid-like density and gas-like transport properties.
Tie line
Line joining the compositions of two liquid layers in equilibrium.
Underflow
Inert solid together with the solution it retains.
