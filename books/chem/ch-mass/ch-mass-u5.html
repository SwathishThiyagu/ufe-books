5.1 Liquid-liquid extraction and ternary (triangular) diagrams

Liquid–liquid extraction pulls a solute out of a liquid feed with a second liquid solvent. Add solvent, mix, let layers settle; solute divides between them. It works when distillation fails: components boil too close, or solute decomposes at its boiling point. It recovers acetic acid from dilute water, purifies antibiotics, separates metals. A triangular diagram gives equilibrium compositions, so stages can be sized.

You need the equilibrium stage and stage efficiency from Unit 2, and the distribution coefficient.

Solute, carrier and solvent: naming the three players

Extraction moves solute A from liquid feed into added solvent S. Carrier B, also called diluent, is the rest of the feed and stays behind. Mix S with feed; A partly leaves B and enters S. The mixture splits into two layers. Extract is rich in S and A. Raffinate is rich in B, holding some A. Settle, draw off layers, recover A from extract (usually by distillation), return S.

Transfer stops at equilibrium, when the layers hold A in a fixed ratio. Each stage is one mix-and-settle.

  • Selectivity: S dissolves much more A than B. Equal uptake only dilutes feed.
  • Distribution coefficient away from 1, so each kilogram of solvent carries useful load.
  • Density difference of about 50 kg/m³ or more between saturated layers, or droplets settle slowly.
  • Interfacial tension high enough for droplets to coalesce after mixing, low enough to redisperse easily.
  • Low mutual solubility of S and B, so solvent is not lost and extract is not diluted.
  • Low viscosity; boiling point well separated from A; cheap, non-corrosive, low toxicity, non-flammable.

The equilateral triangle: one point, one mixture

Three fractions sum to 1, so two are independent. The equilateral triangle holds them: pure A at top, pure B at bottom left, pure S at bottom right. Some syllabi call it a Gibbs triangle. Every mixture has one point inside.

  1. Mass fraction of A at point P: perpendicular from P to B–S side divided by triangle height.
  2. Mass fraction of B: perpendicular to A–S side.
  3. Mass fraction of S: perpendicular to A–B side.
  4. The three fractions add to 1. Reading along grid lines parallel to the sides gives the same numbers.

The binodal curve, or solubility curve, is the boundary. Outside it near the corners is single-phase: one liquid, no split. Inside is two-phase, splitting into layers at the ends of the tie line through the point. Tie lines slope on the equilateral triangle and on a right-angled ternary plot. Horizontal lines are constant A, not tie lines. Only the y-versus-x distribution plot collapses a tie line to a point. Tie lines shorten to the plait point, where layers become identical.

Type 1: one pair, usually B and S, is partly miscible; common case with a plait point, as in water–acetic acid–ether. Type 2: two pairs are partly miscible; two two-phase regions. Type 3: all three pairs are partly miscible; three two-phase regions and a small three-phase region in the middle.

Distribution coefficient and solute-free mass ratios

At fixed temperature the layers hold A in a fixed ratio. On a mass-fraction basis, K = y/x, where y is mass fraction of A in extract and x is mass fraction of A in raffinate.

K = y / x

K is the distribution coefficient (dimensionless); y is kg A per kg extract; x is kg A per kg raffinate; both layers are at the same temperature and in equilibrium.

K is not constant. It drifts with composition because tie lines are not parallel, and changes with temperature. Use one K only where tie lines are nearly parallel; otherwise step stages on the diagram (see 5.3).

X = x / (1 − x) Y = y / (1 − y)

X is kg A per kg carrier B in the raffinate; Y is kg A per kg solvent S in the extract; x and y are mass fractions of A in raffinate and extract. Valid when raffinate is nearly A + B and extract nearly A + S.

Solute-free ratios help because B stays in raffinate and S in extract. Balances in X and Y give straight operating lines, so many designs plot X against Y (repeated stages: 5.2). Here x = 0.0667 gives X = 0.0714 kg acid per kg water; y = 0.20 gives Y = 0.25 kg acid per kg ether.

Exam traps

  • Tie lines slope on the equilateral triangle and on a right-angled ternary plot. Horizontal lines are constant A, not tie lines. Only the y-versus-x plot collapses each tie line to a point.
  • K uses mass fractions of the whole layer. A solute-free ratio such as Y/X gives a different number; here K = 3.0 but Y/X = 3.5.
  • Extract is solvent-rich and raffinate carrier-rich. Swapping them reverses every balance.
  • Neglecting mutual solubility must be justified. If solvent dissolves carrier, the simple balances break down and the full ternary diagram is needed.
  • The plait point is where phases become identical, not the richest extract. Beyond it there is one phase and no separation.

Analyze A student reads a point in the two-phase region, draws a horizontal line to the other branch of the curve, and calls it the tie line. What is wrong and what error follows? 6 marks

A horizontal line on a ternary plot is constant A, not a tie line. Tie lines slope on both plots. The true tie line joins the two equilibrium compositions on the curve. The horizontal guess gives wrong extract composition, hence wrong y, K and recovery.

Analyze Two solvents give the same K for the solute but differ in how much carrier they dissolve. Which do you choose and why? 6 marks

Choose the one with lower mutual solubility of carrier. Less carrier in extract gives purer extract and cheaper solute–solvent separation; less solvent in raffinate means less make-up and cheaper recovery. Equal K means equal solvent flow, so decide on solubility, density difference and cost.

5.2 Single-stage and multistage cross-current extraction

A single equilibrium stage splits a solute between two liquid phases in a ratio fixed by the tie line. One contact leaves plenty of solute behind, so the raffinate is contacted again with fresh solvent, stage after stage. Each contact cuts the solute by the same factor, giving a geometric fall that one formula sums. Cross-current extraction is used for batch recovery of vegetable oil, antibiotics and metal salts, and it is the simplest cascade to design and run.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for tie lines, the plait point and the distribution coefficient K.

One stage, one split

A single stage is one mixer plus one settler. Feed (carrier plus solute) and solvent are stirred until the liquids touch everywhere, then left to settle into two layers: extract, rich in solvent, and raffinate, rich in carrier.

If the contact lasts long enough, the two leaving liquids are in equilibrium, so their compositions sit at opposite ends of one tie line on the triangular diagram. The total balance is F + S = E + R and the solute balance is F x_F + S y_S = E y_E + R x_R. Here F, S, E, R are mass flows in kg/h; x and y are solute mass fractions in carrier-rich and solvent-rich phases.

When carrier and solvent hardly dissolve in each other, carrier flow R stays constant. Use mass ratios: X = kg solute per kg carrier, Y = kg solute per kg solvent. Equilibrium is Y* = K X, with K in mass-ratio units.

X₁ = X₀ / (1 + K·S/R)

X₀ and X₁ are kg solute per kg carrier in the feed and in the raffinate leaving stage 1; S is kg pure solvent fed to the stage; R is kg carrier in the feed; K is the equilibrium ratio Y*/X.

On the diagram, mark F and S, join them, and place mixture point M on FS so FM/MS = S/F by the lever rule. The tie line through M gives both phase compositions; its raffinate end read as a mass ratio is X₁.

Repeating the contact: the cross-current cascade

Cross-current means feed moves stage by stage while fresh solvent enters every stage. Each stage meets solvent with no solute, so the driving force is renewed.

X_n = X₀ / (1 + E)ⁿ with E = K·S/R

X_n is kg solute per kg carrier in the raffinate leaving stage n; X₀ is the feed ratio; E is the extraction factor (dimensionless); K is Y*/X; S is kg pure solvent per stage; R is kg carrier in the feed; n is the number of stages.

Stage n balance: R·X_{n−1} = R·X_n + S·Y_n with Y_n = K·X_n, so X_n = X_{n−1}/(1 + E). Applying n times gives the formula. E is solute leaving in extract divided by solute staying in raffinate for one stage.

  • Doubling n squares the surviving fraction; the fall is geometric, not linear.
  • A larger K or more solvent per stage raises E and lowers X_n.
  • Recovery is (X₀ − X_n)/X₀, not n times the single-stage recovery.

On the triangle each stage has its own M₁, M₂,… and tie line. Raffinate from one is feed to the next, joined to the fresh solvent point. With partially miscible liquids tie lines swing, so the formula fails and the diagram is stepped.

Splitting the solvent, efficiency, batch washing

With fixed total solvent, the split matters. For constant K, equal portions maximise the product of denominators (1 + E) and minimise X_n. Loading one stage and starving others leaves more solute.

The example uses 3 × 200 = 600 kg. All 600 kg in one stage gives X₁ = 0.3333/(1 + 2×600/375) = 0.0794 kg/kg, that is 29.8 kg left and 76.2% recovered, against 14.2 kg and 88.7% for the split. Three clean contacts beat one with the same total.

Real stages never reach equilibrium. Stage efficiency η is actual composition change divided by ideal-stage change. A common short-cut multiplies the equilibrium constant: K_eff = ηK. This is not the same as efficiency on composition change. With K replaced by η·K, n real stages give X_n = X₀/(1 + ηE)ⁿ, equivalent to n·ln(1 + ηE)/ln(1 + E) ideal stages — about 2.3 when η = 0.7 and E = 1.07. The simpler rule ideal stages = η × real stages is a separate overall stage efficiency.

Batch extraction is the same arithmetic in one vessel: charge feed and solvent, stir, settle, draw off extract, repeat with fresh solvent. Two washes of 100 kg beat one wash of 200 kg by the same reason.

5.3 Multistage counter-current extraction; right-angled triangle method

Counter-current extraction runs solvent opposite to the carrier. Fresh solvent meets the most depleted raffinate; the richest extract meets the fresh feed. The solvent leaves heavily loaded, so each kg does more work. The same recovery needs less solvent and fewer stages. The right-angled triangle method counts those stages on a Y-X plot. It is used in antibiotic recovery, vegetable-oil refining and metal extraction. A few universities teach the construction; others stop at the Kremser equation.

You need: 5.1 for the ternary diagram, 5.2 for mass ratios and cross-current stages, and Unit 2 for the operating line.

Counter-current cascade

Let R be kg carrier/h (solute-free), S kg solvent/h (solute-free), X kg solute/kg carrier and Y kg solute/kg solvent. Feed enters stage 1 at X0; fresh solvent enters stage N at YN+1. Raffinate leaves stage N at XN; extract leaves stage 1 at Y1. Around stage n: R Xn−1 + S Yn+1 = R Xn + S Yn. Overall: R X0 + S YN+1 = R XN + S Y1. The extract leaving stage 1 is richest because it meets fresh feed. The solvent leaves heavily loaded, so each kg does more work. The same recovery needs less solvent and fewer stages than cross-current operation.

R Xn−1 + S Yn+1 = R Xn + S Yn

R is kg carrier/h; S is kg solvent/h; X is kg solute/kg carrier; Y is kg solute/kg solvent; n is stage number.

Operating line and right-angled steps

Plot Y against X. The equilibrium curve is Y=KX; K is the distribution coefficient. The operating line joins (X0,Y1) at the feed end to (XN,YN+1) at the solvent end. Its equation is Y=(R/S)X+[YN+1−(R/S)XN]; slope R/S. For fresh solvent YN+1=0, so it passes through (XN,0).

  1. Draw the equilibrium curve.
  2. Mark (X0,Y1) and (XN,YN+1); draw the operating line.
  3. From (X0,Y1), draw horizontal to equilibrium at constant Y; it meets at X1=Y1/K.
  4. Then draw vertical at constant X1 to the operating line; it meets at Y2.
  5. Repeat. Each horizontal plus vertical pair is one stage. Count until X reaches XN or Y reaches YN+1.

Lower S to raise the slope R/S. At Smin the operating line touches equilibrium at the feed end. This pinch has zero driving force, so required stages become infinite. For fresh solvent and linear equilibrium, Smin=R(X0−XN)/(KX0), with XN the target.

Constant K and cross-current comparison

For constant K, define E=KS/R. For fresh solvent, YN+1=0: XN=X0(E−1)/(E^(N+1)−1) for E≠1, and XN=X0/(N+1) for E=1. For loaded solvent: XN=YN+1/K+(X0−YN+1/K)(E−1)/(E^(N+1)−1). Then Y1=(R/S)(X0−XN)+YN+1.

For fresh solvent (YN+1 = 0): XN = X0(E − 1)/(EN+1 − 1)

XN is kg solute/kg carrier leaving stage N; X0 is feed ratio; E=K S/R; N is stage number; E≠1. For loaded solvent: XN = YN+1/K + (X0 − YN+1/K)(E − 1)/(EN+1 − 1).

Three equal cross-current stages with 500 kg/h give 166.7 kg/h each and 60% recovery (X3/X0=0.40). With 500 kg/h split equally, recovery levels off at about 66% (e^−1.0714 = 0.343 left) however many stages are used; five stages give only 62%. To reach 77.5% in five cross-current stages, each stage needs about 162 kg/h, i.e. about 810 kg/h total, against 500 kg/h counter-current.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the operating-line endpoints: it joins (X0,Y1) at the feed end to (XN,YN+1) at the solvent end. Swapping reverses the cascade and gives a negative slope.
  • Using mass fractions w instead of mass ratios X and Y. Y=KX and the straight operating line hold for solute-free ratios; convert with X=w/(1-w).
  • Forgetting YN+1: recycled solvent is not pure. Put its load in the operating-line intercept and overall balance.
  • Drawing steps in the wrong order. Go horizontal at constant Y to equilibrium, then vertical at constant X to the operating line. Vertical first gives a wrong count.
  • Taking minimum solvent as zero. The minimum is the pinch where the operating line touches equilibrium; there stages become infinite. In E=K S/R, do not invert E.

5.4 Extraction equipment: mixer-settlers and extraction columns

Every liquid extractor does two jobs: mix the feed with solvent to make interfacial area, then let the two liquids settle into layers. A mixer-settler does them in two vessels; a column does them in one tall vessel. The mixer volume sets contact time, the settler area sets separation, and a column's height sets the number of transfer units. These ideas size equipment from a beaker test to a plant treating hundreds of cubic metres per hour, as in recovery of acetic acid or purification of antibiotics.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for phase splitting, 5.3 for stage counts, Unit 2 for stage efficiency, and Unit 3 for HTU, NTU and flooding.

Mixer-settler: mix first, settle second

In a mixer-settler, feed and solvent enter a stirred mixer. The stirrer breaks one phase into drops. The drops give interfacial area for solute transfer. The mixture then flows to a settler, a wide shallow tank. Gravity makes drops rise or fall and coalesce. Two layers leave: extract (solvent-rich) and raffinate (carrier-rich).

The mixer is sized by residence time. A long time gives more transfer but a larger vessel. The settler is sized by the area needed for drops to settle, not by the total flow.

t = V / Q

t is the mean residence time in the mixer (h); V is the mixer volume (m³); Q is the total combined feed plus solvent flow (m³/h).

A = Qd / vs

A is the settler horizontal area (m²); Qd is the dispersed-phase volumetric flow (m³/h); vs is the settling velocity of the drops (m/h).

Stage efficiency E is the fraction of the equilibrium change actually achieved in one real stage. If a stage reaches 80% of equilibrium, E = 0.8. The number of real units is Nreal = Ntheoretical / E. This is not the same as Ntheoretical.

Columns: spray, packed and agitated

A column contacts two liquids counter-currently in one vessel. One phase is dispersed as drops; the other is continuous. The dispersed phase should be the one that wets the packing less, so it forms drops instead of coating the surface. In a spray column, nozzles make drops and there is no packing. In a packed column, packing breaks drops and reduces axial mixing, but it can also trap drops and reduce capacity.

Key variables are drop size d, dispersed-phase hold-up φ (volume of drops per volume of column), and flooding. Smaller drops give more area a but settle slowly, so hold-up rises and the column floods at lower flow. Axial mixing, or back mixing, reduces the concentration driving force.

Z = Hoc × Noc

Z is the packed height (m); Hoc is the overall height of a transfer unit (m); Noc is the number of overall transfer units (dimensionless). Hoc falls as interfacial area per volume and overall mass transfer coefficient rise.

Mechanically agitated contactors add stirrers or plates. A rotating-disc contactor (RDC) uses spinning discs on a shaft; a reciprocating-plate column (RPC) moves perforated plates up and down. Both give smaller drops and higher hold-up, so they need less height. Pulsed units are in 5.6.

Choosing the contactor

A mixer-settler gives one equilibrium stage per mixer plus settler. Columns give many stages in one shell. For a small number of stages and dirty liquids, a mixer-settler is easy to clean. For many stages and clean liquids, a column saves floor space and solvent inventory.

Worked example

Question. A mixer-settler stage handles 2 m³/h of combined feed and solvent. The mixer holds 0.5 m³. The dispersed phase flow is 1 m³/h and its drops settle at 0.6 m/h. Find the mixing time in hours and minutes, and the settler area required.

  1. Data: combined flow Q = 2 m³/h; mixer volume V = 0.5 m³; dispersed-phase flow Qd = 1 m³/h; settling velocity vs = 0.6 m/h.
  2. Mixing time: t = V / Q = 0.5 m³ ÷ 2 m³/h = 0.25 h.
  3. Convert to minutes: 0.25 h × 60 min/h = 15 min.
  4. Settler area: A = Qd / vs = 1 m³/h ÷ 0.6 m/h = 1.6667 m².
  5. Unit check: (m³/h) ÷ (m/h) = m², so the area unit is correct.

Answer. Mixing time is 0.25 h (15 min); settler area is 1.6667 m².

Exam traps

  • Sizing the settler on the total flow instead of the dispersed-phase flow. Only Qd settles; the continuous phase leaves as a separate layer.
  • Quoting stage efficiency as if it were the number of stages. E is a fraction per stage; Nreal = Ntheoretical / E.
  • Forgetting which phase is dispersed. Changing the dispersed phase changes Qd, drop size and settling velocity.
  • Using mixer volume to size the settler, or settler area to find contact time. The mixer sets time; the settler sets separation area.
  • Treating flooding as one fixed velocity for all columns. Flooding depends on drop size, hold-up, packing and flow ratio.

5.5 Leaching: principles, equipment and stage calculations

Leaching pulls a soluble solute out of a solid with a liquid solvent. The solvent must wet the solid, dissolve the solute, and diffuse back out. The spent solid always leaves wet, carrying solution with it. So the balance is written on the inert solid, which does not dissolve, and on the solution retained per kg of that inert. This is used to extract oil from seeds, sugar from cane, caffeine from coffee, and metals from ores.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the idea of a distribution between two phases, and Unit 2 for equilibrium stages.

The leaching idea and the inert basis

Leaching is solid-liquid extraction. A liquid solvent enters the pores of a solid, dissolves the soluble part, and the solution diffuses back to the bulk liquid. The solid that does not dissolve is the inert or carrier solid. After contact, the mixture is separated into two streams. The overflow is clear solution, rich in solute. The underflow is spent solid plus the solution that stays with it. That retained solution is the main loss of solute; the spent solid is not dry.

Calculations use the inert solid as the fixed basis because its mass does not change. Let I be kg of inert solid in the feed. Let r be kg of solution retained per kg of inert solid in the underflow. Then the underflow carries L = rI kg of solution. Let X be kg of solute per kg of solution. The same X is used for overflow and underflow when the stage is ideal. The ideal stage assumes the solution inside the solid pores and the bulk solution have the same composition after enough contact. Real stages approach this; stage efficiency measures how close they get.

L = r I

L is kg of solution retained in the underflow; r is kg of solution per kg of inert solid; I is kg of inert solid in the feed.

This basis is used for oilseeds, sugar cane, coffee beans, herbal roots and leached ores. In every case the spent solid leaves wet, so the balance must include that wet solution.

One-stage and cross-current balances

For one equilibrium stage with constant r, write three quantities. Total solution M is all solution after contact: soluble solute in the feed + any liquid already in the feed + the fresh solvent stream. For a dry feed with no liquid, M = msolute,in + S. Underflow solution L = rI. Overflow solution V = M − L. At equilibrium both leave at the same X, so X = total solute in solution/M. Solute in overflow = VX. For pure solvent and dry feed, Recovery = VX/(solute originally in solid feed) × 100%. If the solvent is not pure, the equilibrium X includes the solute it brings. Recovery of feed solute is then (VX − solute in fresh solvent)/(solute originally in solid feed) × 100%. This calculation assumes that all soluble solute dissolves and the solution is unsaturated. For a saturated stage, X is the solubility limit and any undissolved solute is balanced separately.

M = msolute,in + mliquid,in + S;  L = rI;  V = M − L;  X = (msolute,in + msolute,S)/M;  Recovery = (VX − msolute,S)/msolute,solid × 100%

M is total kg of solution after contact; msolute,in is kg of soluble solute originally in the feed, including any already dissolved in feed liquid; msolute,solid is kg of soluble solute originally in the solid feed only (equal to msolute,in for dry feed); mliquid,in is kg of liquid already in the feed, not counting solute; S is total kg of fresh solvent stream; msolute,S is kg of solute in that solvent (0 if pure); L is kg of solution retained in the underflow; r is kg solution per kg inert solid; I is kg inert solid; V is kg of overflow solution; X is kg solute per kg solution at equilibrium, assuming all soluble solute dissolves and the solution is unsaturated. For a saturated stage, X is the solubility limit and undissolved solute is balanced separately. Recovery is % of msolute,solid in the overflow.

Cross-current leaching repeats this contact. The underflow from stage 1, still carrying L kg solution at X1, meets W kg fresh solvent in stage 2. For pure solvent and constant L and W, stage 2 gives X2 = X1 L/(L+W). Each stage multiplies the solute fraction by the same factor L/(L+W). Fresh solvent at every stage gives a fast drop in X, but it makes a large volume of dilute overflow. Longer trains are handled in 5.7.

Stage efficiency corrects the ideal stage. Xin is the composition of the solution entering the stage. Xout is the actual composition of the solution leaving the stage. X* is the composition that would leave at equilibrium with Xin. Define E = (Xin − Xout)/(Xin − X*) × 100%. E is below 100% when contact time is short or the solute must diffuse out of thick solids.

Equipment for leaching

Batch agitated vessel. A tank with an agitator contacts solid and solvent. After the set time, the slurry is filtered or centrifuged. It is simple and flexible, used for herbal extraction and small oil batches. The spent cake still holds solution, so washing may be needed.

Pachuca tank. A tall cylindrical tank with an air lift. Compressed air rises in a central pipe and lifts slurry, which circulates down the annulus. There are no moving parts inside, so it handles fine solids and corrosive liquors. It is common in ore leaching.

Dorr agitator and thickener. The Dorr agitator gives continuous mechanical agitation. The Dorr thickener is a shallow settling tank with a rotating rake. Clear overflow leaves at the top; settled underflow is withdrawn at the bottom. A thickener does not dissolve solute; it separates solution from solids.

Continuous counter-current decantation (CCD). Several thickeners are connected in series. Solids move forward from thickener to thickener; wash liquid moves backward. Each thickener is one contact stage. The train recovers solute from the underflow with fresh water or solvent. Named continuous units include the Bollman, Hildebrandt, Crown and Bonotto extractors for oilseeds. Moving beds and percolation are in 5.7.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg of a solid containing 20 mass% oil and 80 mass% inert material is leached in one stage with 500 kg of pure solvent. The spent solid retains 0.5 kg of solution per kg of inert solid. Find the oil recovered in the overflow and the percentage recovery.

  1. Inert solid I = 1000 × 0.80 = 800 kg.
  2. Oil in feed = 1000 × 0.20 = 200 kg.
  3. Retained solution L = rI = 0.5 × 800 = 400 kg.
  4. Total solution M = oil feed + solvent = 200 + 500 = 700 kg.
  5. Overflow solution V = M − L = 700 − 400 = 300 kg.
  6. At equilibrium X = oil feed / M = 200 / 700 = 0.285714 kg oil/kg solution.
  7. Oil in overflow = VX = 300 × 0.285714 = 85.714 kg.
  8. Recovery = 85.714 / 200 × 100 = 42.857%. Check: oil in underflow = 400 × 0.285714 = 114.286 kg; 85.714 + 114.286 = 200 kg.

Answer. The overflow carries 85.71 kg of oil, a recovery of 42.86%.

Exam traps

  • Balancing on total solid mass instead of inert mass, so rI is wrong.
  • Forgetting that the underflow carries solution, then treating the overflow as all the solvent plus all the solute.
  • Using r as kg solution per kg dry solid including solute, or as kg solvent per kg inert; check the definition.
  • Assuming overflow mass equals solvent charged; here V = M − rI, not S.

5.6 Pulsed, centrifugal and supercritical extraction

A plain extraction column can be tall and slow because gravity is a weak force for settling two liquids. This chapter shows three ways to intensify the job: pulse the liquids in a column, spin them in a centrifuge, or replace the liquid solvent with a supercritical fluid. All three keep the equilibrium idea from Chapter 5.1 but change how fast the phases meet and separate. They appear in nuclear reprocessing, antibiotic purification, decaffeination and spice-oil recovery.

You need: Chapter 5.4 for column flooding, dispersed-phase choice and the mix-then-settle duty of every extractor.

Pulsed columns

A pulsed column is a sieve-plate or packed column with a reciprocating pulse added to the liquid. A piston, diaphragm or air pulse pushes the liquid up and down. The plates or packing stay fixed; the liquid moves. The pulse shears drops, breaks large drops into smaller ones, and renews the film around each drop. Smaller drops give more interfacial area per unit volume, so the column needs fewer metres for the same duty. Hold-up, the volume fraction of drops, rises with moderate pulsing.

vp = 2 A f

vp is the pulse velocity in m/s; A is the amplitude from the mean position to one extreme in m; f is the pulse frequency in Hz (cycles per second). One full cycle moves the liquid 2A, so the average speed is 2Af.

Moderate pulsing raises hold-up and the mass-transfer coefficient. Too much pulse makes an emulsion, lowers settling, and floods the column at a lower throughput. Pulse velocity is the operating handle: pilot tests find the range where efficiency rises without losing capacity. The pulse chamber, seals and supports add mechanical height; they do not add equilibrium stages. Pulsed columns are used in hydrometallurgy, nuclear fuel reprocessing and pharmaceutical extraction where a short, closed column is preferred.

Centrifugal extractors

A centrifugal extractor replaces gravity with a spinning bowl. The two liquids are mixed and then thrown into a rotating field. Settling speed depends on the force, so a small machine can settle drops that would take minutes under gravity. Stokes' law for a dilute drop becomes:

vs = Δρ d² ω² r / (18 μ)

vs is settling velocity in m/s; Δρ is density difference between phases in kg/m³; d is drop diameter in m; ω is angular speed in rad/s; r is radius in m; μ is continuous-phase viscosity in Pa·s. The term ω²r replaces g in the gravity version.

The Podbielniak extractor has a rotating drum with concentric perforated bands. Heavy liquid moves outward and light liquid inward, giving counter-current contact in a small shell. A mixer-settler centrifuge uses a small mixer followed by a centrifugal bowl. Residence time is seconds to a few minutes and hold-up is small, which suits heat-sensitive antibiotics and radioactive solutions. The price is capital cost, seals, balancing and maintenance. Centrifugal force changes settling, not the equilibrium split; stage calculations still follow Chapter 5.3.

Supercritical CO2 extraction

Above its critical point a fluid is neither gas nor liquid. Carbon dioxide reaches this state at 31.1 °C and 73.8 bar. Near that point the density is liquid-like, and small pressure changes give large density changes. Solvent power follows density: at fixed temperature, raising pressure raises density and usually raises solubility. Lowering pressure drops the density and precipitates the solute, so the solvent leaves clean and is recycled.

PropertyValue
Critical temperature31.1 °C
Critical pressure73.8 bar

Supercritical CO2 is non-polar, so it dissolves caffeine, essential oils and many aroma compounds; a little ethanol is added for polar solutes. Extraction runs at moderate temperature, which protects heat-sensitive flavours. Decaffeination of coffee and tea, hops extraction and spice-oil recovery are common uses. The solid feed is a leaching problem, so the material balances of Chapter 5.5 still apply; only the solvent is unusual. The equipment is high-pressure, so capital cost is high.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of ground spice containing 5 mass% essential oil is extracted with supercritical CO2 at 40 °C and 200 bar. The CO2 leaves the bed loaded with 1.5 mass% oil. Find the minimum mass of CO2 needed and the extraction time at a CO2 flow of 5 kg/h. Take 1.5 mass% as the mass fraction of oil in the exiting CO2-oil solution.

  1. Oil in the spice = 2 kg × 0.05 = 0.10 kg. Inert solid = 2 − 0.10 = 1.90 kg.

  3. CO2 in that solution = 6.6667 − 0.10 = 6.5667 kg. So the minimum CO2 is 6.57 kg if all oil is removed and the exit gas is saturated.
  4. Time = 6.5667 kg ÷ 5 kg/h = 1.3133 h = 78.8 min = 1.31 h.

Answer. Minimum CO2 needed is 6.57 kg, and the extraction time at 5 kg/h is 1.31 h (78.8 min).

Exam traps

  • Supercritical CO2 is not a gas with no solvent power. At 40 °C and 200 bar its density is liquid-like; solubility is set mainly by pressure and density.
  • Pulse velocity uses 2Af when A is measured from mean to extreme. If A is peak-to-peak, the formula is Af; read the definition.
  • In the minimum CO2 calculation, use the exit oil mass fraction 0.015, not the feed oil fraction 0.05. The exit stream is the CO2-oil solution.
  • A centrifuge speeds settling and reduces hold-up; it does not change the equilibrium tie-lines or the number of theoretical stages.
  • Mass fraction and mass ratio differ. y = 0.015 means 1.5 kg oil per 100 kg solution, not per 100 kg CO2.

5.7 Leaching by percolation, moving bed and the Shanks system; multistage stage calculations

Leaching pulls a soluble solute out of a solid with a liquid solvent. The solvent can be pushed through a stationary bed, carried against slowly moving solids, or recycled through a row of tanks called the Shanks system. All three arrangements are costed with the same multistage balance, written on the inert solid. The work appears in sugar diffusers, vegetable-oil extraction, and ore leaching.

You need: the inert-solid basis and retention from Chapter 5.5, and the counter-current stage formula from Chapter 5.3.

Percolation through a stationary bed

Percolation is leaching in a stationary bed. Crushed solid is loaded into a tank or diffuser. Solvent is sprayed on top, flows down through the voids, and drains from the bottom. The solute must dissolve at the particle surface and then diffuse out of the pores. Surface solute washes out quickly, so the first runnings are concentrated. Later runnings are weak because solute must travel from inside the particle through partly depleted pores.

A bed is never drained dry. Some solution clings to the solids. The usual cycle is: fill, soak, drain free liquor, re-flood with fresh solvent, soak again, drain, and repeat. Each drain and re-flood is a stage. Commercial percolation batteries use several tanks in series, with liquor pumped forward. Fine particles cause channelling and slow flow; a very fine bed may need agitation or a different contactor.

Moving bed and the Shanks battery

Moving-bed leaching avoids emptying and refilling. Solids are carried slowly by screws, buckets, or belts through a sloping or vertical vessel while solvent flows the opposite way. Fresh solvent meets the most exhausted solids, so the last trace of solute is washed by clean liquid. Continuous counter-current diffusers of this type are used for sugar-beet cossettes and oil-bearing seeds.

The Shanks system is the tank version. A row of tanks holds the solids. The solid charge moves from tank 1 toward tank N, while liquor is pumped from N back to 1. Fresh wash water enters tank N; strong liquor is drawn from tank 1. The wash ratio used in the stage balance is E = V/L, where V is the overflow solution rate and L is the retained solution rate. Fresh wash water fed exceeds the overflow by the liquor advance; it is not the same as E when the feed brings its own solute. The liquor advance is the solution carried forward with the solids from tank to tank. A large wash ratio improves recovery but makes the product liquor weaker and costs more evaporation.

Counter-current stage calculation

For stage calculations use an inert-solid basis. Let B be kg inert solid/h, and r the kg solution retained per kg inert, so L = rB is the retained solution rate. Let V be the constant overflow solution rate, and x the solute mass fraction in solution. Define E = V/L. For N counter-current stages with solute-free fresh solvent, the solute fraction in the liquid leaving with spent solid is x_N. For a dry feed, define x_0 = S/L, where S is the solute fed per hour; this is the concentration the feed solute would make if dissolved in the retained liquid.

x_N/x_0 = (E − 1)/(E^(N+1) − 1), E = V/L

x_N is solute mass fraction in solution retained by spent solid; x_0 is S/L for dry feed; E is the wash ratio; V is overflow solution rate (kg/h); L is retained solution rate (kg/h); N is number of ideal stages.

This holds when every stage is ideal - the solution leaving a stage in the solids has the same composition as the overflow leaving that stage - and when L = rB and V are the same for all stages.

The solute lost is L x_N. Recovery = 1 − L x_N/S. For cross-current washing with fresh solvent each stage, x_i/x_(i-1) = 1/(1 + E), so N contacts give 1/(1 + E)^N. Counter-current gives much lower loss for the same V. Real stages are not perfect: stage efficiency η is the fraction of the equilibrium concentration change actually achieved. Use N_actual = N_ideal/η.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg/h of a solid containing 25 mass% solute and 75 mass% inert is leached in a three-stage counter-current train. Fresh water enters at 600 kg/h and the spent solid retains 0.6 kg of solution per kg of inert solid. Find the solute lost with the spent solid and the percentage recovery.

  1. Inert solid rate: B = 0.75 × 1000 = 750 kg/h.
  2. Solute fed: S = 0.25 × 1000 = 250 kg/h.
  3. Retained solution: L = rB = 0.6 × 750 = 450 kg/h.
  4. Overall solution balance: fresh water + dissolved solute = product overflow + retained solution. So 600 + 250 = V + 450, giving V = 400 kg/h. V is not the fresh-water rate.
  5. Wash ratio: E = V/L = 400/450 = 0.8889.
  6. Equivalent feed concentration: x_0 = S/L = 250/450 = 0.5556.
  7. For N = 3: x_3/x_0 = (E − 1)/(E^4 − 1) = (−0.1111)/(0.6243 − 1) = 0.2957.
  8. Solute fraction in retained solution: x_3 = 0.5556 × 0.2957 = 0.1643.
  9. Solute lost: L x_3 = 450 × 0.1643 = 73.94 kg/h.
  10. Recovery = (250 − 73.94)/250 = 0.7043 = 70.43%.

Answer. Solute lost with the spent solid is 73.94 kg/h and the recovery is 70.43%.

Exam traps

  • Running solids and liquor the same way. If both move left to right, the train behaves like a single stage, not a counter-current train; the wash water leaves loaded with solute instead of the product liquor being strong.
  • Using the fresh-water rate as V. In a dry-feed balance, V is the overflow solution rate after the feed solute has dissolved.
  • Writing the balance on total feed instead of inert solid. B is inert only; solute and retained solution are not part of B.
  • Mixing mass fraction x with kg solute per kg inert. L x gives kg/h only when x is a mass fraction of solution.
  • Reversing the Shanks battery. Strong liquor leaves the tank where fresh solids enter, not the tank where fresh water enters.

Extraction factor EE = K·S/R, where K is the distribution coefficient, S the solvent flow and R the raffinate (carrier) flow; E is the solvent strength relative to the liquid it must treat.5.2
Cross-current extractionFresh solvent contacts the raffinate in each stage; the extracts leave separately and are pooled afterwards.5.2
Cross-current stage formulaxn = x0/(1 + E)n for constant E, where x0 is the feed solute ratio and xn the raffinate ratio after n stages.5.2
Operating line (counter-current)The straight line from a solute balance around stages 1 to n, linking the raffinate leaving stage n to the solvent entering stage n+1.5.3
Difference point ΔThe pole where the operating lines of all stages meet; it fixes the stage stepping on the triangular diagram.5.3
Right-angled triangular methodA graphical stage count on a right-angle ternary plot: locate feed, solvent and Δ, then step between the equilibrium (tie line) and operating lines.5.3
Minimum solvent rateThe smallest solvent flow at which the operating line just touches the equilibrium curve; it needs infinite stages, so design uses 1.2 to 2 times this value.5.3
Mixer-settlerA stirred vessel that disperses one liquid in the other, followed by a quiet settler that lets the two layers separate; one unit is roughly one equilibrium stage.5.4
Rotating disc contactor (RDC)An agitated extraction column with discs on a central shaft that shear the rising or falling drops, giving high interfacial area in a short height.5.4
Inert-solid basisFor leaching, N = mass inert solid ÷ mass solution retained, X = mass solute ÷ mass solvent, and all balances are written on the inert solid B, which never leaves.5.5
Stage efficiencyIdeal (equilibrium) stages divided by actual stages needed for the same separation; real stages are 60 to 90 per cent efficient at best.5.5
Pulsed columnA packed or sieve-plate column in which a reciprocating pulse of liquid adds agitation and interfacial area without moving parts inside the column.5.6
Supercritical extractionExtraction with a fluid held above its critical temperature and pressure, whose density and solvent power change steeply with pressure alone.5.6
Percolation leachingSolvent is sprayed on a stationary bed of crushed solids and drains through the bed, dissolving solute as it flows.5.7
Shanks systemA battery of tanks where the same liquid is pumped tank to tank against the solids, so each tank meets a stronger or weaker solution and fresh solvent is needed only once.5.7

Takeaways

  1. Extraction works only when the solvent dissolves the solute far better than the carrier and the two phases settle apart quickly.
  2. The triangular ternary diagram converts a two-liquid mixture into a picture from which equilibrium splits and stage counts can be read off.
  3. One equilibrium stage gives one split; repeating contact with fresh solvent in cross-current mode drops solute geometrically, according to xn = x0/(1 + E)n.
  4. Counter-current contact lets each stage meet richer solvent, so it achieves the same recovery with fewer stages and less solvent than cross-current.
  5. Every extractor must do two jobs, disperse the phases for area and coalesce them for separation, and each equipment type balances those two jobs differently.
  6. A leaching balance is written on the inert solid because the spent solid always leaves carrying some solution with it.
  7. Pulsation, centrifugal fields and supercritical solvents are used when a plain column is too tall, too slow or too impure for a heat-sensitive solute.
  8. Percolation, moving beds and the Shanks battery all cost out on the same multistage leaching balance, with actual stages found by dividing ideal stages by stage efficiency.

Glossary

Carrier (diluent)
The liquid in which the solute is originally dissolved and which the solvent should not dissolve.
Centrifugal extractor
A machine that spins the two liquids so the heavier phase separates in seconds instead of hours.
Co-current extraction
Feed and solvent flow in the same direction and separate after one contact; limited to one equilibrium split per contact.
Conjugate phases
The pair of liquid compositions at the two ends of a tie line, in equilibrium with each other.
Counter-current extraction
Feed and solvent flow in opposite directions through a train of stages, so the richest solvent meets the richest feed.
Dispersion band
The mixed layer between the two clear phases in a settler where drops are still coalescing.
Distribution coefficient
The equilibrium ratio of solute concentration in the extract to that in the raffinate at the same temperature.
Extract
The solvent-rich phase leaving a stage, carrying the solute that has been removed.
Extraction factor
E = K·S/R, the ratio of solute carried by the solvent to solute left in the raffinate per stage.
Flooding
The condition in an extraction column where one phase can no longer flow past the other and the column stops working.
Inert solid
The insoluble part of the solid that stays unchanged and is used as the balance reference in leaching calculations.
Leaching
Removal of a soluble substance from a solid by contact with a liquid solvent, also called solid-liquid extraction.
Moving-bed leaching
Solids travel slowly through a vessel while solvent flows against them, giving continuous counter-current contact.
Overflow
The clear solution stream leaving a leaching stage, carrying the dissolved solute.
Packed extraction column
A vertical column filled with packing that breaks the dispersed phase into drops and gives contacting area.
Percolation
Passing solvent down through a stationary bed of solids so it dissolves solute on the way.
Ponchon-Savarit method
A graphical construction using an enthalpy-composition plot that gives stages and energy duties together.
Raffinate
The carrier-rich phase left behind after the solute has partly moved into the solvent.
Reflux in extraction
Part of the extract is returned to the top of the column to raise product purity, at the cost of more stages and solvent.
Shanks system
A set of tanks in which solid is shifted tank to tank while liquid is pumped the other way, approximating counter-current leaching.
Solute
The substance being moved from one phase to the other, for example oil leaving seed or caffeine leaving coffee beans.
Solvent selectivity
A measure of how strongly the solvent prefers the solute over the carrier; a value of one means no separation is possible.
Supercritical fluid
A fluid above both its critical temperature and pressure, with liquid-like density and gas-like viscosity and diffusivity.
Ternary diagram
A triangular composition plot for three components, read either as mass or mole fractions summing to one.
Underflow
The spent solid stream leaving a leaching stage, wet with retained solution.
