3.1 Gas solubility in liquids and Henry's law

A gas dissolves in a liquid until the liquid is saturated at the gas partial pressure above it. Henry's law makes that equilibrium a straight line between dissolved amount and partial pressure. A gas is worth absorbing only if it is soluble enough and recoverable later. The same line fixes solvent rate in an absorber. Only a few universities teach this chapter at this depth.

You need: mole fractions and partial pressures (from the topic Molecular diffusion and diffusivity) and the idea of two phases meeting at an interface (from Interphase mass transfer and two-resistance theory).

Solubility and the two Henry forms

Solubility is the equilibrium amount of a gas held in a liquid at fixed temperature and gas partial pressure. A gas is worth absorbing only if it is both soluble in the solvent and recoverable from it.

pA = H xA

pA is the partial pressure of solute A above the liquid, in atm or bar; xA is its mole fraction in the liquid; H is Henry's constant in pressure per mole fraction, for example atm per mole fraction.

A second form uses concentration: pA = H cA. Here cA is liquid concentration in kmol/m³ and H is in pressure per concentration, such as bar·m³/kmol. The number for H changes with the form, so quote the form beside the number.

Henry's law holds for dilute solutions at low to moderate pressure when the gas does not react with the solvent. For gases that react with water, such as SO₂, NH₃ or CO₂, H refers only to the physically dissolved unreacted molecules, not to the total gas taken up.

Temperature, pressure and y* = m x

For dilute absorption, yA = pA/P, where yA is gas mole fraction and P is total pressure. Substituting pA = H xA gives the line used later in absorber design.

y* = m x, with m = H/P

y* is the equilibrium gas mole fraction; x is the liquid mole fraction; m is the slope, dimensionless when y and x are both mole fractions; H is Henry's constant and P is total pressure.

H rises with temperature. Warm solvent dissolves less gas: at fixed x, y* is higher. Total pressure affects only the gas side. At fixed y, raising P raises pA and so raises the equilibrium x. The equilibrium line of absorber design is built from this m; see equilibrium and operating lines.

Figure 3.1.1 draws the worked example below. SO₂ with H = 40 atm per mole fraction gives y* = 40x at 1 atm and y* = 20x at 2 atm. For the same gas, y = 0.08, the water at 2 atm holds twice as much SO₂: x = 0.004 instead of 0.002. A warmer solvent works the other way: H rises, the line steepens, and x falls.

Worked example

Question. A gas at 1 atm total pressure contains 8 mole % SO₂ and the rest air. Henry's constant for SO₂ in water at this temperature is 40 atm per mole fraction. Find the equilibrium mole fraction of SO₂ in the water and the slope m of the equilibrium line y* = m x. Repeat for a total pressure of 2 atm with the same gas composition.

  1. Gas mole fraction of SO₂: y = 8/100 = 0.08.
  2. Partial pressure of SO₂: p = yP = 0.08 × 1 atm = 0.08 atm.
  3. Henry's law in mole-fraction form: x = p/H = 0.08 atm ÷ 40 atm per mole fraction = 0.002.
  4. Slope of the equilibrium line: m = H/P = 40 atm per mole fraction ÷ 1 atm = 40, dimensionless.
  5. Equilibrium line: y* = 40x.
  6. At 2 atm with the same y = 0.08: p = 0.16 atm, x = 0.16/40 = 0.004 and m = 40/2 = 20. Doubling P doubles the SO₂ the water can hold.

Answer. At 1 atm, x = 0.002 and the equilibrium line is y* = 40x (m = 40). At 2 atm the same gas gives x = 0.004 and y* = 20x.

Exam traps

  • Using total pressure P in pA = H xA instead of the solute partial pressure pA = yAP.
  • Mixing an H quoted in bar·m³/kmol with a mole-fraction calculation; the two Henry forms need different H values.
  • Forgetting that H changes with the form chosen, so a bare number for H without units or form is meaningless.
  • Saying total pressure changes H. H depends on solute, solvent and temperature; total pressure changes pA = yP and m = H/P, and so the equilibrium x.
  • Applying Henry's law to a concentrated solution or to the total gas taken up by a reacting solvent (NH₃, SO₂ or CO₂ in water); the law describes only the dilute, physically dissolved part.

3.2 Equilibrium and operating lines in absorption

An absorber moves solute from a gas into a liquid. Two straight lines on a Y–X plot decide whether it can work. The equilibrium line comes from solubility: it says how much solute the liquid can hold at each gas composition. The operating line comes from a solute balance: it says how much solute the liquid actually receives. The vertical gap between the lines is the driving force. Where the gap closes, transfer stops. Every absorber design in this unit starts by drawing these two lines.

You need Chapter 3.1 for the equilibrium line, and mole fraction and mole ratio from Molecular diffusion and diffusivity.

Why mole ratios make the lines straight

Use mole ratios, not mole fractions, when you draw the lines. Define Y = y/(1 − y): Y is kmol solute per kmol inert gas, and y is the mole fraction of solute in the gas. Define X = x/(1 − x): X is kmol solute per kmol solute-free solvent, and x is the mole fraction in the liquid. The inert gas flow G (kmol/h) and the solute-free solvent flow L (kmol/h) stay constant down the column. Total gas and total liquid flows do not: they change as solute moves. That is why the operating line is exactly straight on Y–X paper.

Y = Yout + (L/G)(X − Xin)

Y is the gas mole ratio at any point; Yout is the gas mole ratio leaving the top; X is the liquid mole ratio at that point; Xin is the liquid mole ratio entering the top; L/G is kmol solute-free solvent per kmol inert gas.

Y = Yin + (L/G)(X − Xout)

Yin is the gas mole ratio entering the bottom; Xout is the liquid mole ratio leaving the bottom. The two forms describe the same straight line.

The equilibrium line comes from solubility. For dilute systems it is often Y* = mX, where Y* is the gas mole ratio in equilibrium with liquid X and m is a slope from Henry's law; see Chapter 3.1. The operating line comes from the solute balance. Absorption works only when the operating line lies above the equilibrium line over the whole working range. Stripping is the reverse: operating line below equilibrium. The vertical gap Y − Y* is the driving force. It is not constant along the column, so chapter 3.7 uses a log mean driving force.

Drawing the operating line step by step

Start with the two terminal gas compositions and the two terminal liquid compositions. Convert every mole fraction to a mole ratio before putting it into the balance.

  1. Choose the inert-gas basis. G = total gas flow × (1 − y) at the terminal where the total gas flow is known.
  2. Convert yin, yout, xin, xout to Yin, Yout, Xin, Xout using Y = y/(1 − y) and X = x/(1 − x).
  3. Solute absorbed = G(Yin − Yout), in kmol/h.
  4. Liquid balance: Xout = Xin + G(Yin − Yout)/L.
  5. Slope = L/G. Draw the line through (Xin, Yout) and (Xout, Yin).
  6. Plot the equilibrium line from solubility and check that the operating line is above it for absorption.

For dilute gases, y ≈ Y and x ≈ X. Then the same balance appears as y = yout + (L/G)(x − xin). The approximation is convenient but must be stated. It fails when y or x is above about 0.05 to 0.10; then use mole ratios. Also, L is solvent that carries no solute, not the total liquid flow. If the entering liquid already contains solute, subtract it: L = Ltotal(1 − xin). The same care applies to G.

What the picture tells you

On the Y–X plot, the operating line is fixed by the flows and the required separation. A steeper operating line means more liquid per unit gas. Lower the liquid rate and the line swings down towards the equilibrium line; at the pinch it touches, and the required column becomes infinitely tall. That limiting case is the minimum liquid-to-gas ratio.

At the top of an absorber the gas is lean, so Yout is small. At the bottom the gas is rich and the liquid has picked up solute. The vertical gap Y − Y* is the local driving force. In the worked example the top gap is 0.005025 − 0 = 0.005025 and the bottom gap is 0.052632 − 2(0.022613) = 0.007406, so here the gap is smallest at the top; with less water it would be smallest at the bottom. Figure 3.2.1 draws both lines and both gaps. Because the gap changes, you cannot multiply one gap by the whole column height: tray absorbers step stages between the two lines, and packed absorbers integrate the gap.

Worked example

Question. A column treats 95 kmol/h of inert air carrying acetone vapour, scrubbed with water; in this dilute range the plant's equilibrium data fit Y* = 2X. The entering gas has yin = 0.05 and the leaving gas has yout = 0.005 mole fraction acetone. Pure water enters at 200 kmol/h. Find the operating line and the outlet liquid composition.

  1. Convert the entering gas mole fraction to a mole ratio: Yin = 0.05/(1 − 0.05) = 0.052632 kmol acetone/kmol air.
  2. Convert the leaving gas: Yout = 0.005/(1 − 0.005) = 0.005025 kmol acetone/kmol air.
  3. The solvent is pure water, so Xin = 0 kmol acetone/kmol water.
  4. Solute absorbed = G(Yin − Yout) = 95(0.052632 − 0.005025) = 4.5226 kmol/h.
  5. Liquid balance: Xout = Xin + 4.5226/200 = 0.022613 kmol acetone/kmol water.
  6. Slope = L/G = 200/95 = 2.1053. With Xin = 0, the line is Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053X.
  7. Check at the bottom: Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053 × 0.022613 = 0.052632 = Yin. The line passes through both terminal points.
  8. Driving forces from the line: top Y − Y* = 0.005025 − 2(0) = 0.005025; bottom 0.052632 − 2(0.022613) = 0.007406 kmol acetone/kmol air. Both are positive, so the line lies above equilibrium and the column can work.

Answer. The operating line is Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053X, and the outlet liquid composition is Xout = 0.022613 kmol acetone/kmol water.

Exam traps

  • Putting mole fractions y and x into an operating line drawn in mole ratios Y and X. Convert with Y = y/(1 − y) and X = x/(1 − x) first.
  • Using the total liquid flow instead of the solute-free solvent flow L. If the entering solvent already contains solute, L = Ltotal,in(1 − xin), with xin taken at the inlet; using the outlet x understates L.
  • Mixing the ends: Yout belongs with Xin at the top, and Yin belongs with Xout at the bottom. The intercept is Yout only when Xin = 0.
  • Reversing the rule for absorption: absorption needs the operating line above the equilibrium line; stripping needs it below.
  • Treating the driving force as constant along the column. The gap changes from end to end, which is why packed towers use a log mean driving force.

3.3 Minimum (limiting) liquid-to-gas ratio

The liquid rate in an absorber cannot be made as small as you like. Lower it and the operating line swings down towards equilibrium; the driving force shrinks, and at one rate the line just touches the curve. That touch is the pinch, and the rate there is (L/G)min, the smallest solvent flow that still reaches the required Yout. Design starts by finding it; working columns run at 1.2 to 2 times this value.

You need the operating line and the equilibrium line on the Y–X plot from Chapter 3.2, and solubility from Chapter 3.1.

Why the operating line stops falling

The operating line is a solute balance: Y = Yout + (L/G)(X − Xin). Y is kmol solute per kmol carrier gas, X is kmol solute per kmol solvent, L and G are solvent and carrier-gas flows in kmol/h, so L/G is the slope. Lean gas leaves at the top where fresh solvent enters, so the top point (Xin, Yout) is fixed. Changing L/G rotates the line about that point.

The vertical gap between the operating and equilibrium lines is the driving force. At exactly the minimum slope the gap falls to zero at one point, the pinch, so the column needs infinite stages or packing. Any flatter and the operating line crosses equilibrium: beyond the crossing the driving force is negative and the specified Yin cannot be reached at any height. With a straight equilibrium line, or one that bends upward as X rises, the pinch sits at the rich-gas bottom, where X is largest. If the curve bends towards the operating line part way up, the line can touch it there first (a tangent pinch), so check every X.

Construction and formula

  1. Draw Y* against X from solubility data.
  2. Mark the fixed top point (Xin, Yout) and the horizontal Y = Yin.
  3. Where that horizontal meets the curve is the pinch (X*in, Yin): the liquid in equilibrium with the entering gas.
  4. Join the top point to the pinch; that line's slope is (L/G)min.
(L/G)min = (Yin − Yout) / (X*in − Xin)

Yin, Yout are solute ratios in the entering and leaving gas (kmol solute/kmol carrier gas); Xin is the ratio in the entering solvent; X*in is the liquid ratio in equilibrium with Yin. All four are mole ratios.

For fresh solvent (Xin = 0) and a straight line Y* = mX, X*in = Yin/m, so (L/G)min = m(1 − Yout/Yin). With a curved line, (Y − Yout)/(X* − Xin) is the ratio needed at each X; the largest value is the minimum.

Choosing the design rate

Design uses 1.2 to 2 times the minimum, most often 1.2 to 1.5. Below 1.2 the column becomes very tall; above 2 the height saved is small but pumping keeps costing.

  • A larger L/G raises the driving force, so the column is shorter: fewer stages in Chapter 3.5, less packed height in Chapter 3.7.
  • It means more liquid pumped, bigger pipes and a wider column.
  • It gives a weaker outlet liquid (smaller Xout), which costs more to regenerate in Chapter 3.8.

Worked example

Question. A gas stream of 95 kmol/h of air carries acetone vapour at Yin = 0.0526 kmol acetone/kmol air and must leave at Yout = 0.00503 kmol acetone/kmol air. The solvent is pure water (Xin = 0) and the equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. Find (L/G)min, the liquid rate at 1.5 times the minimum, and the outlet liquid composition.

  1. Liquid in equilibrium with the entering gas: X*in = Yin/m = 0.0526/2 = 0.0263 kmol acetone/kmol water.
  2. Minimum ratio: (L/G)min = (0.0526 − 0.00503)/(0.0263 − 0) = 0.04757/0.0263 = 1.809 kmol water/kmol air.
  3. Check with the straight-line form: m(1 − Yout/Yin) = 2(1 − 0.00503/0.0526) = 2 × 0.9044 = 1.809.
  4. Design ratio: L/G = 1.5 × 1.809 = 2.713 kmol water/kmol air.
  5. Liquid rate: L = 2.713 × 95 = 257.7 kmol/h.
  6. Solute absorbed = G(Yin − Yout) = 95 × 0.04757 = 4.519 kmol/h.
  7. Outlet liquid: Xout = 4.519/257.7 = 0.0175 kmol acetone/kmol water, two-thirds of the pinch value 0.0263.

Answer. (L/G)min = 1.809; at 1.5 times the minimum L/G = 2.713, L = 257.7 kmol/h (about 258 kmol/h) and Xout = 0.0175 kmol acetone/kmol water.

Exam traps

  • Putting the pinch at the top. With fresh solvent and a straight equilibrium line the closest approach is at the bottom, where X is largest.
  • Leaving Xin out of the denominator; the correct span is X*in − Xin.
  • Quoting (L/G)min as the design value; at exactly the minimum the column is infinitely tall.

3.4 Co-current, counter-current and cross-current operation

Gas and liquid can meet in three flow arrangements. In co-current flow both enter at the same end and leave at the other. In counter-current flow gas rises while liquid falls. In cross-current flow gas passes stage after stage and fresh liquid enters each stage. The direction fixes the operating line, the average driving force and the best exit composition. Counter-current is the usual absorber; co-current suits fast washes; cross-current belongs mostly to leaching and extraction.

You need: the operating line and driving force from Chapter 3.2, and the pinch idea from Chapter 3.3.

Co-current: one end, one limit

Co-current. Gas and liquid enter together at one end. Write Y for kmol solute per kmol carrier gas and X for kmol solute per kmol solvent. L/G is the solvent-to-gas molar ratio, kmol solvent per kmol carrier gas. A solute balance from the inlet to any section gives G(Yin − Y) = L(X − Xin). Rearranged: Y = Yin − (L/G)(X − Xin). The operating line is straight with slope −L/G. It joins the mixed inlet point (Xin, Yin) to the mixed outlet point (Xout, Yout).

The equilibrium line is Y* = mX. Here m is the slope Y*/X: with Y and X as mole ratios, m is a dimensionless ratio, numerically kmol solvent per kmol carrier gas. At the outlet both streams leave the last stage together. With enough stages they approach equilibrium there, so Yout = mXout. Put this into the balance: G(Yin − mXout) = L(Xout − Xin). When Xin = 0, Xout = Yin/(m + L/G). More stages cannot push Yout below this value. Extra stages only bring the two outlet streams closer to that one equilibrium point.

Counter-current: opposite ends, largest driving force

Counter-current. Rich gas enters at the bottom. Lean liquid enters at the top. Gas flows up, liquid down. The operating line now runs from (Xout, Yin) at the bottom to (Xin, Yout) at the top. A balance from the top gives G(Y − Yout) = L(X − Xin), so Y = Yout + (L/G)(X − Xin). The slope is +L/G. The vertical gap Y − mX is the driving force. At the top, fresh liquid meets already-lean gas, so Yout can be small. At the bottom, partly loaded liquid meets rich gas, so the liquid can still take up solute. This opposite-end contact keeps a positive driving force over the whole column, as long as L/G is above the minimum (pinch) value from Chapter 3.3; below that value the operating line crosses the equilibrium line inside the column and the driving force turns negative somewhere. With L/G above the pinch, counter-current gives the largest average driving force of the three arrangements. That is why absorbers and strippers use counter-current flow. Packed height for this case is in Chapter 3.7.

Cross-current and choosing the arrangement

Cross-current. Gas moves from stage to stage. Fresh liquid enters each stage and leaves that stage, so each stage has its own short solute balance and the operating line is a set of short segments, not one long line. Stage n receives gas at Yn−1 and leaves it at Yn, and its own fresh liquid goes from X0 to Xn. Each stage starts with fresh liquid, so its inlet driving force is large, but over the cascade the average driving force is smaller than counter-current's and much more solvent is used. Fresh solvent to every stage is costly, so cross-current is rare in gas absorption and common in extraction and leaching (see Single-stage and multistage cross-current extraction).

Choosing. For the same duty and the same solvent rate, counter-current needs the fewest stages and gives the leanest exit gas. Co-current gives the richest exit gas because its outlet is pinned by equilibrium, and it may fail to reach the duty no matter how many stages are added. Cross-current needs more stages than counter-current and more total solvent. Choose co-current for a fast irreversible wash, where the reaction removes the solute and equilibrium does not limit the outlet, and for heat-sensitive duties where a hot zone at one end must be avoided. Choose counter-current for normal absorption and stripping. Cross-current is chosen for leaching and extraction, not for a tall gas absorber.

Types of contactors: stagewise and differential

Equipment for gas absorption falls into two families. In a stagewise contactor gas and liquid meet in separate stages, mix, approach equilibrium and separate before the next stage; composition changes in steps, and design counts stages. In a differential contactor the two streams touch continuously along the height; composition changes smoothly, and design integrates a rate equation over the height. Worked example

Question. A gas stream enters an absorber with Yin = 0.0526 kmol acetone/kmol air. The liquid enters with Xin = 0. The equilibrium line is Y* = 2X, with Y* in kmol acetone/kmol air and X in kmol acetone/kmol solvent. The liquid-to-gas ratio is L/G = 2.105 kmol solvent/kmol air. (a) In counter-current operation with Yout = 0.00503, find Xout and check the bottom driving force. (b) In co-current operation at the same L/G, find the best possible Xout and Yout. (c) Compare the exit gas compositions.

  1. Take a basis of G = 1 kmol air, so L = 2.105 kmol solvent.
  2. Counter-current balance: G(Yin − Yout) = L(Xout − Xin). With Xin = 0, Xout = (Yin − Yout)/(L/G) = (0.0526 − 0.00503)/2.105 = 0.04757/2.105 = 0.0226 kmol acetone/kmol solvent.
  3. Bottom driving force = Yin − 2Xout = 0.0526 − 2(0.0226) = 0.0526 − 0.0452 = 0.0074 kmol acetone/kmol air. It is positive, so the operating line is feasible.
  4. Co-current best outlet: both streams leave the last stage together, so Yout = 2Xout. Balance: G(Yin − Yout) = L(Xout − 0). Substitute Yout = 2Xout: 0.0526 − 2Xout = 2.105Xout. Thus 0.0526 = 4.105Xout and Xout = 0.0128 kmol acetone/kmol solvent.
  5. Then Yout = 2(0.0128) = 0.0256 kmol acetone/kmol air.
  6. Compare: 0.0256/0.00503 = 5.1. The co-current exit gas is about five times richer in SO₂.

Answer. Counter-current: Xout = 0.0226 and Yout = 0.00503. Co-current: Xout = 0.0128 and Yout = 0.0256 kmol acetone/kmol solvent and air respectively; the counter-current exit gas is about 5.1 times leaner.

Exam traps

  • More stages in a co-current absorber cannot give Yout below the value set by equilibrium at the mixed outlet. The outlet is pinned by Yout = mXout.
  • Do not use slope −L/G for a counter-current absorber. On a Y–X plot the counter-current operating line slope is +L/G.
  • Cross-current is not the default for gas absorption. Fresh liquid to every stage is expensive; it is common in leaching and extraction.
  • In co-current flow both inlet streams enter at the same end. Do not draw the operating line from (Xin, Yout) to (Xout, Yin).

3.5 Tray absorbers: theoretical stages and absorption factor

A tray absorber is a column of plates: gas rises, liquid falls, and each plate brings the two streams close to equilibrium, so one plate counts as one theoretical stage. Counting stages on a Y–X plot sizes the column. The absorption factor A = L/(mG) turns that count into one short equation, used to size absorbers for natural-gas sweetening, refinery gas treating and ammonia synthesis gas.

You need: the equilibrium and operating lines of chapter 3.2, the pinch idea of chapter 3.3, and equilibrium stages and stage efficiency from Equilibrium stages, cascades and stage efficiency.

One tray is one step on the Y–X plot

A tray absorber is a vertical shell with horizontal plates. Liquid enters at the top and drops through downcomers; gas rises through the tray holes. Each tray mixes the two streams, and they leave close to equilibrium. So one tray is one theoretical stage.

  1. Draw the equilibrium line Y* = mX and the operating line from a solute balance.
  2. Mark the top of the column: leaving gas Y₁ against entering liquid X₀, on the operating line.
  3. Step across to the equilibrium line: the liquid leaving tray 1 is X₁ = Y₁/m.
  4. Step back to the operating line at X₁: this reads the gas leaving tray 2, Y₂.
  5. Repeat downward until the gas reaches YN+1. The number of steps is N. Figure 3.5.1 steps off the worked example: four steps reach the entering gas.

Each stage is a rectangle between two straight lines. A large liquid flow makes the operating line steep, keeps it far from equilibrium and needs few steps. Lower the liquid rate and the steps grow taller; at the pinch the count becomes infinite.

The absorption factor and the Kremser equation

Work on a solute-free basis: G is the inert gas flow in kmol/h, L is the pure solvent flow in kmol/h, Y and X are kmol solute per kmol inert gas or pure solvent, and m is the slope of Y* = mX. Then A = L/(mG) compares the operating line slope L/G with m, and A > 1 means the operating line is the steeper one.

(YN+1 − Y₁) / (YN+1 − mX₀) = (AN+1 − A) / (AN+1 − 1)

N = theoretical stages; YN+1 = entering gas, kmol solute/kmol inert gas; Y₁ = leaving gas; X₀ = entering liquid, kmol solute/kmol pure solvent; A = L/(mG).

N + 1 = log[(A − R)/(1 − R)] / log A, with R = (YN+1 − Y₁)/(YN+1 − mX₀)

R = fraction of the entering solute removed, corrected for solute already in the solvent; for fresh solvent X₀ = 0, R = f, the fraction removed.

Here ln is the natural logarithm. At A = 1 the formula gives 0/0; its limit is N = R/(1 − R), which is 9 stages for 90 % removal. Below A = 1 a column with fresh solvent can remove at most a fraction A of the solute, however many trays it has. Design between A = 1.25 and A = 1.5: more liquid means fewer trays but a bigger pump, more solvent to strip and a higher solvent bill.

From theoretical stages to real trays

Real trays do not reach equilibrium, so divide by the overall tray efficiency Eo (see Equilibrium stages, cascades and stage efficiency): actual trays = N/Eo, rounded up. Only a few syllabi ask for the Kremser shortcut; most stop at the graphical count. The same equations return in distillation and stripping with the stripping factor S = 1/A = mG/L.

Worked example

Question. A tray absorber removes 90 % of the solute from a gas entering at YN+1 = 0.0526 kmol solute/kmol inert gas. The solvent is fresh (X₀ = 0) and the equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. The liquid rate gives L/G = 2.72 kmol solvent per kmol inert gas. Find (a) the absorption factor, (b) the theoretical stages, (c) the actual trays for an overall efficiency of 60 %.

  4. A − R = 0.46 and 1 − R = 0.10, so (A − R)/(1 − R) = 4.60.
  5. N + 1 = ln 4.60 / ln 1.36 = 1.5261/0.3075 = 4.963, so N = 3.96: use 4 theoretical stages.
  6. Graphical check (table below): operating line Y = 0.00526 + 2.72X. Step from Y₁ across to X₁ = Y₁/2, up to Y₂ = 0.00526 + 2.72X₁, and repeat. Stage 4 gives Y₅ = 0.0534, just past 0.0526, so 4 stages, matching 3.96.

3.6 Packed towers, tower packings and packing characteristics

Packing is the working surface of a packed absorber. It is a bed of shaped pieces or corrugated sheets that gives gas and liquid a large area to meet, with a low gas pressure drop. Three numbers describe a packing: specific surface a, void fraction ε and packing factor Fp. They decide how much solute can transfer and how hard the gas must push. Packed towers are used for CO₂ removal, refinery gas treating, ammonia scrubbing and odour control.

You need: interfacial area and mass-transfer coefficients (from Individual and overall mass transfer coefficients) and equilibrium and operating lines.

Packing supplies the area

A packed absorber is a vertical shell filled with small shaped pieces or a corrugated sheet stack. Gas rises through the gaps; liquid wets the surface and falls. The wetted surface is where solute crosses from gas to liquid. Packing exists to create that surface with as little gas pressure drop as possible. Figure 3.6.1 shows the parts of the worked example's tower.

Three numbers describe a packing. Specific surface a is the surface area of the packing per unit volume of packed bed, in m²/m³. The wetted area, which is what actually transfers solute, is at most this value and falls below it when liquid channels or the packing is not fully wet. Void fraction ε is the empty volume divided by the bed volume, dimensionless. Packing factor Fp is an empirical number, in m⁻¹, that rises as the packing becomes smaller and more obstructive; it is the input to the pressure-drop correlation in Chapter 3.7.

a = Ap / Vbed      ε = Vvoid / Vbed

Ap is the total surface area of the packing in m²; Vbed is the packed-bed volume in m³; Vvoid is the void volume in m³. The maximum interfacial area in a bed is A = a × Vbed; the actual wetted area may be less.

  • Random packings are dumped dry and settle randomly: Raschig rings (hollow cylinders), Pall rings (rings with internal tabs), Berl saddles and Intalox saddles. Common nominal sizes are 25 mm, 38 mm and 50 mm.
  • Structured packings are stacks of corrugated sheets or wire gauze, with a fixed geometry and a high area per volume.
  • Material: ceramic for corrosive service, plastic for cheap low-temperature duty, metal for high capacity.
  • As nominal size rises, a falls: a 25 mm ring gives roughly 200 m²/m³ while a 50 mm ring gives roughly 100 m²/m³. ε usually lies between 0.6 and 0.95 for random packings and ordinary structured sheet packings; wire-gauze and very open structured packings reach about 0.95–0.98.

Loading and flooding

Plot pressure drop per metre of bed ΔP/Z against gas mass flux G. At low G the line is nearly straight and gentle. At the loading point the slope begins to rise: liquid starts to accumulate in the voids, and the gas must push through more liquid. At the flooding point the line turns almost vertical. Liquid is held up and blown back, the bed cannot pass the flows, and operation becomes unstable.

More liquid fills more of the voids, so a higher liquid rate floods at a lower gas rate. Absorbers are commonly designed at 50 to 70 % of the flooding gas mass flux, read at the design liquid rate, and for a pressure drop of about 15 to 50 mm of water per metre of packing. Below the loading point the liquid hold-up hardly depends on the gas rate; between loading and flooding it rises quickly.

Gdesign = (0.5 to 0.7) × Gflood

Gdesign is the design gas mass flux in kg/(m²·s); Gflood is the flooding gas mass flux at the same liquid rate, from the pressure-drop curve or the correlation in Chapter 3.7.

Internals and a fair comparison

A liquid distributor at the top spreads liquid evenly over the whole cross-section. Holes, weirs or troughs feed the bed; a poor distributor gives dry channels and lost area. A redistributor every few metres of bed collects and re-spreads the liquid to undo maldistribution. A support plate at the bottom holds the packing with a large open area for gas and liquid.

HETP, height equivalent to a theoretical plate, is the bed height that does one equilibrium stage; it is measured for a packing and used to size towers. The stage calculation was in Chapter 3.5; packed design with HTU and NTU is in Chapter 3.7.

Exam traps

  • Quoting the nominal size (say 25 mm) as if it were the specific area. A 25 mm ring has a specific surface of about 200 m²/m³, not 25 m²/m³.
  • Using the shell volume instead of the packed-bed volume. Disengaging space and distributors add shell height but not interfacial area.

  • Reading the curves the wrong way: a higher liquid rate floods at a lower gas rate, not a higher one.

3.7 HTU, NTU and design of packed absorbers

A packed absorber is sized, not guessed. Two numbers fix its height: HOG, the height of a transfer unit, says how deep the packing must be to move a fixed share of the solute; NOG, the number of transfer units, says how many such shares the separation needs. Their product gives the bed height. The method holds even when the equilibrium line curves, where a simple stage count would fail.

You need the Y–X operating and equilibrium lines from Chapter 3.2, the packing terms a, ε and Fp from Chapter 3.6, and the integral of dx/x from first-year mathematics.

Two numbers, one height

Design splits into a height and a count. HOG is the depth of packing in which the gas gives up one unit of solute. It depends on the packing and the flows, not on the purity you want. NOG is how many of those units the job needs. It depends on the equilibrium line and on how far the outlet gas is pushed away from it.

Z = HOG × NOG

Z is the packed height (m); HOG the height of an overall gas transfer unit (m); NOG the number of overall gas transfer units (dimensionless).

NOG = ∫ dY / (Y − Y*)

Y is the gas solute mole ratio, kmol solute per kmol inert gas, so Y = y/(1 − y); Y* is the gas mole ratio in equilibrium with the liquid at the same height. Limits run from Yout at the top to Yin at the bottom. The gap (Y − Y*) is the driving force.

HOG = GM / (KYa)

GM is the inert-gas molar flux, kmol/(m²·s); KYa is the overall volumetric gas-phase coefficient, kmol/(m³·s·ΔY); HOG comes out in m. KYa lumps a coefficient and the interfacial area per m³ of bed into one measured number.

Mole ratios keep GM constant up the column, so the integral stays clean.

The integral, and the straight-line shortcut

For a curved equilibrium line, integrate NOG graphically: plot 1/(Y − Y*) against Y and count squares. For straight lines the integral has a closed form, because the driving force then falls exponentially from bottom to top.

  1. Get Xout from the solute balance over the column, using the chosen L/G.
  2. Read Y* at the bottom from the equilibrium line at Xout, and Y* at the top at Xin.
  3. Form the two end driving forces: ΔY1 = Yin − Y*bottom and ΔY2 = Yout − Y*top.
  4. Take the log mean: ΔYlm = (ΔY1 − ΔY2) / ln(ΔY1/ΔY2).
  5. Divide the total change by it: NOG = (Yin − Yout)/ΔYlm.
HOG = HG + λHL,  λ = m GM / LM

HG = GM/(kYa) is the gas-film HTU (m); HL = LM/(kXa) is the liquid-film HTU (m); LM is the solvent molar flux, kmol/(m²·s); m = dY*/dX is the equilibrium-line slope. The two resistances add, the liquid one weighted by λ.

The overall coefficient comes from the film coefficients as two resistances in series: 1/(KYa) = 1/(kYa) + m/(kXa), which is the same statement as HOG = HG + λHL. A liquid-side pair also exists: HOL = LM/(KXa) with NOL = ∫dX/(X* − X), and Z = HOL × NOL. Use the pair whose coefficient you have. Note λ = 1/A, where A is the absorption factor.

HETP and the design sequence

Plant engineers often quote packed height per theoretical stage: HETP = Z/Nt, in m per stage, which lets a packed column be compared with a tray column. For straight lines, HETP = HOG × ln λ/(λ − 1); as λ → 1 the fraction tends to 1 and HETP = HOG. A small HETP means good packing.

  1. Collect the duty: gas flow, Yin, Yout, solvent, Xin, temperature and pressure. Fix L/G above the minimum of Chapter 3.3.
  2. Choose the packing: nominal size, specific surface a, void fraction ε, packing factor Fp.
  3. Find the flooding gas rate from a generalized pressure drop chart using both flows and Fp. Design at 50–70 % of it, away from loading.
  4. Read KYa, or kYa and kXa, for that packing at those flows. Compute HOG.
  5. Compute NOG: integrate if the equilibrium line curves, use the log mean if it is straight.
  6. Multiply: Z = HOG × NOG. Add about 1.2 m for the distributor, sump and extra bed.

In an exam the flooding and KYa charts are given, not memorised. A factor of two on KYa halves the bed. Carry units at every step and round the built height upward to the next half metre.

Worked example

Question. A packed tower scrubs SO2 from air with clean water. Inlet gas Yin = 0.0526 kmol SO2/kmol air, outlet Yout = 0.00503, inlet liquid Xin = 0, and L/G = 2.72 on a mole-ratio basis. The equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. The inert-gas flux is GM = 0.015 kmol/(m²·s) and KYa = 0.05 kmol/(m³·s·ΔY). Find NOG, HOG, the bed height, the design height with 1.2 m added, and the HETP.

  1. Solute balance: Xout = (Yin − Yout)/2.72 = (0.0526 − 0.00503)/2.72 = 0.01749 kmol SO2/kmol water.
  2. Equilibrium at the bottom: Y*bottom = 2 × 0.01749 = 0.03498. At the top Xin = 0, so Y*top = 0.
  3. End driving forces: bottom 0.0526 − 0.03498 = 0.01762; top 0.00503 − 0 = 0.00503.
  4. Log mean: ΔYlm = 0.01259/ln 3.5034 = 0.01259/1.2537 = 0.01004.
  5. NOG = 0.04757/0.01004 = 4.74.
  6. Check with the absorption-factor form: 1/A = 0.7353; NOG = ln[(1 − 0.7353)(0.0526/0.00503) + 0.7353]/(1 − 0.7353) = ln 3.503/0.2647 = 4.74.
  7. HOG = GM/(KYa) = 0.015/0.05 = 0.30 m.
  8. Bed height: Z = 0.30 × 4.74 = 1.42 m.
  9. Design height 1.42 + 1.2 = 2.62 m; building height 3.0 m.
  10. Check with HETP: λ = mG/L = 2/2.72 = 0.7353; HETP = 0.30 × ln 0.7353/(0.7353 − 1) = 0.30 × 1.1616 = 0.348 m, so the bed holds 1.42/0.348 = 4.08 theoretical stages. The Kremser equation for the same duty (A = 1.36, R = 0.9044) also gives 4.08 stages.

Answer. NOG = 4.74, HOG = 0.30 m, bed height 1.42 m, design height 2.62 m (build 3.0 m), HETP 0.348 m (4.08 theoretical stages).

Exam traps

  • Putting mole fractions y into ∫dY/(Y − Y*) instead of mole ratios Y. That changes both the driving force and the integration variable; for a rich gas the bed comes out wrong by a wide margin.
  • Using the arithmetic mean of the end driving forces. For 0.01762 and 0.00503 it is 0.0113 against the correct 0.01004 — about 13 % too much driving force, so NOG reads 4.20 instead of 4.74.
  • Forgetting the 1.2 m distributor and sump allowance, or adding it before multiplying HOG by NOG.
  • Quoting HG as HOG. HOG = HG + λHL is larger than HG unless the liquid resistance is negligible.
  • Taking λ = mG/L and A = L/(mG) as the same number, or using total gas flow G instead of the inert flux GM in HOG.

3.8 Non-isothermal absorption and stripping

Absorption is not isothermal. Dissolving gas releases heat — heat of solution, often latent heat too. Warm solvent holds less gas, so the equilibrium line climbs toward the operating line and the driving force shrinks. Cold-data design underperforms. Amine sweetening, HCl absorption and SO₂ scrubbing all feel this.

You need: Henry's law, the Y–X plot and HTU/NTU.

Where the heat comes from

Three sources add up. Heat of solution ΔHs is released when solute enters the solvent; large for HCl in water, small for CO₂. Latent heat of condensation adds for condensable vapours. Heat of dilution appears as the rich solution concentrates down the tower.

ΔT = (Gs ΔHs) / (L cp)

ΔT is the adiabatic liquid temperature rise in K; Gs the solute absorbed in kmol/h; ΔHs the heat released per kmol solute in kJ/kmol; L the liquid flow in kmol/h; cp the liquid heat capacity in kJ/(kmol·K). For mass units use kg/h and kJ/(kg·K).

Mass form: ΔT = (Gs ΔHs)/(ṁ cp), with ṁ in kg/h and cp in kJ/(kg·K). The formula assumes all heat stays in the liquid. Real towers lose some through the wall, so measured rise is lower — treat ΔT as worst case.

What warmth does to the two lines

Henry's constant H rises with temperature, so m = H/P rises. For acetone in water m grows about 4 % per kelvin near room temperature, roughly as fast as acetone's vapour pressure. On the Y–X plot the equilibrium line swings upward while the operating line stays put. The driving force narrows at the bottom, where the liquid is richest and hottest, and can close. Figure 3.8.1 draws this for the worked example.

On Y–X coordinates the operating line uses solute-free flows Ls and Gs, which stay constant, so the line is straight even for a concentrated tower. Curvature appears only if you plot mole or mass fractions with total flows, or if solvent condenses or evaporates. A large temperature swing curves the equilibrium line, not the operating line.

Designing for it, and stripping

Two routes work. Step stages graphically, reading the equilibrium line at each stage's local liquid temperature and carrying the heat balance forward. Or integrate packed height with an energy balance alongside the mass balance, updating m at every height. Both need solubility data at several temperatures.

Practical fixes are cheaper than a taller tower:

  • Send more solvent: L rises, ΔT falls, and the operating line moves away from equilibrium.
  • Intercool the liquid between packed sections, or split the tower with a cooler between.
  • Feed the solvent cold, using its sensible heat capacity to soak up the heat of solution.

Stripping is the mirror image. Solute leaves the liquid for a gas or steam, so the operating line lies below the equilibrium line. The smallest gas rate makes the gas leave in equilibrium with the entering liquid: (G/L)min = (Xin − Xout)/(Y*out − Yin), with Y*out = mXin. The stripping factor S = mG/L plays the part of A. Stripping takes heat in, from a reboiler or live steam: warming raises m, lifts the equilibrium line away from the operating line and widens the driving force. A stripper run cold would have a falling m and a shrinking gap. Amine regeneration is the standard case.

Question. In the acetone absorber designed at 1.5 times the minimum liquid rate, 4.52 kmol/h of acetone is absorbed into 258 kmol/h of water fed at the top. 3.9 Multicomponent absorption and absorption with chemical reaction

Real gas streams carry several solutes, and one solvent must take them all: each solute has its own equilibrium line, but one L/G serves every component. A reactive solvent goes further: it consumes the dissolved solute, so the liquid holds far more and the absorption rate rises by an enhancement factor. These ideas set solvent choice and column size for amine CO₂ and H₂S removal and lime SO₂ scrubbing.

You need: the absorption factor, HTU and NTU and film theory (from Film, penetration and surface renewal theories).

Several solutes, one solvent

A multicomponent absorber receives a gas with solutes A, B, C and one liquid. At equilibrium each solute obeys its own Henry law: yi = mi xi, where yi is mole fraction in the gas, xi is mole fraction in the liquid, and mi is the slope of that solute's equilibrium line. One m per solute, not one average m for the mixture.

The operating line is shared. A solute balance gives the same liquid flow L and gas flow G for every component, so L/G is one number. For component i the absorption factor is Ai = L/(miG). It is dimensionless. Large Ai means easy absorption; small Ai means hard absorption.

Find the key component first: the solute whose recovery is specified. Compute Ai for each solute and use the Kremser equation component by component: with fresh solvent the fraction of solute i absorbed in N stages is (AiN+1 − Ai)/(AiN+1 − 1). Solutes with Ai well above 1 are almost fully absorbed; those with Ai below 1 are absorbed only to about the fraction Ai. The key component's recovery fixes L/G and N; every other solute then takes what those values give. Some syllabi teach a shortcut that handles all the components together, the Edmister group method. Never replace the individual mi by an average Henry constant.

Absorption with chemical reaction

A reactive solvent removes solute by reaction as it dissolves. Common examples are CO₂ in NaOH or amine, H₂S in amine, and SO₂ in lime slurry. The reaction keeps dissolved solute concentration low, so more gas can dissolve. Film and penetration models with reaction compare reaction rate with diffusion rate; that comparison gives the Hatta number.

Ha = √(k DA) / kL

k is the pseudo-first-order reaction rate constant (s⁻¹); DA is the solute diffusivity (m²/s); kL is the liquid-film mass transfer coefficient (m/s). Ha is dimensionless.

The enhancement factor E is the flux with reaction divided by the flux without reaction at the same interfacial concentration. Its size depends on Ha.

  • Slow reaction, Ha below about 0.3: reaction occurs mainly in the bulk liquid, E ≈ 1, and the rate is close to physical absorption.
  • Fast reaction, Ha above about 3 and much smaller than Ei: reaction occurs inside the film and E ≈ Ha, so the flux is multiplied by Ha. Ei is the instantaneous enhancement factor defined in the next bullet.
  • Instantaneous reaction: A and B meet at a sharp reaction plane inside the film. When Ha is comparable to or larger than Ei, use E = Ei = 1 + DBcB/(b DAcAi), where DB is the diffusivity of the liquid reactant B (m²/s), cB its bulk concentration (kmol/m³), b the moles of B used per mole of A, DA the solute diffusivity and cAi the interfacial solute concentration (kmol/m³).

Consequences: solvent capacity rises, so the liquid rate falls; for an irreversible reaction the dissolved solute exerts almost no back pressure, so the equilibrium gas mole fraction is y* ≈ 0 and the driving force y − y* ≈ y; the liquid-film resistance shrinks and the gas film may control. Figure 3.9.1 draws the film for three cases from the chapter's numbers. The film model and the penetration model give almost the same E for fast reactions, so either can be used in an exam.

Design consequences and limits

For a fast reaction with y* ≈ 0, the driving force y − y* ≈ y, so the operating line has almost the full gas mole fraction as driving force. The NTU and HTU method from Chapter 3.7 still applies, but if the liquid film resistance disappears, the overall gas coefficient is close to the gas-film coefficient. The tower becomes gas-film controlled, so packing wetting and gas velocity matter more than liquid-side kinetics.

With several solutes and a reaction, each solute has its own mi, Hai and Ei: an amine may take H₂S fast and CO₂ slowly, so selectivity comes from kinetics as well as solubility.

Worked example

Question. CO₂ is absorbed into a solution in which it reacts with a pseudo-first-order rate constant k = 100 s⁻¹. The CO₂ diffusivity is DA = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, the liquid film coefficient is kL = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, and the interfacial concentration is cAi = 0.03 kmol/m³. Find Ha, the enhancement factor, and the absorption flux. Compare with physical absorption.

  1. Write the data: k = 100 s⁻¹, DA = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, kL = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, cAi = 0.03 kmol/m³.
  2. Compute k DA = 100 × 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s².
  3. Take the square root: √(1.6 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.
  4. Find Ha = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ / (2 × 10⁻⁵) = 20. Ha is dimensionless.
  5. Ha = 20 is well above 3. The amine is in large excess, so Ei is far above 20 and the fast-reaction rule applies: E ≈ Ha = 20.
  6. Flux with reaction = E kL cAi = 20 × 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.03 = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ kmol/(m²·s).
  7. Physical flux = kL cAi = 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.03 = 6 × 10⁻⁷ kmol/(m²·s).
  8. Gain = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ / 6 × 10⁻⁷ = 20.

Answer. Ha = 20, E ≈ 20, and the reactive flux is 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ kmol/(m²·s), twenty times the physical flux of 6 × 10⁻⁷ kmol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Using one average Henry constant for a mixture. Each solute has its own mi, so each has its own equilibrium line.
  • Setting a separate L/G for each solute. One liquid and one gas flow serve every component; the key component fixes the shared L/G.
  • Applying E = Ha outside the fast-reaction range. E ≈ Ha is valid only when Ha is above about 3 and much smaller than Ei; for slow reaction E is near 1, and when Ha is comparable to or larger than Ei use E = Ei.
  • Mixing units in Ha: k must be in s⁻¹ for pseudo-first-order reaction, DA in m²/s, and kL in m/s.
  • Using Ei for a slow reaction; it applies only when the reaction is far faster than diffusion and B is in the liquid.

Takeaways

  1. Solubility fixes equilibrium, and Henry's law turns dilute gas-liquid equilibrium into a straight line on a Y–X plot.
  2. The operating line is a solute balance; the vertical gap between it and the equilibrium line is the driving force.
  3. The minimum liquid-to-gas ratio occurs when the operating line touches the equilibrium line at a pinch.

Understand State Henry's law in the form pA = H xA and explain how it becomes Y* = mX on a solute-free plot. 10 marks

Points to cover. define pA, xA and H; convert dilute y = m x to Y ≈ y and X ≈ x; straight equilibrium line through the origin for dilute absorption; effect of temperature on H and m.

Model answer.

  1. Henry's law. For a dilute solution of a gas that does not react with the solvent, the partial pressure of solute A over the liquid is proportional to its mole fraction in the liquid: pA = H xA. pA is in atm (or bar), xA is the liquid mole fraction and H is Henry's constant in atm per mole fraction. H depends on the solute, the solvent and the temperature, not on the total pressure. A concentration form pA = H'cA also exists, with H' in atm·m³/kmol; quote the form with the number.
  2. From pressure to mole fractions. In the gas, pA = yA P (Dalton), where P is the total pressure. Equating, yA P = H xA at equilibrium, so y* = (H/P) x = m x. The slope m = H/P is dimensionless. Example from the solubility chapter: SO₂ in water with H = 40 atm per mole fraction gives m = 40 at 1 atm and m = 20 at 2 atm, so raising the pressure lowers m and helps absorption.
  3. From mole fractions to mole ratios. In an absorber the carrier gas G (kmol/h) and the solute-free solvent L (kmol/h) do not change from top to bottom, so design uses Y = y/(1 − y) and X = x/(1 − x). Substituting y = Y/(1 + Y) and x = X/(1 + X) into y = mx gives Y/(1 + Y) = mX/(1 + X), which is a curve. For dilute systems (y and x below about 0.05) 1 + Y ≈ 1 and 1 + X ≈ 1, so Y* ≈ mX, a straight line through the origin of the Y–X plot.

Understand Compare co-current, counter-current and cross-current gas-liquid contact for absorption. Include operating-line shape and average driving force. 10 marks

Points to cover. co-current: same direction, operating line ends at equilibrium, low driving force; counter-current: opposite directions, largest average driving force, standard absorber; cross-current: gas contacts fresh liquid in stages, no single straight operating line; choice depends on recovery, solvent rate and pressure drop.

Model answer.

  1. Co-current. Gas and liquid enter at the same end and leave together at the other. A balance from the inlet gives G(Yin − Y) = L(X − Xin), a straight line of slope −L/G on Y–X axes. Its best possible end is equilibrium at the outlet, Yout = mXout, so Xout = Yin/(m + L/G) for fresh solvent. Adding stages only brings the outlet closer to that point; it can never pass it.
  2. Counter-current. Rich gas enters at the bottom and lean solvent at the top. The operating line Y = Yout + (L/G)(X − Xin) has slope +L/G and runs from (Xin, Yout) at the top to (Xout, Yin) at the bottom. Fresh liquid meets the leanest gas and the richest liquid meets the richest gas, so a driving force exists at both ends as long as L/G is above the minimum.
  3. Cross-current. The gas passes from stage to stage and every stage gets its own fresh solvent, which leaves from that stage. Each stage has a short operating line starting from X0, so the path is a set of segments, not one line. The first contact in each stage has a large driving force, but the solvent leaves each stage weak, so the total solvent is much larger. It is used in extraction and leaching, rarely in absorption.

Understand Explain why non-isothermal absorption can reduce or destroy the driving force in a packed absorber. 10 marks

Points to cover. heat of solution raises liquid temperature; solubility falls as temperature rises; equilibrium line moves up or becomes curved; driving force shrinks; a temperature bulge may appear.

Model answer.

  1. Heat release. Solute entering the liquid gives up its heat of solution (and its latent heat if it is a condensable vapour). With little heat loss the liquid warms as it flows down: ΔT = (solute absorbed × ΔHs)/(liquid mass flow × cp).
  2. Solubility falls with temperature. H, and so m = H/P, rises with temperature. At any liquid composition the equilibrium gas composition Y* = mX is higher in warm liquid.
  3. Where the effect is largest. The liquid is coolest at the top (fresh solvent) and hottest at the bottom, where it has absorbed the most solute. So the equilibrium line stays near its cold position at the lean end and curves upward towards the rich end. The operating line, a balance on solute-free flows, does not move.
  4. Numbers from the acetone example: 4.52 kmol/h absorbed into 258 kmol/h water, ΔHs = 30 000 kJ/kmol, cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K), m rising 4 % per K.

Analyze Analyse the effect of exothermic absorption on column design. Explain when an isothermal design is unsafe and how a non-isothermal design changes the operating and equilibrium lines. 13 marks

Points to cover. heat of solution raises liquid temperature; m rises with temperature so the equilibrium line steepens; operating line changes if liquid rate or temperature changes; driving force falls, so more height or area is needed; isothermal design is unsafe for high heat of solution and concentrated solute.

Model answer.

  1. Heat release. Each kmol absorbed releases ΔHs (heat of solution plus latent heat for a vapour). The liquid warms by ΔT = (absorbed × ΔHs)/(liquid flow × cp) if little heat is lost.
  2. Effect on the two lines in a sketch: draw Y* = 2X dashed (cold) and a curve leaving it at the origin and rising to m = 2.56 at X = 0.0175. The operating line from (0, 0.00503) to (0.0175, 0.0526) is unchanged. The gap between the operating line and the curve shrinks steadily towards the bottom, which is the picture examiners look for.
  3. Equilibrium line. H and m = H/P rise with temperature, so the equilibrium line steepens where the liquid is warm. The liquid is coolest at the top and warmest at the bottom, so the line curves upward towards the rich end.
  4. Operating line. On Y–X axes with solute-free flows the operating line stays straight; it moves only if the designer changes L/G (for example, more solvent to cool the liquid).
  5. Numbers (acetone example):
    ΔT = 4.52 × 30 000 / (258 × 18 × 4.18) = 7.0 K
m: 2.00 at the top → 2.56 at the bottom
bottom gap: 0.0176 (isothermal) → 0.0078 (adiabatic)
NOG:       4.75 → 6.06   (Simpson over seven points)
bed:        1.42 → 1.82 m with HOG = 0.30 m
  6. Driving force. Less than half the isothermal gap remains at the bottom, so NOG grows by 28 %. An isothermal design of 1.42 m would deliver less than the specified recovery.
  7. When an isothermal design is unsafe: (a) a large heat of solution (HCl, NH₃, SO₂, amine–CO₂, condensing vapours); (b) a rich gas, so much solute is absorbed per kmol of liquid; (c) a solvent rate close to the minimum, where the small gap can close completely; (d) a small liquid-to-gas ratio, so the liquid has little heat capacity. A quick test: if ΔT changes m by more than about 10 %, design non-isothermally.
  8. How the design changes. Step stages or integrate the height with a heat balance carried alongside the mass balance, reading m at each local temperature. Remedies: more or colder solvent, intercoolers between packed sections, cooling coils on trays.
  9. Closing check. With twice the water (L/G = 5.43) ΔT = 3.5 K and m(bottom) = 2.28; the operating line is steeper too, so the bottom gap opens to 0.0526 − 2.28 × 0.00876 = 0.0326.
Evaluate Evaluate multicomponent absorption with a reacting solvent. Explain why one equilibrium line is not enough, how solutes compete, and what the enhancement factor changes. 13 marks

Points to cover. each solute has its own m and equilibrium line; write a solute balance for each component with a common solvent; competition for solvent lowers driving force for some components; reaction consumes solute and raises rate; E = rate with reaction ÷ physical rate; E depends on kinetics, concentration and contact time.

Model answer.

  1. One equilibrium line per solute. Each solute i has its own Henry constant and slope mi, so on Y–X axes each has its own line Yi* = miX_i. An average m would over-absorb some solutes and under-absorb others.
  2. One operating ratio for all. All solutes share one gas stream and one liquid stream, so L/G is the same for every component; each component has its own absorption factor Ai = L/(miG).
  3. Component balances. For each solute, G(Yi,in − Yi,out) = L(Xi,out − Xi,in). With straight lines, the fraction of i absorbed in N stages is (AiN+1 − Ai)/(AiN+1 − 1).
  4. Lean-oil example (N = 4, L/G = 1.0):

  5. How solutes compete. Solutes with Ai well above 1 are almost fully absorbed; those with Ai below 1 are capped near Ai. The specified (key) solute fixes L/G and N, and the others take what those give. In a concentrated system the absorbed solutes also warm the liquid and swell its flow, which shifts every mi; and a solute that reacts uses up the solvent's reagent, leaving less for the others.
  6. Reaction. A reacting solvent consumes the dissolved solute, keeping its concentration low, so the back pressure is near zero (y* ≈ 0) and the solvent capacity rises. The enhancement factor E = (flux with reaction)/(physical flux at the same interfacial concentration) measures the gain in rate.
  7. What E depends on. Ha = √(kDA)/kL. Slow (Ha < 0.3): E ≈ 1. Fast (3 < Ha ≪ Ei): E ≈ Ha, for example Ha = 20 gives a flux 20 × 6 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ kmol/(m²·s). Instantaneous: E = Ei = 1 + DBc_B/(bDAc_Ai), for example 6. E therefore depends on kinetics (k), reagent concentration (cB) and contact time (through kL).
  8. Selectivity. Each solute has its own Hai and Ei, so a solvent can take H₂S (instantaneous with amines) much faster than CO₂ (finite rate): selectivity comes from kinetics, which short contact times exploit.
  9. Evaluation. Multicomponent design needs one equilibrium line per solute, one L/G and a key component; reaction adds one enhancement factor per solute. Closing check: 2.21 + 3.51 + 2.93 + 1.00 = 9.65 kmol/h absorbed of 17 kmol/h of solutes.
Evaluate Evaluate the idea of operating an absorber at the minimum liquid-to-gas ratio. Include pinch, column height and solvent pumping cost. 12 marks

Points to cover. at minimum L/G the operating line touches equilibrium at the pinch; driving force is zero at the pinch so height becomes infinite; operate above minimum, commonly 1.2 to 2 times; more liquid lowers stage height but raises pumping and regeneration cost; optimum balances capital and operating cost.

Model answer.

  1. Minimum L/G. Lowering the solvent rate turns the operating line down about the fixed top point until it touches the equilibrium line: the pinch. For the acetone example (Yin = 0.0526, Yout = 0.00503, Y* = 2X) (L/G)min = 0.04757/0.0263 = 1.809.
  2. Height at the pinch. The driving force Y − Y* is zero at the pinch, so ∫dY/(Y − Y*) diverges: infinite stages, infinite height. Below the minimum the lines cross and the specification cannot be met at any height.
  3. Where the pinch is. For a straight equilibrium line through the origin with fresh solvent, the gap Yout + (L/G − m)X falls as X rises whenever L/G < m, so the pinch is at the bottom (rich end). With a curved equilibrium line the pinch can be a tangent part way up the column, and the minimum must be found by checking every X.
  4. Diameter and flooding. More solvent also raises the liquid load on the packing, lowers the flooding gas rate and so widens the column; at 3 times the minimum the liquid flow is 515.5 kmol/h against 257.7 at 1.5 times, which can force a larger diameter even though the bed is shorter.

  6. Capital cost. Near the minimum the height rises steeply: going from 1.2 to 1.05 times the minimum saves 12 % of the solvent but nearly doubles the bed.
  7. Operating cost. Solvent must be pumped, then regenerated in a stripper, which needs heat roughly in proportion to the solvent flow. Above about 2 times the minimum the bed shrinks little (1.11 → 0.92 m from 2 to 3 times) while the solvent rate rises by half.
  8. Risk. At 1.05 times the minimum a small rise in temperature (m up a few per cent) or in gas rate closes the gap and the outlet goes off specification. The heat-effects example showed m rising 28 % at the bottom, which alone would cross a near-minimum line.
  9. Optimum. Total annual cost = capital (height, diameter) + solvent pumping and regeneration. The usual optimum lies at 1.2 to 1.5 times the minimum (absorption factor 1.25 to 1.5).

Glossary

Absorption
Transfer of one or more gas components into a liquid solvent.
Absorption factor
A = L/(mG), the operating-line slope L/G divided by the equilibrium slope m.
Driving force
The difference between actual composition and equilibrium composition at a point in the contactor.
Enhancement factor
E = actual absorption rate with reaction ÷ physical absorption rate without reaction.
Equilibrium line
The locus of gas compositions in equilibrium with liquid compositions at a fixed temperature and pressure.
Hatta number
Ha = √(kDA)/kL, the ratio of reaction in the liquid film to diffusion through it.
Height of an overall gas-phase transfer unit
HOG, the packed height that gives one overall gas-phase transfer unit.
Henry's law
At equilibrium the partial pressure of a gas equals a constant times its liquid mole fraction.
HETP
Height equivalent to a theoretical plate, the packed height per equilibrium stage.
Kremser equation
A formula relating the number of stages, absorption factor and solute recovery for straight lines.
Minimum liquid-to-gas ratio
The smallest solvent flow per gas flow that can achieve a specified separation.
Multicomponent absorption
Absorption of two or more solutes into one solvent, where each solute has its own equilibrium line.
Non-isothermal absorption
Absorption in which heat release changes the liquid temperature and therefore the solubility.
Number of overall gas-phase transfer units
NOG, the dimensionless separation duty obtained from equilibrium and operating lines.
Operating line
The locus of gas and liquid compositions that satisfy the solute balance in a contactor.
Overall volumetric mass-transfer coefficient
KYa, the overall gas-phase coefficient multiplied by the interfacial area per unit bed volume.
Packing factor
An empirical number for a packing used to estimate pressure drop and capacity.
Pinch
A point where the operating line meets the equilibrium line, making the driving force zero.
Reactive absorption
Absorption in which the dissolved solute reacts chemically with the solvent or a reagent in it.
Solute-free mole ratio
Moles of solute per mole of carrier gas or solvent, excluding the solute itself.
Specific surface
Total surface of the packing per unit volume of packed bed, usually m²/m³.
Stage efficiency
Ratio of actual solute removed on a tray to solute removed by an ideal theoretical stage.
Stripping
Removal of dissolved gas from a liquid by contact with a gas lean in that solute.
Stripping factor
S = mG/L = 1/A, the stripping counterpart of the absorption factor.
Theoretical stage
An ideal contact stage in which outgoing gas and liquid are in equilibrium.
Void fraction
The fraction of the packed-bed volume that is empty space.
