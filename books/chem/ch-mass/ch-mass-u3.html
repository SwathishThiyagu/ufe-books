3.1 Gas solubility in liquids and Henry's law

A gas dissolves in a liquid until the liquid is saturated at the gas partial pressure above it. Henry's law makes that equilibrium a straight line between dissolved amount and partial pressure. A gas is worth absorbing only if it is soluble enough and recoverable later. The same line fixes solvent rate in an absorber. Only a few universities teach this chapter at this depth.

You need Unit 1 for partial pressure and mole fractions, and Unit 2 for two phases in equilibrium at an interface.

Solubility and the two Henry forms

Solubility is the equilibrium amount of a gas held in a liquid at fixed temperature and gas partial pressure. A gas is worth absorbing only if it is both soluble in the solvent and recoverable from it.

pA = H xA

pA is the partial pressure of solute A above the liquid, in atm or bar; xA is its mole fraction in the liquid; H is Henry's constant in pressure per mole fraction, for example atm per mole fraction.

A second form uses concentration: pA = H cA. Here cA is liquid concentration in kmol/m³ and H is in pressure per concentration, such as bar·m³/kmol. The number for H changes with the form, so quote the form beside the number.

Henry's law holds for dilute solutions at low to moderate pressure when the gas does not react with the solvent. For gases that react with water, such as SO2, NH3 or CO2, H refers only to the physically dissolved unreacted molecules, not to the total gas taken up.

Temperature, pressure and y* = m x

For dilute absorption, yA = pA/P, where yA is gas mole fraction and P is total pressure. Substituting pA = H xA gives the line used later in absorber design.

y* = m x, with m = H/P

y* is the equilibrium gas mole fraction; x is the liquid mole fraction; m is the slope, dimensionless when y and x are both mole fractions; H is Henry's constant and P is total pressure.

H rises with temperature. Warm solvent dissolves less gas: at fixed x, y* is higher. Total pressure affects only the gas side. At fixed y, raising P raises pA and so raises equilibrium x. The equilibrium line in absorber design is built from this m; see chapter 3.2.

The figure plots y* against x at 10 °C and 30 °C. The 30 °C line is steeper because H is larger.

Worked example

Question. A gas at 1 atm total pressure contains 8 mole % SO2 and the rest air. Henry's constant for SO2 in water at this temperature is 40 atm per mole fraction. Find the equilibrium mole fraction of SO2 in the water and the slope m of the equilibrium line y* = m x.

  1. Gas mole fraction of SO2: y = 8/100 = 0.08.
  2. Partial pressure of SO2: p = yP = 0.08 × 1 atm = 0.08 atm.
  3. Henry's law in mole-fraction form: x = p/H = 0.08 atm ÷ 40 atm per mole fraction = 0.002.
  4. Slope of the equilibrium line: m = H/P = 40 atm per mole fraction ÷ 1 atm = 40, dimensionless.
  5. Equilibrium line: y* = 40x.

Answer. The equilibrium mole fraction is x = 0.002, and the equilibrium line is y* = 40x with m = 40.

Exam traps

  • Using total pressure P in pA = H xA instead of the solute partial pressure pA = yAP.
  • Mixing an H quoted in bar·m³/kmol with a mole-fraction calculation; the two Henry forms need different H values.
  • Forgetting that H changes with the form chosen, so a bare number for H without units or form is meaningless.
  • Saying total pressure changes H. H depends on solute, solvent and temperature; total pressure changes pA and therefore the equilibrium x.

3.2 Equilibrium and operating lines in absorption

An absorber moves solute from a gas into a liquid. Two straight lines on a Y–X plot decide whether it can work. The equilibrium line comes from solubility: it says how much solute the liquid can hold at each gas composition. The operating line comes from a solute balance: it says how much solute the liquid actually receives. The vertical gap between the lines is the driving force. Where the gap closes, transfer stops. Every absorber design in this unit starts by drawing these two lines.

You need Chapter 3.1 for the equilibrium line, and Unit 1 for mole fraction and mole ratio.

Why mole ratios make the lines straight

Use mole ratios, not mole fractions, when you draw the lines. Define Y = y/(1 − y): Y is kmol solute per kmol inert gas, and y is the mole fraction of solute in the gas. Define X = x/(1 − x): X is kmol solute per kmol solute-free solvent, and x is the mole fraction in the liquid. The inert gas flow G (kmol/h) and the solute-free solvent flow L (kmol/h) stay constant down the column. Total gas and total liquid flows do not: they change as solute moves. That is why the operating line is exactly straight on Y–X paper.

Y = Y_out + (L/G)(X − X_in)

Y is the gas mole ratio at any point; Y_out is the gas mole ratio leaving the top; X is the liquid mole ratio at that point; X_in is the liquid mole ratio entering the top; L/G is kmol solute-free solvent per kmol inert gas.

Y = Y_in + (L/G)(X − X_out)

Y_in is the gas mole ratio entering the bottom; X_out is the liquid mole ratio leaving the bottom. The two forms describe the same straight line.

The equilibrium line comes from solubility. For dilute systems it is often Y* = mX, where Y* is the gas mole ratio in equilibrium with liquid X and m is a slope from Henry's law; see Chapter 3.1. The operating line comes from the solute balance. Absorption works only when the operating line lies above the equilibrium line over the whole working range. Stripping is the reverse: operating line below equilibrium. The vertical gap Y − Y* is the driving force. It is not constant along the column, so chapter 3.7 uses a log mean driving force.

Drawing the operating line step by step

Start with the two terminal gas compositions and the two terminal liquid compositions. Convert every mole fraction to a mole ratio before putting it into the balance.

  1. Choose the inert-gas basis. G = total gas flow × (1 − y) at the terminal where the total gas flow is known.
  2. Convert y_in, y_out, x_in, x_out to Y_in, Y_out, X_in, X_out using Y = y/(1 − y) and X = x/(1 − x).
  3. Solute absorbed = G(Y_in − Y_out), in kmol/h.
  4. Liquid balance: X_out = X_in + G(Y_in − Y_out)/L.
  5. Slope = L/G. Draw the line through (X_in, Y_out) and (X_out, Y_in).
  6. Plot the equilibrium line from solubility and check that the operating line is above it for absorption.

For dilute gases, y ≈ Y and x ≈ X. Then the same balance appears as y = y_out + (L/G)(x − x_in). The approximation is convenient but must be stated. It fails when y or x is above about 0.05 to 0.10; then use mole ratios. Also, L is solvent that carries no solute, not the total liquid flow. If the entering liquid already contains solute, subtract it: L = L_total(1 − x_in). The same care applies to G.

What the picture tells you

On the Y–X plot, the operating line is fixed by the flows and the required separation. A steeper operating line means more liquid per unit gas. Lower the liquid rate and the line swings down towards the equilibrium line; at the pinch it touches, and the required column becomes infinitely tall. That limiting case is Chapter 3.3.

At the top of an absorber the gas is lean, so Y_out is small. At the bottom the gas is rich and the liquid has picked up solute. The vertical gap Y − Y* is the local driving force. In the worked example, top gap = 0.005025 − 0 = 0.005025; bottom gap = 0.052632 − 2(0.022613) = 0.007406. The gap changes, so you cannot multiply one gap by the whole column height. Chapter 3.5 steps stages between the two lines; Chapter 3.7 integrates the gap for a packed tower.

Worked example

Question. A column treats 95 kmol/h of inert air carrying SO2. The entering gas has y_in = 0.05 and the leaving gas has y_out = 0.005 mole fraction SO2. Pure water enters at 200 kmol/h. The equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. Find the operating line and the outlet liquid composition.

  1. Convert the entering gas mole fraction to a mole ratio: Y_in = 0.05/(1 − 0.05) = 0.052632 kmol SO2/kmol air.
  2. Convert the leaving gas: Y_out = 0.005/(1 − 0.005) = 0.005025 kmol SO2/kmol air.
  3. The solvent is pure water, so X_in = 0 kmol SO2/kmol water.
  4. Solute absorbed = G(Y_in − Y_out) = 95(0.052632 − 0.005025) = 4.5226 kmol/h.
  5. Liquid balance: X_out = X_in + 4.5226/200 = 0.022613 kmol SO2/kmol water.
  6. Slope = L/G = 200/95 = 2.1053. With X_in = 0, the line is Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053X.
  7. Check at the bottom: Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053 × 0.022613 = 0.052632 = Y_in. The line passes through both terminal points.

Answer. The operating line is Y = 0.005025 + 2.1053X, and the outlet liquid composition is X_out = 0.022613 kmol SO2/kmol water.

Exam traps

  • Putting mole fractions y and x into an operating line drawn in mole ratios Y and X. Convert with Y = y/(1 − y) and X = x/(1 − x) first.
  • Using the total liquid flow instead of the solute-free solvent flow L. If the solvent already contains solute, L = L_total(1 − x).
  • Mixing the ends: Y_out belongs with X_in at the top, and Y_in belongs with X_out at the bottom. The intercept is Y_out only when X_in = 0.
  • Reversing the rule for absorption: absorption needs the operating line above the equilibrium line; stripping needs it below.
  • Treating the driving force as constant along the column. The gap changes, which is why chapter 3.7 uses a log mean driving force.

3.3 Minimum (limiting) liquid-to-gas ratio

The liquid rate in an absorber cannot be made as small as you like. Lower it and the operating line swings down towards equilibrium; the driving force shrinks, and at one rate the line just touches the curve. That touch is the pinch, and the rate there is (L/G)_min, the smallest solvent flow that still reaches the required Y_out. Design starts by finding it; working columns run at 1.2 to 2 times this value.

You need the operating line and the equilibrium line on the Y–X plot from Chapter 3.2, and solubility from Chapter 3.1.

Why the operating line stops falling

The operating line is a solute balance: Y = Y_out + (L/G)(X − X_in). Y is kmol solute per kmol carrier gas, X is kmol solute per kmol solvent, L and G are solvent and carrier-gas flows in kmol/h, so L/G is the slope. Lean gas leaves at the top where fresh solvent enters, so the top point (X_in, Y_out) is fixed. Changing L/G rotates the line about that point.

The vertical gap between the operating and equilibrium lines is the driving force. At exactly the minimum slope the gap falls to zero at one point — the pinch — so the column needs infinite stages. Any flatter and the operating line crosses equilibrium: the driving force goes negative and the separation is impossible at any height. With a straight or convex equilibrium line the pinch sits at the rich-gas bottom, where X is largest; a strongly concave curve can pinch in the middle, so check every X.

Construction and formula

  1. Draw Y* against X from solubility data.
  2. Mark the fixed top point (X_in, Y_out) and the horizontal Y = Y_in.
  3. Where that horizontal meets the curve is the pinch (X*_in, Y_in): the liquid in equilibrium with the entering gas.
  4. Join the top point to the pinch; that line's slope is (L/G)_min.

(L/G)_min = (Y_in − Y_out) / (X*_in − X_in)

Y_in, Y_out are solute ratios in the entering and leaving gas (kmol solute/kmol carrier gas); X_in is the ratio in the entering solvent; X*_in is the liquid ratio in equilibrium with Y_in. All four are mole ratios.

For fresh solvent (X_in = 0) and a straight line Y* = mX, X*_in = Y_in/m, so (L/G)_min = m(1 − Y_out/Y_in). With a curved line, (Y − Y_out)/(X* − X_in) is the ratio needed at each X; the largest value is the minimum.

Choosing the design rate

Design uses 1.2 to 2 times the minimum, most often 1.2 to 1.5. Below 1.2 the column becomes very tall; above 2 the height saved is small but pumping keeps costing.

  • A larger L/G raises the driving force, so the column is shorter: fewer stages in Chapter 3.5, less packed height in Chapter 3.7.
  • It means more liquid pumped, bigger pipes and a wider column.
  • It gives a weaker outlet liquid (smaller X_out), which costs more to regenerate in Chapter 3.8.

Worked example

Question. A gas stream of 95 kmol/h of air carries SO2 at Y_in = 0.0526 kmol SO2/kmol air and must leave at Y_out = 0.00503 kmol SO2/kmol air. The solvent is pure water (X_in = 0) and the equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. Find (L/G)_min, the liquid rate at 1.5 times the minimum, and the outlet liquid composition.

  1. Liquid in equilibrium with the entering gas: X*_in = Y_in/m = 0.0526/2 = 0.0263 kmol SO2/kmol water.
  2. Minimum ratio: (L/G)_min = (0.0526 − 0.00503)/(0.0263 − 0) = 0.04757/0.0263 = 1.81 kmol water/kmol air.
  3. Design ratio: L/G = 1.5 × 1.81 = 2.713 (rounds to 2.71).
  4. Liquid rate: L = 2.713 × 95 = 257.7 kmol/h.
  5. Solute absorbed = G(Y_in − Y_out) = 95 × 0.04757 = 4.519 kmol/h.
  6. Outlet liquid: X_out = 4.519/257.7 = 0.0175 kmol SO2/kmol water.

Answer. (L/G)_min = 1.81; the design ratio 2.71 gives L = 257.7 kmol/h (about 258 kmol/h) and X_out = 0.0175 kmol SO2/kmol water.

Exam traps

  • Putting the pinch at the top. In absorption the closest approach is at the bottom, where X is largest.
  • Leaving X_in out of the denominator; the correct span is X*_in − X_in.
  • Quoting (L/G)_min as the design value; at exactly the minimum the column is infinitely tall.

3.4 Co-current, counter-current and cross-current operation

Gas and liquid can meet in three flow arrangements. In co-current flow both enter at the same end and leave at the other. In counter-current flow gas rises while liquid falls. In cross-current flow gas passes stage after stage and fresh liquid enters each stage. The direction fixes the operating line, the average driving force and the best exit composition. Counter-current is the usual absorber; co-current suits fast washes; cross-current belongs mostly to leaching and extraction.

You need: the operating line and driving force from Chapter 3.2, and the pinch idea from Chapter 3.3.

Co-current: one end, one limit

Co-current. Gas and liquid enter together at one end. Write Y for kmol solute per kmol carrier gas and X for kmol solute per kmol solvent. L/G is the solvent-to-gas molar ratio, kmol solvent per kmol carrier gas. A solute balance from the inlet to any section gives G(Yin − Y) = L(X − Xin). Rearranged: Y = Yin − (L/G)(X − Xin). The operating line is straight with slope −L/G. It joins the mixed inlet point (Xin, Yin) to the mixed outlet point (Xout, Yout).

The equilibrium line is Y* = mX. Here m is the slope Y*/X: with Y and X as mole ratios, m is a dimensionless ratio, numerically kmol solvent per kmol carrier gas. At the outlet both streams leave the last stage together. With enough stages they approach equilibrium there, so Yout = mXout. Put this into the balance: G(Yin − mXout) = L(Xout − Xin). When Xin = 0, Xout = Yin/(m + L/G). More stages cannot push Yout below this value. Extra stages only bring the two outlet streams closer to that one equilibrium point.

Counter-current: opposite ends, largest driving force

Counter-current. Rich gas enters at the bottom. Lean liquid enters at the top. Gas flows up, liquid down. The operating line now runs from (Xout, Yin) at the bottom to (Xin, Yout) at the top. A balance from the top gives G(Y − Yout) = L(X − Xin), so Y = Yout + (L/G)(X − Xin). The slope is +L/G. The vertical gap Y − mX is the driving force. At the top, fresh liquid meets already-lean gas, so Yout can be small. At the bottom, partly loaded liquid meets rich gas, so the liquid can still take up solute. This opposite-end contact keeps a positive driving force over the whole column, as long as L/G is above the minimum (pinch) value from Chapter 3.3; below that value the operating line crosses the equilibrium line inside the column and the driving force turns negative somewhere. With L/G above the pinch, counter-current gives the largest average driving force of the three arrangements. That is why absorbers and strippers use counter-current flow. Packed height for this case is in Chapter 3.7.

Cross-current and choosing the arrangement

Cross-current. Gas moves from stage to stage. Fresh liquid enters each stage and leaves that stage. Each stage has its own short solute balance, so the operating line is a set of short segments, not one long line. The first stage receives gas at Yin and leaves gas at Y1, with its liquid going from X0 to X1. Stage n receives gas at Yn−1 and leaves it at Yn, and its own fresh liquid goes from X0 to Xn. Every stage starts again from the same X0. Cross-current uses more liquid for the same removal. Each stage starts with fresh liquid, so its inlet driving force is larger than in counter-current at the same gas composition; over the whole cascade the average driving force is still smaller than counter-current's, which is why cross-current needs more solvent. In gas absorption it is rare because fresh solvent to every stage is costly. It is common in leaching and extraction; see Unit 5, Liquid Extraction and Leaching.

Choosing. For the same duty and the same solvent rate, counter-current needs the fewest stages and gives the leanest exit gas. Co-current gives the richest exit gas because its outlet is pinned by equilibrium, and it may fail to reach the duty no matter how many stages are added. Cross-current needs more stages than counter-current and more total solvent. Choose co-current for a fast irreversible wash, where the reaction removes the solute and equilibrium does not limit the outlet, and for heat-sensitive duties where a hot zone at one end must be avoided. Choose counter-current for normal absorption and stripping. Cross-current is chosen for leaching and extraction, not for a tall gas absorber.

Worked example

Question. A gas stream enters an absorber with Y_in = 0.0526 kmol SO2/kmol air. The liquid enters with X_in = 0. The equilibrium line is Y* = 2X, with Y* in kmol SO2/kmol air and X in kmol SO2/kmol solvent. The liquid-to-gas ratio is L/G = 2.105 kmol solvent/kmol air. (a) In counter-current operation with Y_out = 0.00503, find X_out and check the bottom driving force. (b) In co-current operation at the same L/G, find the best possible X_out and Y_out. (c) Compare the exit gas compositions.

  1. Take a basis of G = 1 kmol air, so L = 2.105 kmol solvent.
  2. Counter-current balance: G(Y_in − Y_out) = L(X_out − X_in). With X_in = 0, X_out = (Y_in − Y_out)/(L/G) = (0.0526 − 0.00503)/2.105 = 0.04757/2.105 = 0.0226 kmol SO2/kmol solvent.
  3. Bottom driving force = Y_in − 2X_out = 0.0526 − 2(0.0226) = 0.0526 − 0.0452 = 0.0074 kmol SO2/kmol air. It is positive, so the operating line is feasible.
  4. Co-current best outlet: both streams leave the last stage together, so Y_out = 2X_out. Balance: G(Y_in − Y_out) = L(X_out − 0). Substitute Y_out = 2X_out: 0.0526 − 2X_out = 2.105X_out. Thus 0.0526 = 4.105X_out and X_out = 0.0128 kmol SO2/kmol solvent.
  5. Then Y_out = 2(0.0128) = 0.0256 kmol SO2/kmol air.
  6. Compare: 0.0256/0.00503 = 5.1. The co-current exit gas is about five times richer in SO2.

Answer. Counter-current: X_out = 0.0226 and Y_out = 0.00503. Co-current: X_out = 0.0128 and Y_out = 0.0256 kmol SO2/kmol solvent and air respectively; the counter-current exit gas is about 5.1 times leaner.

Exam traps

  • More stages in a co-current absorber cannot give Y_out below the value set by equilibrium at the mixed outlet. The outlet is pinned by Y_out = mX_out.
  • Do not use slope −L/G for a counter-current absorber. On a Y–X plot the counter-current operating line slope is +L/G.
  • Cross-current is not the default for gas absorption. Fresh liquid to every stage is expensive; it is common in leaching and extraction.
  • In co-current flow both inlet streams enter at the same end. Do not draw the operating line from (X_in, Y_out) to (X_out, Y_in).

3.5 Tray absorbers: theoretical stages and absorption factor

A tray absorber is a column of plates: gas rises, liquid falls, and each plate brings the two streams close to equilibrium, so one plate counts as one theoretical stage. Counting stages on a Y–X plot sizes the column. The absorption factor A = L/(mG) turns that count into one short equation, used to size absorbers for natural-gas sweetening, refinery gas treating and ammonia synthesis gas.

You need: the equilibrium and operating lines of chapter 3.2, the pinch idea of chapter 3.3, and equilibrium stages and stage efficiency from Unit 2.

One tray is one step on the Y–X plot

A tray absorber is a vertical shell with horizontal plates. Liquid enters at the top and drops through downcomers; gas rises through the tray holes. Each tray mixes the two streams, and they leave close to equilibrium. So one tray is one theoretical stage.

  1. Draw the equilibrium line Y* = mX and the operating line from a solute balance.
  2. Mark the top of the column: leaving gas Y₁ against entering liquid X₀, on the operating line.
  3. Step across to the equilibrium line: the liquid leaving tray 1 is X₁ = Y₁/m.
  4. Step back to the operating line at X₁: this reads the gas leaving tray 2, Y₂.
  5. Repeat downward until the gas reaches Y_{N+1}. The number of steps is N.

Each stage is a rectangle between two straight lines. A large liquid flow makes the operating line steep, keeps it far from equilibrium and needs few steps. Lower the liquid rate and the steps grow taller; at the pinch the count becomes infinite.

The absorption factor and the Kremser equation

Work on a solute-free basis: G is the inert gas flow in kmol/h, L is the pure solvent flow in kmol/h, Y and X are kmol solute per kmol inert gas or pure solvent, and m is the slope of Y* = mX. Then A = L/(mG) compares the operating line slope L/G with m, and A > 1 means the operating line is the steeper one.

(Y_{N+1} − Y₁) / (Y_{N+1} − mX₀) = (A^(N+1) − A) / (A^(N+1) − 1)

N = theoretical stages; Y_{N+1} = entering gas, kmol solute/kmol inert gas; Y₁ = leaving gas; X₀ = entering liquid, kmol solute/kmol pure solvent; A = L/(mG).

N + 1 = log[(A − R)/(1 − R)] / log A, with R = (Y_{N+1} − Y₁)/(Y_{N+1} − mX₀)

R = fraction of the entering solute removed, corrected for solute already in the solvent; for fresh solvent X₀ = 0, R = f, the fraction removed.

At A = 1 the formula gives 0/0; its limit is N = R/(1 − R), which is 9 stages for 90 % removal. Below A = 1 a column can remove at most a fraction A of the solute, however many trays it has. Design between A = 1.25 and A = 1.5: more liquid means fewer trays but a bigger pump, more solvent to strip and a higher solvent bill.

From theoretical stages to real trays

Real trays do not reach equilibrium, so divide by the overall tray efficiency E_o from Unit 2: actual trays = N/E_o, rounded up. Only a few universities teach this shortcut; most stop at the graphical count, and the same equations return in distillation (Unit 4) with the stripping factor S = 1/A = mG/L.

Worked example

Question. A tray absorber removes 90 % of the solute from a gas entering at Y_{N+1} = 0.0526 kmol solute/kmol inert gas. The solvent is fresh (X₀ = 0) and the equilibrium line is Y* = 2X. 3.6 Packed towers, tower packings and packing characteristics

Packing is the working surface of a packed absorber. It is a bed of shaped pieces or corrugated sheets that gives gas and liquid a large area to meet, with a low gas pressure drop. Three numbers describe a packing: specific surface a, void fraction ε and packing factor Fp. They decide how much solute can transfer and how hard the gas must push. Packed towers are used for CO₂ removal, refinery gas treating, ammonia scrubbing and odour control.

You need Unit 2 for interfacial area and mass transfer coefficients, and Chapter 3.2 for the driving force.

Packing supplies the area

A packed absorber is a vertical shell filled with small shaped pieces or a corrugated sheet stack. Gas rises through the gaps; liquid wets the surface and falls. The wetted surface is where solute crosses from gas to liquid. Packing exists to create that surface with as little gas pressure drop as possible.

Three numbers describe a packing. Specific surface a is the surface area of the packing per unit volume of packed bed, in m²/m³. The wetted area, which is what actually transfers solute, is at most this value and falls below it when liquid channels or the packing is not fully wet. Void fraction ε is the empty volume divided by the bed volume, dimensionless. Packing factor Fp is an empirical number, in m⁻¹, that rises as the packing becomes smaller and more obstructive; it is the input to the pressure-drop correlation in Chapter 3.7.

a = Ap / Vbed      ε = Vvoid / Vbed

Ap is the total surface area of the packing in m²; Vbed is the packed-bed volume in m³; Vvoid is the void volume in m³. The maximum interfacial area in a bed is A = a × Vbed; the actual wetted area may be less.

  • Random packings are dumped dry and settle randomly: Raschig rings (hollow cylinders), Pall rings (rings with internal tabs), Berl saddles and Intalox saddles. Common nominal sizes are 25 mm, 38 mm and 50 mm.
  • Structured packings are stacks of corrugated sheets or wire gauze, with a fixed geometry and a high area per volume.
  • Material: ceramic for corrosive service, plastic for cheap low-temperature duty, metal for high capacity.
  • As nominal size rises, a falls: a 25 mm ring gives roughly 200 m²/m³ while a 50 mm ring gives roughly 100 m²/m³. ε usually lies between 0.6 and 0.95 for random packings and ordinary structured sheet packings; wire-gauze and very open structured packings reach about 0.95–0.98.

Question. A packed tower of 1.2 m inside diameter holds a 4 m deep bed of 25 mm rings with specific surface a = 200 m²/m³ and void fraction ε = 0.75. Find the total interfacial area in the bed, the volume of voids, and the area left if the packing is replaced by a 50 mm size with a = 100 m²/m³.

Exam traps

  • Quoting the nominal size (say 25 mm) as if it were the specific area. A 25 mm ring has a specific surface of about 200 m²/m³, not 25 m²/m³.
  • Using the shell volume instead of the packed-bed volume. Disengaging space and distributors add shell height but not interfacial area.
  • Treating Fp as dimensionless. It has units of m⁻¹ (older charts use ft⁻¹), so convert before putting it into a correlation.
  • Confusing the loading point with the flooding point. Loading is the bend; flooding is the near-vertical turn-up. Design margin is set against flooding.
  • Reading the curves the wrong way: a higher liquid rate floods at a lower gas rate, not a higher one.

3.7 HTU, NTU and design of packed absorbers

A packed absorber is sized, not guessed. Two numbers fix its height: HOG, the height of a transfer unit, says how deep the packing must be to move a fixed share of the solute; NOG, the number of transfer units, says how many such shares the separation needs. Their product gives the bed height. The method holds even when the equilibrium line curves, where a simple stage count would fail.

You need the Y–X operating and equilibrium lines from Chapter 3.2, the packing terms a, ε and Fp from Chapter 3.6, and the integral of dx/x from first-year mathematics.

Two numbers, one height

Design splits into a height and a count. HOG is the depth of packing in which the gas gives up one unit of solute. It depends on the packing and the flows, not on the purity you want. NOG is how many of those units the job needs. It depends on the equilibrium line and on how far the outlet gas is pushed away from it.

Z = HOG × NOG

Z is the packed height (m); HOG the height of an overall gas transfer unit (m); NOG the number of overall gas transfer units (dimensionless).

NOG = ∫ dY / (Y − Y*)

Y is the gas solute mole ratio, kmol solute per kmol inert gas, so Y = y/(1 − y); Y* is the gas mole ratio in equilibrium with the liquid at the same height. Limits run from Yout at the top to Yin at the bottom. The gap (Y − Y*) is the driving force.

HOG = GM / (Kya)

GM is the inert-gas molar flux, kmol/(m²·s); Kya is the overall volumetric gas-phase coefficient, kmol/(m³·s·ΔY); HOG comes out in m. Kya lumps a coefficient and the interfacial area per m³ of bed into one measured number.

The mole ratio basis matters. Y removes the shrinking gas volume from the arithmetic, so GM stays constant up the column and the integral stays clean.

The integral, and the straight-line shortcut

For a curved equilibrium line, integrate NOG graphically: plot 1/(Y − Y*) against Y and count squares. For straight lines the integral has a closed form, because the driving force then falls exponentially from bottom to top.

  1. Get Xout from the solute balance over the column, using the chosen L/G.
  2. Read Y* at the bottom from the equilibrium line at Xout, and Y* at the top at Xin.
  3. Form the two end driving forces: ΔY1 = Yin − Y*bottom and ΔY2 = Yout − Y*top.
  4. Take the log mean: ΔYlm = (ΔY1 − ΔY2) / ln(ΔY1/ΔY2).
  5. Divide the total change by it: NOG = (Yin − Yout)/ΔYlm.
HOG = HG + λHL,  λ = m GM / LM

HG = GM/(kya) is the gas-film HTU (m); HL = LM/(kxa) is the liquid-film HTU (m); LM is the solvent molar flux, kmol/(m²·s); m = dY*/dX is the equilibrium-line slope. The two resistances add, the liquid one weighted by λ.

A liquid-side pair also exists: HOL = LM/(Kxa) with NOL = ∫dX/(X* − X), and Z = HOL × NOL. Use the pair whose coefficient you have. Note λ = 1/A, where A is the absorption factor of Chapter 3.5.

HETP and the design sequence

Plant engineers often quote packed height per theoretical stage: HETP = Z/Nt, in m per stage, which lets a packed column be compared with a tray column. For straight lines, HETP = HOG × (λ ln λ)/(λ − 1); as λ → 1 the fraction tends to 1. A small HETP means good packing.

  1. Collect the duty: gas flow, Yin, Yout, solvent, Xin, temperature and pressure. Fix L/G above the minimum of Chapter 3.3.
  2. Choose the packing: nominal size, specific surface a, void fraction ε, packing factor Fp.
  3. Find the flooding gas rate from a generalized pressure drop chart using both flows and Fp. Design at 50–70 % of it, away from loading.
  4. Read Kya, or kya and kxa, for that packing at those flows. Compute HOG.
  5. Compute NOG: integrate if the equilibrium line curves, use the log mean if it is straight.
  6. Multiply: Z = HOG × NOG. Add about 1.2 m for the distributor, sump and extra bed.

In an exam the flooding and Kya charts are given, not memorised. A factor of two on Kya halves the bed. Carry units at every step and round the built height upward to the next half metre.

Worked example

Question. A packed tower scrubs SO2 from air with clean water. Inlet gas Yin = 0.0526 kmol SO2/kmol air, outlet Yout = 0.00503, inlet liquid Xin = 0, and L/G = 2.72 on a mole-ratio basis. 3.8 Non-isothermal absorption and stripping

Absorption is not isothermal. Dissolving gas releases heat — heat of solution, often latent heat too. Warm solvent holds less gas, so the equilibrium line climbs toward the operating line and the driving force shrinks. Cold-data design underperforms. Amine sweetening, HCl absorption and SO2 scrubbing all feel this.

You need: Henry's law, the Y–X plot and HTU/NTU.

Where the heat comes from

Three sources add up. Heat of solution ΔH_s is released when solute enters the solvent; large for HCl in water, small for CO2. Latent heat of condensation adds for condensable vapours. Heat of dilution appears as the rich solution concentrates down the tower.

ΔT = (G_s ΔH_s) / (L c_p)

ΔT is the adiabatic liquid temperature rise in K; G_s the solute absorbed in kmol/h; ΔH_s the heat released per kmol solute in kJ/kmol; L the liquid flow in kmol/h; c_p the liquid heat capacity in kJ/(kmol·K). For mass units use kg/h and kJ/(kg·K).

Mass form: ΔT = (G_s ΔH_s)/(ṁ c_p), with ṁ in kg/h and c_p in kJ/(kg·K). The formula assumes all heat stays in the liquid. Real towers lose some through the wall, so measured rise is lower — treat ΔT as worst case.

What warmth does to the two lines

Henry constant H rises with temperature, so m = H/P rises. For SO2 in water m grows about 4 % per kelvin. On the Y–X plot the equilibrium line swings upward while the operating line stays put. The driving force narrows at the bottom, where liquid is richest and hottest, and can close.

On Y–X coordinates the operating line uses solute-free flows L_s and G_s, which stay constant, so the line is straight even for a concentrated tower. Curvature appears only if you plot mole or mass fractions with total flows, or if solvent condenses or evaporates. A large temperature swing curves the equilibrium line, not the operating line.

Designing for it, and stripping

Two routes work. Step stages graphically, reading the equilibrium line at each stage's local liquid temperature and carrying the heat balance forward. Or integrate packed height with an energy balance alongside the mass balance, updating m at every height. Both need solubility data at several temperatures.

Practical fixes are cheaper than a taller tower:

  • Send more solvent: L rises, ΔT falls, and the operating line moves away from equilibrium.
  • Intercool the liquid between packed sections, or split the tower with a cooler between.
  • Feed the solvent cold, using its sensible heat capacity to soak up the heat of solution.

Stripping is the mirror image. It takes heat in — heat of desorption plus sensible heat — from a reboiler or live steam. Part of that heat warms the liquid; the rising m pushes the equilibrium line up, away from the operating line (which lies below it), so the driving force grows. If the tower ran cold, m would fall, the equilibrium line would drop towards the operating line, and the driving force would shrink. Amine regeneration is the standard case.

Worked example

Question. In a packed tower, 4.52 kmol/h SO2 is absorbed into 258 kmol/h water. ΔH_s = 30 000 kJ/kmol, c_p = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K), M_w = 18 kg/kmol. (a) Find the adiabatic liquid temperature rise. (b) If H rises 4 % per kelvin, find m when cold m = 2.0.

  1. Heat = 4.52 × 30 000 = 1.356 × 10⁵ kJ/h.
  2. Solvent mass = 258 × 18 = 4644 kg/h.
  3. Heat capacity rate = 4644 × 4.18 = 1.941 × 10⁴ kJ/(h·K).
  4. ΔT = 1.356 × 10⁵ / 1.941 × 10⁴ = 7.0 K.
  5. m = 2.0 × (1 + 0.04 × 7.0) = 2.56.

Answer. So ΔT = 7.0 K and m rises from 2.0 to 2.56; the equilibrium line is 28 % steeper and driving force is smaller except at the lean end, where both lines meet near the origin.

Exam traps

  • Using isothermal solubility data for a strongly exothermic solute such as HCl or SO2.
  • Forgetting that in stripping the operating line must lie below the equilibrium line, not above.
  • Mixing the heat of solution (kJ/kmol solute) with heat capacity (kJ/(kg·K)) and losing a factor of 18.
  • Ignoring that the hottest liquid sits at the bottom, so the pinch arrives there first.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Industrial absorbers use rate-based simulators solving mass, energy and equilibrium together at every stage, because the equilibrium line moves as the liquid warms. Research focuses on aqueous amine CO2 capture, where heat of reaction drives large temperature swings and intercooling is the main lever.

3.9 Multicomponent absorption and absorption with chemical reaction

Real gas streams carry several solutes, and one solvent must remove all of them. Each solute has its own Henry constant and its own equilibrium line, but the liquid and gas flow together, so one L/G serves every component. A reactive solvent goes further: the dissolved solute is consumed by a chemical reaction, so the liquid film can hold far more and the absorption rate rises by an enhancement factor. These ideas set the solvent choice and column size for CO2 or H2S removal with amines, SO2 scrubbing with lime, and many gas-cleaning plants.

You need: Henry's law, equilibrium and operating lines, absorption factor, and HTU/NTU; film theory from Unit 2.

Several solutes, one solvent

A multicomponent absorber receives a gas with solutes A, B, C and one liquid. At equilibrium each solute obeys its own Henry law: yi = mi xi, where yi is mole fraction in the gas, xi is mole fraction in the liquid, and mi is the slope of that solute's equilibrium line. One m per solute, not one average m for the mixture.

The operating line is shared. A solute balance gives the same liquid flow L and gas flow G for every component, so L/G is one number. For component i the absorption factor is Ai = L/(miG). It is dimensionless. Large Ai means easy absorption; small Ai means hard absorption.

Find the key component first. Compute Ai for each solute. The component with the smallest Ai is hardest to absorb and usually needs the most stages; it fixes L/G and the stage count. Then apply the Kremser equation component by component, using each solute's own Ai and its own inlet and outlet mole ratios. Some syllabi call the shortcut group method the Edmister method; it handles several components together by grouping. Never replace the individual mi values by an average Henry constant.

Absorption with chemical reaction

A reactive solvent removes solute by reaction as it dissolves. Common examples are CO2 in NaOH or amine, H2S in amine, and SO2 in lime slurry. The reaction keeps dissolved solute concentration low, so more gas can dissolve. Film and penetration models with reaction compare reaction rate with diffusion rate; that comparison gives the Hatta number.

Ha = √(k DA) / kL

k is the pseudo-first-order reaction rate constant (s⁻¹); DA is the solute diffusivity (m²/s); kL is the liquid-film mass transfer coefficient (m/s). Ha is dimensionless.

The enhancement factor E is the flux with reaction divided by the flux without reaction at the same interfacial concentration. Its size depends on Ha.

  • Slow reaction, Ha below about 0.3: reaction occurs mainly in the bulk liquid, E ≈ 1, and the rate is close to physical absorption.
  • Fast reaction, Ha above about 3 and much smaller than Ei: reaction occurs inside the film and E ≈ Ha, so the flux is multiplied by Ha. Ei is the instantaneous enhancement factor defined in the next bullet.
  • Instantaneous reaction: the reaction plane is sharp. When Ha is comparable to or larger than Ei, use E = Ei = 1 + DL cB / (b DA cAi), where DL is diffusivity of liquid reactant B (m²/s), cB is its bulk concentration (kmol/m³), b is the stoichiometric coefficient of B per mole of A, DA is solute diffusivity (m²/s), and cAi is interfacial solute concentration (kmol/m³).

Consequences: solvent capacity rises, so the liquid rate falls; the back pressure of solute is nearly zero, so the equilibrium gas mole fraction is y* ≈ 0; the driving force is y − y* ≈ y; the liquid-film resistance shrinks and the gas film may control. In the figure, the fast-reaction curve is the approximation exp(−Ha x/δ) with Ha = 3; for a finite film with zero bulk solute, the exact first-order profile is cA/cAi = sinh[Ha(1 − x/δ)]/sinh(Ha), which is zero at the film edge x/δ = 1.

Design consequences and limits

For a fast reaction with y* ≈ 0, the driving force y − y* ≈ y, so the operating line has almost the full gas mole fraction as driving force. The NTU and HTU method from Chapter 3.7 still applies, but if the liquid film resistance disappears, the overall gas coefficient is close to the gas-film coefficient. The tower becomes gas-film controlled, so packing wetting and gas velocity matter more than liquid-side kinetics.

Multicomponent plus reaction means each solute has its own mi, Hai, and Ei. A reactive solvent may remove H2S fast and CO2 slowly; selectivity then comes from kinetics, not only solubility. The key component can change, so solve each component's flux with its own Ei and then satisfy the single L/G. Check the gas film before reducing the liquid rate too far.

Worked example

Question. CO2 is absorbed into a solution in which it reacts with a pseudo-first-order rate constant k = 100 s⁻¹. The CO2 diffusivity is D_A = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, the liquid film coefficient is k_L = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, and the interfacial concentration is c_Ai = 0.03 kmol/m³. Find Ha, the enhancement factor, and the absorption flux. Compare with physical absorption.

  1. Write the data: k = 100 s⁻¹, D_A = 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, k_L = 2 × 10⁻⁵ m/s, c_Ai = 0.03 kmol/m³.
  2. Compute k D_A = 100 × 1.6 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s².
  3. Take the square root: √(1.6 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.
  4. Find Ha = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ / (2 × 10⁻⁵) = 20. Ha is dimensionless.
  5. Ha = 20 is well above 3, so the reaction is fast and E ≈ Ha = 20.
  6. Flux with reaction = E k_L c_Ai = 20 × 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.03 = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ kmol/(m²·s).
  7. Physical flux = k_L c_Ai = 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.03 = 6 × 10⁻⁷ kmol/(m²·s).
  8. Gain = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ / 6 × 10⁻⁷ = 20.

Answer. Ha = 20, E ≈ 20, and the reactive flux is 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ kmol/(m²·s), twenty times the physical flux of 6 × 10⁻⁷ kmol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Using one average Henry constant for a mixture. Each solute has its own mi, so each has its own equilibrium line.
  • Setting a separate L/G for each solute. One liquid and one gas flow serve every component; the key component fixes the shared L/G.
  • Applying E = Ha outside the fast-reaction range. E ≈ Ha is valid only when Ha is above about 3 and much smaller than Ei; for slow reaction E is near 1, and when Ha is comparable to or larger than Ei use E = Ei.
  • Mixing units in Ha: k must be in s⁻¹ for pseudo-first-order reaction, DA in m²/s, and kL in m/s.
  • Treating the instantaneous-reaction formula as general. It applies only when the reaction is extremely fast and reactant B is present in the liquid.

Remember Define the enhancement factor E in absorption with chemical reaction. 2 marks

Analyze A reacting system gives Ha = 0.5. Explain whether E ≈ Ha is a good estimate. 4 marks

No. Ha = 0.5 is not in the fast-reaction range. E ≈ Ha needs Ha above about 3. At 0.5 the reaction is largely in the bulk liquid and E is closer to 1; using 0.5 would understate the flux.

Solvent capacity rises, so the liquid rate can fall. The equilibrium back pressure is nearly zero, so y* ≈ 0 and the driving force is y − y* ≈ y. Liquid-film resistance shrinks, so the gas film may control and the tower becomes gas-film controlled.

Takeaways

  1. Solubility fixes equilibrium, and Henry's law turns dilute gas-liquid equilibrium into a straight line on a Y–X plot.
  2. The operating line is a solute balance; the vertical gap between it and the equilibrium line is the driving force.
  3. The minimum liquid-to-gas ratio occurs when the operating line touches the equilibrium line at a pinch.
  4. Counter-current contact gives more average driving force than co-current contact and is the usual choice for absorption.
  5. The absorption factor A = L/(mG) measures how far the liquid rate exceeds the minimum and controls the number of stages.
  6. Packing area a, void fraction ε and packing factor Fp decide interfacial area, capacity and pressure drop.
  7. Packed height Z = H_OG × N_OG separates the mass-transfer coefficient from the separation duty.
  8. Heat release raises liquid temperature and lowers solubility; reaction consumes solute and raises the rate through E.
