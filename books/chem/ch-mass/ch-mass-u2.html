2.1 Convective mass transfer and mass transfer coefficients

Air blown over a wet surface dries it faster than still air. The vapour still crosses the layer next to the surface by diffusion, but moving air sweeps the vapour away and keeps that layer thin and its gradient steep. One number, the convective mass transfer coefficient kc, captures how well a flowing fluid carries a species to or from a surface, with no film thickness needed. Absorbers, cooling towers and dryers are sized with it.

You need Fick's first law, molar flux and the steady diffusion result for a stagnant film, from the topic of molecular diffusion and diffusivity.

The coefficient replaces a film thickness

For a stagnant film, molecular diffusion gave NA = DAB(CA1 − CA2)/δ, where DAB is diffusivity (m²/s) and δ is film thickness (m). In a flowing fluid no fixed δ exists. The layer next to the surface is thin at the leading edge, grows downstream and breaks into eddies in turbulent flow. So δ cannot be measured, and the formula cannot be used as it stands.

NA = kc (CAi − CA∞)

NA is the molar flux of A across the interface, mol/(m²·s); CAi is the concentration of A just at the interface, mol/m³; CA∞ is the concentration of A in the well-mixed bulk fluid, mol/m³; kc is the convective mass transfer coefficient, m/s.

The unit follows: mol/(m²·s) divided by mol/m³ gives m/s. So kc looks like a velocity, but it is not the speed of the fluid. It is the molar flux per unit concentration difference. It is a transport coefficient, not a speed.

All the unknown fluid mechanics — film thickness, turbulence, surface shape — is pushed inside kc. Theory cannot give kc from first principles for most equipment. It is measured, or predicted from correlations kc = (DAB/L) × f(Re, Sc). Those groups and correlations are in Chapter 2.3.

Gas and liquid forms, local and average

For a gas, partial pressure is the natural driving force. With pA = CART, where pA is partial pressure (atm), R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature (K), the flux law is NA = kG(pAi − pA∞). Comparing forms gives kG = kc/(RT). With R in m³·atm/(kmol·K), kG comes out in kmol/(m²·s·atm). The liquid-side coefficient kL uses liquid concentrations and has units of m/s.

The coefficients come in two types, because the flux formula for molecular diffusion differs with what B does. For A diffusing through stagnant B (NB = 0, as when water evaporates into air), a film of thickness z gives NA = DAB P (pA1 − pA2)/(R T z pBm), so kG = DAB P/(R T z pBm). For equimolar counter-diffusion (NA = −NB, as in distillation) the film gives k′G = DAB/(R T z) with no pBm. The two are linked by kG pBm = k′G P, and in mole fractions by ky yBm = k′y. A correlation measured at low flux gives the primed value; for diffusion through stagnant B divide it by yBm, the log mean of the B mole fractions at the two ends of the film.

A local coefficient applies at one point and varies with position because the boundary layer changes. An average coefficient applies over the whole surface: total molar flow equals average coefficient × total area × mean driving force. Design uses averages. A gas-side coefficient belongs to the gas film, a liquid-side coefficient to the liquid film. When both matter they combine into an overall coefficient, as Chapter 2.2 shows.

Boundary layer, turbulence and the size of kc

Air flows over a flat plate. A concentration boundary layer of thickness δc (m) builds up, inside which concentration falls from CAi at the wall to CA∞ outside. The wall flux is NA = −DAB(∂CA/∂y) at y = 0, where y is normal to the plate. A thin layer holds a steep gradient, so flux and kc are large.

The layer thickens roughly as √x from the leading edge. The wall gradient weakens, so local kc falls with distance. The average over a plate of length L is larger than the trailing-edge local value because the leading part transfers well. Hence the average is the honest number for a whole surface.

In turbulent flow, eddies add an eddy diffusivity far larger than the molecular one, so bulk transport is fast. That is why gas velocity strongly affects kc. A thin laminar sublayer always survives at the wall, and species cross it by molecular diffusion. That sublayer is the bottleneck, so kc rises with velocity but never without limit. This picture leads to film and surface-renewal theories in Chapter 2.4.

So coefficients are sorted three ways: by position (local or average), by combination (individual film or overall) and by driving force, which also fixes the phase and the unit.

Worked example

Question. Air at 300 K and 1 atm (101.325 kPa) with 30 % relative humidity flows over a water surface, also at 300 K. The vapour pressure of water at 300 K is 3.54 kPa. The gas-side coefficient, measured at low flux, is k′c = 0.020 m/s. Find the evaporation flux in mol/(m²·s) and kg/(m²·h), find kG and ky, and correct for diffusion through stagnant air.

  1. Interface: the gas just above the water is saturated, pAi = 3.54 kPa, so CAi = pAi/(RT) = 3540/(8.314 × 300) = 1.42 mol/m³.
  2. Bulk: 30 % relative humidity gives pA∞ = 0.30 × 3.54 = 1.06 kPa and CA∞ = 0.30 × 1.419 = 0.426 mol/m³.
  3. Flux: NA = kc(CAi − CA∞) = 0.020 × (1.419 − 0.426) = 0.020 × 0.994 = 1.99×10⁻² mol/(m²·s).
  4. In mass per hour: 1.99×10⁻² × 0.018 kg/mol × 3600 s/h = 1.29 kg/(m²·h). The flux runs from the surface into the air, so water evaporates.
  5. Gas-phase forms: kG = kc/(RT) = 0.020/(8.314 × 300) = 8.02×10⁻⁶ mol/(m²·s·Pa) = 0.812 mol/(m²·s·atm), and ky = kG P = 0.812 mol/(m²·s). Check: yAi − yA∞ = (3.54 − 1.06)/101.3 = 0.0245, and 0.812 × 0.0245 = 1.99×10⁻² mol/(m²·s), the same flux.
  6. Stagnant air: pB1 = 101.33 − 3.54 = 97.79 kPa at the surface and pB2 = 101.33 − 1.06 = 100.27 kPa in the bulk, so pBm = (100.27 − 97.79)/ln(100.27/97.79) = 99.02 kPa and P/pBm = 1.023.
  7. Corrected flux: 1.023 × 1.99×10⁻² = 2.03×10⁻² mol/(m²·s), 1.32 kg/(m²·h).

Answer. NA ≈ 2.0×10⁻² mol/(m²·s), about 1.3 kg of water per m² per hour; kG = 8.0×10⁻⁶ mol/(m²·s·Pa) and ky = 0.81 mol/(m²·s). The stagnant-air correction is only 2.3 % here, because water vapour is dilute in the air.

Exam traps

  • Matching coefficient to driving force. kG needs a partial pressure difference in atm; kc needs a concentration difference in mol/m³.
  • Forgetting kc is per unit interfacial area. In a packed tower the measured group is often kc·a, where a is area per m³, with units 1/s.
  • Using the other phase's bulk concentration instead of the interface value. The interface is set by equilibrium (Chapter 2.6).
  • Comparing kG and kL directly. Their driving forces differ; convert both to one overall coefficient based on one phase (Chapter 2.2).

2.2 Individual and overall mass transfer coefficients

In a gas absorber, a solute leaves the gas film and enters the liquid film. Chapter 2.1 gave one coefficient for each film. Plant calculations need one coefficient for the whole gas phase or whole liquid phase, because the interface composition is hard to measure. The individual coefficients add as resistances in series. That gives Ky based on gas mole fraction and Kx based on liquid mole fraction. The same numbers tell whether the gas film or the liquid film controls the rate.

You need the individual coefficient kc from Chapter 2.1 and the interface equilibrium idea from Chapter 2.6.

Two films, two overall coefficients

In the gas film the driving force is y − yi; in the liquid film it is xi − x. At steady state the flux NA is the same through both films. Define ky = NA/(y − yi) and kx = NA/(xi − x). ky is the gas-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s). kx is the liquid-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s). The interface is in equilibrium, yi = m xi, where m is the slope of y* = m x. The interface itself has no storage. The composition change is split between the two films. The interface composition cannot be sampled in a plant, so calculations use bulk compositions. Ky is the overall gas coefficient: NA = Ky(y − y*), where y* = m x is the gas composition in equilibrium with the bulk liquid. Kx is the overall liquid coefficient: NA = Kx(x* − x), where x* = y/m is the liquid composition in equilibrium with the bulk gas. Ky and Kx are bookkeeping devices. They put both film resistances on one driving-force basis.

Adding resistances in series

Add the two film drops on the gas basis. Across the gas film, y − yi = NA/ky. Across the liquid film, yi − y* = m(xi − x) = m NA/kx. Add and compare with NA = Ky(y − y*).

1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx

Ky is the overall gas coefficient, kmol/(m²·s); ky is the gas-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s); kx is the liquid-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s); m is the slope of y* = m x, dimensionless.

On the liquid basis, x* − xi = (y − yi)/m = NA/(m ky) and xi − x = NA/kx. Add and compare with NA = Kx(x* − x).

1/Kx = 1/(m ky) + 1/kx

Kx is the overall liquid coefficient, kmol/(m²·s); the gas term 1/(m ky) converts gas-film resistance to the x basis; the liquid term 1/kx is the liquid-film resistance.

The factor m converts one phase's composition to the other phase's basis. The two overall coefficients are linked by Kx = m Ky. Use Ky when the gas composition is measured; use Kx when the liquid composition is measured. Never mix Δy and Δx in one sum.

When the equilibrium line is curved, m in 1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx is the slope m′ of the chord from (x, y*) to (xi, yi), and m in 1/Kx is the slope m″ of the chord from (xi, yi) to (x*, y). A single m is exact only for a straight line. On a partial-pressure and liquid-concentration basis, with Henry's law p = H C, the same sum reads 1/KG = 1/kG + H/kL.

Which film controls?

Resistance is 1/coefficient. The gas-film share of the resistance behind Ky is (1/ky)/(1/Ky). The liquid-film share is (m/kx)/(1/Ky). The same percentages apply to Kx because Kx = m Ky. If one share is above about 80%, that film controls. Gas-film control means extra liquid mixing gives little gain; increase gas velocity or gas turbulence. Liquid-film control means improve liquid mixing or add a reaction. In the worked example the gas film has 51.6 %, so neither film controls; both must be kept in the design. Watch m, not 1/kx alone. For a very soluble gas, m is small, so m/kx is small and the gas film often controls. For a sparingly soluble gas, m is large, so the liquid term often controls. A scrubber can therefore be gas-film controlled for ammonia and liquid-film controlled for carbon dioxide.

Worked example

Question. A solute is absorbed from a gas into a liquid. The individual coefficients are ky = 0.050 kmol/(m²·s) and kx = 0.080 kmol/(m²·s), each per unit mole-fraction difference, and the equilibrium line is y* = 1.5x. At one point in the column the bulk gas has y = 0.10 and the bulk liquid x = 0.02. Find Ky, Kx, the gas-film share of the resistance, the flux and the interface compositions.

  1. m = 1.5. Gas-film resistance = 1/ky = 1/0.050 = 20 m²·s/kmol.
  2. Liquid-film resistance on the gas basis = m/kx = 1.5/0.080 = 18.75 m²·s/kmol.
  3. 1/Ky = 20 + 18.75 = 38.75 m²·s/kmol. Ky = 1/38.75 = 0.0258 kmol/(m²·s).
  4. 1/Kx = 1/(m ky) + 1/kx = 1/(1.5×0.050) + 1/0.080 = 13.33 + 12.50 = 25.83 m²·s/kmol. Kx = 1/25.83 = 0.0387 kmol/(m²·s).
  5. Gas-film share = 20/38.75 = 0.516 = 51.6%. Liquid-film share = 48.4%.
  6. Overall driving force: y* = 1.5 × 0.02 = 0.030, so NA = Ky(y − y*) = 0.0258 × (0.10 − 0.030) = 1.81×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s).
  7. Interface: yi = y − NA/ky = 0.10 − 1.81×10⁻³/0.050 = 0.0639, and xi = yi/1.5 = 0.0426.
  8. Check on the liquid side: kx(xi − x) = 0.080 × (0.0426 − 0.020) = 1.81×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s). With x* = y/m = 0.0667, Kx(x* − x) = 0.0387 × 0.0467 = 1.81×10⁻³ as well.

Answer. Ky = 0.0258 and Kx = 0.0387 kmol/(m²·s); the gas film carries 51.6 % of the resistance. The flux is 1.81×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s), with yi = 0.0639 and xi = 0.0426.

Exam traps

  • For 1/Ky the liquid term is m/kx. For 1/Kx the gas term is 1/(m ky). Reversing m is the commonest error.
  • Do not add 1/ky and 1/kx directly. They sit on different driving forces. Convert with m first.
  • Ky and Kx have different numerical values and different driving-force bases. A resistance fraction must use the same basis as the coefficient.
  • m is the slope of y* = m x. If the equilibrium line is curved, use the chord slopes m′ (for Ky) and m″ (for Kx); a single m is valid only for a straight line.
  • The interface is in equilibrium, not the bulk phases. y* = m x uses the bulk liquid x, not the interface xi.

2.3 Dimensionless groups and mass transfer correlations

A mass transfer coefficient depends on velocity, length, density, viscosity and diffusivity. Measuring it for every fluid and speed is hopeless. So we pack those variables into three dimensionless numbers — Sherwood, Reynolds and Schmidt — and fit one curve through many experiments. Feed in Re and Sc, read out Sh, then convert to kc.

You need Chapter 2.1 for kc, and the Reynolds number and boundary layer from fluid mechanics.

The three groups

At the surface the flux is still diffusive, NA = kc Δc, but stirring steepens the concentration gradient near the wall and so raises kc. Compare this with the purely diffusive flux DAB Δc/L over the same length; the ratio is the Sherwood number.

Sh = kc L / DAB

Sh is the Sherwood number (dimensionless), kc the mass transfer coefficient in m/s, L the characteristic length in m, DAB the diffusivity of A in B in m²/s.

Reynolds number compares inertia with viscous force and decides whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent. Schmidt number compares momentum spreading with mass spreading.

Re = ρ u L / μ = u L / ν      Sc = μ / (ρ DAB) = ν / DAB

ρ is density in kg/m³, u the free-stream velocity in m/s, μ the viscosity in Pa·s, ν = μ/ρ the kinematic viscosity in m²/s.

Sh does in mass transfer what Nu = hL/k does in heat transfer, and Sc is the mass version of the Prandtl number. Dimensional analysis explains why three groups are enough: kc depends on u, L, ρ, μ and DAB, six quantities in three dimensions (mass, length, time), so the Buckingham π theorem leaves three groups, and Sh = φ(Re, Sc). Experiment supplies the function φ.

Three standard correlations

Steady laminar flow over a flat plate of length L, with Re below about 5×10⁵, gives an average coefficient:

Sh = 0.664 Re1/2 Sc1/3

averaged over the plate length L, with Re = uL/ν and u the free-stream velocity.

Turbulent from the leading edge on the same plate:

Sh = 0.037 Re0.8 Sc1/3

valid roughly from Re = 5×10⁵ to 10⁷; L is the plate length from the leading edge.

A real plate is laminar near the nose and turbulent further on. That mixed plate needs the 871 correction:

Sh = (0.037 Re0.8 − 871) Sc1/3

the same plate and the same L; the 871 is the laminar stretch subtracted out, not a term to add.

For a single sphere of diameter d in a flowing stream:

Sh = 2 + 0.6 Re1/2 Sc1/3

L = d. At Re = 0 it returns Sh = 2, pure diffusion to a sphere in still fluid.

The first two are boundary-layer fits for a flat plate; the sphere fit is an empirical correlation for a different geometry, which is why it carries the additive 2. Sc1/3 appears in all three because the laminar sublayer near the surface controls the mass transfer resistance in each case.

Inside a pipe of diameter d the length is d. In fully developed laminar flow (Re below about 2100) with a constant wall concentration, Sh = 3.66, whatever the velocity; near the entrance Sh is larger. In turbulent flow the wetted-wall column data of Gilliland and Sherwood (vaporisation of liquids into air, Re from about 2000 to 35 000, Sc from 0.6 to 2.5) give:

Sh = 0.023 Re0.83 Sc0.44

Sh = kc d/DAB and Re = ρ u d/μ with d the pipe diameter in m and u the mean velocity in m/s. Linton and Sherwood, using dissolving solid tubes, fitted Sc1/3 in place of Sc0.44 for Sc up to about 3000.

Choosing L and properties

Each correlation fixes its own length and its own property temperature. Check both before substituting anything.

  1. Pick the geometry: flat plate, single sphere or pipe.
  2. Set L in metres: plate length along the flow, sphere diameter, or pipe diameter.
  3. Evaluate ρ, μ and DAB at the film temperature Tf = (Ts + T_∞)/2.
  4. Compute ν = μ/ρ, then Re = uL/ν and Sc = ν/DAB.
  5. Choose the correlation whose Re range contains your Re; compute Sh.
  6. Convert back: kc = Sh DAB / L, in m/s.

For gases Sc usually lies between about 0.2 and 3, near 0.6 for water vapour in air; in liquids Sc reaches hundreds or thousands. Outside the tested Re band a correlation is extrapolation, not prediction. Two correlations may share the algebra but not the length: compare definitions, never symbols.

Worked example

Question. Air at 2 m/s flows over a flat plate 0.5 m long, with DAB = 2.6×10⁻⁵ m²/s and ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Use Sh = 0.664 Re1/2 Sc1/3 to find Re, Sc, Sh and kc.

  1. Plate, so L = 0.5 m.
  2. Re = uL/ν = (2 × 0.5)/(1.5×10⁻⁵) = 6.67×10⁴. Below 5×10⁵, so the laminar fit applies.
  3. Sc = ν/DAB = 1.5×10⁻⁵/2.6×10⁻⁵ = 0.577, and Sc1/3 = 0.832. Also √Re = 258.2.
  4. Sh = 0.664 × 258.2 × 0.832 = 142.7.
  5. kc = Sh DAB/L = 142.7 × 2.6×10⁻⁵/0.5 = 7.42×10⁻³ m/s.

Answer. Re = 6.67×10⁴, Sc = 0.577, Sh = 142.7 and kc = 7.42×10⁻³ m/s.

Worked example 2

Question. Water evaporates from the inside wall of a wetted-wall column, a pipe of diameter 25 mm. Air at 3.0 m/s flows through it, with ν = 1.6×10⁻⁵ m²/s and DAB = 2.6×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Find kc. What would it be at 0.30 m/s?

  1. L = d = 0.025 m. Re = u d/ν = 3.0 × 0.025/1.6×10⁻⁵ = 4690: turbulent, inside the Gilliland–Sherwood range.
  2. Sc = ν/DAB = 1.6/2.6 = 0.615.
  3. Sh = 0.023 × 46900.83 × 0.6150.44 = 0.023 × 1114 × 0.808 = 20.7.
  4. kc = Sh DAB/d = 20.7 × 2.6×10⁻⁵/0.025 = 2.15×10⁻² m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using plate length for a sphere or diameter for a plate. Each correlation defines its own L; a wrong L spoils Re and kc together.
  • Evaluating μ and DAB at the wall or bulk temperature instead of the film temperature Tf.
  • Treating Sh = 0.037 Re0.8 Sc1/3 as a whole-plate average. It assumes turbulence from the leading edge; a plate that starts laminar needs Sh = (0.037 Re0.8 − 871) Sc1/3. The correction is a subtraction, not an added laminar term.
  • Swapping Sh with Nu, or Sc with Pr. The algebra is identical, the symbols are not, and the definition is the first thing marked.

2.4 Film, penetration and surface renewal theories

The mass transfer coefficient kc (m/s) is measured for a given flow, but it is not a fluid property alone. Film, penetration and surface-renewal theories each explain kc by a simple picture of the fluid next to the interface. Film theory uses an equivalent stagnant thickness δ. Penetration theory uses a short exposure time t. Surface-renewal theory uses a fractional renewal rate s. Film theory makes kc proportional to the diffusivity D (m²/s); the other two make it proportional to √D. They guide absorber and packed-tower design, where liquid-film coefficients control transfer of sparingly soluble gases.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the definition of kc, and the topic of molecular diffusion for Fick's law and diffusion in a stagnant layer.

Film theory: equivalent stagnant layer

The film model imagines a stagnant layer of thickness δ next to the interface. The bulk fluid is well mixed, so its concentration Cb is uniform up to the outer edge of the layer. Across the layer, mass moves by molecular diffusion only, and the concentration profile is taken as linear. Fick's law gives the flux as NA = (D/δ)(Cb − Ci). Comparing with NA = kc(Cb − Ci) gives kc = D/δ. Here D is diffusivity (m²/s), δ is the equivalent film thickness (m), and kc is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s). The model is simple and fits two-resistance calculations, but δ is not a measured physical thickness. It is chosen so that D/δ matches an observed kc. The model predicts kc ∝ D: doubling D doubles kc. Real interfaces have eddies that penetrate close to the surface, so the true profile is curved and the film picture is approximate.

kc = D/δ

kc is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); δ is the equivalent film thickness (m).

Penetration theory: short exposure

Penetration theory treats contact as short and unsteady. An eddy brings fresh liquid to the interface. That element stays for an exposure time t (s), then mixes back into the bulk. During t, gas diffuses into a semi-infinite liquid. The average flux over the exposure gives NA = 2√(D/(πt))(Ci − C0), where C0 is the initial solute concentration in the liquid. Therefore kc = 2√(D/(πt)). The factor 2 comes from averaging over the exposure; the instantaneous value is √(D/(πt)). The model predicts kc ∝ √D. If D becomes four times larger, kc doubles. Penetration theory suits short-contact equipment: packed towers, falling films and bubbles. The exposure time is set by flow, not by a fitted film thickness. Its weakness is that all surface elements are given the same t, which is rarely true in a stirred vessel.

kc = 2√(D/(πt))

kc is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); t is exposure time (s); π is 3.1416.

Surface renewal: random replacement

Surface renewal theory removes the single exposure time. Fresh liquid reaches the surface at random times, so elements have a range of ages. Danckwerts defined s, the fractional surface renewal rate (s⁻¹). In a small time interval, a fraction s dt of the surface is replaced by fresh liquid. Integrating over all ages gives NA = √(D s)(Ci − C0), so kc = √(D s). A large s means fast renewal and a large kc. The D dependence is again √D, not D. This single fact separates the film model from the other two. For a sparingly soluble gas in water, liquid-film resistance often controls, and measured kc values usually follow √D better than D. The reason is eddy diffusion near the interface: eddies carry solute close to the surface and make a fixed stagnant film unrealistic. Film theory remains useful as a limiting picture and as the basis of two-resistance design.

kc = √(D s)

kc is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); s is the fractional surface renewal rate (s⁻¹).

Measured exponents of D usually fall between 0.5 and 1. Toor and Marchello's film-penetration model covers that range: surface elements that stay only briefly behave as in penetration theory (kc ∝ √D), while elements that stay long enough reach the steady linear profile of film theory (kc ∝ D).

Worked example

Question. Oxygen (D = 2.1×10⁻⁹ m²/s) is absorbed into water in a stirred vessel, and the measured liquid-film coefficient is kL = 4.0×10⁻⁵ m/s. (a) Fit each theory to this measurement: find the film thickness δ, the exposure time t and the renewal rate s. (b) Predict kL for a larger solute molecule with D = 1.2×10⁻⁹ m²/s in the same vessel and stirring.

  1. Film: kL = D/δ, so δ = D/kL = 2.1×10⁻⁹/4.0×10⁻⁵ = 5.25×10⁻⁵ m = 52.5 µm.
  2. Penetration: kL = 2√(D/(πt)), so t = 4D/(π kL²) = 4 × 2.1×10⁻⁹/(π × 1.6×10⁻⁹) = 1.67 s.
  3. Surface renewal: kL = √(D s), so s = kL²/D = 1.6×10⁻⁹/2.1×10⁻⁹ = 0.762 s⁻¹.
  4. The flow is unchanged, so δ, t and s stay the same. Film: kL = 1.2×10⁻⁹/5.25×10⁻⁵ = 2.29×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  5. Penetration: kL = 2√(1.2×10⁻⁹/(π × 1.67)) = 3.02×10⁻⁵ m/s. Surface renewal: kL = √(1.2×10⁻⁹ × 0.762) = 3.02×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  6. Shortcut: penetration and renewal both give kL ∝ √D, so kL = 4.0×10⁻⁵ × √(1.2/2.1) = 3.02×10⁻⁵ m/s; film gives 4.0×10⁻⁵ × 1.2/2.1 = 2.29×10⁻⁵ m/s.

Answer. δ = 52.5 µm, t = 1.67 s and s = 0.76 s⁻¹ all reproduce the oxygen measurement. For the second solute film theory predicts 2.3×10⁻⁵ m/s and the other two 3.0×10⁻⁵ m/s, a 32 % difference. Measuring the second solute is how the theories are told apart: one data point fits any of them.

Exam traps

  • Treating δ as a real measured stagnant film. δ is an equivalent thickness chosen to match kc; it changes with flow and is not a property of the fluid.
  • A unit slip: δ in mm must be converted to m before D/δ. With D in m²/s, δ in m gives kc in m/s.
  • Using kc ∝ D for all data. Film theory doubles kc when D doubles; penetration and surface renewal increase it by only √2.
  • Omitting the factor 2 in penetration theory. The average flux over exposure time t has 2√(D/(πt)); dropping 2 halves the predicted kc.
  • Confusing surface renewal rate s with 1/t. In penetration theory t is a fixed exposure time; in surface renewal s is a random replacement rate.

2.5 Analogies between momentum, heat and mass transfer

Momentum, heat and mass all cross the same thin fluid layer near a wall. In turbulent flow the same eddies carry all three. This shared motion lets one measured pressure drop predict a heat-transfer coefficient or a mass-transfer coefficient kc. Engineers use it when a correlation is missing or when a quick check is needed, for example to estimate evaporation from a straight smooth duct using the skin-friction part of its pressure drop.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for kc and Chapter 2.3 for Sh, Re and Sc, plus basic fluid mechanics (friction factor) and heat transfer (Nu, Pr).

The shared eddy diffusivity

In turbulent flow, a fluid packet carries momentum, enthalpy and solute together. Near a wall the total shear stress, heat flux and mass flux each have a molecular part and an eddy part. The eddy diffusivity for momentum, εM (m²/s), for heat, εH, and for mass, εD, is nearly the same size when the turbulence is fully mixed. That shared eddy motion is the physical basis of every analogy here.

The three fluxes are τ = ρ(ν + εM) du/dy, q/A = −ρ cp(α + εH) dT/dy and NA = −(DAB + εD) dCA/dy. Here τ is shear stress (Pa), q/A is heat flux (W/m²), NA is molar flux (mol/(m²·s)), ν is kinematic viscosity (m²/s), α is thermal diffusivity (m²/s), DAB is molecular diffusivity (m²/s), and y is distance from the wall (m).

Two dimensionless groups compare molecular transport: Pr = ν/α = μ cp/k and Sc = ν/DAB = μ/(ρDAB). Pr is the Prandtl number (dimensionless), Sc the Schmidt number, μ viscosity (Pa·s), cp specific heat (J/(kg·K)), k thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)).

St = f/2 (for Pr = 1)

St = h/(ρ cp u) is the Stanton number; h is the heat-transfer coefficient (W/(m²·K)); u is bulk velocity (m/s); f is the Fanning friction factor, defined by τw = f (1/2) ρ u², with τw the wall shear stress (Pa).

This is the Reynolds analogy. It says the dimensionless temperature profile and the dimensionless velocity profile have the same shape when Pr = 1. It is quick but narrow: it ignores the laminar sublayer.

Chilton-Colburn j-factors

Chilton and Colburn kept the same idea but absorbed the molecular-property effect into j-factors. The heat j-factor is jH = Nu/(Re Pr1/3). The mass j-factor is jD = Sh/(Re Sc1/3). Here Nu = hL/kf is the Nusselt number, Sh = kcL/DAB is the Sherwood number, and Re = ρuL/μ is the Reynolds number; L is the characteristic length (m), kf is fluid thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)), and kc is the mass-transfer coefficient (m/s). For a flat plate, L is the plate length. For pipe flow, the characteristic length is the pipe diameter D, so use Re = ρuD/μ, Sh = kcD/DAB, and Nu = hD/kf.

jH = Nu/(Re Pr1/3) = f/2,   jD = Sh/(Re Sc1/3) = f/2

jH and jD are dimensionless; f is the Fanning friction factor; all other symbols are defined above. Hence jH = jD = f/2.

The equality is used to estimate kc from a measured pressure drop. Follow these steps.

  1. Measure Δp (Pa) over a smooth pipe length L (m) at bulk velocity u (m/s) and density ρ (kg/m³); D is the pipe diameter (m) and L is the length between pressure taps.
  2. Get the Fanning friction factor from Δp = 2f(L/D)ρu², so f = Δp D/(2Lρu²).
  3. Set jD = f/2.
  4. Compute Sc = μ/(ρDAB) and Re = ρuD/μ. Use D, not L, as the characteristic length for pipe flow.
  5. Compute Sh = jD Re Sc1/3.
  6. Compute kc = Sh DAB/D.

For a flat plate, use the local or average skin-friction coefficient from boundary-layer tables in place of the pipe formula. The j-factor route is the same. For a flat plate use L as the characteristic length in Nu = hL/kf, Sh = kcL/DAB, and Re = ρuL/μ. The analogy is popular in compact heat exchangers and drying because pressure drop is easy to measure, while kc is not.

Refinements and limits

Prandtl-Taylor and von Kármán refined the analogy by keeping the laminar sublayer. Prandtl-Taylor gives St = (f/2)/[1 + 5√(f/2)(Pr − 1)] for heat transfer, and the same form with Sc for mass transfer. von Kármán adds the buffer layer and gives a better fit when Pr or Sc is far from 1, such as oil (Pr about 1000) or a liquid with Sc in the thousands, such as salts or sugars in water. Most gas pairs have Sc between about 0.5 and 2, close enough to 1 for the simple analogy to work well.

The analogy assumes skin friction, not form drag. A pressure drop across a valve, a bend or a rough packing includes form drag, so the f you calculate is too large. The predicted kc is then too high. It also assumes small mass-transfer rates. Strong blowing or suction changes the velocity profile and breaks jD = f/2; use a high-mass-transfer correction or a direct correlation in that case.

Worked example

Question. Air at 298 K (ρ = 1.18 kg/m³, μ = 1.85×10⁻⁵ Pa·s) flows at 5.0 m/s through a smooth tube of diameter 50 mm whose inner wall is coated with naphthalene (DAB = 6.1×10⁻⁶ m²/s in air). The pressure drop over a straight 3.0 m length is 24.9 Pa. Use the Chilton–Colburn analogy to estimate kc, and compare with the Linton–Sherwood pipe correlation Sh = 0.023 Re0.83 Sc1/3.

  3. jD = f/2 = 3.52×10⁻³.
  4. ν = μ/ρ = 1.57×10⁻⁵ m²/s, so Sc = ν/DAB = 1.57×10⁻⁵/6.1×10⁻⁶ = 2.57 and Sc1/3 = 1.37.
  5. Sh = jD Re Sc1/3 = 3.52×10⁻³ × 1.59×10⁴ × 1.37 = 76.8, so kc = Sh DAB/d = 76.8 × 6.1×10⁻⁶/0.050 = 9.37×10⁻³ m/s.
  6. Linton–Sherwood: Sh = 0.023 × (1.59×10⁴)0.83 × 1.37 = 0.023 × 3078 × 1.37 = 97.0, kc = 1.18×10⁻² m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using the Darcy friction factor instead of the Fanning factor. Darcy f is four times Fanning f; jD = f/2 needs Fanning. A factor-of-four error gives kc four times too large.
  • Taking f from a pressure drop that includes form drag. The analogy uses wall skin friction only. Valves, bends and rough packings give a high f and a high predicted kc.
  • Applying jD = jH = f/2 at high mass-transfer rates. Blowing or suction alters the boundary layer. Use a correction or a direct correlation.
  • Mixing the exponents: jD uses Sc1/3 in the denominator; the mass Stanton number follows jD = Stm Sc2/3. Write the definition before substituting.
  • Forgetting units: Sh and Re are dimensionless; DAB is m²/s; L is m; kc comes out in m/s.

2.6 Interphase mass transfer and two-resistance theory

Two phases touch at an interface. The gas and liquid are not in equilibrium in their bulk, but they are in equilibrium at the interface itself. Solute crosses a gas film and a liquid film in series. The two-resistance theory uses this picture to find the interface composition, the flux, and which film controls. It is the working method behind absorber and stripper calculations, and it explains why ammonia leaves air into water easily while oxygen does not.

You need individual and overall mass transfer coefficients, and the idea of an equilibrium line y* = m x from solubility data.

The interface is in equilibrium

Let y be the solute mole fraction in the bulk gas and x the solute mole fraction in the bulk liquid. At the interface these become yi and xi. The two-resistance model makes three assumptions. First, the gas and liquid at the interface are in equilibrium, so yi = m xi. Here m is the slope of the equilibrium line y = m x on a mole-fraction plot. Second, no solute accumulates at the interface. Third, the same flux NA passes through both films.

Each film carries flux with its own coefficient: NA = ky(y − yi) = kx(xi − x). ky is the gas-film coefficient and kx the liquid-film coefficient, both in kmol/(m²·s) per unit mole-fraction difference. The equality is the key equation. It says the gas-side driving force (y − yi) and the liquid-side driving force (xi − x) adjust until the two fluxes match.

  1. Write the interface equilibrium relation yi = m xi.
  2. Substitute into the equal-flux equation: ky(y − m xi) = kx(xi − x).
  3. Solve for xi.
  4. Find yi = m xi.
  5. Put either side back into the flux equation to get NA.

On the y-x plot the interface is where the equilibrium line meets a straight line from the bulk point (x, y). That line has slope −kx/ky, because (y − yi)/(x − xi) = −kx/ky.

NA = ky(y − yi) = kx(xi − x)

NA is the solute flux in kmol/(m²·s); ky is the gas-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s); kx is the liquid-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s); y and x are bulk mole fractions; yi and xi are interface mole fractions.

Overall driving forces and resistances

Designers prefer one driving force, not two. Define y* = m x: the gas mole fraction that would be in equilibrium with the bulk liquid. Define x* = y/m: the liquid mole fraction that would be in equilibrium with the bulk gas. Then the overall gas driving force is y − y*, and the overall liquid driving force is x* − x.

Write NA = Ky(y − y*) and NA = Kx(x* − x). Ky and Kx are overall coefficients. They are built from the film coefficients by adding resistances in series.

1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx

Ky is the overall gas-phase coefficient in kmol/(m²·s); ky and kx are the individual film coefficients; m is the slope of the equilibrium line y = m x.

1/Kx = 1/(m ky) + 1/kx

Kx is the overall liquid-phase coefficient in kmol/(m²·s); the other symbols are as above.

The factor m appears because kx uses Δx while Ky uses Δy. This is why ky and kx cannot simply be added. The gas-film share of the total resistance is (1/ky)/(1/ky + m/kx). The liquid-film share is (m/kx)/(1/ky + m/kx).

Read the shares before designing. If the gas share is near 1, the gas film controls: changing gas turbulence helps, changing liquid mixing hardly helps. If the liquid share is near 1, the liquid film controls. Ammonia in water is gas-film controlled: ammonia is very soluble, m is small, and m/kx is small. Oxygen in water is liquid-film controlled: oxygen is sparingly soluble, m is large, and m/kx dominates. The same reasoning tells you where to spend pressure drop and packing.

Reading the plot and checking the answer

The figure uses y = 2x, bulk gas y = 0.10, bulk liquid x = 0.02, ky = 0.05 and kx = 0.08 kmol/(m²·s). The bulk point is above the equilibrium line, so solute goes from gas to liquid. The interface point (0.03667, 0.07333) sits on the equilibrium line. Notice that xi > x and yi < y: the interface is richer in solute than the bulk liquid and poorer than the bulk gas. That is how both films keep positive driving forces.

The tie line from (0.02, 0.10) to (0.03667, 0.07333) has slope −0.08/0.05 = −1.6. Check: (0.07333 − 0.10)/(0.03667 − 0.02) = −1.6. Any other interface point on y = 2x would give unequal fluxes. The equality of fluxes picks exactly one point.

Use the same check in exams: compute NA from the gas side and from the liquid side. They must match. If they do not, you have mixed bulk and interface values, or forgotten m.

Worked example

Question. In an absorption column, bulk gas y = 0.10 and bulk liquid x = 0.02 (mole fractions). The equilibrium line is y* = 2x. The individual coefficients are ky = 0.05 kmol/(m²·s) and kx = 0.08 kmol/(m²·s). Find the interface compositions and the flux.

  1. At the interface, equilibrium holds: yi = 2 xi.
  2. Set equal fluxes: 0.05(0.10 − yi) = 0.08(xi − 0.02).
  3. Substitute yi = 2 xi: 0.05(0.10 − 2 xi) = 0.08(xi − 0.02).
  4. Expand: 0.005 − 0.10 xi = 0.08 xi − 0.0016.
  5. Collect: 0.0066 = 0.18 xi, so xi = 0.03667.
  6. Then yi = 2(0.03667) = 0.07333.
  7. Gas-side flux: NA = 0.05(0.10 − 0.07333) = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s). Liquid side gives the same: 0.08(0.03667 − 0.02) = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s).
  8. Check with the overall coefficient: 1/Ky = 1/0.05 + 2/0.08 = 45, so Ky = 0.0222 kmol/(m²·s); y* = 2 × 0.02 = 0.04, and Ky(y − y*) = 0.0222 × 0.06 = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s), the same flux without any interface values.

Answer. xi = 0.03667, yi = 0.07333, and NA = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Assuming the bulk gas and bulk liquid are in equilibrium. Equilibrium holds only at the interface; y* = m x is a hypothetical gas in equilibrium with the bulk liquid, not the actual bulk gas.
  • Mixing interface and bulk values: using y* = m xi instead of y* = m x, or yi = m x instead of yi = m xi.
  • Adding ky and kx directly. They use different driving forces, so the m factor must appear: 1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx, not 1/ky + 1/kx.
  • Placing the interface point off the equilibrium line. It lies on the equilibrium line, exactly where the line of slope −kx/ky drawn from the bulk point meets it.
  • Forgetting units: ky and kx are in kmol/(m²·s) per unit mole-fraction difference, and mole fractions have no unit. Check that each NA comes out in kmol/(m²·s).

2.7 Equilibrium stages, cascades and stage efficiency

An equilibrium stage is an idealised contactor in which the two leaving streams are allowed to reach equilibrium. Real trays cannot wait that long. This chapter defines the ideal stage, measures how far a real tray falls short with the Murphree efficiency, links ideal stages to actual trays with the overall efficiency, and shows how cascades of stages are counted with operating lines and the Kremser equation. It is the bridge between equilibrium data and the number of trays you buy.

You need Chapter 2.6 for interphase equilibrium and the idea of resistances, and an equilibrium curve y* against x for the system.

Ideal stage and Murphree efficiency

A stage is one contacting unit: vapor bubbles through liquid on a tray, or liquid sprays into gas. In an ideal equilibrium stage, the vapor and liquid leaving the stage are in equilibrium. The stage is perfectly mixed and contact time is infinite. No real tray reaches this limit. The vapour leaving a real tray, yn, falls short of the equilibrium value yn*.

EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1)

EM is the Murphree vapor efficiency (dimensionless); yn is the mole fraction of solute in vapor leaving stage n; yn+1 is the mole fraction in vapor entering from below; yn* is the mole fraction in vapor that would be in equilibrium with the liquid leaving stage n, at the tray temperature and pressure. All y values are mole fractions.

EM = 1 means the tray is ideal. EM = 0 means no change in vapor composition. The value is per tray and per component. A liquid-phase Murphree efficiency uses x values and is defined similarly; do not mix the two.

Eo = Nideal / Nactual

Eo is the overall efficiency (dimensionless); Nideal is the number of equilibrium stages from a stepping method; Nactual is the number of real trays. Rearranged, Nactual = Nideal/Eo, rounded up to a whole tray.

Eo and EM measure different things, so Eo can be larger or smaller than EM. If every tray has the same EM and the equilibrium and operating lines are both straight, Lewis showed that the two are linked through λ = mV/L, the ratio of the equilibrium slope to the operating-line slope:

Eo = ln[1 + EM(λ − 1)] / ln λ

λ = mV/L (dimensionless), with m the equilibrium slope and V and L the vapour and liquid molar flows. Eo exceeds EM when λ is above 1, as in a stripper; it falls below EM when λ is below 1, as in most absorbers; the two are equal when λ = 1. Entrainment and weeping lower both.

Cascades: cross-current and counter-current

A cascade is a series of stages. In cross-current contact, fresh solvent enters each stage and each extract is removed. In counter-current contact, liquid and vapor flow in opposite directions through the cascade. Counter-current is the usual choice because the average driving force is larger; a given separation needs fewer stages.

For a counter-current cascade, number stages from the top down. Vapor enters stage n from below at yn+1 and leaves at yn. Liquid enters from above at xn−1 and leaves at xn. A solute balance around stage n gives V(yn+1 − yn) = L(xn − xn−1), where V is vapor molar flow (kmol/s), L is liquid molar flow (kmol/s), and x and y are mole fractions. Rearranged, this is the operating line y = (L/V)x + b. The line is straight when L and V are constant.

Graphical stepping: plot the equilibrium curve y* against x and the operating line on the same axes. Start at the bottom composition, step horizontally to the equilibrium curve for one ideal stage, then vertically back to the operating line. Each pair of steps is one ideal stage. Count until the top composition is reached. These are ideal stages, not real trays.

Kremser equation for a fixed stage count

Kremser gives the fraction absorbed when the stage count N is fixed and the equilibrium line is straight. Use solute-free mole ratios Y and X to keep the balance simple. The equilibrium relation is Y* = mX, where m is the dimensionless slope. Define absorption factor A = L/(mG), where L is liquid molar flow (kmol/s) and G is gas molar flow (kmol/s).

(YN+1 − Y1) / (YN+1 − mX0) = (AN+1 − A) / (AN+1 − 1)

YN+1 is the solute-free mole ratio in entering gas; Y1 is the solute-free mole ratio in leaving gas; X0 is the solute-free mole ratio in entering liquid; N is the number of ideal stages; A is the absorption factor (dimensionless). For A = 1, the fraction absorbed is N/(N+1). For stripping, replace A by S = 1/A and swap gas and liquid labels.

A larger A means more liquid per unit gas, so the same stages remove more solute. The equation assumes constant L, G and m, and isothermal operation. It counts ideal stages. To get real trays, divide N by Eo. The Kremser result is a stage count; the Murphree efficiency is a tray composition ratio. They answer different questions.

Worked example

Question. On tray n of a distillation column, for the more volatile component, vapor entering from below has yn+1 = 0.10 and vapor leaving has yn = 0.16 (mole fractions). The vapor leaving would be in equilibrium with the liquid leaving the tray at yn* = 0.20. (a) Find the Murphree vapor efficiency. (b) The ideal stage count is 10 and the overall efficiency is 0.60. Find the actual number of stages.

  1. Write the Murphree vapor efficiency: EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1).
  2. Substitute the compositions: yn − yn+1 = 0.16 − 0.10 = 0.06; yn* − yn+1 = 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10. Both are mole fractions, so the ratio is dimensionless.
  3. EM = 0.06/0.10 = 0.60, or 60%.
  4. Write the overall efficiency: Eo = Nideal/Nactual, so Nactual = Nideal/Eo.
  5. Nactual = 10/0.60 = 16.7. A real column has whole trays, so use 17 stages.

Answer. The Murphree vapor efficiency is 0.60 (60%), and 17 actual stages are needed.

Worked example 2

Question. An absorber treats 100 kmol/h of solute-free gas with YN+1 = 0.050 (kmol solute per kmol carrier) using 240 kmol/h of solute-free solvent (X0 = 0). The equilibrium line is Y* = 1.6X. How many ideal stages recover 95 % of the solute, and how many real trays at Eo = 0.65?

  1. A = L/(mG) = 240/(1.6 × 100) = 1.50.
  2. Fraction absorbed 0.95, so Y1 = 0.05 × 0.050 = 0.0025.
  3. Solve the Kremser equation for N: N = ln[((YN+1 − mX0)/(Y1 − mX0))(1 − 1/A) + 1/A]/ln A = ln[20 × 0.333 + 0.667]/ln 1.5 = ln 7.33/0.405 = 4.91.
  4. Check with N = 5: (1.56 − 1.5)/(1.56 − 1) = (11.39 − 1.5)/(11.39 − 1) = 0.952, just over 95 %.
  5. Real trays: 4.91/0.65 = 7.6, rounded up to 8.

Answer. About 4.9 ideal stages (5 give 95.2 % recovery) and 8 real trays.

Exam traps

  • Using yn* with the entering liquid or entering vapor. Murphree efficiency uses the vapor in equilibrium with the liquid leaving the tray.
  • Confusing Murphree efficiency with overall efficiency. Murphree compares compositions on one tray; overall compares ideal stage count to actual tray count.
  • Multiplying Nideal by Eo instead of dividing. A column with lower efficiency needs more actual trays: Nactual = Nideal/Eo.
  • Leaving the stage count as a decimal. Trays are whole items, so round the actual count up.
  • Writing A upside down in Kremser. For absorption A = L/(mG), not mG/L.

2.8 Stage-wise and differential contactors

Two contactor families answer one height question. Trays and mixers give discrete stages: each stage mixes the phases and then separates them. Packed towers give continuous contact: gas and liquid touch over every metre of packing. Tray height comes from stage count and stage efficiency; packed height comes from Z = HTU × NTU. Absorbers, strippers and distillation columns use both. This chapter shows how to turn a required separation into metres of contactor, and why HETP and HTU are different numbers even though both have units of length.

You need Chapter 2.2 for overall mass transfer coefficients and Chapter 2.7 for ideal stages and stage efficiency.

Stage-wise contactors: trays and mixers

In a stage-wise contactor, the fluids meet in a tray, a mixer or a mixer-settler, then leave. On an ideal stage the leaving streams are in equilibrium. A real stage is not perfect, so it needs an efficiency below 1.

Let Nactual be the number of real trays, Nideal the number of ideal stages, E the overall stage efficiency, and Z the tray-section height in metres. Then Nideal = E Nactual. The height per real tray is Z/Nactual. HETP is the height equivalent to a theoretical plate, so HETP = Z/Nideal = Z/(E Nactual).

HETP = Z/Nideal = Z/(E Nactual)

HETP is height equivalent to one theoretical plate (m); Z is tray-section height (m); Nideal is ideal stages; E is overall stage efficiency; Nactual is real trays.

Efficiency E is rarely 1. Liquid back-mixing, short contact time and poor vapour distribution lower E. A column therefore needs more real trays than ideal stages. If E = 0.5, ten ideal stages need twenty real trays.

Tray spacing sets the real height. Tray spacing is usually 0.45 m to 0.6 m, and may range from about 0.15 m to 0.9 m; the number is chosen from hydraulics, not from mass transfer alone. See Chapter 2.7 for stage efficiency.

Differential contactors: HTU × NTU

A packed tower has no trays. Design uses two lengths. HTU is the height of one transfer unit; NTU is the number of transfer units. The packed height is Z = HTU × NTU.

Z = HTU × NTU

Z is packed height (m); HTU is height of a transfer unit (m); NTU is number of transfer units (dimensionless).

HTU = G/(Kya)

G is molar gas flux (kmol/(m²·s)); Kya is volumetric overall gas-phase coefficient (kmol/(m³·s)); a is interfacial area per m³ of packing (m²/m³).

NTU measures how hard the separation is. For absorption of a dilute solute, NOG = ∫ dy/(y − y*), taken from y2, the gas leaving at the top, to y1, the gas entering at the bottom. Here y is the gas mole fraction and y* the gas mole fraction in equilibrium with the liquid at the same height. In absorption y > y* everywhere, so the integrand and NOG are positive. When the operating and equilibrium lines are both straight the integral has a closed form:

NOG = (y1 − y2)/(y − y*)lm

(y − y*)lm = [(y1 − y1*) − (y2 − y2*)]/ln[(y1 − y1*)/(y2 − y2*)] is the log mean of the driving forces at the two ends; subscript 1 is the bottom and 2 the top of the tower.

HTU measures packing and flow quality. A small HTU means good contact and a short tower. NTU measures the duty. This is the same overall coefficient idea as Chapter 2.2.

Same question, two designs

Both methods answer one question: what height is needed for a required separation? For trays, find Nideal, divide by E to get Nactual, then multiply by tray spacing. For packing, find NTU by the integral, get HTU from a correlation or measured Kya, then multiply.

Worked example

Question. A packed absorber takes gas with y1 = 0.020 down to y2 = 0.0010 using solute-free liquid (x2 = 0) at L/G = 2.0. The equilibrium line is y* = 1.2x. The gas molar flux is G = 0.020 kmol/(m²·s) and Kya = 0.050 kmol/(m³·s). Find HOG, NOG and the packed height, and check the height through HETP.

  4. NOG = (0.020 − 0.0010)/3.53×10⁻³ = 5.38.

Answer. HOG = 0.40 m, NOG = 5.38 and Z = 2.15 m. The same tower holds 4.21 ideal stages of HETP 0.51 m: more transfer units than stages, because λ < 1.

Exam traps

  • HETP is height per ideal stage; HTU is height per transfer unit. They are not interchangeable.
  • In the NTU integral, divide by y − y*, the local overall driving force, not by y alone, and integrate from the outlet y2 up to the inlet y1.
  • Kya is volumetric, per m³ of packed volume. Ky without a is per m² of interface and cannot be used directly in HTU = G/(Kya).
  • Stage efficiency is E = Nideal/Nactual. Actual trays = ideal/E, so E below 1 always raises the tray count.
  • The terminal compositions in NTU are gas mole fractions yin and yout, not tray numbers or liquid compositions.

2.9 Mass transfer with chemical reaction

When a gas dissolves in a liquid that also reacts with it, the liquid film near the interface carries diffusion and reaction together. The reaction eats the dissolved gas, keeps the interfacial gradient steep, and pushes the flux above the physical value. The ratio is the enhancement factor E. The Hatta number Ha compares reaction speed with diffusion speed and decides the regime. CO₂ scrubbing with amine and H₂S scrubbing with alkali depend on this effect.

You need: Chapter 2.4 for the film picture and kL, Chapter 2.6 for film resistances and interface equilibrium, and first-order kinetics.

Reaction inside the film steepens the gradient

Start with physical absorption. Gas A dissolves at the interface at CAi and diffuses through a liquid film of thickness δ into the bulk at CAb. The flux is NA = kL(CAi − CAb), with NA in mol/(m²·s), kL in m/s and C in mol/m³. The profile is a straight line, and the flux equals DA times the slope at the interface.

Now let A react first order in the liquid, rate = k·CA with k in s⁻¹. Inside the film A is removed by reaction as well as diffusion. The profile sags below the straight line and can reach zero before the film edge (figure). The flux is still NA = −DA(dCA/dx) at x = 0, so a steeper starting slope means a bigger flux.

E = NA with reaction ÷ NA without reaction   (same CAi, same kL)

E is the enhancement factor, dimensionless and ≥ 1; NA is the molar flux of A in mol/(m²·s); both fluxes are taken at the same interfacial concentration and the same physical kL.

Keep two effects apart. Reaction inside the film raises E. Reaction in the bulk lowers CAb towards zero, which protects the driving force (CAi − CAb) but does not raise E. A slow reaction gives E ≈ 1 and still helps by keeping the driving force at its maximum.

The Hatta number sorts the regimes

Ha = √(k·DA) ÷ kL

Ha is the Hatta number (dimensionless); k is the first-order rate constant in s⁻¹; DA is the liquid diffusivity of A in m²/s; kL is the physical liquid film coefficient in m/s, the value you would have with no reaction.

The film thickness is roughly δ = DA/kL, so Ha ≈ √(k·δ²/DA) = δ ÷ √(DA/k). The length √(DA/k) is the distance A travels before it reacts. Ha measures film thickness in units of that length. Big Ha means the reaction wins.

E = Ha ÷ tanh(Ha)

E is the enhancement factor for a first-order reaction in the film; tanh is the hyperbolic tangent. For Ha > 3, tanh(Ha) ≈ 1 and E ≈ Ha. For Ha < 0.3, tanh(Ha) ≈ Ha and E ≈ 1.

The fast pseudo-first-order result needs 3 < Ha << E∞. As Ha approaches E∞, a reaction plane forms and E saturates at E∞ = 1 + (DB·CB)/(b·DA·CAi), where CB is bulk concentration of B in mol/m³, DB its liquid diffusivity in m²/s, and b the moles of B per mole of A. More B or faster diffusion of B is the only help.

Where it is used: reactive absorption

Carbon dioxide scrubbing with aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) is the standard example. The reaction is second order, but with the amine in large excess it behaves as pseudo-first order with k = k2CMEA. With k2 of a few thousand m³/(kmol·s) at 25 °C and a few kmol/m³ of amine, k is of order 10⁴ s⁻¹, and with DA near 1.5×10⁻⁹ m²/s and kL near 10⁻⁴ m/s, Ha comes out at several tens. Then E ≈ Ha, and the liquid-film resistance drops from 1/kL to 1/(E·kL). The tower height falls by about E only when the liquid film controls; with a strong gas-film resistance the height hardly changes.

Hydrogen sulphide in dilute caustic soda behaves differently. The proton transfer is essentially instantaneous, so the column runs at E∞, limited by how fast OH⁻ diffuses to the reaction plane. Fresh caustic enters at the top and loads on the way down; near the bottom, where B is lowest and the gas richest, E∞ falls.

  • Design flux: NA = E·kL·CAi, valid only when CAb ≈ 0 (fast regime).
  • kL must be the physical coefficient from a correlation (Chapter 2.3), never back-calculated from an experiment with reaction.
  • Raising liquid rate raises kL, lowers Ha and lowers E. Each extra unit of pumping buys less than one unit of flux.
  • Interfacial CAi comes from Henry's law and gas-side resistance (Chapter 2.6).

Worked example

Question. A gas A dissolves in a liquid in which it undergoes a first-order reaction with rate constant k = 10 s⁻¹. The liquid diffusivity of A is DA = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/s and the physical liquid-film coefficient is kL = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m/s. The interfacial concentration is CAi = 0.05 mol/m³ and the bulk concentration of A is negligible. (a) Find the Hatta number. (b) Name the regime and find the enhancement factor. (c) Find the flux with and without reaction.

  1. Ha = √(k·DA)/kL. k·DA = 10 × 2×10⁻⁹ = 2×10⁻⁸ m²/s².
  2. √(2×10⁻⁸) = 1.414×10⁻⁴ m/s.
  3. Ha = 1.414×10⁻⁴ ÷ 1.0×10⁻⁵ = 14.14.
  4. Ha > 3: fast pseudo-first-order; CAb ≈ 0.
  5. E = Ha/tanh(Ha); tanh(14.14) ≈ 1, so E = 14.14.
  6. N⁰ = kL·CAi = 1.0×10⁻⁵ × 0.05 = 5.0×10⁻⁷ mol/(m²·s).
  7. N = E·N⁰ = 14.14 × 5.0×10⁻⁷ = 7.07×10⁻⁶ mol/(m²·s).

Answer. Ha = 14.14, the reaction is fast pseudo-first-order, E = 14.14, and the flux rises from 5.0×10⁻⁷ to 7.07×10⁻⁶ mol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Using the gas-side coefficient in Ha. kL and DA are liquid-side values; putting kG or a gas diffusivity gives a meaningless number.
  • Unit slips: k in s⁻¹, DA in m²/s, kL in m/s. Mixing cm²/s with m/s shifts Ha by a factor of 100, because 1 cm²/s = 10⁻⁴ m²/s and the square root halves that power of ten. A factor of 100 can flip the regime.
  • Quoting E ≈ Ha everywhere. It holds only for 3 < Ha << E∞; below use E = Ha/tanh(Ha); at the limit use E∞ = 1 + (DB·CB)/(b·DA·CAi).
  • Treating E as a ratio of measured kL values from two runs. E is a flux ratio at the same CAi and physical kL.
  • Thinking a slow reaction is useless because E ≈ 1. It keeps CAb near zero, so the full driving force survives — a bulk effect, not film enhancement.

Takeaways

  1. A convective mass transfer coefficient kc turns a complex flow problem into a simple flux equation NA = kc ΔCA.
  2. Individual film coefficients add as resistances; the overall coefficient depends on which phase's driving force you choose.
  3. Dimensionless correlations write Sh as a function of Re, Sc and geometry, so one measured system predicts another.
  4. Film theory makes kc proportional to D; penetration and surface-renewal theories make it proportional to √D, which matches data at gas–liquid surfaces.
  5. Momentum, heat and mass analogies let a friction factor or heat transfer coefficient estimate kc.
  6. At the interface the two phases are in equilibrium, so yi = m xi, and the two film resistances set the flux.
  7. An ideal stage is an equilibrium stage; stage efficiency converts ideal stages into real trays.
  8. Tray columns are counted in ideal stages divided by an overall efficiency; packed columns are sized by Z = HOG × NOG.
  9. A reaction in the liquid film can raise flux by E, and Ha tells whether the reaction is slow or fast.

Understand Define the convective mass transfer coefficient kc for transfer between a solid surface and a flowing fluid. Write the flux equation, state the unit of every term, and give the equivalent gas-phase forms. Illustrate with a benzoic acid plate (solubility 3.44 kg/m³ in water, M = 122.1 kg/kmol) dissolving into water with kc = 2.0×10⁻⁵ m/s. 10 marks

Points to cover. Definition: flux per unit concentration difference; NA = kc(CAi − CA∞) with units; Why kc replaces an unknown film thickness; Gas forms kG and ky and their conversion; Stagnant-B and equimolar types; Worked number for the benzoic acid plate.

Model answer.

  1. When a fluid flows past a surface that gives up or takes up species A, the flux depends on the flow, the geometry and the fluid properties, and no film thickness can be measured. The convective mass transfer coefficient kc lumps all of that into one number, defined as the molar flux per unit concentration difference between the surface and the bulk fluid.
  2. The defining equation and its units:
    NA = kc (CAi − CA∞)

NA    molar flux of A, mol/(m²·s)
CAi   concentration of A in the fluid at the surface, mol/m³
CA∞   concentration of A in the well-mixed bulk, mol/m³
kc    mass transfer coefficient, m/s
  3. The unit of kc follows from the equation: mol/(m²·s) divided by mol/m³ is m/s. It looks like a velocity but it is not the fluid speed; it measures how fast the near-wall layer is renewed and thinned by the flow. For a stagnant film of thickness δ, molecular diffusion gives kc = DAB/δ, so a moving fluid behaves like a film of thickness DAB/kc.
  4. A local coefficient applies at one point of the surface; an average coefficient applies to the whole area, so that total transfer rate = kc,avg × area × driving force. Design uses averages.
  5. For a gas the driving force can be written three ways, and each has its own coefficient:
    Driving forceFlux equationCoefficient unitConversion
    concentrationNA = kc ΔCAm/s—
    partial pressureNA = kG ΔpAmol/(m²·s·Pa)kG = kc/(RT)
    mole fractionNA = ky ΔyAmol/(m²·s)ky = kG P = kc c
  6. The coefficient also depends on what the other species does. For A through stagnant B the flux is larger than for equimolar counter-diffusion at the same driving force, and the two types are linked by kG pBm = k′G P. For a dilute solute pBm ≈ P and the difference vanishes.
  7. Benzoic acid example. The water at the plate surface is saturated: CAi = 3.44/122.1 = 0.0282 kmol/m³ = 28.2 mol/m³. Fresh water flows past, so CA∞ ≈ 0.

Understand Compare the film, penetration and surface-renewal theories of mass transfer. Give the kL expression from each, state one limitation of each, and evaluate all three for CO₂ (D = 1.9×10⁻⁹ m²/s) absorbed into a liquid surface that is renewed every 0.5 s on average. 13 marks

Points to cover. Physical picture of each theory; kL = D/δ, 2√(D/(πt)), √(D s); Dependence on D; Limitations; Numbers for CO₂; Which to use when.

Model answer.

  1. All three theories try to explain the liquid-film coefficient kL in NA = kL(CAi − CA) from a simple picture of the liquid next to the interface. Each needs one parameter that must be fitted to a measurement, so none predicts kL from first principles.
  2. Film theory (Whitman). A stagnant layer of thickness δ sits at the interface; beyond it the liquid is well mixed. Steady diffusion across the layer gives a straight profile and kL = D/δ. The parameter δ is chosen to match the measured kL. Limitation: it predicts kL ∝ D, while measured coefficients at gas–liquid surfaces usually vary as D0.5 to D0.7; and no real stagnant layer of that thickness exists.
  3. Penetration theory (Higbie). An eddy carries a fresh liquid element to the surface, where it stays for a fixed exposure time t. During that time the solute diffuses into it as into a semi-infinite medium, because the penetration depth √(D t) is small. Averaging the unsteady flux over t gives kL = 2√(D/(πt)). Limitation: every element is given the same age, which is reasonable for a falling film or a rising bubble but not for a stirred, turbulent surface.
  4. Surface-renewal theory (Danckwerts). Elements are replaced at random, with a fractional renewal rate s (s⁻¹) independent of their age; averaging over the resulting age distribution gives kL = √(D s). Limitation: s cannot be measured directly and must be fitted, so the theory gives the √D dependence but not the magnitude.
  5. For CO₂ with t = 0.5 s (and s = 1/t would be a rough first guess):
    TheoryFormulaWorkingkL, m/s

Film theory stays the working tool for two-resistance and reaction calculations because its algebra is simplest and its enhancement factors are close to those of the other two.
Understand Explain the two-resistance theory of interphase mass transfer. Why may the interface be assumed to be at equilibrium? Support the argument with an estimate for oxygen (partial pressure 0.21 atm at 298 K) absorbing into water with kL = 4.0×10⁻⁵ m/s and saturation concentration 0.26 mol/m³. 10 marks

Points to cover. Two films in series with the interface between; Steady state, equal fluxes; Equilibrium at the interface; Kinetic-theory estimate of the interface exchange rate; Interface point on the y–x diagram; Consequence: resistances add.

Model answer.

  1. Whitman's two-resistance theory pictures the region near a gas–liquid interface as two films in series: a gas film, across which the solute mole fraction falls from the bulk value y to yi, and a liquid film, across which it falls from xi to the bulk value x. Beyond the films each phase is well mixed.
  2. At steady state nothing accumulates at the interface, so the same flux crosses both films: NA = ky(y − yi) = kx(xi − x). The bulk phases are not in equilibrium; that difference is what drives the transfer.
  3. The third assumption is that the interface itself has no resistance, so yi and xi lie on the equilibrium curve, yi = m xi for a straight line. This closes the problem: three equations fix yi, xi and NA.
  4. Why equilibrium is reasonable: molecules strike the interface from the gas far faster than the films can carry them. Kinetic theory gives the striking rate as p/√(2π M R T).
  5. Z = p/√(2π M R T)
  = 0.21 × 101325 / √(2π × 0.032 × 8.314 × 298)
  = 21 278 / 22.3
  = 953 mol/(m²·s)
  6. The film-limited flux into water is at most NA = kL Csat = 4.0×10⁻⁵ × 0.26 = 1.04×10⁻⁵ mol/(m²·s). The interface exchange capacity is about 953/1.04×10⁻⁵ ≈ 9×10⁷ times larger. Even if only a small fraction of the collisions led to dissolution, the departure from equilibrium needed to pass the film flux would be a negligible fraction of the total driving force. The interface only fails to be at equilibrium when a surfactant layer or a very high flux slows the crossing itself.
  7. On a y–x diagram the bulk point (x, y) lies above the equilibrium line for absorption. A line of slope −kx/ky drawn from it meets the equilibrium line at the interface point (xi, yi); the vertical distance y − yi is the gas-film drop and the horizontal distance xi − x the liquid-film drop.
  8. Because the drops are in series, resistances add: 1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx. The film with the larger share controls: the gas film for very soluble gases (small m), the liquid film for sparingly soluble gases such as the oxygen here, where nearly all the resistance is on the liquid side.
  9. Check: for oxygen m is about 4×10⁴ (Henry's constant in atmospheres at 1 atm total pressure), so m/kx dwarfs 1/ky for any realistic coefficients, and the whole flux estimate rests on kL alone, as used above.

Apply A gas dissolves into a liquid (DA = 1.8×10⁻⁹ m²/s, kL = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m/s, CAi = 1.0 mol/m³) where it undergoes a first-order reaction. Analyze Compare tray and packed contactors for gas absorption. Discuss stage efficiency, HTU, pressure drop, turndown, liquid rate and fouling, using the duty of a dilute absorber that needs NOG = 7.02 with HOG = 0.375 m (6.11 ideal stages, λ = 0.752) and sieve trays with EMV = 0.70 at 0.6 m spacing. 16 marks

Points to cover. How each contactor is sized; Height for the stated duty, both ways; Pressure drop; Turndown and liquid rate; Fouling, foaming, corrosion, heat removal; Recommendation.

Model answer.

  1. Trays make contact in steps: on each tray the gas bubbles through a liquid pool, the phases approach equilibrium and separate. Height follows from ideal stages, tray efficiency and tray spacing. Packing gives continuous contact over a wetted surface; height follows from Z = HOG × NOG, where HOG = G/(Ky a) carries the packing performance and NOG the difficulty of the separation.
  2. Packed height for the duty: Z = 0.375 × 7.02 = 2.63 m of packing (HETP = HOG ln λ/(λ − 1) = 0.431 m, and 6.11 × 0.431 = 2.63 m).
  3. Tray height: Lewis's relation gives Eo = ln[1 + 0.70 × (0.752 − 1)]/ln 0.752 = ln 0.826/ln 0.752 = 0.669. Real trays = 6.11/0.669 = 9.1, so 10 trays; at 0.6 m spacing that is 10 × 0.6 = 6.0 m of tray section, more than twice the packed height.
  4. Pressure drop. Each sieve tray costs the gas the head of its liquid pool plus the dry-plate loss, typically about 0.5 to 1 kPa; ten trays take roughly 5 to 10 kPa. Random packing in absorbers is usually designed for a few hundred pascals per metre, so 2.63 m costs well under 1 kPa. Analyze Analyse how gas velocity and liquid viscosity affect kG, kL and the overall resistance in a packed absorber. Use the base case ky = 0.050 kmol/(m²·s), kx = 0.080 kmol/(m²·s), m = 1.5, and the exponents of Onda's correlations: kG ∝ G0.7 and kL ∝ L2/3 μL−5/6 DL1/2. 16 marks

Points to cover. Physical reasons: thinner gas film, thicker and slower liquid film; Correlation exponents and diffusivity link to viscosity; Base-case resistances; Case A: gas rate +50 %; Case B: liquid viscosity doubled; Effect on HOG; limits from flooding and pressure drop.

Model answer.

  1. Gas velocity. A faster gas thins the gas-side boundary layer and adds turbulence, so kG (and ky) rises. In packed beds the gas-side Sherwood number varies roughly as ReG0.7, which gives kG ∝ G0.7 at fixed packing. Gas velocity hardly touches kL. It also raises the pressure drop, roughly with the square of velocity, and brings the column towards flooding.
  2. Liquid viscosity. A more viscous liquid flows as a thicker, slower film with less surface renewal, and its diffusivity falls: the Wilke–Chang and Stokes–Einstein relations give DL ∝ T/μL. With Onda's exponents, kL ∝ μL−5/6 at fixed D, and with D ∝ 1/μ another factor μL−1/2, so kL ∝ μL−4/3 overall. Viscosity also lowers the wetted area, which this estimate ignores.

  6. Case B. Doubling μL multiplies kx by 2−4/3 = 0.397, so the liquid term grows from 18.75 to 47.25 and Ky falls by 42 %. The controlling film shifts: the liquid now carries 70 % of the resistance. Even if D did not change, the flow effect alone (factor 2−5/6 = 0.561) would cut Ky by 27 %.

To exploit the gas side, raise the gas rate up to about 70 to 80 % of flooding, not beyond.

Glossary

Chilton–Colburn analogy
An analogy linking mass transfer and friction through jD = (kc/u)Sc2/3 = f/2.
Convective mass transfer
Mass transfer from a moving fluid to a surface or another fluid, helped by bulk flow and turbulence.
Eddy diffusion
Turbulent mixing that increases transport beyond molecular diffusion, described by eddy diffusivity.
Enhancement factor
The factor by which a chemical reaction increases mass flux above physical absorption.
Equilibrium curve
A plot of equilibrium vapor composition against liquid composition at fixed temperature and pressure.
Film theory
A model that treats resistance as a stagnant film of fixed thickness δ, giving kc = D/δ.
Hatta number
A dimensionless number comparing reaction rate to diffusion rate in the liquid film.
Height of a transfer unit
The packed height needed for one transfer unit, HOG = G/(Ky a).
Henry's law constant
A constant relating gas partial pressure to the liquid composition at equilibrium, pA = H xA; divided by the total pressure it gives the slope m of y* = m x.
Ideal stage
A hypothetical stage that leaves its exiting vapor and liquid in equilibrium.
Individual mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient for one phase film, such as gas-film ky or liquid-film kx.
Interphase mass transfer
Transfer of a component from one phase to another across their interface, as in absorption or extraction.
Mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient that gives mass flux per unit concentration driving force across a fluid phase.
Number of transfer units
A dimensionless measure of separation duty, NOG = ∫ dy/(y − y*).
Operating line
A material-balance line relating vapor and liquid compositions in a stage or packed contactor.
Overall mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient based on the total driving force from bulk gas to bulk liquid, written Ky or Kx.
Packed column
A continuous-contact column filled with packing that provides interfacial area for mass transfer.
Penetration theory
A model in which fluid elements stay at the interface for a fixed exposure time t, giving kc = 2√(D/(πt)).
Reactive absorption
Gas absorption in which the dissolved gas reacts with a component in the liquid.
Reynolds number
A dimensionless ratio of inertial to viscous forces, Re = ρuL/μ, used in flow correlations.
Schmidt number
A dimensionless ratio of momentum diffusivity to mass diffusivity, Sc = μ/(ρDAB).
Sherwood number
A dimensionless mass transfer coefficient, Sh = kc L/DAB.
Stage efficiency
The ratio of the composition change in a real stage to that in an ideal equilibrium stage.
Surface renewal theory
A model in which surface elements are replaced at a renewal rate s, giving kc = √(Ds).
Tray column
A stagewise column with plates or trays on which vapor bubbles through liquid.
Two-film theory
A model with one gas film and one liquid film in series, with equilibrium at the interface.
