2.1 Convective mass transfer and mass transfer coefficients

Air blown over a wet surface dries it faster than still air. The vapour still crosses by diffusion, but moving air sweeps away the vapour-rich layer and keeps the gradient steep. One number, the convective mass transfer coefficient kc, captures how well a flowing fluid carries a species to or from a surface. It gives a flux from a concentration difference alone, with no film thickness needed. Absorbers, cooling towers and catalytic surfaces are sized with kc.

You need Fick's first law, molar flux and the steady diffusion result for a stagnant film, from Unit 1 (Molecular Diffusion and Diffusivity).

The coefficient replaces a film thickness

For a stagnant film, Unit 1 gave NA = DAB(CA1 − CA2)/δ, where DAB is diffusivity (m²/s) and δ is film thickness (m). In a flowing fluid no fixed δ exists. The layer next to the surface is thin at the leading edge, grows downstream and breaks into eddies in turbulent flow. So δ cannot be measured, and the formula cannot be used as it stands.

NA = kc (CAi − CA∞)

NA is the molar flux of A across the interface, mol/(m²·s); CAi is the concentration of A just at the interface, mol/m³; CA∞ is the concentration of A in the well-mixed bulk fluid, mol/m³; kc is the convective mass transfer coefficient, m/s.

The unit follows: mol/(m²·s) divided by mol/m³ gives m/s. So kc looks like a velocity, but it is not the speed of the fluid. It is the molar flux per unit concentration difference. Numerically, when the concentration difference is 1 mol/m³, it equals the volume of fluid cleared of A per unit interfacial area per second. It is a transport coefficient.

All the unknown fluid mechanics — film thickness, turbulence, surface shape — is pushed inside kc. Theory cannot give kc from first principles for most equipment. It is measured, or predicted from correlations kc = (DAB/L) × f(Re, Sc). Those groups and correlations are in Chapter 2.3.

Gas and liquid forms, local and average

For a gas, partial pressure is the natural driving force. With pA = CART, where pA is partial pressure (atm), R is the gas constant and T is absolute temperature (K), the flux law is NA = kG(pAi − pA∞). Comparing forms gives kG = kc/(RT). With R in m³·atm/(kmol·K), kG comes out in kmol/(m²·s·atm). The liquid-side coefficient kL uses liquid concentrations and has units of m/s.

Driving forceEquationCoefficientUnit
Concentration (either phase)NA = kc (CAi − CA∞)kcm/s
Partial pressure (gas)NA = kG (pAi − pA∞)kGkmol/(m²·s·atm)
Mole fraction (gas)NA = ky (yAi − yA∞)kykmol/(m²·s)
Concentration (liquid)NA = kL (CAi − CA∞)kLm/s

A local coefficient applies at one point and varies with position because the boundary layer changes. An average coefficient applies over the whole surface: total molar flow equals average coefficient × total area × mean driving force. Design uses averages. A gas-side coefficient belongs to the gas film, a liquid-side coefficient to the liquid film. When both matter they combine into an overall coefficient, as Chapter 2.2 shows.

Boundary layer, turbulence and the size of kc

Air flows over a flat plate. A concentration boundary layer of thickness δc (m) builds up, inside which concentration falls from CAi at the wall to CA∞ outside. The wall flux is NA = −DAB(∂CA/∂y) at y = 0, where y is normal to the plate. A thin layer holds a steep gradient, so flux and kc are large.

The layer thickens roughly as √x from the leading edge. The wall gradient weakens, so local kc falls with distance. The average over a plate of length L is larger than the trailing-edge local value because the leading part transfers well. Hence the average is the honest number for a whole surface.

In turbulent flow, eddies add an eddy diffusivity far larger than the molecular one, so bulk transport is fast. That is why gas velocity strongly affects kc. A thin laminar sublayer always survives at the wall, and species cross it by molecular diffusion. That sublayer is the bottleneck, so kc rises with velocity but never without limit. This picture leads to film and surface-renewal theories in Chapter 2.4.

Coefficients are classified by position, by combination and by driving force; the driving force also fixes the phase.

  • By position: local and average coefficients.
  • By combination: individual film coefficients and overall coefficients.
  • By driving force (and so by phase): kc or kL on a concentration difference (m/s), kG on a partial pressure difference (kmol/(m²·s·atm)), ky on a mole fraction difference (kmol/(m²·s)).

Worked example

Question. Air at 300 K and 1 atm flows over a water surface. The convective mass transfer coefficient for the transferred species is kc = 0.02 m/s. The interface concentration in the gas is CAi = 0.50 mol/m³ and the bulk gas concentration is CA∞ = 0.10 mol/m³. Find the molar flux NA and express it in kmol/(m²·s).

  1. Use NA = kc(CAi − CA∞).
  2. Driving force = 0.50 − 0.10 = 0.40 mol/m³.
  3. Unit check: (m/s)(mol/m³) = mol/(m²·s).
  4. NA = 0.02 × 0.40 = 0.008 mol/(m²·s).
  5. Convert: 0.008/1000 = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ kmol/(m²·s).
  6. The interface concentration is higher, so flux goes from interface into bulk gas; water evaporates.

Answer. NA = 0.008 mol/(m²·s) = 8.0 × 10⁻⁶ kmol/(m²·s), from the interface into the bulk gas.

Exam traps

  • Matching coefficient to driving force. kG needs a partial pressure difference in atm; kc needs a concentration difference in mol/m³.
  • Forgetting kc is per unit interfacial area. In a packed tower the measured group is often kc·a, where a is area per m³, with units 1/s.
  • Reading kc as gas velocity because units are m/s. It is a transport rate per unit area per unit concentration, not a speed.
  • Using the other phase's bulk concentration instead of the interface value. The interface is set by equilibrium (Chapter 2.6).
  • Comparing kG and kL directly. Their driving forces differ; convert both to one overall coefficient based on one phase (Chapter 2.2).

2.2 Individual and overall mass transfer coefficients

In a gas absorber, a solute leaves the gas film and enters the liquid film. Chapter 2.1 gave one coefficient for each film. Plant calculations need one coefficient for the whole gas phase or whole liquid phase, because the interface composition is hard to measure. The individual coefficients add as resistances in series. That gives K_y based on gas mole fraction and K_x based on liquid mole fraction. The same numbers tell whether the gas film or the liquid film controls the rate.

You need the individual coefficient k_c from Chapter 2.1 and the interface equilibrium idea from Chapter 2.6.

Two films, two overall coefficients

In the gas film the driving force is y - y_i; in the liquid film it is x_i - x. At steady state the flux N_A is the same through both films. Define k_y = N_A/(y - y_i) and k_x = N_A/(x_i - x). k_y is the gas-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δy). k_x is the liquid-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δx). The interface is in equilibrium, y_i = m x_i, where m is the slope of y* = m x. The interface itself has no storage. The composition change is split between the two films. The interface composition cannot be sampled in a plant, so calculations use bulk compositions. K_y is the overall gas coefficient: N_A = K_y(y - y*), where y* = m x is the gas composition in equilibrium with the bulk liquid. K_x is the overall liquid coefficient: N_A = K_x(x* - x), where x* = y/m is the liquid composition in equilibrium with the bulk gas. K_y and K_x are bookkeeping devices. They put both film resistances on one driving-force basis.

Adding resistances in series

Add the two film drops on the gas basis. Across the gas film, y - y_i = N_A/k_y. Across the liquid film, y_i - y* = m(x_i - x) = m N_A/k_x. Add and compare with N_A = K_y(y - y*).

1/K_y = 1/k_y + m/k_x

K_y is the overall gas coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δy); k_y is the gas-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δy); k_x is the liquid-film coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δx); m is the slope of y* = m x, dimensionless.

On the liquid basis, x* - x_i = (y - y_i)/m = N_A/(m k_y) and x_i - x = N_A/k_x. Add and compare with N_A = K_x(x* - x).

1/K_x = 1/(m k_y) + 1/k_x

K_x is the overall liquid coefficient, kmol/(m²·s·Δx); the gas term 1/(m k_y) converts gas-film resistance to the x basis; the liquid term 1/k_x is the liquid-film resistance.

The factor m converts one phase's composition to the other phase's basis. The two overall coefficients are linked by K_x = m K_y. Use K_y when the gas composition is measured; use K_x when the liquid composition is measured. Never mix Δy and Δx in one sum.

Which film controls?

Resistance is 1/coefficient. The gas-film share of the resistance behind K_y is (1/k_y)/(1/K_y). The liquid-film share is (m/k_x)/(1/K_y). The same percentages apply to K_x because K_x = m K_y. If one share is above about 80%, that film controls. Gas-film control means extra liquid mixing gives little gain; increase gas velocity or gas turbulence. Liquid-film control means improve liquid mixing or add a reaction. In the worked example the gas film has 51.6%, so neither film is fully controlling. Watch m, not 1/k_x alone. For a very soluble gas, m is small, so m/k_x is small and the gas film often controls. For a sparingly soluble gas, m is large, so the liquid term often controls. A scrubber can therefore be gas-film controlled for ammonia and liquid-film controlled for carbon dioxide.

Worked example

Question. A solute is absorbed from a gas into a liquid. The individual coefficients are k_y = 0.050 kmol/(m²·s·Δy) and k_x = 0.080 kmol/(m²·s·Δx). The equilibrium line is y* = 1.5x. Find K_y, K_x, and the percentage of the gas-film resistance in K_y.

  1. m = 1.5. Gas-film resistance = 1/k_y = 1/0.050 = 20 m²·s·Δy/kmol.
  2. Liquid-film resistance on the gas basis = m/k_x = 1.5/0.080 = 18.75 m²·s·Δy/kmol.
  3. 1/K_y = 20 + 18.75 = 38.75 m²·s·Δy/kmol. K_y = 1/38.75 = 0.0258 kmol/(m²·s·Δy).
  4. 1/K_x = 1/(m k_y) + 1/k_x = 1/(1.5×0.050) + 1/0.080 = 13.33 + 12.50 = 25.83 m²·s·Δx/kmol. K_x = 1/25.83 = 0.0387 kmol/(m²·s·Δx).
  5. Gas-film share = 20/38.75 = 0.516 = 51.6%. Liquid-film share = 48.4%.

Answer. K_y = 0.0258 kmol/(m²·s·Δy), K_x = 0.0387 kmol/(m²·s·Δx), and the gas film supplies 51.6% of the resistance in K_y.

Exam traps

  • For 1/K_y the liquid term is m/k_x. For 1/K_x the gas term is 1/(m k_y). Reversing m is the commonest error.
  • Do not add 1/k_y and 1/k_x directly. They sit on different driving forces. Convert with m first.
  • K_y and K_x have different numerical values and different driving-force bases. A resistance fraction must use the same basis as the coefficient.
  • m is the slope of y* = m x. If the equilibrium line is curved, use a local slope at the interface; a single m is valid only over the working range.
  • The interface is in equilibrium, not the bulk phases. y* = m x uses the bulk liquid x, not the interface x_i.

2.3 Dimensionless groups and mass transfer correlations

A mass transfer coefficient depends on velocity, length, density, viscosity and diffusivity. Measuring it for every fluid and speed is hopeless. So we pack those variables into three dimensionless numbers — Sherwood, Reynolds and Schmidt — and fit one curve through many experiments. Feed in Re and Sc, read out Sh, then convert to k_c.

You need Chapter 2.1 for k_c, and the Reynolds number and boundary layer from fluid mechanics.

The three groups

At the surface the flux is still diffusive, N_A = k_c Δc, but stirring steepens the concentration gradient near the wall and so raises k_c. Compare this with the purely diffusive flux D_AB Δc/L over the same length; the ratio is the Sherwood number.

Sh = k_c L / D_AB

Sh is the Sherwood number (dimensionless), k_c the mass transfer coefficient in m/s, L the characteristic length in m, D_AB the diffusivity of A in B in m²/s.

Reynolds number compares inertia with viscous force and decides whether the boundary layer is laminar or turbulent. Schmidt number compares momentum spreading with mass spreading.

Re = ρ u L / μ = u L / ν      Sc = μ / (ρ D_AB) = ν / D_AB

ρ is density in kg/m³, u the free-stream velocity in m/s, μ the viscosity in Pa·s, ν = μ/ρ the kinematic viscosity in m²/s.

Sh does in mass transfer what Nu = hL/k does in heat transfer, and Sc is the mass version of the Prandtl number. Every correlation therefore reads Sh as a function of Re and Sc.

Three standard correlations

Steady laminar flow over a flat plate of length L, with Re below about 5×10⁵, gives an average coefficient:

Sh = 0.664 Re^(1/2) Sc^(1/3)

averaged over the plate length L, with Re = uL/ν and u the free-stream velocity.

Turbulent from the leading edge on the same plate:

Sh = 0.037 Re^0.8 Sc^(1/3)

valid roughly from Re = 5×10⁵ to 10⁷; L is the plate length from the leading edge.

A real plate is laminar near the nose and turbulent further on. That mixed plate needs the 871 correction:

Sh = (0.037 Re^0.8 − 871) Sc^(1/3)

the same plate and the same L; the 871 is the laminar stretch subtracted out, not a term to add.

For a single sphere of diameter d in a flowing stream:

Sh = 2 + 0.6 Re^(1/2) Sc^(1/3)

L = d. At Re = 0 it returns Sh = 2, pure diffusion to a sphere in still fluid.

The first two are boundary-layer fits for a flat plate; the sphere fit is an empirical correlation for a different geometry, which is why it carries the additive 2. Sc^(1/3) appears in all three because the laminar sublayer near the surface controls the mass transfer resistance in each case.

Choosing L and properties

Each correlation fixes its own length and its own property temperature. Check both before substituting anything.

  1. Pick the geometry: flat plate or single sphere.
  2. Set L in metres: plate length along the flow, or sphere diameter.
  3. Evaluate ρ, μ and D_AB at the film temperature T_f = (T_s + T_∞)/2.
  4. Compute ν = μ/ρ, then Re = uL/ν and Sc = ν/D_AB.
  5. Choose the correlation whose Re range contains your Re; compute Sh.
  6. Convert back: k_c = Sh D_AB / L, in m/s.

For gases Sc usually lies between about 0.2 and 3, near 0.6 for water vapour in air; in liquids Sc reaches hundreds or thousands. Outside the tested Re band a correlation is extrapolation, not prediction. Two correlations may share the algebra but not the length: compare definitions, never symbols.

Worked example

Question. Air at 2 m/s flows over a flat plate 0.5 m long, with D_AB = 2.6×10⁻⁵ m²/s and ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s. Use Sh = 0.664 Re^(1/2) Sc^(1/3) to find Re, Sc, Sh and k_c.

  1. Plate, so L = 0.5 m.
  2. Re = uL/ν = (2 × 0.5)/(1.5×10⁻⁵) = 6.67×10⁴. Below 5×10⁵, so the laminar fit applies.
  3. Sc = ν/D_AB = 1.5×10⁻⁵/2.6×10⁻⁵ = 0.577, and Sc^(1/3) = 0.832. Also √Re = 258.2.
  4. Sh = 0.664 × 258.2 × 0.832 = 142.7.
  5. k_c = Sh D_AB/L = 142.7 × 2.6×10⁻⁵/0.5 = 7.42×10⁻³ m/s.

Answer. Re = 6.67×10⁴, Sc = 0.577, Sh = 142.7 and k_c = 7.42×10⁻³ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using plate length for a sphere or diameter for a plate. Each correlation defines its own L; a wrong L spoils Re and k_c together.
  • Evaluating μ and D_AB at the wall or bulk temperature instead of the film temperature T_f.
  • Treating Sh = 0.037 Re^0.8 Sc^(1/3) as a whole-plate average. It assumes turbulence from the leading edge; a plate that starts laminar needs Sh = (0.037 Re^0.8 − 871) Sc^(1/3). The correction is a subtraction, not an added laminar term.
  • Swapping Sh with Nu, or Sc with Pr. The algebra is identical, the symbols are not, and the definition is the first thing marked.

2.4 Film, penetration and surface renewal theories

The mass transfer coefficient k_c (m/s) is measured for a given flow, but it is not a fluid property alone. Film, penetration and surface-renewal theories each explain k_c by a simple picture of the fluid next to the interface. Film theory uses an equivalent stagnant thickness δ. Penetration theory uses a short exposure time t. Surface-renewal theory uses a fractional renewal rate s. The three pictures give different dependence on diffusivity D (m²/s). They guide absorber and packed-tower design, where liquid-film coefficients control transfer of sparingly soluble gases.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the definition of k_c, and Unit 1 for Fick's law and diffusion in a stagnant layer.

Film theory: equivalent stagnant layer

The film model imagines a stagnant layer of thickness δ next to the interface. The bulk fluid is well mixed, so its concentration C_b is uniform up to the outer edge of the layer. Across the layer, mass moves by molecular diffusion only, and the concentration profile is taken as linear. Fick's law gives the flux as N_A = (D/δ)(C_b − C_i). Comparing with N_A = k_c(C_b − C_i) gives k_c = D/δ. Here D is diffusivity (m²/s), δ is the equivalent film thickness (m), and k_c is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s). The model is simple and fits two-resistance calculations, but δ is not a measured physical thickness. It is chosen so that D/δ matches an observed k_c. The model predicts k_c ∝ D: doubling D doubles k_c. Real interfaces have eddies that penetrate close to the surface, so the true profile is curved and the film picture is approximate.

k_c = D/δ

k_c is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); δ is the equivalent film thickness (m).

Penetration theory: short exposure

Penetration theory treats contact as short and unsteady. An eddy brings fresh liquid to the interface. That element stays for an exposure time t (s), then mixes back into the bulk. During t, gas diffuses into a semi-infinite liquid. The average flux over the exposure gives N_A = 2√(D/(πt))(C_i − C_0), where C_0 is the initial solute concentration in the liquid. Therefore k_c = 2√(D/(πt)). The factor 2 comes from averaging over the exposure; the instantaneous value is √(D/(πt)). The model predicts k_c ∝ √D. If D becomes four times larger, k_c doubles. Penetration theory suits short-contact equipment: packed towers, falling films and bubbles. The exposure time is set by flow, not by a fitted film thickness. Its weakness is that all surface elements are given the same t, which is rarely true in a stirred vessel.

k_c = 2√(D/(πt))

k_c is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); t is exposure time (s); π is 3.1416.

Surface renewal: random replacement

Surface renewal theory removes the single exposure time. Fresh liquid reaches the surface at random times, so elements have a range of ages. Danckwerts defined s, the fractional surface renewal rate (s⁻¹). In a small time interval, a fraction s dt of the surface is replaced by fresh liquid. Integrating over all ages gives N_A = √(D s)(C_i − C_0), so k_c = √(D s). A large s means fast renewal and a large k_c. The D dependence is again √D, not D. This single fact separates the film model from the other two. For a sparingly soluble gas in water, liquid-film resistance often controls, and measured k_c values usually follow √D better than D. The reason is eddy diffusion near the interface: eddies carry solute close to the surface and make a fixed stagnant film unrealistic. Film theory remains useful as a limiting picture and as the basis of two-resistance design.

k_c = √(D s)

k_c is the mass transfer coefficient (m/s); D is diffusivity (m²/s); s is the fractional surface renewal rate (s⁻¹).

Worked example

Question. A sparingly soluble gas has D = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/s. A film model fits the data with δ = 0.2 mm and gives k_L = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m/s. (a) Check the film prediction. (b) Find the penetration prediction for t = 0.5 s. (c) Find the surface-renewal prediction for s = 2 s⁻¹. Use liquid-film coefficient k_L.

  1. List data: D = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/s; δ = 0.2 mm = 0.2×10⁻³ m = 2×10⁻⁴ m; t = 0.5 s; s = 2 s⁻¹.
  2. Film: k_L = D/δ = (2×10⁻⁹ m²/s)/(2×10⁻⁴ m) = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  3. Penetration: D/(πt) = (2×10⁻⁹)/(π×0.5) = 1.273×10⁻⁹ m²/s. √ = 3.568×10⁻⁵ m/s. k_L = 2×3.568×10⁻⁵ = 7.14×10⁻⁵ m/s ≈ 7.1×10⁻⁵ m/s.
  4. Surface renewal: D s = (2×10⁻⁹)(2) = 4.0×10⁻⁹ m²/s². √ = 6.32×10⁻⁵ m/s ≈ 6.3×10⁻⁵ m/s.

Answer. Film gives 1.0×10⁻⁵ m/s, penetration 7.1×10⁻⁵ m/s, and surface renewal 6.3×10⁻⁵ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Treating δ as a real measured stagnant film. δ is an equivalent thickness chosen to match k_c; it changes with flow and is not a property of the fluid.
  • A unit slip: δ in mm must be converted to m before D/δ. With D in m²/s, δ in m gives k_c in m/s.
  • Using k_c ∝ D for all data. Film theory doubles k_c when D doubles; penetration and surface renewal increase it by only √2.
  • Omitting the factor 2 in penetration theory. The average flux over exposure time t has 2√(D/(πt)); dropping 2 halves the predicted k_c.
  • Confusing surface renewal rate s with 1/t. In penetration theory t is a fixed exposure time; in surface renewal s is a random replacement rate.

2.5 Analogies between momentum, heat and mass transfer

Momentum, heat and mass all cross the same thin fluid layer near a wall. In turbulent flow the same eddies carry all three. This shared motion lets one measured pressure drop predict a heat-transfer coefficient or a mass-transfer coefficient k_c. Engineers use it when a correlation is missing or when a quick check is needed, for example to estimate evaporation from a straight smooth duct using the skin-friction part of its pressure drop.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for k_c and Chapter 2.3 for Sh, Re and Sc, plus basic fluid mechanics (friction factor) and heat transfer (Nu, Pr).

The shared eddy diffusivity

In turbulent flow, a fluid packet carries momentum, enthalpy and solute together. Near a wall the total shear stress, heat flux and mass flux each have a molecular part and an eddy part. The eddy diffusivity for momentum, εM (m²/s), for heat, εH, and for mass, εD, is nearly the same size when the turbulence is fully mixed. That shared eddy motion is the physical basis of every analogy here.

The three fluxes are τ = ρ(ν + εM) du/dy, q/A = −ρ cp(α + εH) dT/dy and NA = −(DAB + εD) dCA/dy. Here τ is shear stress (Pa), q/A is heat flux (W/m²), NA is molar flux (mol/(m²·s)), ν is kinematic viscosity (m²/s), α is thermal diffusivity (m²/s), DAB is molecular diffusivity (m²/s), and y is distance from the wall (m).

Two dimensionless groups compare molecular transport: Pr = ν/α = μ cp/k and Sc = ν/DAB = μ/(ρDAB). Pr is the Prandtl number (dimensionless), Sc the Schmidt number, μ viscosity (Pa·s), cp specific heat (J/(kg·K)), k thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)).

St = f/2 (for Pr = 1)

St = h/(ρ cp u) is the Stanton number; h is the heat-transfer coefficient (W/(m²·K)); u is bulk velocity (m/s); f is the Fanning friction factor, defined by τw = f (1/2) ρ u², with τw the wall shear stress (Pa).

This is the Reynolds analogy. It says the dimensionless temperature profile and the dimensionless velocity profile have the same shape when Pr = 1. It is quick but narrow: it ignores the laminar sublayer.

Chilton-Colburn j-factors

Chilton and Colburn kept the same idea but absorbed the molecular-property effect into j-factors. The heat j-factor is jH = Nu/(Re Pr1/3). The mass j-factor is jD = Sh/(Re Sc1/3). Here Nu = hL/kf is the Nusselt number, Sh = kcL/DAB is the Sherwood number, and Re = ρuL/μ is the Reynolds number; L is the characteristic length (m), kf is fluid thermal conductivity (W/(m·K)), and kc is the mass-transfer coefficient (m/s). For a flat plate, L is the plate length. For pipe flow, the characteristic length is the pipe diameter D, so use Re = ρuD/μ, Sh = kcD/DAB, and Nu = hD/kf.

jH = Nu/(Re Pr1/3) = f/2,   jD = Sh/(Re Sc1/3) = f/2

jH and jD are dimensionless; f is the Fanning friction factor; all other symbols are defined above. Hence jH = jD = f/2.

The equality is used to estimate kc from a measured pressure drop. Follow these steps.

  1. Measure Δp (Pa) over a smooth pipe length L (m) at bulk velocity u (m/s) and density ρ (kg/m³); D is the pipe diameter (m) and L is the length between pressure taps.
  2. Get the Fanning friction factor from Δp = 2f(L/D)ρu², so f = Δp D/(2Lρu²).
  3. Set jD = f/2.
  4. Compute Sc = μ/(ρDAB) and Re = ρuD/μ. Use D, not L, as the characteristic length for pipe flow.
  5. Compute Sh = jD Re Sc1/3.
  6. Compute kc = Sh DAB/D.

For a flat plate, use the local or average skin-friction coefficient from boundary-layer tables in place of the pipe formula. The j-factor route is the same. For a flat plate use L as the characteristic length in Nu = hL/kf, Sh = kcL/DAB, and Re = ρuL/μ. The analogy is popular in compact heat exchangers and drying because pressure drop is easy to measure, while kc is not.

Refinements and limits

Prandtl-Taylor and von Kármán refined the analogy by keeping the laminar sublayer. Prandtl-Taylor gives St = (f/2)/[1 + 5√(f/2)(Pr − 1)] for heat transfer, and the same form with Sc for mass transfer. von Kármán adds the buffer layer and gives a better fit when Pr or Sc is far from 1, such as oil (Pr about 1000) or a liquid with Sc in the thousands, such as salts or sugars in water. Gases have Sc about 0.6, close to 1, and the simple analogy handles them well.

The analogy assumes skin friction, not form drag. A pressure drop across a valve, a bend or a rough packing includes form drag, so the f you calculate is too large. The predicted kc is then too high. It also assumes small mass-transfer rates. Strong blowing or suction changes the velocity profile and breaks jD = f/2; use a high-mass-transfer correction or a direct correlation in that case.

Worked example

Question. Air flows over a smooth flat plate. The Fanning friction factor is f = 0.008. Estimate jD, Sh and kc for Re = 5×10⁴, Sc = 0.6, DAB = 2.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s and L = 0.5 m.

  1. jD = f/2 = 0.008/2 = 0.004 (dimensionless).
  2. Sc1/3 = 0.61/3 = 0.843 (dimensionless).
  3. Sh = jD Re Sc1/3 = 0.004 × 5×10⁴ × 0.843 = 168.7 (dimensionless).
  4. kc = Sh DAB/L = 168.7 × 2.5×10⁻⁵ / 0.5 = 8.4×10⁻³ m/s (0.0084 m/s).

Answer. jD = 0.004, Sh = 168.7, and kc = 8.4×10⁻³ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using the Darcy friction factor instead of the Fanning factor. Darcy f is four times Fanning f; jD = f/2 needs Fanning. A factor-of-four error gives kc four times too large.
  • Taking f from a pressure drop that includes form drag. The analogy uses wall skin friction only. Valves, bends and rough packings give a high f and a high predicted kc.
  • Applying jD = jH = f/2 at high mass-transfer rates. Blowing or suction alters the boundary layer. Use a correction or a direct correlation.
  • Mixing the exponents: jD uses Sc1/3 in the denominator; the mass Stanton number follows jD = Stm Sc2/3. Write the definition before substituting.
  • Forgetting units: Sh and Re are dimensionless; DAB is m²/s; L is m; kc comes out in m/s.

2.6 Interphase mass transfer and two-resistance theory

Two phases touch at an interface. The gas and liquid are not in equilibrium in their bulk, but they are in equilibrium at the interface itself. Solute crosses a gas film and a liquid film in series. The two-resistance theory uses this picture to find the interface composition, the flux, and which film controls. It is the working method behind absorber and stripper calculations, and it explains why ammonia leaves air into water easily while oxygen does not.

You need: individual and overall mass transfer coefficients, and vapour-liquid equilibrium from Unit 4.

The interface is in equilibrium

Let y be the solute mole fraction in the bulk gas and x the solute mole fraction in the bulk liquid. At the interface these become yi and xi. The two-resistance model makes three assumptions. First, the gas and liquid at the interface are in equilibrium, so yi = m xi. Here m is the slope of the equilibrium line y = m x on a mole-fraction plot. Second, no solute accumulates at the interface. Third, the same flux NA passes through both films.

Each film carries flux with its own coefficient: NA = ky(y − yi) = kx(xi − x). ky is the gas-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δy); kx is the liquid-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δx). The equality is the key equation. It says the gas-side driving force (y − yi) and the liquid-side driving force (xi − x) adjust until the two fluxes match.

  1. Write the interface equilibrium relation yi = m xi.
  2. Substitute into the equal-flux equation: ky(y − m xi) = kx(xi − x).
  3. Solve for xi.
  4. Find yi = m xi.
  5. Put either side back into the flux equation to get NA.

On the y-x plot the interface is where the equilibrium line meets a straight line from the bulk point (x, y). That line has slope −kx/ky, because (y − yi)/(x − xi) = −kx/ky.

NA = ky(y − yi) = kx(xi − x)

NA is the solute flux in kmol/(m²·s); ky is the gas-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δy); kx is the liquid-film coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δx); y and x are bulk mole fractions; yi and xi are interface mole fractions.

Overall driving forces and resistances

Designers prefer one driving force, not two. Define y* = m x: the gas mole fraction that would be in equilibrium with the bulk liquid. Define x* = y/m: the liquid mole fraction that would be in equilibrium with the bulk gas. Then the overall gas driving force is y − y*, and the overall liquid driving force is x* − x.

Write NA = Ky(y − y*) and NA = Kx(x* − x). Ky and Kx are overall coefficients. They are built from the film coefficients by adding resistances in series.

1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx

Ky is the overall gas-phase coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δy); ky and kx are the individual film coefficients; m is the slope of the equilibrium line y = m x.

1/Kx = 1/(m ky) + 1/kx

Kx is the overall liquid-phase coefficient in kmol/(m²·s·Δx); the other symbols are as above.

The factor m appears because kx uses Δx while Ky uses Δy. This is why ky and kx cannot simply be added. The gas-film share of the total resistance is (1/ky)/(1/ky + m/kx). The liquid-film share is (m/kx)/(1/ky + m/kx).

Read the shares before designing. If the gas share is near 1, the gas film controls: changing gas turbulence helps, changing liquid mixing hardly helps. If the liquid share is near 1, the liquid film controls. Ammonia in water is gas-film controlled: ammonia is very soluble, m is small, and m/kx is small. Oxygen in water is liquid-film controlled: oxygen is sparingly soluble, m is large, and m/kx dominates. The same reasoning tells you where to spend pressure drop and packing.

Reading the plot and checking the answer

The figure uses y = 2x, bulk gas y = 0.10, bulk liquid x = 0.02, ky = 0.05 and kx = 0.08. The bulk point is above the equilibrium line, so solute goes from gas to liquid. The interface point (0.03667, 0.07333) sits on the equilibrium line. Notice that xi > x and yi < y: the interface is richer in solute than the bulk liquid and poorer than the bulk gas. That is how both films keep positive driving forces.

The tie line from (0.02, 0.10) to (0.03667, 0.07333) has slope −0.08/0.05 = −1.6. Check: (0.07333 − 0.10)/(0.03667 − 0.02) = −1.6. Any other interface point on y = 2x would give unequal fluxes. The equality of fluxes picks exactly one point.

Use the same check in exams: compute NA from the gas side and from the liquid side. They must match. If they do not, you have mixed bulk and interface values, or forgotten m.

Worked example

Question. In an absorption column, bulk gas y = 0.10 and bulk liquid x = 0.02 (mole fractions). The equilibrium line is y* = 2x. The individual coefficients are ky = 0.05 kmol/(m²·s·Δy) and kx = 0.08 kmol/(m²·s·Δx). Find the interface compositions and the flux.

  1. At the interface, equilibrium holds: yi = 2 xi.
  2. Set equal fluxes: 0.05(0.10 − yi) = 0.08(xi − 0.02).
  3. Substitute yi = 2 xi: 0.05(0.10 − 2 xi) = 0.08(xi − 0.02).
  4. Expand: 0.005 − 0.10 xi = 0.08 xi − 0.0016.
  5. Collect: 0.0066 = 0.18 xi, so xi = 0.03667.
  6. Then yi = 2(0.03667) = 0.07333.
  7. Gas-side flux: NA = 0.05(0.10 − 0.07333) = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s). Liquid side gives the same: 0.08(0.03667 − 0.02) = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s).

Answer. xi = 0.03667, yi = 0.07333, and NA = 1.333×10⁻³ kmol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Assuming the bulk gas and bulk liquid are in equilibrium. Equilibrium holds only at the interface; y* = m x is a hypothetical gas in equilibrium with the bulk liquid, not the actual bulk gas.
  • Mixing interface and bulk values: using y* = m xi instead of y* = m x, or yi = m x instead of yi = m xi.
  • Adding ky and kx directly. They use different driving forces, so the m factor must appear: 1/Ky = 1/ky + m/kx, not 1/ky + 1/kx.
  • Putting the interface point beyond the bulk point. The interface lies between the bulk point and the equilibrium line, and the tie line slope is −kx/ky.
  • Forgetting units: ky has Δy in its denominator and kx has Δx. Check that each NA comes out in kmol/(m²·s).

2.7 Equilibrium stages, cascades and stage efficiency

An equilibrium stage is a tiny contactor where the two leaving streams are allowed to reach equilibrium. Real trays cannot wait that long. This chapter defines the ideal stage, measures how far a real tray falls short with the Murphree efficiency, links ideal stages to actual trays with the overall efficiency, and shows how cascades of stages are counted with operating lines and the Kremser equation. It is the bridge between equilibrium data and the number of trays you buy.

You need: Chapter 2.6 for interphase equilibrium and the idea of resistances, and the equilibrium curves introduced in Unit 4.

Ideal stage and Murphree efficiency

A stage is one contacting unit: vapor bubbles through liquid on a tray, or liquid sprays into gas. In an ideal equilibrium stage, the vapor and liquid leaving the stage are in equilibrium. The stage is perfectly mixed and contact time is infinite. No real tray reaches this limit. The leaving vapor yn is closer to the entering vapor yn+1 than to equilibrium.

EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1)

EM is the Murphree vapor efficiency (dimensionless); yn is the mole fraction of solute in vapor leaving stage n; yn+1 is the mole fraction in vapor entering from below; yn* is the mole fraction in vapor that would be in equilibrium with the liquid leaving stage n, at the tray temperature and pressure. All y values are mole fractions.

EM = 1 means the tray is ideal. EM = 0 means no change in vapor composition. The value is per tray and per component. A liquid-phase Murphree efficiency uses x values and is defined similarly; do not mix the two.

Eo = Nideal / Nactual

Eo is the overall efficiency (dimensionless); Nideal is the number of equilibrium stages from a stepping method; Nactual is the number of real trays. Rearranged, Nactual = Nideal/Eo, rounded up to a whole tray.

Eo is usually less than EM because it also includes entrainment, weeping and flow maldistribution. Murphree efficiency is a local composition ratio. Overall efficiency is a count ratio. Neither changes the equilibrium curve.

Cascades: cross-current and counter-current

A cascade is a series of stages. In cross-current contact, fresh solvent enters each stage and each extract is removed. In counter-current contact, liquid and vapor flow in opposite directions through the cascade. Counter-current is the usual choice because the average driving force is larger; a given separation needs fewer stages.

For a counter-current cascade, number stages from bottom up. Vapor enters stage n from below at yn+1 and leaves at yn. Liquid enters from above at xn−1 and leaves at xn. A solute balance around stage n gives V(yn+1 − yn) = L(xn − xn−1), where V is vapor molar flow (kmol/s), L is liquid molar flow (kmol/s), and x and y are mole fractions. Rearranged, this is the operating line y = (L/V)x + b. The line is straight when L and V are constant.

Graphical stepping: plot the equilibrium curve y* against x and the operating line on the same axes. Start at the bottom composition, step horizontally to the equilibrium curve for one ideal stage, then vertically back to the operating line. Each pair of steps is one ideal stage. Count until the top composition is reached. These are ideal stages, not real trays.

Kremser equation for a fixed stage count

Kremser gives the fraction absorbed when the stage count N is fixed and the equilibrium line is straight. Use solute-free mole ratios Y and X to keep the balance simple. The equilibrium relation is Y* = mX, where m is the dimensionless slope. Define absorption factor A = L/(mG), where L is liquid molar flow (kmol/s) and G is gas molar flow (kmol/s).

(YN+1 − Y1) / (YN+1 − mX0) = (AN+1 − A) / (AN+1 − 1)

YN+1 is the solute-free mole ratio in entering gas; Y1 is the solute-free mole ratio in leaving gas; X0 is the solute-free mole ratio in entering liquid; N is the number of ideal stages; A is the absorption factor (dimensionless). For A = 1, the fraction absorbed is N/(N+1). For stripping, replace A by S = 1/A and swap gas and liquid labels.

A larger A means more liquid per unit gas, so the same stages remove more solute. The equation assumes constant L, G and m, and isothermal operation. It counts ideal stages. To get real trays, divide N by Eo. The Kremser result is a stage count; the Murphree efficiency is a tray composition ratio. They answer different questions.

Worked example

Question. On tray n of an absorber, vapor entering from below has yn+1 = 0.10 and vapor leaving has yn = 0.16 (mole fractions). The vapor leaving would be in equilibrium with the liquid leaving the tray at yn* = 0.20. (a) Find the Murphree vapor efficiency. (b) The ideal stage count is 10 and the overall efficiency is 0.60. Find the actual number of stages.

  1. Write the Murphree vapor efficiency: EM = (yn − yn+1) / (yn* − yn+1).
  2. Substitute the compositions: yn − yn+1 = 0.16 − 0.10 = 0.06; yn* − yn+1 = 0.20 − 0.10 = 0.10. Both are mole fractions, so the ratio is dimensionless.
  3. EM = 0.06/0.10 = 0.60, or 60%.
  4. Write the overall efficiency: Eo = Nideal/Nactual, so Nactual = Nideal/Eo.
  5. Nactual = 10/0.60 = 16.7. A real column has whole trays, so use 17 stages.

Answer. The Murphree vapor efficiency is 0.60 (60%), and 17 actual stages are needed.

Exam traps

  • Using yn* with the entering liquid or entering vapor. Murphree efficiency uses the vapor in equilibrium with the liquid leaving the tray.
  • Confusing Murphree efficiency with overall efficiency. Murphree compares compositions on one tray; overall compares ideal stage count to actual tray count.
  • Multiplying Nideal by Eo instead of dividing. A column with lower efficiency needs more actual trays: Nactual = Nideal/Eo.
  • Leaving the stage count as a decimal. Trays are whole items, so round the actual count up.
  • Writing A upside down in Kremser. For absorption A = L/(mG), not mG/L.

2.8 Stage-wise and differential contactors

Two contactor families answer one height question. Trays and mixers give discrete stages: each stage mixes the phases and then separates them. Packed towers give continuous contact: gas and liquid touch over every metre of packing. Tray height comes from stage count and stage efficiency; packed height comes from Z = HTU × NTU. Absorbers, strippers and distillation columns use both. This chapter shows how to turn a required separation into metres of contactor, and why HETP and HTU are different numbers even though both have units of length.

You need Chapter 2.2 for overall mass transfer coefficients and Chapter 2.7 for ideal stages and stage efficiency.

Stage-wise contactors: trays and mixers

In a stage-wise contactor, the fluids meet in a tray, a mixer or a mixer-settler, then leave. On an ideal stage the leaving streams are in equilibrium. A real stage is not perfect, so it needs an efficiency below 1.

Let Nactual be the number of real trays, Nideal the number of ideal stages, E the overall stage efficiency, and Z the tray-section height in metres. Then Nideal = E Nactual. The height per real tray is Z/Nactual. HETP is the height equivalent to a theoretical plate, so HETP = Z/Nideal = Z/(E Nactual).

HETP = Z/Nideal = Z/(E Nactual)

HETP is height equivalent to one theoretical plate (m); Z is tray-section height (m); Nideal is ideal stages; E is overall stage efficiency; Nactual is real trays.

Efficiency E is rarely 1. Liquid back-mixing, short contact time and poor vapour distribution lower E. A column therefore needs more real trays than ideal stages. If E = 0.5, ten ideal stages need twenty real trays.

Tray spacing sets the real height. Tray spacing is usually 0.45 m to 0.6 m, and may range from about 0.15 m to 0.9 m; the number is chosen from hydraulics, not from mass transfer alone. See Chapter 2.7 for stage efficiency.

Differential contactors: HTU × NTU

A packed tower has no trays. Design uses two lengths. HTU is the height of one transfer unit; NTU is the number of transfer units. The packed height is Z = HTU × NTU.

Z = HTU × NTU

Z is packed height (m); HTU is height of a transfer unit (m); NTU is number of transfer units (dimensionless).

HTU = G/(Kya)

G is molar gas flux (kmol/(m²·s)); Kya is volumetric overall gas-phase coefficient (kmol/(m³·s·Δy)); a is interfacial area per m³ of packing (m²/m³); Δy is mole-fraction driving force (dimensionless).

NTU measures how hard the separation is. For absorption, NTU = ∫ dy/(y* − y) between the terminal gas compositions yin and yout. Here y is gas mole fraction, y* is the gas mole fraction in equilibrium with the liquid at that point, and y* − y is the local driving force. The integral is positive when taken from the lower yout to the higher yin; reversing the limits changes the sign.

HTU measures packing and flow quality. A small HTU means good contact and a short tower. NTU measures the duty. This is the same overall coefficient idea as Chapter 2.2.

Same question, two designs

Both methods answer one question: what height is needed for a required separation? For trays, find Nideal, divide by E to get Nactual, then multiply by tray spacing. For packing, find NTU by the integral, get HTU from a correlation or measured Kya, then multiply.

Worked example

Question. A packed absorber has gas molar flux G = 0.02 kmol/(m²·s) and volumetric overall coefficient Kya = 0.05 kmol/(m³·s·Δy). The required NTU is 3.5. Find HTU and packed height Z.

  1. Write the packed-height equation: Z = HTU × NTU.
  2. Compute HTU = G/(Kya) = 0.02/0.05 = 0.4 m. Unit check: kmol/(m²·s) divided by kmol/(m³·s·Δy) gives m, because Δy is dimensionless.
  3. Compute Z = HTU × NTU = 0.4 m × 3.5 = 1.4 m.

Answer. HTU = 0.4 m and the required packed height is Z = 1.4 m.

Exam traps

  • HETP is height per ideal stage; HTU is height per transfer unit. They are not interchangeable.
  • In the NTU integral, divide by y* − y, the local driving force, not by y alone. Reversing the limits changes the sign.
  • Kya is volumetric, per m³ of packed volume. Ky without a is per m² of interface and cannot be used directly in HTU = G/(Kya).
  • Stage efficiency is E = Nideal/Nactual. Actual trays = ideal/E, so E below 1 always raises the tray count.
  • The terminal compositions in NTU are gas mole fractions yin and yout, not tray numbers or liquid compositions.

2.9 Mass transfer with chemical reaction

When a gas dissolves in a liquid that also reacts with it, the liquid film near the interface carries diffusion and reaction together. The reaction eats the dissolved gas, keeps the interfacial gradient steep, and pushes the flux above the physical value. The ratio is the enhancement factor E. The Hatta number Ha compares reaction speed with diffusion speed and decides the regime. CO₂ scrubbing with amine and H₂S scrubbing with alkali depend on this effect.

You need: Chapter 2.4 for the film picture and k_L, Chapter 2.6 for film resistances and interface equilibrium, and first-order kinetics.

Reaction inside the film steepens the gradient

Start with physical absorption. Gas A dissolves at the interface at C_Ai and diffuses through a liquid film of thickness δ into the bulk at C_Ab. The flux is N_A = k_L(C_Ai − C_Ab), with N_A in mol/(m²·s), k_L in m/s and C in mol/m³. The profile is a straight line, and the flux equals D_A times the slope at the interface.

Now let A react first order in the liquid, rate = k·C_A with k in s⁻¹. Inside the film A is removed by reaction as well as diffusion. The profile sags below the straight line and can reach zero before the film edge (figure). The flux is still N_A = −D_A(dC_A/dx) at x = 0, so a steeper starting slope means a bigger flux.

E = N_A with reaction ÷ N_A without reaction   (same C_Ai, same k_L)

E is the enhancement factor, dimensionless and ≥ 1; N_A is the molar flux of A in mol/(m²·s); both fluxes are taken at the same interfacial concentration and the same physical k_L.

Keep two effects apart. Reaction inside the film raises E. Reaction in the bulk lowers C_Ab towards zero, which protects the driving force (C_Ai − C_Ab) but does not raise E. A slow reaction gives E ≈ 1 and still helps by keeping the driving force at its maximum.

The Hatta number sorts the regimes

Ha = √(k·D_A) ÷ k_L

Ha is the Hatta number (dimensionless); k is the first-order rate constant in s⁻¹; D_A is the liquid diffusivity of A in m²/s; k_L is the physical liquid film coefficient in m/s, the value you would have with no reaction.

The film thickness is roughly δ = D_A/k_L, so Ha ≈ √(k·δ²/D_A) = δ ÷ √(D_A/k). The length √(D_A/k) is the distance A travels before it reacts. Ha measures film thickness in units of that length. Big Ha means the reaction wins.

E = Ha ÷ tanh(Ha)

E is the enhancement factor for a first-order reaction in the film; tanh is the hyperbolic tangent. For Ha > 3, tanh(Ha) ≈ 1 and E ≈ Ha. For Ha < 0.3, tanh(Ha) ≈ Ha and E ≈ 1.

The fast pseudo-first-order result needs 3 < Ha << E∞. As Ha approaches E∞, a reaction plane forms and E saturates at E∞ = 1 + (D_B·C_B)/(b·D_A·C_Ai), where C_B is bulk concentration of B in mol/m³, D_B its liquid diffusivity in m²/s, and b the moles of B per mole of A. More B or faster diffusion of B is the only help.

Where it is used: reactive absorption

Carbon dioxide scrubbing with aqueous MEA is the standard example. CO₂ enters the film and reacts with the amine quickly, so Ha is of order 10 and E ≈ Ha. The flux is roughly ten times the physical flux. The liquid-film resistance drops from 1/k_L to 1/(E·k_L), so the HTU falls by about E only when the liquid film controls; with strong gas-film resistance the height hardly changes (see Unit 3).

Hydrogen sulphide in dilute caustic soda behaves differently. The proton transfer is essentially instantaneous, so the column runs at E∞, limited by how fast OH⁻ diffuses to the reaction plane. Fresh caustic enters at the bottom, loads on the way up, and the enhancement falls as B runs out.

  • Design flux: N_A = E·k_L·C_Ai, valid only when C_Ab ≈ 0 (fast regime).
  • k_L must be the physical coefficient from a correlation (Chapter 2.3), never back-calculated from an experiment with reaction.
  • Raising liquid rate raises k_L, lowers Ha and lowers E. Each extra unit of pumping buys less than one unit of flux.
  • Interfacial C_Ai comes from Henry's law and gas-side resistance (Chapter 2.6).

Worked example

Question. A gas A dissolves in a liquid in which it undergoes a first-order reaction with rate constant k = 10 s⁻¹. The liquid diffusivity of A is D_A = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/s and the physical liquid-film coefficient is k_L = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m/s. The interfacial concentration is C_Ai = 0.05 mol/m³ and the bulk concentration of A is negligible. (a) Find the Hatta number. (b) Name the regime and find the enhancement factor. (c) Find the flux with and without reaction.

  1. Step 1 — Ha = √(k·D_A)/k_L. k·D_A = 10 × 2×10⁻⁹ = 2×10⁻⁸ m²/s².
  2. Step 2 — √(2×10⁻⁸) = 1.414×10⁻⁴ m/s.
  3. Step 3 — Ha = 1.414×10⁻⁴ ÷ 1.0×10⁻⁵ = 14.14.
  4. Step 4 — Ha > 3: fast pseudo-first-order; C_Ab ≈ 0.
  5. Step 5 — E = Ha/tanh(Ha); tanh(14.14) ≈ 1, so E = 14.14.
  6. Step 6 — N⁰ = k_L·C_Ai = 1.0×10⁻⁵ × 0.05 = 5.0×10⁻⁷ mol/(m²·s).
  7. Step 7 — N = E·N⁰ = 14.14 × 5.0×10⁻⁷ = 7.07×10⁻⁶ mol/(m²·s).

Exam traps

  • Using the gas-side coefficient in Ha. k_L and D_A are liquid-side values; putting k_G or a gas diffusivity gives a meaningless number.
  • Unit slips: k in s⁻¹, D_A in m²/s, k_L in m/s. Mixing cm²/s with m/s shifts Ha by a factor of 100, because 1 cm²/s = 10⁻⁴ m²/s and the square root halves that power of ten. A factor of 100 can flip the regime.
  • Quoting E ≈ Ha everywhere. It holds only for 3 < Ha << E∞; below use E = Ha/tanh(Ha); at the limit use E∞ = 1 + (D_B·C_B)/(b·D_A·C_Ai).
  • Treating E as a ratio of measured k_L values from two runs. E is a flux ratio at the same C_Ai and physical k_L.
  • Thinking a slow reaction is useless because E ≈ 1. It keeps C_Ab near zero, so the full driving force survives — a bulk effect, not film enhancement.

Takeaways

  1. A convective mass transfer coefficient k_c turns a complex flow problem into a simple flux equation N_A = k_c ΔC_A.
  2. Individual film coefficients add as resistances; the overall coefficient depends on which phase's driving force you choose.
  3. Dimensionless correlations write Sh as a function of Re, Sc and geometry, so one measured system predicts another.
  4. Film, penetration and surface-renewal theories give different physical pictures of the same k_c.
  5. Momentum, heat and mass analogies let a friction factor or heat transfer coefficient estimate k_c.
  6. At the interface the two phases are in equilibrium, so y_i = m x_i, and the two film resistances set the flux.
  7. An ideal stage is an equilibrium stage; stage efficiency converts ideal stages into real trays.
  8. Tray columns are counted in stages; packed columns are sized by H_OG multiplied by N_OG.
  9. A reaction in the liquid film can raise flux by E, and Ha tells whether the reaction is slow or fast.

Glossary

Chilton–Colburn analogy
An analogy linking mass transfer and friction through j_D = (k_c/u)Sc^(2/3) = f/2.
Convective mass transfer
Mass transfer from a moving fluid to a surface or another fluid, helped by bulk flow and turbulence.
Eddy diffusion
Turbulent mixing that increases transport beyond molecular diffusion, described by eddy diffusivity.
Enhancement factor
The factor by which a chemical reaction increases mass flux above physical absorption.
Equilibrium curve
A plot of equilibrium vapor composition against liquid composition at fixed temperature and pressure.
Film theory
A model that treats resistance as a stagnant film of fixed thickness δ, giving k_c = D/δ.
Hatta number
A dimensionless number comparing reaction rate to diffusion rate in the liquid film.
Height of a transfer unit
The packed height needed for one transfer unit, H_OG = G/(K_y a).
Henry's law constant
A constant relating gas partial pressure to liquid mole fraction at equilibrium, p_A = H x_A.
Ideal stage
A hypothetical stage that leaves its exiting vapor and liquid in equilibrium.
Individual mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient for one phase film, such as gas-film k_y or liquid-film k_x.
Interphase mass transfer
Transfer of a component from one phase to another across their interface, as in absorption or extraction.
Mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient that gives mass flux per unit concentration driving force across a fluid phase.
Number of transfer units
A dimensionless measure of separation duty, N_OG = ∫ dy/(y − y*).
Operating line
A material-balance line relating vapor and liquid compositions in a stage or packed contactor.
Overall mass transfer coefficient
A coefficient based on the total driving force from bulk gas to bulk liquid, written K_y or K_x.
Packed column
A continuous-contact column filled with packing that provides interfacial area for mass transfer.
Penetration theory
A model in which fluid elements contact the interface for a short time t, giving k_c ∝ √(D/t).
Reactive absorption
Gas absorption in which the dissolved gas reacts with a component in the liquid.
Reynolds number
A dimensionless ratio of inertial to viscous forces, Re = ρuL/μ, used in flow correlations.
Schmidt number
A dimensionless ratio of momentum diffusivity to mass diffusivity, Sc = μ/(ρD_AB).
Sherwood number
A dimensionless mass transfer coefficient, Sh = k_c L/D_AB.
Stage efficiency
The ratio of the composition change in a real stage to that in an ideal equilibrium stage.
Surface renewal theory
A model in which surface elements are replaced at a renewal rate s, giving k_c = √(Ds).
Tray column
A stagewise column with plates or trays on which vapor bubbles through liquid.
Two-film theory
A model with one gas film and one liquid film in series, with equilibrium at the interface.
