7.1 Humidity terms: absolute and relative humidity, dew point

Every property of moist air comes from two measured numbers: the dry-bulb temperature and the amount of water vapour. Absolute humidity Y is the mass of vapour per kg of dry air; relative humidity compares the vapour pressure with the saturation value; the dew point is where saturation begins. All of them rest on the vapour pressure of pure water. Cooling towers, dryers, air-conditioning ducts and humidifiers are sized from these numbers, because the water content decides how much heat and water the air can carry.

You need: school-level vapour pressure and the gas laws, and the pure-water steam tables for saturation pressures and latent heats.

Humidity on a dry-air basis

Moist air is a mixture of dry air and water vapour. The dry air never changes as humidity changes, so we count water against dry air. Absolute humidity (also called humidity ratio) Y is the mass of water vapour carried by 1 kg of dry air. Its unit is kg water per kg dry air.

Y = 0.622 pA / (P − pA)

Y is the absolute humidity in kg water/kg dry air; pA is the partial pressure of water vapour in kPa; P is the total pressure in kPa; 0.622 = 18.02/28.97, the ratio of the molar mass of water to that of air.

The same ratio comes from Dalton's law. The vapour and the air fill the same volume at the same temperature, so the mole ratio equals the pressure ratio. The number 0.622 converts that mole ratio into a mass ratio.

At a given temperature there is a maximum vapour pressure the air can hold: the saturation pressure pAS, read from steam tables. The humidity at that maximum is the saturation humidity Ys, obtained by putting pAS in place of pA.

Ys = 0.622 pAS / (P − pAS)

Ys is the saturation humidity in kg water/kg dry air at that temperature; pAS is the vapour pressure of water at that temperature in kPa.

Relative humidity (RH) is a percentage. It compares the actual vapour pressure with the saturation pressure at the same temperature.

RH = 100 pA / pAS

RH is the relative humidity in per cent; pA is the actual vapour pressure in kPa; pAS is the saturation pressure at the same dry-bulb temperature in kPa.

Percentage humidity (percentage saturation) compares humidities instead of pressures: 100 Y/Ys at the same temperature. The two numbers are close when the air is nearly saturated and drift apart as the air dries, because Y is not linear in pA. Use the one the question names: given a percentage humidity, find Y = (percentage/100) × Ys; given a relative humidity, find pA first.

Saturation pressure rises fast with temperature: at 40 °C it is 7.38 kPa, at 12.7 °C about 1.48 kPa. So warm air at a fixed Y always shows a lower RH, which is why a room feels dry on a hot afternoon.

Dew point above 0 °C

Dew point is the temperature at which the vapour present just saturates the air at constant pressure. Below it, water condenses as dew or fog. To find it, take the actual pA and look up the saturation temperature of water at that pressure.

Dew point is fixed by Y and P alone, not by the dry-bulb temperature. Two air samples with the same Y share one dew point. That is why weather reports quote it as a comfort measure. Air at 30 °C and 70 % RH on a humid coastal afternoon has pA = 0.70 × 4.25 = 2.97 kPa and a dew point near 24 °C; sweat on skin at about 34 °C then evaporates slowly and the air feels sticky. At 30 °C and 40 % RH, pA = 1.70 kPa and the dew point is about 15 °C, and the same temperature feels comfortable.

Air below 0 °C has a frost point instead of a dew point. The psychrometric chart of Chapter 7.2 draws all these quantities as families of lines.

Volume, humid heat and enthalpy

Humid volume vH is the volume occupied by 1 kg of dry air plus its vapour, in m³/kg dry air. Treat the mixture as ideal and use the molar volume 22.414 m³/kmol at 0 °C and 101.325 kPa. The formula below holds at that total pressure; at another total pressure P, multiply it by 101.325/P.

vH = (1/28.97 + Y/18.02) × 22.414 × (T/273.15)

vH is the humid volume in m³/kg dry air; Y is the absolute humidity; T is the absolute temperature in K; 28.97 and 18.02 are the molar masses of air and water in kg/kmol.

Humid heat cs is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of dry air plus its vapour by 1 K. The two specific heats 1.005 and 1.88 kJ/(kg·K) belong to dry air and to water vapour.

cs = 1.005 + 1.88 Y

cs is the humid heat in kJ/(kg dry air·K); Y is the absolute humidity in kg/kg dry air; 1.005 and 1.88 are the specific heats of dry air and water vapour in kJ/(kg·K).

Enthalpy H of moist air is measured above a reference of dry air and liquid water at 0 °C. It has a sensible part for warming the mixture and a latent part for the vapour already formed. The latent heat of water at 0 °C is taken as 2500 kJ/kg.

H = cs (T − Tref) + Y λref

H is the enthalpy in kJ/kg dry air; T is the dry-bulb temperature in °C; Tref = 0 °C; λref = 2500 kJ/kg, the latent heat of vaporisation of water at the reference temperature.

The 2500 and the 1.88 are the pure-water numbers of Section 1. These definitions feed directly into Chapter 7.3 and into the cooling-tower heat balances of Chapter 7.5.

Worked example

Question. Air at 40 °C and 20 % relative humidity flows at 101.3 kPa. The saturation pressure of water at 40 °C is 7.38 kPa. Find (a) the partial pressure of water vapour, (b) the absolute humidity, (c) the dew point, (d) the humid volume, (e) the humid heat and (f) the enthalpy above 0 °C.

  1. (a) Partial pressure: pA = RH × pAS = 0.20 × 7.38 = 1.476 kPa, say 1.48 kPa.
  2. (b) Absolute humidity: Y = 0.622 × 1.48 / (101.3 − 1.48) = 0.9206 / 99.82 = 0.00922 kg water/kg dry air.
  3. (c) Dew point: find the saturation temperature at pA = 1.48 kPa. From steam tables this is about 12.7 °C. The air begins to condense water below that temperature.
  4. (d) Humid volume: vH = (1/28.97 + 0.00922/18.02) × 22.414 × (313.15/273.15) = (0.03452 + 0.000512) × 22.414 × 1.1464 = 0.90 m³/kg dry air.
  5. (e) Humid heat: cs = 1.005 + 1.88 × 0.00922 = 1.005 + 0.01733 = 1.022 kJ/(kg dry air·K).
  6. (f) Enthalpy: H = 1.022 × 40 + 0.00922 × 2500 = 40.88 + 23.05 = 63.9 kJ/kg dry air.

Answer. pA = 1.48 kPa, Y = 0.00922 kg/kg dry air, dew point ≈ 12.7 °C, vH = 0.90 m³/kg dry air, cs = 1.022 kJ/(kg·K) and H = 63.9 kJ/kg dry air.

Exam traps

  • Reading relative humidity as the water content: at the same Y, warmer air has a lower RH, so 20 % RH at 40 °C holds more water than 20 % RH at 20 °C.
  • Using percentage humidity and relative humidity interchangeably. They are equal only at saturation, and differ noticeably for dry air.
  • Putting pA in the denominator of the humidity formula as P + pA, or using P = 1 atm without converting to the kPa of the steam tables.
  • Forgetting the dry-air basis: the humid heat and enthalpy are per kg dry air, not per kg of moist air.
  • Mixing dew point with wet-bulb temperature: wet-bulb is an adiabatic saturation value, dew point is a saturation-from-vapour-pressure value, and they coincide only at 100 % RH.

7.2 Psychrometric (humidity) chart and its use

A psychrometric chart, also called a humidity chart, puts every property of moist air on one sheet. Dry-bulb temperature runs along the bottom and humidity up the side. Any two independent readings fix a point, and the rest — dew point, relative humidity, wet-bulb temperature, enthalpy, humid volume — are read from lines through that point. It exists to save repeated calculations in drying, cooling towers and air conditioning. The standard chart is drawn for air–water at 1 atm.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for dry-bulb temperature, absolute humidity, relative humidity and dew point.

What the chart draws

The horizontal axis is dry-bulb temperature T in °C. The vertical axis is humidity Y in kg water vapour per kg dry air. A point on the sheet is one state of moist air. The saturation curve is the 100% relative-humidity line; below it air is unsaturated, and above it the mixture would be fog.

Curves of constant relative humidity run below the saturation curve. A horizontal line is constant Y. Its left end meets the saturation curve at the dew point, because cooling at constant Y raises relative humidity until condensation starts. The wet-bulb temperature is the steady reading of a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in a wet wick in moving air; Chapter 7.3 shows that for air–water it equals the adiabatic saturation temperature. Wet-bulb lines start on the saturation curve and slope down to the right. Enthalpy lines lie nearly parallel to them; the reason is in Chapter 7.3. Humid-volume lines give volume per kg dry air.

h = 1.005 T + Y (2500 + 1.88 T)

h is moist-air enthalpy in kJ/kg dry air; T is dry-bulb temperature in °C; Y is humidity in kg water/kg dry air. The constants 1.005 kJ/(kg·K), 2500 kJ/kg and 1.88 kJ/(kg·K) are the dry-air specific heat, the latent heat of water at 0 °C and the water-vapour specific heat.

One chart is valid for one total pressure, usually 1 atm, and for air–water only. At another pressure the same Y gives a different water-vapour partial pressure, so every curve shifts. Check the pressure printed on the chart.

Fixing a state and following a process

  1. Start with two independent readings: usually dry-bulb plus wet-bulb, or dry-bulb plus dew point.
  2. For dry-bulb plus wet-bulb, intersect the vertical dry-bulb line with the sloping wet-bulb line marked with that temperature.
  3. For dry-bulb plus dew point, find the dew point on the saturation curve. Run a horizontal from there to the dry-bulb vertical.
  4. From the point, read Y on the y-axis, dew point along the horizontal to saturation, relative humidity from the nearest curve, enthalpy from the sloping enthalpy scale through the point (close to the wet-bulb line but not the same line), and humid volume from its own line.

Processes are paths. Heating is horizontal to the right: Y stays fixed, so no water is added or removed. Cooling is horizontal to the left until the saturation curve. Further cooling follows the saturation curve and water condenses. Humidification with recirculated water runs up an adiabatic line, as Chapter 7.4 shows.

Do not read enthalpy by moving horizontally. A horizontal move changes only sensible heat; the chart's enthalpy lines are the sloping ones.

Mixing two streams: the lever rule

Two air streams that mix without heat or work loss give a point on the straight line joining their states. Use dry-air mass flows, not moist-air flows. For stream A with dry-air flow mA and stream B with mB, the mixture humidity and enthalpy are weighted averages.

Ymix = (mA YA + mB YB)/(mA + mB)

Ymix is mixture humidity; YA and YB are stream humidities; mA and mB are dry-air mass flows, all in consistent units. Enthalpy uses the same weights: hmix = (mA hA + mB hB)/(mA + mB).

The point divides the line in inverse proportion to the dry-air flows. If mA is larger, the mixture lies nearer A. Distance from A to the mixture divided by distance from A to B equals mB/(mA+mB). Once Ymix and hmix are known, the mixture temperature follows from the enthalpy formula or is read from the chart.

In the worked example, 100 kg dry air/h at 30 °C and Y = 0.010 mixes with 50 kg dry air/h at 40 °C and Y = 0.020. The mixture is one-third of the way from A to B.

Worked example

Question. At 1 atm, 100 kg dry air/h at 30 °C with humidity 0.010 kg/kg dry air mixes with 50 kg dry air/h at 40 °C with humidity 0.020 kg/kg dry air. Find the mixture humidity, enthalpy and dry-bulb temperature. Check the position by the lever rule.

  1. Basis: mA = 100 kg dry air/h, TA = 30 °C, YA = 0.010 kg/kg dry air; mB = 50 kg dry air/h, TB = 40 °C, YB = 0.020 kg/kg dry air; total m = 150 kg dry air/h.
  2. Ymix = (100 × 0.010 + 50 × 0.020)/150 = (1.000 + 1.000)/150 = 0.01333 kg/kg dry air.
  3. hA = 1.005 × 30 + 0.010 × (2500 + 1.88 × 30) = 30.15 + 0.010 × 2556.4 = 30.15 + 25.564 = 55.714 kJ/kg dry air.
  4. hB = 1.005 × 40 + 0.020 × (2500 + 1.88 × 40) = 40.20 + 0.020 × 2575.2 = 40.20 + 51.504 = 91.704 kJ/kg dry air.
  5. hmix = (100 × 55.714 + 50 × 91.704)/150 = (5571.4 + 4585.2)/150 = 67.711 kJ/kg dry air.
  6. 67.711 = 1.005 T + 0.01333(2500 + 1.88 T) = 33.333 + 1.03007 T. Hence T = (67.711 − 33.333)/1.03007 = 34.378/1.03007 = 33.374 °C.
  7. The distance from A is mB/(mA+mB) = 50/150 = 1/3 of AB, so the mixture is closer to A, consistent with the plotted point.

Answer. Ymix = 0.0133 kg/kg dry air, hmix = 67.7 kJ/kg dry air, Tmix = 33.4 °C; the point lies one-third of the way from stream A to stream B.

Exam traps

  • Using a 1 atm chart for air at 2 atm or for a vacuum dryer. Y, relative humidity and wet-bulb lines all change with total pressure.
  • Reading enthalpy along a horizontal line. Enthalpy lines are the sloping lines nearly parallel to the wet-bulb lines.
  • Confusing wet-bulb temperature with dew point. They are equal only at saturation; in unsaturated air the wet-bulb is higher.
  • Mixing with moist-air mass flows. The lever rule and Y balance use dry-air flows; moist-air flow includes the water vapour.
  • Cooling below the dew point by a horizontal path. Once saturation is reached the path bends along the saturation curve and liquid water leaves the air.

7.3 Adiabatic saturation and wet-bulb temperature

An adiabatic saturator is a chamber where air meets recirculated water with no heat through the walls. The air cools and takes up water vapour until it becomes saturated. The temperature it reaches is the adiabatic saturation temperature. A wet-bulb thermometer follows the same path: a wet wick cools until sensible heat from the air equals the latent heat of evaporation. Both give humidity from two temperatures, and both set the lower limit for evaporative cooling. They appear in cooling towers, air conditioning, dryer design and weather measurement.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for humidity and enthalpy, and Chapter 7.2 for reading the chart.

Adiabatic saturator

An adiabatic saturator is a well-insulated chamber. Air enters with dry-bulb temperature T and humidity Y. It flows over a large water surface. The water is recirculated, so it stays at the temperature the air reaches. No external heat enters. Some water evaporates. The latent heat comes from the air, so the air cools. The air leaves saturated at the adiabatic saturation temperature Tas. Its humidity is Ys(Tas), the saturation humidity at that temperature.

Y = Ys(Tas) − (cs/λs)(T − Tas)

Y is humidity of the entering air (kg water/kg dry air); T is dry-bulb temperature (°C); Tas is adiabatic saturation temperature (°C); Ys(Tas) is saturation humidity at Tas; cs is humid heat ≈1.00+1.88Y kJ/(kg dry air·K); λs is latent heat at Tas (kJ/kg water).

The enthalpy balance says the sensible heat lost by the air equals the latent heat used to evaporate the water. On a chart of Y against T, this equation is a straight line with slope −cs/λs. The line starts at the known air state and ends on the saturation curve at Tas. For air–water, λs is about 2450 kJ/kg near room temperature, so the slope is about −0.00042 kg/kg per K. The line is nearly flat.

Wet-bulb thermometer

A wet-bulb thermometer has a bulb covered by a wet cloth wick. Air flows past the wick. Water evaporates from the wick. The wick cools below the air temperature. At steady state, sensible heat from the air to the wick equals latent heat carried away by evaporation.

hc(T − Tw) = kYλ(Ys(Tw) − Y)

hc is convective heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K); Tw is wet-bulb temperature (°C); kY is mass transfer coefficient (kg/m²·s·ΔY); λ is latent heat at Tw (J/kg water); Ys(Tw) is saturation humidity at Tw; Y is humidity of the air.

For a reliable reading, keep the wick wet, keep air speed above about 3 m/s, and shield the wick from radiation. Low air speed or a dry wick gives a reading closer to the dry-bulb temperature.

The Lewis relation for air–water is hc/(kYcs) ≈ 1, in consistent units. Put this into the wet-bulb equation and it becomes the same adiabatic saturation equation. So Tw ≈ Tas for air–water. For organic vapours the Lewis relation does not hold, so wet-bulb temperature and adiabatic saturation temperature are different.

Reading humidity and the cooling limit

On the chart, start at the saturation curve at Tw. Move along the adiabatic line to the dry-bulb temperature T. Read Y at the air state. For T = 40 °C and Tw = 22 °C, Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg and the line gives Y ≈ 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. Only two temperatures are needed.

The wet-bulb temperature is the lower limit of adiabatic cooling for air–water. Air can cool toward Tw by evaporating water into it, but not below Tw in that process. In a cooling tower the water outlet approaches the entering air wet-bulb temperature, not its dry-bulb temperature; see Chapter 7.5.

Worked example

Question. Air at 40 °C dry bulb and 20 % relative humidity has Y = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. It passes a wet-bulb thermometer and then an adiabatic saturator. Find the wet-bulb temperature and the humidity after the saturator.

  1. Entering humidity is given: Y = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. A check from 20 % relative humidity at 40 °C gives the same value.
  2. Try Tw = 22 °C. The saturation humidity at 22 °C is Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air.
  3. Use cs = 1.005 + 1.88 × 0.0092 = 1.022 kJ/(kg·K) and λs ≈ 2450 kJ/kg. The adiabatic line gives Y = 0.0167 − (1.022/2450)(40 − 22) = 0.0167 − 0.0075 = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air.
  4. This matches the entering 0.0092, so the wet-bulb and adiabatic saturation temperature is Tw ≈ 22 °C.
  5. In an ideal saturator the air leaves saturated at 22 °C, so Yout = Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air.
  6. Water gained = 0.0167 − 0.0092 = 0.0075 kg water per kg dry air.

Answer. The wet-bulb temperature is about 22 °C, the air leaves at Y = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air, and it gains 0.0075 kg water per kg dry air.

Exam traps

  • Writing wet-bulb temperature equal to adiabatic saturation temperature for every vapour. They are equal only for air–water, where the Lewis relation holds.
  • Using the dry-bulb temperature instead of Tw or Tas when finding Ys. The saturation humidity must be taken at the wet-bulb or adiabatic saturation temperature.
  • Mixing units: λ is in kJ/kg while cs is in kJ/(kg·K). Using λ in J/kg without converting the other term gives a factor-of-1000 error.
  • Forgetting the practical conditions. A dry wick, low air speed or strong radiation makes the wet-bulb reading unreliable.
  • Getting the sign wrong on the adiabatic line. As T falls, Y rises, so the slope is negative: −cs/λs.

7.4 Humidification and dehumidification operations

Air is humidified when water evaporates into it and dehumidified when water leaves it. Both are moves on the psychrometric chart: heating, cooling, adding water, removing water, or mixing two air streams. A spray of recirculated water humidifies air along a wet-bulb line and stops at saturation. Cooling below the dew point, liquid desiccants, solid adsorbents and compression remove water. These operations set the air condition in rooms, drying plants and cooling towers. Here Y is the humidity ratio: kg of water vapour per kg of dry air.

You need: humidity terms, the psychrometric chart, adiabatic saturation and wet-bulb temperature, and the transfer-unit (NTU) method from the gas absorption topic.

Methods of humidification

Humidification means adding water vapour to air. Three common methods do this in different ways.

Spray chamber or air washer. Air passes up through a chamber while water sprays down from nozzles. A mist eliminator removes drops. The water collects in a basin and is pumped back. With no external heating or cooling, the recirculated water settles at the adiabatic saturation temperature, and the air follows a wet-bulb line. The air can only approach saturation; it cannot cross the saturation curve.

Wetted-surface humidifier. Air flows over a wet pad or wetted plates. Water evaporates from the surface. This is simple and is used in evaporative coolers and small air-conditioning units. The pad must stay wet and clean.

Steam injection. Dry steam is injected into the air duct. It adds water vapour and heat together. The humidity rises; the dry-bulb temperature also tends to rise. It is used in clean rooms and hospitals where fine control and no water droplets are needed.

In spray chambers and wetted-surface humidifiers the driving force is the difference between the vapour pressure at the water surface and the partial pressure of water vapour in the air. Steam injection adds moisture by direct mixing, not by evaporation from a surface.

Methods of dehumidification

Dehumidification means removing water vapour. A cold surface, a liquid desiccant, a solid adsorbent or compression can do it.

Cooling below the dew point. Cool the air sensibly at constant humidity until its dew point. Further cooling makes water condense on the coil. The air then follows the saturation curve downwards. This is the usual room air-conditioner coil. The condensate must be drained.

Liquid absorption. A concentrated lithium chloride or glycol solution has a low water vapour pressure. Water vapour from the air dissolves into it. The solution becomes dilute and must be regenerated by heating. Absorption releases heat, so the air may need cooling afterwards.

Solid adsorption. Silica gel or activated alumina holds water on its surface. The bed must be regenerated with hot air. This gives very dry air, down to low dew points.

Compression. Compression raises the partial pressure of the water vapour while the saturation vapour pressure depends only on temperature, so the air becomes supersaturated and water condenses. Cooling the compressed air in an aftercooler lowers the saturation pressure further and removes more water. This is used in small compressed-air dryers.

A cold water spray is not a dehumidifier for ordinary air. Unless the water is below the air dew point, the spray can only humidify the air towards saturation.

Chart paths and packed humidifier design

On the chart, every operation is a path. Heating is a horizontal line to the right: Y is constant, relative humidity falls. Cooling is horizontal to the left until the dew point, then it bends down along the saturation curve and water condenses. Humidifying moves upward. An adiabatic spray follows a wet-bulb line. Dehumidifying moves downward, by cooling, desiccant or compression. Mixing two streams gives a point on the straight line joining them, fixed by the mass balance.

Packed humidifier design uses the same transfer-unit idea as a packed absorber, and two cases arise. When a heater or chiller holds the water at a fixed temperature, the air enthalpy moves toward the saturated enthalpy Hs at that water temperature, and the enthalpy difference is the driving force:

NTU = ∫ dH / (Hs − H), from Hin to Hout
Z = HTU × NTU
HTU = G / (KHa)

H is the moist-air enthalpy (kJ per kg dry air); Hs is the saturated-air enthalpy at the water temperature (kJ per kg dry air); NTU is the number of transfer units (dimensionless); HTU is the height of a transfer unit (m); Z is the packed height (m); G is the dry-air mass velocity (kg dry air per m² s); KH is the enthalpy transfer coefficient and a is the packing area per unit volume, so KHa is the volumetric enthalpy transfer coefficient.

For a spray chamber with recirculated water, the water temperature is nearly constant, so Hs is nearly constant. Then: NTU = ln[(Hs − Hin)/(Hs − Hout)]. The design steps are: fix inlet and outlet air states; find Hin, Hout and Hs; compute NTU; choose packing and flow to get HTU; multiply to get Z. A larger NTU means the outlet air is closer to saturation.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg dry air/h enters an adiabatic spray chamber at 35 °C with Y = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air and leaves at 25 °C. (a) Find the outlet humidity using a wet-bulb slope of 0.00042 kg/kg per K. (b) Find the water evaporated. (c) If the outlet air is cooled to 15 °C, find the water condensed. The saturation humidity at 15 °C is 0.0106 kg/kg.

  1. Temperature drop = 35 − 25 = 10 K.
  2. On the wet-bulb line the slope is about cs/λ ≈ 0.00042 kg/kg per K, where cs is the humid heat and λ is the latent heat of water. ΔY = 0.00042 × 10 = 0.0042 kg/kg.
  3. Yout = 0.010 + 0.0042 = 0.0142 kg/kg.
  4. Water evaporated = 1000 × (0.0142 − 0.010) = 1000 × 0.0042 = 4.2 kg/h.
  5. At 15 °C the saturation humidity is Ysat = 0.0106 kg/kg. The excess water is 0.0142 − 0.0106 = 0.0036 kg/kg.
  6. Water condensed = 1000 × 0.0036 = 3.6 kg/h.

Answer. The outlet humidity is 0.0142 kg/kg dry air, 4.2 kg/h of water evaporates, and 3.6 kg/h condenses when the outlet air is cooled to 15 °C.

7.5 Cooling towers: types, construction and design

A cooling tower is an evaporative cooler for water. Hot water falls through packing while air rises or crosses. A small fraction of the water evaporates and carries latent heat away, so the rest leaves colder. For large heat loads this is cheaper than a closed heat exchanger. Power stations, refineries, petrochemical plants and big air-conditioning systems use cooling towers to reuse cooling water. The design is set by enthalpy, not by a plain temperature difference: the wet-bulb temperature fixes the lowest water temperature, and the Merkel integral converts the required cooling into fill volume.

You need 7.3 for wet-bulb temperature and 7.4 for the air-water enthalpy balance; the mass transfer coefficient topic supplies the coefficients behind Ka.

What a cooling tower does and how it is built

A cooling tower rejects heat from circulating water to air. Hot water is sprayed over a fill. Air passes through the fill. A small part of the water evaporates, and the latent heat leaves with the vapour. The remaining water becomes colder. This is not a closed heat exchanger; the cooling comes from evaporation.

  • Natural-draught atmospheric towers rely on wind and a chimney effect. Natural-draught hyperbolic towers use a tall concrete shell to create draught; large power stations use them.
  • Mechanical-draught towers use fans: forced-draught towers blow air in at the bottom, induced-draught towers pull it out at the top. Induced draught is more common, because the warm, humid exhaust leaves fast and is not drawn back into the inlet.
  • Counter-flow towers have water falling down and air rising up. Cross-flow towers have water falling down and air moving horizontally.
  • Construction: fill or packing gives thin films and large contact area; nozzles distribute hot water; drift eliminators catch droplets; louvers guide air; the fan moves air; the cold water basin collects cooled water.

Range is hot-water temperature minus cold-water temperature. Approach is cold-water temperature minus entering-air wet-bulb temperature. For 40 °C hot, 30 °C cold and 24 °C wet bulb, range is 10 K and approach is 6 K. The wet bulb is the lower limit because evaporation cannot cool water below it. A smaller approach needs a larger fill. Dry-bulb temperature alone does not set the size.

Enthalpy driving force and the Merkel equation

At any level, water loses heat L cp dT and air gains enthalpy G dh. For constant L/G, air enthalpy changes linearly with water temperature: h = h1 + (L cp/G)(T − Tc). The local driving force is h* − h. Here h* is the saturation enthalpy of air at the water temperature. h is the bulk air enthalpy at that level.

L cp dT = G dh

L is water mass flow in kg/s; cp is water specific heat, about 4.187 kJ/(kg K); dT is a small water temperature change in K; G is dry-air mass flow in kg/s; dh is the small air enthalpy change in kJ/kg dry air.

KaV/L = cp ∫ dT/(h* − h) from Tc to Th

KaV/L is the tower characteristic, dimensionless; cp is water specific heat, 4.187 kJ/(kg K) when h is in kJ/kg dry air and T is in K, and this factor is what makes KaV/L dimensionless; K is the mass transfer coefficient, a is interfacial area per unit volume, V is fill volume in m³, L is water flow in kg/s; Tc and Th are cold and hot water temperatures; h* is saturation enthalpy at the water temperature and h is bulk air enthalpy, both in kJ/kg dry air.

KaV/L is the number of transfer units, NTU. With fill height Z, Z = HTU × NTU and HTU = L/(Ka A), where A is the tower cross-sectional area in m²; then HTU is in metres and Z comes out in metres. The tower characteristic depends on L/G: more air per kg water lowers h at the same water temperature, raises h* − h, and lowers KaV/L, but fan power rises. Manufacturers give KaV/L against L/G for each fill.

A four-point Chebyshev estimate uses four fixed nodes: evaluate 1/(h* − h) at T = Tc + 0.1R, Tc + 0.4R, Tc + 0.6R and Tc + 0.9R, where R = Th − Tc, and take KaV/L ≈ cp R/4 times the sum. For three equally spaced points, Simpson's rule is KaV/L ≈ cp (Th − Tc)/6 [fbottom + 4 fmid + ftop], where f = 1/(h* − h).

Water losses, blowdown and fan power

Evaporation loss is E = G(Y2 − Y1), where Y1 and Y2 are inlet and outlet air humidity in kg water/kg dry air. Pure water leaves and salts stay, so dissolved solids concentrate. Blowdown B is deliberate drain. With cycles of concentration N and negligible drift, B ≈ E/(N − 1). More cycles save water but raise scaling risk.

Fan power is P ≈ Qair Δp/η, where Qair is air volume flow in m³/s, Δp is fan pressure rise in Pa, and η is fan efficiency. Higher air flow lowers L/G, which lowers the tower characteristic and so can reduce fill size, but fan power rises.

Worked example

Question. A tower cools 1.0 kg/s of water from 40 °C to 30 °C. Air enters at a wet bulb of 24 °C and leaves at about 38 °C; dry-air flow is 0.9 kg/s. Saturation enthalpies h* at 30, 35 and 40 °C are 99.7, 129.0 and 166.9 kJ/kg dry air. Inlet air has h1 = 72.2 kJ/kg and Y1 = 0.0189 kg/kg dry air; outlet air has Y2 = 0.031 kg/kg dry air. Find the heat load, the tower characteristic and the evaporation loss.

  1. Heat load: Q = L cp (Th − Tc) = 1.0 × 4.187 × (40 − 30) = 41.87 kW.
  2. Air enthalpy rise: dh = Q/G = 41.87/0.9 = 46.52 kJ/kg dry air. Outlet enthalpy h2 = 72.2 + 46.52 = 118.7 kJ/kg dry air.
  3. Bottom driving force: h* at 30 °C is 99.7; h1 is 72.2; h* − h = 99.7 − 72.2 = 27.5 kJ/kg.
  4. Middle: water at 35 °C, h* = 129.0; air enthalpy h = 72.2 + 46.52/2 = 95.5; driving force = 129.0 − 95.5 = 33.6 kJ/kg.
  5. Top: h* at 40 °C is 166.9; h2 is 118.7; driving force = 166.9 − 118.7 = 48.2 kJ/kg.
  6. Simpson: KaV/L = cp (Th − Tc)/6 [1/27.5 + 4/33.6 + 1/48.2] = 4.187 × 0.2936 = 1.23.
  7. Evaporation: E = G(Y2 − Y1) = 0.9 × (0.031 − 0.0189) = 0.0109 kg/s ≈ 40 kg/h. This is 0.0109/1.0 × 100 = 1.1 % of circulation.

Answer. The heat load is 41.9 kW, the tower characteristic is about 1.2 (dimensionless), and evaporation is about 0.011 kg/s (roughly 40 kg/h, 1.1 % of circulation).

7.6 Drying mechanism and drying equilibria

A wet solid is not a bucket of water. Water sits in pores, on surfaces and inside cell walls, and part of it is held so tightly that ordinary air cannot pull it out. Drying stops when the solid's moisture equals the equilibrium moisture of the air around it. Engineers use moisture content on a dry basis, equilibrium isotherms and free moisture to judge how much water a dryer can remove and where the drying rate will change. This chapter builds that picture and links it to drying rate curves.

You need: 7.1 for absolute humidity and 7.2 for relative humidity and the psychrometric chart.

Moisture content on dry and wet bases

A solid sample has mass ms of dry solid and mw of water. The dry-basis moisture content X is kg water per kg dry solid. The wet-basis moisture w is kg water per kg wet solid. Dryers and drying curves use X because the dry solid mass does not change; buyers and labels often use w.

X = mw / ms

X is kg water per kg dry solid; mw is kg water; ms is kg dry solid.

w = mw / (mw + ms) = X / (1 + X)

w is kg water per kg wet solid; X is kg water per kg dry solid.

The reverse conversion is X = w/(1 − w). A sample that is 30 % water on a wet basis has w = 0.30 and X = 0.30/0.70 = 0.429 kg/kg dry solid, not 0.30. Keep the basis on every number.

Bound, unbound, free and equilibrium moisture

Bound moisture is held by sorption on surfaces and inside cell walls; it exerts a vapour pressure below that of pure water. Unbound moisture is liquid in large pores and capillaries; it behaves like ordinary water. Free moisture is the water above the equilibrium value, X − X*. Only free moisture can be removed by air of a given state.

Xf = X − X*

Xf is free moisture, kg/kg dry solid; X is current moisture, kg/kg dry solid; X* is equilibrium moisture, kg/kg dry solid.

Equilibrium moisture content X* is the moisture a solid holds when it neither gains nor loses water to air at a fixed temperature and relative humidity. At X = X*, evaporation and condensation are equal. A perfectly non-hygroscopic solid such as clean sand has X* close to zero at every relative humidity, because its water sits in open pores and escapes as freely as pure water. Hygroscopic solids such as wood, clay and food retain water even in dry air, so X* > 0.

  • Hygroscopic: X* rises steeply near high relative humidity; water is sorbed in cell walls.
  • Non-hygroscopic: X* is small or zero; water sits in pores.
  • Hysteresis: the desorption curve lies above the adsorption curve. At the same relative humidity a solid that is being dried holds more water than the same solid taking up water from dry air. A dried solid exposed to humid air follows the lower adsorption branch.

Temperature shifts the isotherm: warmer air gives a lower X* at the same relative humidity. Use the isotherm at the dryer temperature, not a room-temperature curve.

Mechanism of drying

Drying has three resistances in series: movement inside the solid, vapour transport away from the surface, and heat supply to the surface. The slowest step sets the rate.

  1. Capillary flow: surface tension pulls water along narrow pores toward the drying surface.
  2. Liquid diffusion: water moves through the solid phase down a concentration gradient.
  3. Vapour diffusion: water evaporates inside the solid and vapour diffuses through pores to the surface.
  4. Surface evaporation: at the interface, water changes to vapour; the surface temperature falls toward the wet-bulb temperature.
  5. Heat and mass transfer: air supplies sensible heat and carries vapour away; the rates depend on air temperature, humidity, velocity and solid thickness.

Early in drying the surface is wet. Capillary flow and surface evaporation keep it saturated, so the rate is nearly constant and controlled by the air. This is the constant-rate period. Later the surface dries, the evaporation front moves inward, and internal diffusion controls the rate. The rate falls as X falls; this is the falling-rate period. Chapter 7.7 turns these periods into drying-time calculations.

The constant-rate period ends at the critical moisture content Xc, in kg water per kg dry solid, where the surface can no longer stay fully wet. The falling-rate period that follows often has two parts: an unsaturated-surface part, where dry patches grow between wet ones, and a part controlled entirely by internal diffusion. A thin solid in fast air may show only the second part.

Thick solids, low air velocity and high air humidity all slow drying. A high air temperature raises surface evaporation but can case-harden food or crack clay; the solid sets the allowable temperature. The surface heat and mass transfer coefficients follow the convective correlations, Sherwood number against Reynolds and Schmidt numbers, and their heat-transfer twins.

Worked example

Question. 100 kg of wet solid contains 30 kg of water. It is placed in air at 40 % relative humidity, for which the equilibrium moisture content is 0.10 kg/kg dry solid. Find the moisture on dry and wet bases, the free moisture and the mass of water that can be removed.

  2. Dry-basis moisture X = mw/ms = 30/70 = 0.4286 = 0.429 kg/kg dry solid.

  4. Free moisture = X − X* = 0.4286 − 0.10 = 0.3286 = 0.329 kg/kg dry solid.
  5. Water left at equilibrium = X* × ms = 0.10 × 70 = 7.0 kg.

  • Confusing free moisture with unbound moisture. Free is above equilibrium; unbound is pore water.
  • Using a room-temperature isotherm for a hot dryer. X* falls as temperature rises, and hysteresis matters.

7.7 Drying rate curves and estimation of drying time

Drying is not one speed. A wet solid first loses surface water at a steady rate, then slows as water must travel from inside. The drying rate curve plots rate R against moisture X and makes both stages visible. Its shape is measured in a simple batch test: weigh the sample, convert to X, differentiate, divide by drying area. The drying time is (ms/A) times the area under the 1/R curve between the two moistures, with ms in kg dry solid and A in m². This is how batch times, dryer capacity and the effect of air conditions are estimated before sizing equipment.

You need Chapter 7.6 for moisture content X, equilibrium moisture X* and bound and free water.

From weighings to a rate curve

A batch drying test uses a thin sample in air at fixed dry-bulb temperature, humidity and velocity. Record the sample mass m at times t. If ms is the dry-solid mass, then X = (m − ms)/ms kg water per kg dry solid. Plot X against t; the slope dX/dt at each point is negative. The drying rate is R = −(ms/A)(dX/dt), where A is the exposed drying area in m² and R is kg water evaporated per m² per hour. The dry-solid mass ms carries units kg dry solid.

  1. Dry a known wet sample at constant air conditions.
  2. Weigh it at fixed intervals and compute X at each time.
  3. Draw X against t and find slopes dX/dt.
  4. Multiply by −ms/A to get R at each X.
  5. Plot R against X; mark the flat part and the fall.

R = −(ms/A) dX/dt

R is drying rate in kg/(m²·h); ms is dry-solid mass in kg; A is drying area in m²; X is moisture in kg/kg dry solid; t is time in h.

Constant rate, critical point, falling rate

In the constant-rate period the surface is wet. Water evaporates as from a free liquid surface, so R stays at Rc. This ends at the critical moisture Xc, when the surface can no longer supply free water. Below Xc the rate falls. In a simple linear falling-rate stage the rate falls in proportion to the free moisture above X*: R = Rc(X − X*)/(Xc − X*). Here X* is the equilibrium moisture, the value at which drying stops. Many solids show a second, slower diffusion-controlled stage near dryness.

The constant rate can be predicted from the air. The surface sits at the wet-bulb temperature, and the heat convected to it is all used to evaporate water:

Rc = h (T − Tw)/λw,   h = 0.0204 G0.8

Rc is the constant drying rate in kg/(m²·s); h is the heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m²·K); T and Tw are the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures of the air; λw is the latent heat at Tw in J/kg; G is the air mass velocity in kg/(m²·h). The correlation for h is for air flowing parallel to the surface, G from about 2450 to 29 300 kg/(m²·h).

tc = (ms/(A Rc))(X₁ − Xc)

tc is constant-rate time in h; X₁ is initial moisture; Xc is critical moisture; Rc is constant drying rate; other symbols as before.

tf = (ms (Xc − X*)/(A Rc)) ln((Xc − X*)/(X₂ − X*))

tf is falling-rate time in h; Xc − X* is free moisture at the critical point; Rc is the constant drying rate in kg/(m²·h); A is drying area in m²; ms is dry-solid mass in kg; X₂ is final moisture above X*; ln is the natural logarithm. Use this for one straight falling line ending at X*.

If X₂ is above Xc, only tc applies. If X₂ is below Xc, total time is tc + tf. For a curved falling stage, integrate graphically: t = (ms/A) ∫ dX/R from X₂ to X₁, which is the area under the 1/R curve.

What moves the curve

Higher air temperature and lower humidity raise Rc and often Xc, because evaporation is faster. Air velocity raises Rc by thinning the boundary layer, but it barely changes the falling-rate period, where internal diffusion controls. Doubling the bed thickness roughly doubles ms/A and the time, but a thick bed can dry unevenly. Shrinkage reduces A, so the real rate falls faster than the ideal line. Case hardening seals the surface, trapping water inside and adding a slow second falling stage. The measured curve, not a single formula, is the safe basis for design.

Worked example

Question. A batch dryer holds 50 kg of dry solid with an exposed drying area A = 1 m². The solid is dried from X₁ = 0.40 to X₂ = 0.10 kg/kg dry solid. Tests give Xc = 0.20, X* = 0.05 and Rc = 1.5 kg/(m²·h). Find the constant-rate time, falling-rate time and total drying time.

  1. Dry-solid mass ms = 50 kg; area A = 1 m²; Rc = 1.5 kg/(m²·h).
  2. Initial moisture X₁ = 0.40 is above Xc = 0.20, so a constant-rate period exists.
  3. Constant-rate time: tc = (ms/(A Rc))(X₁ − Xc) = (50/(1 × 1.5))(0.40 − 0.20) h = 33.33 × 0.20 h = 6.67 h.
  4. Final moisture X₂ = 0.10 is below Xc, so the falling-rate period follows.
  5. For one straight falling line to X* = 0.05: tf = (ms (Xc − X*)/(A Rc)) ln((Xc − X*)/(X₂ − X*)).
  6. Substitute: tf = (50 × (0.20 − 0.05)/(1 × 1.5)) ln((0.20 − 0.05)/(0.10 − 0.05)) h = (7.5/1.5) ln(0.15/0.05) h = 5.0 ln 3 h.
  7. ln 3 = 1.0986, so tf = 5.0 × 1.0986 h = 5.49 h.
  8. Total time t = tc + tf = 6.67 + 5.49 h = 12.16 h, about 12.2 h.

Answer. The constant-rate time is 6.67 h, the falling-rate time is 5.49 h and the total drying time is about 12.2 h.

7.8 Batch and continuous dryers; through-circulation drying

Dryers remove water from a wet solid by bringing it into contact with hot, dry air. Batch dryers dry a fixed lot; continuous dryers take a steady feed and give a steady product. The choice follows the solid: slabs, pastes, slurries and granules each need a different contact pattern. In through-circulation drying, air is forced through a bed of granular solid, so the air picks up water and cools as it climbs. That humidity rise sets the drying time. The same idea sizes tray, belt and some continuous dryers in pottery, minerals, food and pharma.

You need drying rate curves and drying time from 7.7 and psychrometry from 7.2.

Batch and continuous dryers

Dryers are grouped by how they handle solids. Batch dryers hold a lot and dry it for a set time. Tray or compartment dryers put wet solid on trays and blow hot air over them; they suit pottery, pigments and pastes. Vacuum shelf dryers do the same under low pressure, so water boils below 100 °C; they suit heat-sensitive chemicals. Freeze dryers freeze the solid and remove ice by sublimation; they suit vaccines and instant coffee.

Continuous dryers receive wet solid and deliver dry solid without stopping. Tunnel dryers move trucks of trays through zones. Belt dryers carry a layer on a perforated belt. Rotary dryers tumble granules in a sloping cylinder with hot gas. Drum dryers dry a paste on a heated rotating drum. Spray dryers atomise a slurry into hot gas and make powder, as in milk powder. Fluidised-bed dryers suspend free-flowing particles in upward air. Pneumatic or flash dryers carry fine particles in a fast gas stream for seconds. The feed form decides the contact pattern: slabs need surface air, slurries need atomisation, granules need tumbling or fluidisation.

Through-circulation drying

In through-circulation drying, air is pushed through a bed of granular solid held on a perforated tray or belt. The air enters the bottom face at dry-bulb T1 and humidity Y1. As it climbs, it picks up water. At the top face its humidity is Y2 and its temperature T2, lower because evaporation takes latent heat. The bed therefore sees air that is driest and hottest at the inlet face and wettest and coolest at the outlet face. Pressure drop rises with bed depth, air velocity and fines; it must be paid for in fan power. Cross-circulation, by contrast, blows air over the tray surface, not through the bed.

The water balance on the air gives the drying rate. If mda kg dry air/h flows through and its humidity rises from Y1 to Y2 kg water/kg dry air, water picked up = mda (Y2 − Y1) kg/h. For Ms kg dry solid drying from X1 to X2 kg water/kg dry solid, water to remove = Ms (X1 − X2) kg.

t = Ms (X1 − X2) / [mda (Y2 − Y1)]

t is drying time in h; Ms is dry solid mass in kg; X1 and X2 are initial and final solid moistures in kg water/kg dry solid; mda is dry-air mass flow in kg dry air/h; Y1 and Y2 are inlet and outlet air humidities in kg water/kg dry air.

Choosing a dryer

Selection rules follow from the solid and the scale. Heat-sensitive solids go to vacuum, freeze or low-temperature continuous dryers; high temperature ruins drugs, enzymes and some foods. Particle size matters: fine slurries are spray dried; free-flowing granules go to fluidised beds; lumps and minerals go to rotary dryers. Tonnage decides batch versus continuous: a few hundred kilograms per batch suits trays; steady output of many tonnes per hour suits continuous units. Direct heating mixes hot gas with the solid. It is simple and efficient, but the gas must be clean enough for food or pharma. Indirect heating uses a hot wall or steam tubes. It keeps combustion products away from the product and suits flammable solvents, but it is slower. A continuous unit is not automatically better; it needs steady feed and steady product quality. The exam favourite is to match feed form to dryer, not to memorise a list.

Worked example

Question. A through-circulation tray dryer handles 100 kg of dry solid, to be dried from X1 = 0.25 to X2 = 0.05 kg water/kg dry solid. Air enters the bed at 60 °C with Y1 = 0.010 at 500 kg dry air/h and leaves at Y2 = 0.022. Find the drying time. Check that the outlet air is not saturated.

  1. Dry solid mass Ms = 100 kg. Initial moisture X1 = 0.25 kg water/kg dry solid. Final moisture X2 = 0.05 kg water/kg dry solid.
  2. Water to remove = Ms (X1 − X2) = 100 × (0.25 − 0.05) = 20 kg.
  3. Dry air flow mda = 500 kg dry air/h. Inlet humidity Y1 = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air. Outlet humidity Y2 = 0.022 kg water/kg dry air.
  4. Water picked up by air = mda (Y2 − Y1) = 500 × (0.022 − 0.010) = 6 kg/h.
  5. Drying time t = water to remove / water pickup = 20 kg / 6 kg/h = 3.3 h.
  6. Check outlet: at about 30 °C the saturation humidity is near 0.027 kg/kg, above 0.022, so the air leaves unsaturated and will not condense water back onto the solid.

Answer. The drying time is about 3.3 h.

Exam traps

  • Putting tunnel dryers in the batch list: a tunnel dryer is continuous because trucks move through it without stopping.
  • Confusing through-circulation with cross-circulation: through means air passes through the bed; cross means air passes over the tray surface.
  • Using wet-basis moisture in place of dry-basis X: the formula uses kg water per kg dry solid.
  • Forgetting the saturation limit: if outlet air would be above saturation, the computed Y2 is impossible and the time is longer.
  • Mixing units: mda in kg dry air/h and t in h; if mda is in kg/s, t comes in seconds.

7.9 Design of dryers, including rotary dryers

A dryer design is three balances and two sizing rules. The water balance gives the kg of water to remove each hour. The air balance gives the kg of dry air that carries it. The heat balance gives the fuel or steam duty. Then the air mass velocity fixes the diameter, and a length-to-diameter ratio fixes the length. That is how rotary dryers for sand, fertiliser, detergents and clay are sized before any drawing is made.

You need: 7.1 for humidity and humid volume, 7.7 for drying time, and 7.8 for dryer types.

Water balance, air balance, heat duty

Start with the bone-dry solid S, kg/h. It does not change. The feed carries water W₁ kg/h and the product carries W₂ kg/h. The water evaporated is W = W₁ − W₂. Moisture is usually given on a wet basis, so convert: dry-basis ratio X = x/(1 − x), where x is the wet-basis mass fraction.

W = S × [x₁/(1 − x₁) − x₂/(1 − x₂)] = F·x₁ − P·x₂, or W = S(X₁ − X₂)

W is water evaporated, kg/h; S is bone-dry solid, kg/h; F and P are wet feed and product, kg/h; x₁ and x₂ are wet-basis mass fractions; X₁ and X₂ are dry-basis kg water per kg dry solid.

The dry air carries that water: mₐ = W/(Y₂ − Y₁), where mₐ is kg dry air/h and Y is kg water per kg dry air. A small humidity rise means a huge air flow and a fat shell. A large rise means the outlet air is near saturation and the solids may re-wet. Keep outlet air below about 80 % relative humidity.

Useful heat has three parts: evaporate water, heat the solids, heat the water left in the product. The heat supplied is found on the air side: heating mₐ of air from ambient T₀ to the inlet temperature Tin needs Qsupplied = mₐ cs (Tin − T₀). It covers the useful heat plus the heat that leaves with the warm exhaust and through the shell. Drying efficiency = W·λ/Qsupplied, typically 40 to 70 % for a direct rotary dryer.

Quseful = W·λ + S·cₚₛ·(T₂ − T₁) + W₂·cₚw·(T₂ − T₁)

Quseful is useful heat, kJ/h; λ is latent heat at the wet-bulb temperature, about 2400 kJ/kg; cₚₛ is specific heat of dry solid, kJ/(kg·K); cₚw is 4.18 kJ/(kg·K); T₁ and T₂ are solid inlet and outlet temperatures, °C.

Sizing the rotary shell

A rotary dryer is a slightly inclined cylinder, 0.5° to 5° to the horizontal, turning at 1 to 8 rpm. Wet feed enters one end, tumbles down the shell, meets hot air, and leaves dry at the other end. Two empirical numbers fix the geometry.

  1. Get mₐ in kg/s from the water balance and the allowed humidity rise.
  2. Choose air mass velocity G, usually 1.5 to 2.5 kg/(m²·s) on the empty shell cross-section. Higher G means smaller diameter but more dust carry-over and fan power.
  3. Cross-section A = mₐ/G, then diameter D = √(4A/π). Round up to a standard shell size.
  4. Pick L/D between 4 and 10. Take L = 6D for a typical case.
  5. Check residence time, roughly 5 to 120 min. It must exceed the drying time from the rate curve of Chapter 7.7.
  6. Compute heat duty and efficiency. Check outlet air temperature against the solid's tolerance.

Fan volumetric flow uses humid volume at the outlet: vH = (1/29 + Y/18) × 22.4 × (T + 273)/273 m³/kg dry air. Multiply by mₐ, never by total humid air mass. Air cools and gains water, so the outlet humid volume is usually smaller than the hot inlet volume. Size the exhaust fan on outlet conditions.

Flights inside the shell, and flow arrangement

Inside the shell are longitudinal fins called flights. As the shell turns, a flight lifts a wedge of solids and showers it through the hot gas. Most heat and mass transfer happens in that shower. Spiral flights at the feed end move wet sticky solids in quickly. Straight flights in the middle give a full curtain. Short flights near discharge stop dusting.

In cocurrent, air and solids move the same way. The hottest gas meets the wettest solid, so heat-sensitive materials such as PVC and soap are safe. But the exhaust gas leaves warmer than in counter-current, so more sensible heat is lost and thermal efficiency is lower. In counter-current the dry solid meets the hottest gas, giving the best heat economy and the driest product, but the solid must tolerate the high outlet temperature. Rule: heat-sensitive solid, cocurrent; fuel cost matters, counter-current.

Use a rotary dryer for free-flowing granular solids, not for pastes that ball up. For pastes, use a drum dryer or another paste-capable dryer; spray dryers suit pumpable solutions and slurries. Chapter 7.8 compares the dryer types.

Question. A rotary dryer treats 1000 kg/h of wet solid at 25 % moisture (wet basis) and delivers it at 2 % moisture (wet basis). Air enters at Y = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air and leaves at Y = 0.035. The air mass velocity is 2.0 kg/(m²·s) and L/D = 6. Find the water evaporated, the dry air rate, the shell diameter and the shell length.

  2. Water in feed: W₁ = 1000 × 0.25 = 250 kg/h.

  4. Water evaporated: W = 250 − 15.3 = 234.7 kg/h ≈ 235 kg/h.

Question. For the same dryer, the solids enter at 25 °C and leave at 70 °C. The dry solid has cₚₛ = 1.2 kJ/(kg·K). Find the useful heat duty (kJ/h) and the fraction used for evaporation.

Exam traps

  • Using wet-basis percentage as kg water per kg dry solid. Convert before the balance.
  • Using total humid air mass instead of dry air mass in the volumetric flow, so the fan is oversized.
  • Forgetting sensible heat for solids and remaining moisture; the duty comes out too low and the air heater is undersized.
  • Taking fan volumetric flow at inlet temperature. Air cools and gains water; the outlet humid volume is usually smaller than the hot inlet volume, so using inlet T oversizes the fan.

Takeaways

  1. Two numbers, the dry-bulb temperature and the humidity ratio, fix every other property of moist air; the rest are computed, not measured.
  2. The psychrometric chart is only those computations drawn once, so a state is located by any two independent properties and everything else is read off.
  3. For air and water the wet-bulb temperature and the adiabatic saturation temperature are the same because the Lewis relation holds.
  4. Humidifying air with recirculated water follows a constant wet-bulb line, so the adiabatic saturation temperature is the lowest temperature that water alone can reach.
  5. Cooling-tower design integrates an enthalpy driving force between the cold-water and hot-water temperatures, and the answer is the tower characteristic.
  6. A solid stops drying at the equilibrium moisture content of the surrounding air, so that water cannot be removed by that air at any longer time.
  7. Drying time is the area under the curve of 1/(drying rate) plotted against moisture content, taken between the starting and ending moistures.
  8. A dryer design is three balances in a row: water, dry air and heat; sizing of the shell follows from the air velocity the solid can tolerate.

Model answer.

  1. Moist air is dry air plus water vapour. The dry air does not change during humidification or drying, so every humidity is counted against 1 kg of dry air.
  2. Absolute humidity (humidity ratio) Y is the mass of water vapour carried by 1 kg of dry air. From Dalton's law the mole ratio of vapour to air equals the pressure ratio, and 0.622 = 18.02/28.97 turns it into a mass ratio:
    Y = 0.622 pA/(P − pA)        kg water/kg dry air

pA   partial pressure of water vapour, kPa
P     total pressure, kPa
  3. Relative humidity compares the vapour actually present with the most the air could hold at the same temperature:
    RH = 100 pA/pAS             per cent

pAS  vapour pressure of water at the dry-bulb temperature, kPa
  4. Percentage humidity compares humidities instead of pressures:
    percentage humidity = 100 Y/Ys     per cent
Ys = 0.622 pAS/(P − pAS)

Model answer.

  1. The psychrometric (humidity) chart is the humidity calculations drawn once, for air–water at one total pressure, usually 101.3 kPa. The horizontal axis is the dry-bulb temperature T in °C. The vertical axis is the humidity Y in kg water per kg dry air. Each point is one state of moist air.
  2. The lines on the chart:
    LineWhat it showsShape
    Saturation curve100 % RH, Ys against Trises steeply to the right
    Constant RH curves10 %, 20 % … of pASbelow and like the saturation curve
    Horizontal linesconstant Y, hence constant dew pointflat

    Enthalpy scalekJ per kg dry airnearly parallel to wet-bulb lines
    Humid volume linesm³ per kg dry airsteep, sloping left
  3. Locating a state. Any two independent properties fix one point. With dry bulb and wet bulb, go up the vertical line of the dry bulb until it meets the wet-bulb line that starts on the saturation curve at the wet-bulb temperature. With dry bulb and dew point, go across from the saturation curve at the dew point to the dry-bulb line. Dew point and Y are not independent, because each fixes the other.

From the point, Y is read on the side scale; the dew point by moving horizontally left to the saturation curve; the RH from the nearest RH curve; the wet bulb by following the wet-bulb line up to saturation; the enthalpy from its own scale; the humid volume by interpolating between its lines.
  5. Processes are paths: heating and sensible cooling are horizontal; adiabatic humidification runs up a wet-bulb line; cooling below the dew point follows the saturation curve down.

Understand Explain the adiabatic saturation process and show that for the air–water system the wet-bulb temperature equals the adiabatic saturation temperature. State the assumption used, and find both temperatures for air at 40 °C with Y = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. 13 marks

Points to cover. Insulated saturator, recirculated water, air leaves saturated at Tas; Enthalpy balance: sensible heat lost = latent heat to evaporate; Adiabatic saturation line Y = Yas − (cs/λas)(T − Tas); Wet-bulb: convection in, evaporation out, steady state; Lewis relation hc/(kY cs) ≈ 1 makes the two equations identical; Assumptions; Trial solution for 40 °C and 0.0092.

Model answer.

  1. An adiabatic saturator is a long, insulated chamber in which air flows over or through a spray of water. The water is recirculated and no make-up heat is added, so the water settles at a steady temperature. Air enters at dry bulb T and humidity Y and leaves saturated at the adiabatic saturation temperature Tas, with humidity Yas = Ys(Tas). Make-up water enters at Tas.
  2. Enthalpy balance per kg dry air, with the reference at Tas: the air gives up sensible heat cs(T − Tas), and this evaporates (Yas − Y) kg of water at Tas:
    cs (T − Tas) = λas (Yas − Y)

Y = Yas − (cs/λas)(T − Tas)

cs    humid heat of the entering air, kJ/(kg dry air·K)
λas   latent heat of water at Tas, kJ/kg
  3. On a Y–T chart this is a straight line of slope −cs/λas, about −0.00042 kg/kg per K for air–water. Every air state on the line has the same Tas.
  4. Wet-bulb thermometer. A wick on a thermometer bulb is kept wet in a fast air stream. The wick cools until the heat convected to it just supplies the latent heat of the water evaporating from it. At steady state, per m² of wick:
    hc (T − Tw) = kY λw (Yw − Y)

Y = Yw − [hc/(kY λw)](T − Tw)

hc   convective heat-transfer coefficient, W/(m²·K)
kY   mass-transfer coefficient, kg/(m²·s·unit ΔY)
Yw   saturation humidity at Tw
  5. The two lines have the same form. They coincide when hc/kY = cs, that is when the Lewis number ratio hc/(kY cs) equals 1. For air–water this ratio is close to 1 (about 0.95 to 1.0 in the usual range of air speed), so Tw ≈ Tas and the chart carries one family of lines for both. For air–toluene or other organic vapours the ratio is near 1.8, and the wet bulb lies well above the adiabatic saturation temperature.
  6. Assumptions: the Lewis relation holds; radiation to the wick and conduction along the thermometer stem are negligible; air speed is above about 3 m/s; the wick is fully wet with pure water; and the saturator is long enough for the air to reach saturation.
  7. Trial solution for T = 40 °C and Y = 0.0092 (cs = 1.005 + 1.88 × 0.0092 = 1.022 kJ/(kg·K), λ ≈ 2450 kJ/kg):
    Trial Tas (°C)Ys(Tas)Y from the lineCompare with 0.0092
    200.01470.0147 − 0.000417 × 20 = 0.0064too low
    220.01670.0167 − 0.000417 × 18 = 0.0092matches
    240.01890.0189 − 0.000417 × 16 = 0.0122too high
  8. So Tas = Tw ≈ 22 °C. The air leaving the saturator has Y = 0.0167, so each kg of dry air picks up 0.0167 − 0.0092 = 0.0075 kg of water and cools by 18 K. Check by energy: 1.022 × 18 = 18.4 kJ of sensible heat, and 0.0075 × 2450 = 18.4 kJ of latent heat.
  9. The practical result is that two thermometer readings, dry bulb and wet bulb, give the humidity of air directly, and the wet bulb is the lowest temperature that evaporation of water alone can reach.

Evaluate Compare natural-draft, forced-draft and induced-draft cooling towers, and counterflow with crossflow layouts, in terms of air flow, land area, upkeep and effectiveness. Recommend a type for a large coastal power plant and justify the choice. 13 marks

Points to cover. How each draft is produced; Forced vs induced: recirculation, fan size, icing; Counterflow vs crossflow: driving force, pressure drop, access; Water losses: evaporation, drift, blowdown; Fan power estimate; Recommendation for a large coastal plant.

Model answer.

  1. All cooling towers cool water mainly by evaporating a small part of it into air, so all are limited by the entering-air wet bulb. They differ in how the air is moved and in the direction it meets the water.
  2. Draft:
    TypeHow air movesStrengthsWeaknesses

    Forced draftfan at the base blows air infan in cool dry air, easy to reach, vibration-free baseslow exhaust can be drawn back in (recirculation); fan size limited; icing of inlet in cold weather
    Induced draftfan at the top pulls air throughfast exhaust plume avoids recirculation; even air distribution; compactfan works in hot humid air, more corrosion of the drive
  3. Flow layout:
    LayoutContactEffect
    Counterflowair rises against falling waterthe coldest water meets the driest air, so the enthalpy driving force is used best and a smaller fill volume gives a given approach
    Crossflowair moves horizontally across falling waterlower air pressure drop and pumping head, easy access to the distribution basins, but a larger fill for the same duty
  4. Effectiveness is judged by the approach that a given fill volume can reach. A smaller approach needs a much larger tower characteristic: from the Merkel integral, the driving force h* − h at the cold end shrinks as the cold water nears the wet bulb.
  5. A sense of the fan power. A mechanical tower cell circulating 100 kg/s of water at L/G = 1 moves about 100 kg/s of dry air, about 90 m³/s. With a fan pressure rise of 150 Pa and fan efficiency 0.70:
    P = Q Δp/η = 90 × 150/0.70 = 19.3 kW per cell
  6. A large plant needs dozens of such cells, so fan power becomes a few megawatts that a natural-draft tower does not use.
  7. Water losses, for a 10 K range with most heat removed by evaporation:
    E ≈ L cp ΔT/λ = 4.187 × 10/2410 ≈ 1.7 % of circulation (an upper bound)
Blowdown at 5 cycles of concentration: B = E/(N − 1) = 1.7/4 ≈ 0.43 %
  8. Drift is held below about 0.01 % of circulation by drift eliminators in either type.
  9. Recommendation for a large coastal power plant. A natural-draft counterflow hyperbolic tower suits a base-load plant of several hundred megawatts: the heat load is steady, the range is large (around 10 K), the land is usually available, and saving fan power over decades outweighs the capital cost. Seawater or brackish make-up needs corrosion-resistant fill and concrete, and a limit on cycles of concentration. Where land is short, the plant cycles its load, or a close approach is needed, an induced-draft counterflow mechanical tower is the better choice; crossflow suits smaller plants where low pumping head and easy maintenance matter more than fill volume.
  10. Environmental points: the visible plume, salt drift from seawater towers onto nearby land, noise from fans, and the make-up water drawn.
Evaluate A heat-sensitive granular solid must be dried from a high moisture content to a low one. Compare tray, tunnel, rotary and fluidized-bed dryers on contact time, uniformity, dust, and suitability for heat-sensitive solids, and choose one with justification. 13 marks

Points to cover. What makes a solid heat-sensitive: product temperature, not air temperature; Tray, tunnel, rotary, fluidized bed compared; Wet-bulb protection during the constant-rate period; Falling-rate period controls the product temperature; Choice and conditions.

Model answer.

  1. For a heat-sensitive solid what matters is the temperature of the solid, and for how long. While the surface is wet (the constant-rate period) the solid stays near the wet-bulb temperature of the air. Air at 120 °C with Y = 0.010 has a wet bulb of only about 38 °C, so even hot air is safe at first. Danger comes in the falling-rate period, when the surface dries and its temperature climbs toward the air temperature. A good dryer for such a solid gives every particle the same short exposure and lets the air temperature be lowered as the solid dries.
  2. Comparison:
    DryerContact timeUniformityDustHeat-sensitive solids
    Tray (batch)hourspoor: top layers dry first, corners differvery lowgood at low temperature, but slow and labour-heavy; vacuum trays for the most sensitive
    Tunnel (continuous trays)hoursfair; first in, first outlowfair; air can be staged hotter at the wet end, cooler at the dry end
    Rotaryabout 5 to 120 minfair; spread of residence times in the shellhigh; tumbling breaks particlesonly cocurrent, where the hottest gas meets the wettest solid; long hold-up at the hot end is risky
    Fluidized bedminutesexcellent: the bed is well mixed at one temperaturemoderate; fines carried out, cyclone neededvery good: bed temperature is set and controlled closely
  3. Analysis. The tray and tunnel dryers are gentle but slow, and their thick layers dry unevenly, so the top layer may overheat while the bottom is still wet. The rotary dryer handles large tonnage cheaply, but its spread of residence times means some particles stay far longer than the average, and attrition makes dust. The fluidized bed mixes particles so well that the whole bed sits at one temperature, a little above the outlet-air wet bulb while free moisture remains, and that temperature can be held below the limit of the solid. Heat and mass transfer to suspended particles are fast, so contact times are short.
  4. Choice. A fluidized-bed dryer, provided the granules are free-flowing, of fairly narrow size range (very roughly 0.05 to 2 mm) and not sticky when wet. For a high initial moisture, a two-stage arrangement works well: a first stage with hotter air removes the free water while the solid is protected at the wet bulb, and a second stage with cooler air (or a plug-flow bed) finishes the falling-rate period without overheating. A batch fluid bed is common for pharmaceutical granules.
  5. If the solid is sticky at high moisture, back-mixing in a fluid bed with dry product is used, or a tray or vacuum dryer takes over. For large tonnage of a moderately sensitive mineral, a cocurrent rotary dryer remains the economical choice.
  6. Design point: since the falling-rate period controls both the time and the product temperature, the test drying curve of the solid, not the dryer catalogue, fixes the second-stage air temperature and the residence time.
Analyze Examine the assumptions behind the Lewis relation and hence the wet-bulb thermometer. Under what conditions does the wet-bulb reading become unreliable, and what corrections or precautions are used? 13 marks

Points to cover. Wet-bulb balance hc(T − Tw) = kY λw (Yw − Y); Lewis relation hc/(kY cs) ≈ 1 and why it holds for air–water; Other gas–vapour pairs; Radiation, conduction, low air speed; Near saturation: small depression, large error; Precautions: aspirated psychrometer, shield, clean wick; Numbers showing the sensitivity.

Model answer.

  1. The wet-bulb thermometer gives humidity through its steady-state balance:
    hc (T − Tw) = kY λw (Yw − Y)
Y = Yw − [hc/(kY λw)](T − Tw)
  2. To use the same line as the adiabatic saturator, Y = Yas − (cs/λ)(T − Tas), the ratio hc/kY must equal the humid heat cs. This is the Lewis relation, hc/(kY cs) ≈ 1. It rests on the heat-and-mass-transfer analogy: hc/(kY cs) = (Sc/Pr)2/3 = Le2/3. For water vapour in air, Sc ≈ 0.60 and Pr ≈ 0.71, so the ratio is about 0.9 to 1.0. That near-coincidence is a property of water and air only; for toluene or benzene in air the ratio is near 1.8, and the wet bulb is well above the adiabatic saturation temperature.
  3. Assumptions and where they fail:
  4. AssumptionFailureEffect on reading
    heat reaches the wick by convection onlyradiation from warm walls or sunreading too high; Y overestimated
    no heat along the stemconduction from the warm thermometer stemreading too high
    air speed high enough (above about 3 m/s)still or slow airradiation becomes a large share; reading too high
    wick fully wet with pure waterdry patches, dirty wick, dissolved saltsreading drifts toward the dry bulb
    air–water, near 1 atm, below 100 °Cother vapours; superheated steam conditionsthe wet bulb no longer equals Tas
  5. Size of the radiation error. A wick at 300 K exchanging radiation with surroundings has a radiative coefficient
    hr = 4 ε σ T³ = 4 × 0.95 × 5.67×10⁻⁸ × 300³ = 5.8 W/(m²·K)
  6. In still air the convective coefficient is only about 5 to 10 W/(m²·K), so radiation adds as much heat as convection and the reading may be several degrees high. At 3 to 5 m/s, hc is around 30 to 50 W/(m²·K) and the error falls to a fraction of a degree. That is why the sling and aspirated psychrometers move the air past the wick.
  7. Sensitivity. For air at 40 °C dry bulb, a wet bulb of 25.0 °C gives Y = 0.0138 kg/kg dry air, while 24.5 °C gives 0.0129 and 25.5 °C gives 0.0146: a reading error of half a degree changes Y by about 6 %.
  8. Near saturation the depression T − Tw is small, so the same half-degree error is a large fraction of it. At 30 °C a wet bulb of 29 °C gives Y = 0.0252, and 28.5 °C gives 0.0242, while saturated air holds 0.0272; the relative humidity can be wrong by several points. Dew-point hygrometers or capacitive sensors are better there.
  9. Precautions and corrections: use an aspirated (Assmann-type) or sling psychrometer with air speed above 3 m/s; shield the wet bulb from radiation; keep a clean wick wetted with distilled water and wait for a steady reading; read well away from the stem conduction path; use the psychrometric equation with the measured pressure if the pressure is not 1 atm; and for organic vapours use the measured psychrometric ratio, not 1.
Analyze Explain what happens inside a deep bed during through-circulation drying: how the air temperature and humidity change as the air climbs, how the drying zone travels, and what this does to the total drying time compared with cross-circulation. Use a bed of 100 kg dry solid dried from X = 0.25 to 0.05 by 500 kg/h of air entering at 60 °C with Y = 0.010. 13 marks

Points to cover. Air cools along its adiabatic line as it climbs; Exit state from the number of heat-transfer units of the bed; Limit: adiabatic saturation of the inlet air; Three zones and the moving drying front; Time set by the last layer; Comparison with cross-circulation; Numbers for the bed.

Model answer.

  1. In through-circulation the air is forced up through a bed of granular solid on a perforated floor. In each thin layer the air gives sensible heat to evaporate water and takes the vapour away. While the particles are wet it moves along its adiabatic saturation line, cooling and gaining humidity, so each layer sees cooler, wetter air than the one below.
  2. For the inlet air (60 °C, Y = 0.010): Tas = 27.6 °C, Yas = 0.0236, cs = 1.024 kJ/(kg·K), λ = 2435 kJ/kg. With a wet bed of depth z the air temperature falls exponentially toward Tas:
    (T2 − Tas)/(T1 − Tas) = exp(−N),   N = h a z/(G cs)
Y2 = Y1 + cs (T1 − T2)/λ
  3. Exit air and the time it would give if the whole bed stayed wet (water to remove 100 × 0.20 = 20 kg):
    N (bed units)T2 (°C)Y2share of maximum pickuptime = 20/[500(Y2 − Y1)] (h)
    0.547.30.01540.397.5

  4. A bed of two or three units already brings the air close to saturation. Beyond that, more depth adds pressure drop but hardly any pickup. The absolute limit is Yas: the bed cannot dry in less than 20/[500 × (0.0236 − 0.010)] = 2.9 h with this air.
  5. Zones and the moving front. At the start the whole bed is wet. The bottom layers meet the hottest, driest air and dry first, down to the equilibrium moisture of that air. Above them is a drying zone, a few bed units deep, where the air does its cooling and humidifying. Above that the solid is still wet and only warms to the wet bulb, since air reaching it is already almost saturated. As drying proceeds the drying zone moves upward like a front. For most of the run the exit air stays near Tas and Yas, and the bed dries at almost the maximum possible rate.
  6. The drying time is set by the last layer to dry, at the top. Near the end the front reaches the top, the exit air temperature rises and the exit humidity falls; the bottom has been at the inlet air temperature for a long time, so a heat-sensitive solid must be judged by the bottom layer, not the average. Reversing the air direction part way through evens out the moisture.
  7. Comparison with cross-circulation. In cross-circulation the air passes over the tray surface, touching only the top face of the layer; the water must move by capillary flow and diffusion to that face. Through-circulation puts every particle in contact with the air, so the area per kg of solid is many times larger, the external heat- and mass-transfer coefficients are higher, and the falling-rate period, controlled by internal diffusion over a particle radius rather than a bed depth, is much shorter. Through-circulation drying times are typically several times shorter for granular, pelleted or extruded solids.
  8. The cost is pressure drop: the fan must push air through the bed, and the drop rises with depth, air velocity and fines. Pastes and fine powders that would pack or blow away cannot be dried this way unless they are first formed into extrudates or granules.
  9. For the stated bed, if it provides about 2 units the air leaves near 32 °C with Y ≈ 0.022 and the run takes about 3.3 to 3.4 h, close to the 2.9 h minimum; a cross-circulation tray dryer on the same air would take several times longer.

Glossary

Blowdown
The deliberate purging of circulating water to keep dissolved solids below a limit that would cause scaling.
Bound moisture
Water held inside the solid by chemical or cellular binding, whose vapour pressure is lower than that of free water.
Compression dehumidification
Removing water vapour by compressing moist air, which raises its vapour partial pressure above the saturation value so water condenses, then cooling it to condense more.
Constant-rate period
The drying stage in which the solid surface stays wet and the drying rate stays fixed by the external heat and mass transfer.
Cooling below the dew point
A dehumidification method in which the gas is cooled so that water condenses out, leaving a saturated gas at the lower temperature.
Dehumidification
Any operation that removes water vapour from a gas stream, by cooling below the dew point, absorption or adsorption.
Drift
Fine water droplets carried out of a cooling tower by the air stream, removed by drift eliminators.
Dry-bulb depression
The difference between the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures; it measures how much the air can still take up by evaporation.
Drying
The removal of a volatile liquid, usually water, from a wet solid by evaporation into a gas stream.
Drying rate curve
A plot of the drying rate, in kg water per (m²·h), against the moisture content, showing the warming-up, constant-rate and falling-rate periods.
Falling-rate period
The drying stage in which the surface is no longer fully wet and the drying rate drops, controlled by the movement of moisture inside the solid.
Fluidized-bed dryer
A dryer in which hot air suspends the particles of solid, giving fast, uniform drying at a nearly constant temperature.
Heat balance of a dryer
The statement that the heat supplied by the air equals the heat used to evaporate water plus the sensible heat of the solid and the losses.
Humidification
Any operation that increases the water-vapour content of a gas stream.
Humidity
The general term for the water vapour present in a gas, measured in several ways such as absolute humidity or relative humidity.
Liquid absorption
Dehumidification in which the gas contacts a hygroscopic liquid such as lithium chloride solution or glycol, which takes up water.
Makeup water
Water added to a cooling tower or washer to replace evaporation, drift and blowdown losses.
Molal humidity
Moles of water vapour per mole of vapour-free gas, which equals pw/(P − pw).
Percentage humidity
One hundred times the ratio of the actual humidity ratio to the saturation humidity ratio at the same dry-bulb temperature.
Percentage relative humidity
One hundred times the ratio of the actual water-vapour partial pressure to the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature.
Saturation humidity
The humidity ratio of air that is saturated with water vapour at the given dry-bulb temperature.
Solid adsorption
Dehumidification in which water vapour is held on the surface of a solid such as silica gel or activated alumina.
Steam injection
A humidification method in which live steam is sparged into the air; both the humidity and the temperature rise.
Tunnel dryer
A continuous dryer in which trucks or trays of solid move through a long chamber against a stream of hot air.
Unbound moisture
Water held in the larger pores of a solid whose vapour pressure equals that of free water at the same temperature.
Wetted-surface humidifier
A device in which air passes over a large wetted surface, picking up water vapour without any spray or liquid droplets.
