7.1 Humidity terms: absolute and relative humidity, dew point

Every property of moist air comes from two measured numbers: the dry-bulb temperature and the amount of water vapour. Absolute humidity Y is the mass of vapour per kg of dry air; relative humidity compares the vapour pressure to the saturation value; the dew point is where saturation begins. Engineers use these numbers to size cooling towers, dryers, air-conditioning ducts and humidifiers, where the water content decides how much heat and water the air can carry.

You need: school-level vapour pressure and the gas laws, and the pure-water steam tables for saturation pressures and latent heats.

Humidity on a dry-air basis

Moist air is a mixture of dry air and water vapour. The dry air never changes as humidity changes, so we count water against dry air. Absolute humidity (also called humidity ratio) Y is the mass of water vapour carried by 1 kg of dry air. It is a pure number in kg water per kg dry air.

Y = 0.622 p_A / (P − p_A)

Y is the absolute humidity in kg water/kg dry air; p_A is the partial pressure of water vapour in kPa; P is the total pressure in kPa; 0.622 = 18.02/28.97, the ratio of the molar mass of water to that of air.

The same ratio comes from Dalton's law. The vapour and the air fill the same volume at the same temperature, so the mole ratio equals the pressure ratio. The number 0.622 converts that mole ratio into a mass ratio.

At a given temperature there is a maximum vapour pressure the air can hold: the saturation pressure p_AS, read from steam tables. The humidity at that maximum is the saturation humidity Y_s, obtained by putting p_AS in place of p_A.

Y_s = 0.622 p_AS / (P − p_AS)

Y_s is the saturation humidity in kg water/kg dry air at that temperature; p_AS is the vapour pressure of water at that temperature in kPa.

Relative humidity (RH) is a percentage. It compares the actual vapour pressure with the saturation pressure at the same temperature.

RH = 100 p_A / p_AS

RH is the relative humidity in per cent; p_A is the actual vapour pressure in kPa; p_AS is the saturation pressure at the same dry-bulb temperature in kPa.

Percentage humidity (percentage saturation) compares humidities instead of pressures: 100 Y/Y_s at the same temperature. The two numbers are close when the air is nearly saturated and drift apart as the air dries, because Y is not linear in p_A. An examiner who writes RH when the calculation needs percentage humidity changes the answer.

Saturation pressure rises fast with temperature: at 40 °C it is 7.38 kPa, at 12.7 °C about 1.48 kPa. So warm air at a fixed Y always shows a lower RH, which is why a room feels dry on a hot afternoon.

Dew point above 0 °C

Dew point is the temperature at which the vapour present just saturates the air at constant pressure. Below it, water condenses as dew or fog. To find it, take the actual p_A and look up the saturation temperature of water at that pressure.

Dew point is fixed by Y and P alone, not by the dry-bulb temperature. Two air samples with the same Y share one dew point. That is why dew point is a comfort measure in weather reports: air at 30 °C and 40 % RH feels sticky in Chennai because its dew point is near 16 °C, high enough to stop sweat evaporating.

Air below 0 °C has a frost point instead of a dew point. Only a few universities teach this chapter on its own; most fold it into the psychrometric chart, covered in 7.2.

Volume, humid heat and enthalpy

Humid volume v_H is the volume occupied by 1 kg of dry air plus its vapour, in m³/kg dry air. Treat the mixture as ideal and use the molar volume 22.414 m³/kmol at 0 °C and 101.3 kPa.

v_H = (1/28.97 + Y/18.02) × 22.414 × (T/273.15)

v_H is the humid volume in m³/kg dry air; Y is the absolute humidity; T is the absolute temperature in K; 28.97 and 18.02 are the molar masses of air and water in kg/kmol.

Humid heat c_s is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of dry air plus its vapour by 1 K. The two specific heats 1.005 and 1.88 kJ/(kg·K) belong to dry air and to water vapour.

c_s = 1.005 + 1.88 Y

c_s is the humid heat in kJ/(kg dry air·K); Y is the absolute humidity in kg/kg dry air; 1.005 and 1.88 are the specific heats of dry air and water vapour in kJ/(kg·K).

Enthalpy H of moist air is measured above a reference of dry air and liquid water at 0 °C. It has a sensible part for warming the mixture and a latent part for the vapour already formed. The latent heat of water at 0 °C is taken as 2500 kJ/kg.

H = c_s (T − T_ref) + Y λ_ref

H is the enthalpy in kJ/kg dry air; T is the dry-bulb temperature in °C; T_ref = 0 °C; λ_ref = 2500 kJ/kg, the latent heat of vaporisation of water at the reference temperature.

These definitions feed directly into 7.3 and into cooling-tower heat balances in 7.5.

Worked example

Question. Air at 40 °C and 20 % relative humidity flows at 101.3 kPa. The saturation pressure of water at 40 °C is 7.38 kPa. Find (a) the partial pressure of water vapour, (b) the absolute humidity, (c) the dew point, (d) the humid volume, (e) the humid heat and (f) the enthalpy above 0 °C.

  1. (a) Partial pressure: p_A = RH × p_AS = 0.20 × 7.38 = 1.476 kPa, say 1.48 kPa.
  2. (b) Absolute humidity: Y = 0.622 × 1.48 / (101.3 − 1.48) = 0.9206 / 99.82 = 0.00922 kg water/kg dry air.
  3. (c) Dew point: find the saturation temperature at p_A = 1.48 kPa. From steam tables this is about 12.7 °C. The air begins to condense water below that temperature.
  4. (d) Humid volume: v_H = (1/28.97 + 0.00922/18.02) × 22.414 × (313.15/273.15) = (0.03452 + 0.000512) × 22.414 × 1.1464 = 0.90 m³/kg dry air.
  5. (e) Humid heat: c_s = 1.005 + 1.88 × 0.00922 = 1.005 + 0.01733 = 1.022 kJ/(kg dry air·K).
  6. (f) Enthalpy: H = 1.022 × 40 + 0.00922 × 2500 = 40.88 + 23.05 = 63.9 kJ/kg dry air.

Answer. p_A = 1.48 kPa, Y = 0.00922 kg/kg dry air, dew point ≈ 12.7 °C, v_H = 0.90 m³/kg dry air, c_s = 1.022 kJ/(kg·K) and H = 63.9 kJ/kg dry air.

Exam traps

  • Reading relative humidity as the water content: at the same Y, warmer air has a lower RH, so 20 % RH at 40 °C holds more water than 20 % RH at 20 °C.
  • Using percentage humidity and relative humidity interchangeably. They are equal only at saturation, and differ noticeably for dry air.
  • Putting p_A in the denominator of the humidity formula as P + p_A, or using P = 1 atm without converting to the kPa of the steam tables.
  • Forgetting the dry-air basis: the humid heat and enthalpy are per kg dry air, not per kg of moist air.
  • Mixing dew point with wet-bulb temperature: wet-bulb is an adiabatic saturation value, dew point is a saturation-from-vapour-pressure value, and they coincide only at 100 % RH.

7.2 Psychrometric (humidity) chart and its use

A psychrometric chart, also called a humidity chart, puts every property of moist air on one sheet. Dry-bulb temperature runs along the bottom and humidity up the side. Any two independent readings fix a point, and the rest — dew point, relative humidity, wet-bulb temperature, enthalpy, humid volume — are read from lines through that point. It exists to save repeated calculations in drying, cooling towers and air conditioning. In India it is used to set air for a dryer in a Mumbai monsoon or to check a cooling tower on a hot Rajasthan afternoon. The standard chart is drawn for air–water at 1 atm.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for dry-bulb temperature, absolute humidity, relative humidity and dew point.

What the chart draws

The horizontal axis is dry-bulb temperature T in °C. The vertical axis is humidity Y in kg water vapour per kg dry air. A point on the sheet is one state of moist air. The saturation curve is the 100% relative-humidity line; below it air is unsaturated, and above it the mixture would be fog.

Curves of constant relative humidity run below the saturation curve. A horizontal line is constant Y. Its left end meets the saturation curve at the dew point, because cooling at constant Y raises relative humidity until condensation starts. Wet-bulb and adiabatic saturation lines slope down to the right. Enthalpy lines lie nearly parallel to them; the reason is in Chapter 7.3. Humid-volume lines give volume per kg dry air.

h = 1.005 T + Y (2500 + 1.88 T)

h is moist-air enthalpy in kJ/kg dry air; T is dry-bulb temperature in °C; Y is humidity in kg water/kg dry air. The constants 1.005 kJ/(kg·K), 2500 kJ/kg and 1.88 kJ/(kg·K) are the dry-air specific heat, the latent heat of water at 0 °C and the water-vapour specific heat.

One chart is valid for one total pressure, usually 1 atm, and for air–water only. At another pressure the same Y gives a different water-vapour partial pressure, so every curve shifts. Check the pressure printed on the chart.

Fixing a state and following a process

  1. Start with two independent readings: usually dry-bulb plus wet-bulb, or dry-bulb plus dew point.
  2. For dry-bulb plus wet-bulb, intersect the vertical dry-bulb line with the sloping wet-bulb line marked with that temperature.
  3. For dry-bulb plus dew point, find the dew point on the saturation curve. Run a horizontal from there to the dry-bulb vertical.
  4. From the point, read Y on the y-axis, dew point along the horizontal to saturation, relative humidity from the nearest curve, enthalpy along the local wet-bulb line, and humid volume from its own line.

Processes are paths. Heating is horizontal to the right: Y stays fixed, so no water is added or removed. Cooling is horizontal to the left until the saturation curve. Further cooling follows the saturation curve and water condenses. Humidification with recirculated water runs up an adiabatic line, as Chapter 7.4 shows.

Do not read enthalpy by moving horizontally. A horizontal move changes only sensible heat; the chart's enthalpy lines are the sloping ones.

Mixing two streams: the lever rule

Two air streams that mix without heat or work loss give a point on the straight line joining their states. Use dry-air mass flows, not moist-air flows. For stream A with dry-air flow m_A and stream B with m_B, the mixture humidity and enthalpy are weighted averages.

Y_mix = (m_A Y_A + m_B Y_B)/(m_A + m_B)

Y_mix is mixture humidity; Y_A and Y_B are stream humidities; m_A and m_B are dry-air mass flows, all in consistent units. Enthalpy uses the same weights: h_mix = (m_A h_A + m_B h_B)/(m_A + m_B).

The point divides the line in inverse proportion to the dry-air flows. If m_A is larger, the mixture lies nearer A. Distance from A to the mixture divided by distance from A to B equals m_B/(m_A+m_B). Once Y_mix and h_mix are known, the mixture temperature follows from the enthalpy formula or is read from the chart.

In the worked example, 100 kg dry air/h at 30 °C and Y = 0.010 mixes with 50 kg dry air/h at 40 °C and Y = 0.020. The mixture is one-third of the way from A to B.

Worked example

Question. At 1 atm, 100 kg dry air/h at 30 °C with humidity 0.010 kg/kg dry air mixes with 50 kg dry air/h at 40 °C with humidity 0.020 kg/kg dry air. Find the mixture humidity, enthalpy and dry-bulb temperature. Check the position by the lever rule.

  1. Basis: m_A = 100 kg dry air/h, T_A = 30 °C, Y_A = 0.010 kg/kg dry air; m_B = 50 kg dry air/h, T_B = 40 °C, Y_B = 0.020 kg/kg dry air; total m = 150 kg dry air/h.
  2. Y_mix = (100 × 0.010 + 50 × 0.020)/150 = (1.000 + 1.000)/150 = 0.01333 kg/kg dry air.
  3. h_A = 1.005 × 30 + 0.010 × (2500 + 1.88 × 30) = 30.15 + 0.010 × 2556.4 = 30.15 + 25.564 = 55.714 kJ/kg dry air.
  4. h_B = 1.005 × 40 + 0.020 × (2500 + 1.88 × 40) = 40.20 + 0.020 × 2575.2 = 40.20 + 51.504 = 91.704 kJ/kg dry air.
  5. h_mix = (100 × 55.714 + 50 × 91.704)/150 = (5571.4 + 4585.2)/150 = 67.711 kJ/kg dry air.
  6. 67.711 = 1.005 T + 0.01333(2500 + 1.88 T) = 33.333 + 1.03007 T. Hence T = (67.711 − 33.333)/1.03007 = 34.378/1.03007 = 33.374 °C.
  7. The distance from A is m_B/(m_A+m_B) = 50/150 = 1/3 of AB, so the mixture is closer to A, consistent with the plotted point.

Answer. Y_mix = 0.0133 kg/kg dry air, h_mix = 67.7 kJ/kg dry air, T_mix = 33.4 °C; the point lies one-third of the way from stream A to stream B.

Exam traps

  • Using a 1 atm chart for air at 2 atm or for a vacuum dryer. Y, relative humidity and wet-bulb lines all change with total pressure.
  • Reading enthalpy along a horizontal line. Enthalpy lines are the sloping lines nearly parallel to the wet-bulb lines.
  • Confusing wet-bulb temperature with dew point. They are equal only at saturation; in unsaturated air the wet-bulb is higher.
  • Mixing with moist-air mass flows. The lever rule and Y balance use dry-air flows; moist-air flow includes the water vapour.
  • Cooling below the dew point by a horizontal path. Once saturation is reached the path bends along the saturation curve and liquid water leaves the air.

7.3 Adiabatic saturation and wet-bulb temperature

An adiabatic saturator is a chamber where air meets recirculated water with no heat through the walls. The air cools and takes up water vapour until it becomes saturated. The temperature it reaches is the adiabatic saturation temperature. A wet-bulb thermometer follows the same path: a wet wick cools until sensible heat from the air equals the latent heat of evaporation. Both give humidity from two temperatures, and both set the lower limit for evaporative cooling. They appear in cooling towers, air conditioning, dryer design and weather measurement.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for humidity and enthalpy, and Chapter 7.2 for reading the chart.

Adiabatic saturator

An adiabatic saturator is a well-insulated chamber. Air enters with dry-bulb temperature T and humidity Y. It flows over a large water surface. The water is recirculated, so it stays at the temperature the air reaches. No external heat enters. Some water evaporates. The latent heat comes from the air, so the air cools. The air leaves saturated at the adiabatic saturation temperature Tas. Its humidity is Ys(Tas), the saturation humidity at that temperature.

Y = Ys(Tas) − (cs/λs)(T − Tas)

Y is humidity of the entering air (kg water/kg dry air); T is dry-bulb temperature (°C); Tas is adiabatic saturation temperature (°C); Ys(Tas) is saturation humidity at Tas; cs is humid heat ≈1.00+1.88Y kJ/(kg dry air·K); λs is latent heat at Tas (kJ/kg water).

The enthalpy balance says the sensible heat lost by the air equals the latent heat used to evaporate the water. On a chart of Y against T, this equation is a straight line with slope −cs/λs. The line starts at the known air state and ends on the saturation curve at Tas. For air–water, λs is about 2450 kJ/kg near room temperature, so the slope is about −0.00042 kg/kg per K. The line is nearly flat.

Wet-bulb thermometer

A wet-bulb thermometer has a bulb covered by a wet cloth wick. Air flows past the wick. Water evaporates from the wick. The wick cools below the air temperature. At steady state, sensible heat from the air to the wick equals latent heat carried away by evaporation.

hc(T − Tw) = kYλ(Ys(Tw) − Y)

hc is convective heat transfer coefficient (W/m²·K); Tw is wet-bulb temperature (°C); kY is mass transfer coefficient (kg/m²·s·ΔY); λ is latent heat at Tw (J/kg water); Ys(Tw) is saturation humidity at Tw; Y is humidity of the air.

For a reliable reading, keep the wick wet, keep air speed above about 3 m/s, and shield the wick from radiation. Low air speed or a dry wick gives a reading closer to the dry-bulb temperature.

The Lewis relation for air–water is hc/(kYcs) ≈ 1, in consistent units. Put this into the wet-bulb equation and it becomes the same adiabatic saturation equation. So Tw ≈ Tas for air–water. For organic vapours the Lewis relation does not hold, so wet-bulb temperature and adiabatic saturation temperature are different.

Reading humidity and the cooling limit

On the chart, start at the saturation curve at Tw. Move along the adiabatic line to the dry-bulb temperature T. Read Y at the air state. For T = 40 °C and Tw = 22 °C, Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg and the line gives Y ≈ 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. Only two temperatures are needed.

The wet-bulb temperature is the lower limit of adiabatic cooling for air–water. Air can cool toward Tw by evaporating water into it, but not below Tw in that process. In a cooling tower the water outlet approaches the entering air wet-bulb temperature, not its dry-bulb temperature; see Chapter 7.5.

Worked example

Question. Air at 40 °C dry bulb and 20 % relative humidity has Y = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. It passes a wet-bulb thermometer and then an adiabatic saturator. Find the wet-bulb temperature and the humidity after the saturator.

  1. Entering humidity is given: Y = 0.0092 kg/kg dry air. A check from 20 % relative humidity at 40 °C gives the same value.
  2. Try Tw = 22 °C. The saturation humidity at 22 °C is Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air.
  3. Use cs ≈ 1.04 kJ/(kg·K) and λs ≈ 2450 kJ/kg. The adiabatic line gives Y = 0.0167 − (1.04/2450)(40 − 22) = 0.0167 − 0.0076 = 0.0091 kg/kg dry air.
  4. This 0.0091 is close to the entering 0.0092, so the wet-bulb and adiabatic saturation temperature is Tw ≈ 22 °C.
  5. In an ideal saturator the air leaves saturated at 22 °C, so Yout = Ys(22) = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air.
  6. Water gained = 0.0167 − 0.0092 = 0.0075 kg water per kg dry air.

Answer. The wet-bulb temperature is about 22 °C, the air leaves at Y = 0.0167 kg/kg dry air, and it gains 0.0075 kg water per kg dry air.

Exam traps

  • Writing wet-bulb temperature equal to adiabatic saturation temperature for every vapour. They are equal only for air–water, where the Lewis relation holds.
  • Using the dry-bulb temperature instead of Tw or Tas when finding Ys. The saturation humidity must be taken at the wet-bulb or adiabatic saturation temperature.
  • Mixing units: λ is in kJ/kg while cs is in kJ/(kg·K). Using λ in J/kg without converting the other term gives a factor-of-1000 error.
  • Forgetting the practical conditions. A dry wick, low air speed or strong radiation makes the wet-bulb reading unreliable.
  • Getting the sign wrong on the adiabatic line. As T falls, Y rises, so the slope is negative: −cs/λs.

7.4 Humidification and dehumidification operations

Air is humidified when water evaporates into it and dehumidified when water leaves it. Both are moves on the psychrometric chart: heating, cooling, adding water, removing water, or mixing two air streams. A spray of recirculated water humidifies air along a wet-bulb line and stops at saturation. Cooling below the dew point, liquid desiccants, solid adsorbents and compression remove water. These operations set the air condition in rooms, drying plants and cooling towers. Here Y is the humidity ratio: kg of water vapour per kg of dry air.

You need: humidity terms, the psychrometric chart, adiabatic saturation and wet-bulb temperature, and Unit 3 for NTU and the enthalpy driving force.

Methods of humidification

Humidification means adding water vapour to air. Three common methods do this in different ways.

Spray chamber or air washer. Air passes up through a chamber while water sprays down from nozzles. A mist eliminator removes drops. The water collects in a basin and is pumped back. With no external heating or cooling, the recirculated water settles at the adiabatic saturation temperature, and the air follows a wet-bulb line. The air can only approach saturation; it cannot cross the saturation curve.

Wetted-surface humidifier. Air flows over a wet pad or wetted plates. Water evaporates from the surface. This is simple and is used in evaporative coolers and small air-conditioning units. The pad must stay wet and clean.

Steam injection. Dry steam is injected into the air duct. It adds water vapour and heat together. The humidity rises; the dry-bulb temperature also tends to rise. It is used in clean rooms and hospitals where fine control and no water droplets are needed.

In spray chambers and wetted-surface humidifiers the driving force is the difference between the vapour pressure at the water surface and the partial pressure of water vapour in the air. Steam injection adds moisture by direct mixing, not by evaporation from a surface.

Methods of dehumidification

Dehumidification means removing water vapour. A cold surface, a liquid desiccant, a solid adsorbent or compression can do it.

Cooling below the dew point. Cool the air sensibly at constant humidity until its dew point. Further cooling makes water condense on the coil. The air then follows the saturation curve downwards. This is the usual room air-conditioner coil. The condensate must be drained.

Liquid absorption. A concentrated lithium chloride or glycol solution has a low water vapour pressure. Water vapour from the air dissolves into it. The solution becomes dilute and must be regenerated by heating. Absorption releases heat, so the air may need cooling afterwards.

Solid adsorption. Silica gel or activated alumina holds water on its surface. The bed must be regenerated with hot air. This gives very dry air, down to low dew points.

Compression. Compression raises the partial pressure of the water vapour while the saturation vapour pressure depends only on temperature, so the air becomes supersaturated and water condenses. Cooling the compressed air in an aftercooler lowers the saturation pressure further and removes more water. This is used in small compressed-air dryers.

A cold water spray is not a dehumidifier for ordinary air. Unless the water is below the air dew point, the spray can only humidify the air towards saturation.

Chart paths and packed humidifier design

On the chart, every operation is a path. Heating is a horizontal line to the right: Y is constant, relative humidity falls. Cooling is horizontal to the left until the dew point, then it bends down along the saturation curve and water condenses. Humidifying moves upward. An adiabatic spray follows a wet-bulb line. Dehumidifying moves downward, by cooling, desiccant or compression. Mixing two streams gives a point on the straight line joining them, fixed by the mass balance.

Packed humidifier design uses the same NTU idea as a packed absorber. The driving force is an enthalpy difference, not a concentration difference.

NTU = ∫ dH / (Hs − H), from Hin to Hout
Z = HTU × NTU
HTU = G / (KHa)

H is the moist-air enthalpy (kJ per kg dry air); Hs is the saturated-air enthalpy at the water temperature (kJ per kg dry air); NTU is the number of transfer units (dimensionless); HTU is the height of a transfer unit (m); Z is the packed height (m); G is the dry-air mass velocity (kg dry air per m² s); KH is the enthalpy transfer coefficient and a is the packing area per unit volume, so KHa is the volumetric enthalpy transfer coefficient.

For a spray chamber with recirculated water, the water temperature is nearly constant, so Hs is nearly constant. Then: NTU = ln[(Hs − Hin)/(Hs − Hout)]. The design steps are: fix inlet and outlet air states; find Hin, Hout and Hs; compute NTU; choose packing and flow to get HTU; multiply to get Z. A larger NTU means the outlet air is closer to saturation.

Worked example

Question. 1000 kg dry air/h enters an adiabatic spray chamber at 35 °C with Y = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air and leaves at 25 °C. (a) Find the outlet humidity using a wet-bulb slope of 0.00042 kg/kg per K. (b) Find the water evaporated. (c) If the outlet air is cooled to 15 °C, find the water condensed. The saturation humidity at 15 °C is 0.0106 kg/kg.

  1. Temperature drop = 35 − 25 = 10 K.
  2. On the wet-bulb line the slope is about cs/λ ≈ 0.00042 kg/kg per K, where cs is the humid heat and λ is the latent heat of water. ΔY = 0.00042 × 10 = 0.0042 kg/kg.
  3. Yout = 0.010 + 0.0042 = 0.0142 kg/kg.
  4. Water evaporated = 1000 × (0.0142 − 0.010) = 1000 × 0.0042 = 4.2 kg/h.

7.5 Cooling towers: types, construction and design

A cooling tower is an evaporative cooler for water. Hot water falls through packing while air rises or crosses. A small fraction of the water evaporates and carries latent heat away, so the rest leaves colder. For large heat loads this is cheaper than a closed heat exchanger. Power stations, refineries, petrochemical plants and big air-conditioning systems use cooling towers to reuse cooling water. The design is set by enthalpy, not by a plain temperature difference: the wet-bulb temperature fixes the lowest water temperature, and the Merkel integral converts the required cooling into fill volume.

You need 7.3 for wet-bulb temperature and 7.4 for the air-water enthalpy balance; Unit 2 supplies the mass transfer coefficients behind Ka.

What a cooling tower does and how it is built

A cooling tower rejects heat from circulating water to air. Hot water is sprayed over a fill. Air passes through the fill. A small part of the water evaporates, and the latent heat leaves with the vapour. The remaining water becomes colder. This is not a closed heat exchanger; the cooling comes from evaporation.

  • Natural-draught atmospheric towers rely on wind and a chimney effect. Natural-draught hyperbolic towers use a tall concrete shell to create draught; large power stations use them.
  • Mechanical-draught forced towers blow air in at the bottom; induced-draught towers pull air out at the top. Induced draught is common.
  • Counter-flow towers have water falling down and air rising up. Cross-flow towers have water falling down and air moving horizontally.
  • Construction: fill or packing gives thin films and large contact area; nozzles distribute hot water; drift eliminators catch droplets; louvers guide air; the fan moves air; the cold water basin collects cooled water.

Range is hot-water temperature minus cold-water temperature. Approach is cold-water temperature minus entering-air wet-bulb temperature. For 40 °C hot, 30 °C cold and 24 °C wet bulb, range is 10 K and approach is 6 K. The wet bulb is the lower limit because evaporation cannot cool water below it. A smaller approach needs a larger fill. Dry-bulb temperature alone does not set the size.

Enthalpy driving force and the Merkel equation

At any level, water loses heat L cp dT and air gains enthalpy G dh. For constant L/G, air enthalpy changes linearly with water temperature: h = h1 + (L cp/G)(T − Tc). The local driving force is h* − h. Here h* is the saturation enthalpy of air at the water temperature. h is the bulk air enthalpy at that level.

L c_p dT = G dh

L is water mass flow in kg/s; c_p is water specific heat, about 4.187 kJ/(kg K); dT is a small water temperature change in K; G is dry-air mass flow in kg/s; dh is the small air enthalpy change in kJ/kg dry air.

KaV/L = c_p ∫ dT/(h* − h) from Tc to Th

KaV/L is the tower characteristic, dimensionless; c_p is water specific heat, 4.187 kJ/(kg K) when h is in kJ/kg dry air and T is in K, and this factor is what makes KaV/L dimensionless; K is the mass transfer coefficient, a is interfacial area per unit volume, V is fill volume in m³, L is water flow in kg/s; Tc and Th are cold and hot water temperatures; h* is saturation enthalpy at the water temperature and h is bulk air enthalpy, both in kJ/kg dry air.

KaV/L is the number of transfer units, NTU. With fill height Z, Z = HTU × NTU and HTU = L/(Ka A), where A is the tower cross-sectional area in m²; then HTU is in metres and Z comes out in metres. The tower characteristic depends on L/G: more air per kg water lowers h at the same water temperature, raises h* − h, and lowers KaV/L, but fan power rises. Manufacturers give KaV/L against L/G for each fill.

A four-point Chebyshev estimate uses four fixed nodes: evaluate 1/(h* − h) at T = Tc + 0.1R, Tc + 0.4R, Tc + 0.6R and Tc + 0.9R, where R = Th − Tc, and take KaV/L ≈ c_p R/4 times the sum. For three equally spaced points, Simpson's rule is KaV/L ≈ c_p (Th − Tc)/6 [f_bottom + 4 f_mid + f_top], where f = 1/(h* − h).

Water losses, blowdown and fan power

Evaporation loss is E = G(Y2 − Y1), where Y1 and Y2 are inlet and outlet air humidity in kg water/kg dry air. Pure water leaves and salts stay, so dissolved solids concentrate. Blowdown B is deliberate drain. With cycles of concentration N and negligible drift, B ≈ E/(N − 1). More cycles save water but raise scaling risk.

Fan power is P ≈ Q_air Δp/η, where Q_air is air volume flow in m³/s, Δp is fan pressure rise in Pa, and η is fan efficiency. Higher air flow lowers L/G, which lowers the tower characteristic and so can reduce fill size, but fan power rises.

Worked example

Question. A tower cools 1.0 kg/s of water from 40 °C to 30 °C. Air enters at a wet bulb of 24 °C and leaves at about 38 °C; dry-air flow is 0.9 kg/s. Saturation enthalpies h* at 30, 35 and 40 °C are 99.7, 129.0 and 166.9 kJ/kg dry air. Inlet air has h1 = 72.2 kJ/kg and Y1 = 0.0189 kg/kg dry air; outlet air has Y2 = 0.031 kg/kg dry air. Find the heat load, the tower characteristic and the evaporation loss.

  1. Heat load: Q = L cp (Th − Tc) = 1.0 × 4.187 × (40 − 30) = 41.87 kW.
  2. Air enthalpy rise: dh = Q/G = 41.87/0.9 = 46.52 kJ/kg dry air. Outlet enthalpy h2 = 72.2 + 46.52 = 118.7 kJ/kg dry air.
  3. Bottom driving force: h* at 30 °C is 99.7; h1 is 72.2; h* − h = 99.7 − 72.2 = 27.5 kJ/kg.
  4. Middle: water at 35 °C, h* = 129.0; air enthalpy h = 72.2 + 46.52/2 = 95.5; driving force = 129.0 − 95.5 = 33.6 kJ/kg.
  5. Top: h* at 40 °C is 166.9; h2 is 118.7; driving force = 166.9 − 118.7 = 48.2 kJ/kg.
  6. Simpson: KaV/L = c_p (Th − Tc)/6 [1/27.5 + 4/33.6 + 1/48.2] = 4.187 × 0.2936 = 1.23.
  7. Evaporation: E = G(Y2 − Y1) = 0.9 × (0.031 − 0.0189) = 0.0109 kg/s ≈ 40 kg/h. This is 0.0109/1.0 × 100 = 1.1 % of circulation.

7.6 Drying mechanism and drying equilibria

A wet solid is not a bucket of water. Water sits in pores, on surfaces and inside cell walls, and part of it is held so tightly that ordinary air cannot pull it out. Drying stops when the solid's moisture equals the equilibrium moisture of the air around it. Engineers use moisture content on a dry basis, equilibrium isotherms and free moisture to judge how much water a dryer can remove and where the drying rate will change. This chapter builds that picture and links it to drying rate curves.

You need: 7.1 for absolute humidity and 7.2 for relative humidity and the psychrometric chart.

Moisture content on dry and wet bases

A solid sample has mass ms of dry solid and mw of water. The dry-basis moisture content X is kg water per kg dry solid. The wet-basis moisture w is kg water per kg wet solid. Dryers and drying curves use X because the dry solid mass does not change; buyers and labels often use w.

X = mw / ms

X is kg water per kg dry solid; mw is kg water; ms is kg dry solid.

w = mw / (mw + ms) = X / (1 + X)

w is kg water per kg wet solid; X is kg water per kg dry solid.

The reverse conversion is X = w/(1 − w). A sample that is 30 % water on a wet basis has w = 0.30 and X = 0.30/0.70 = 0.429 kg/kg dry solid, not 0.30. Keep the basis on every number.

Bound, unbound, free and equilibrium moisture

Bound moisture is held by sorption on surfaces and inside cell walls; it exerts a vapour pressure below that of pure water. Unbound moisture is liquid in large pores and capillaries; it behaves like ordinary water. Free moisture is the water above the equilibrium value, X − X*. Only free moisture can be removed by air of a given state.

Xf = X − X*

Xf is free moisture, kg/kg dry solid; X is current moisture, kg/kg dry solid; X* is equilibrium moisture, kg/kg dry solid.

Equilibrium moisture content X* is the moisture a solid holds when it neither gains nor loses water to air at a fixed temperature and relative humidity. At X = X*, evaporation and condensation are equal. A perfectly non-hygroscopic solid such as clean sand has X* close to zero at every relative humidity, because its water sits in open pores and escapes as freely as pure water. Hygroscopic solids such as wood, clay and food retain water even in dry air, so X* > 0.

  • Hygroscopic: X* rises steeply near high relative humidity; water is sorbed in cell walls.
  • Non-hygroscopic: X* is small or zero; water sits in pores.
  • Hysteresis: the desorption curve lies above the adsorption curve. At the same relative humidity a solid that is being dried holds more water than the same solid taking up water from dry air. A dried solid exposed to humid air follows the lower adsorption branch.

Temperature shifts the isotherm: warmer air gives a lower X* at the same relative humidity. Use the isotherm at the dryer temperature, not a room-temperature curve.

Mechanism of drying

Drying has three resistances in series: movement inside the solid, vapour transport away from the surface, and heat supply to the surface. The slowest step sets the rate.

  1. Capillary flow: surface tension pulls water along narrow pores toward the drying surface.
  2. Liquid diffusion: water moves through the solid phase down a concentration gradient.
  3. Vapour diffusion: water evaporates inside the solid and vapour diffuses through pores to the surface.
  4. Surface evaporation: at the interface, water changes to vapour; the surface temperature falls toward the wet-bulb temperature.
  5. Heat and mass transfer: air supplies sensible heat and carries vapour away; the rates depend on air temperature, humidity, velocity and solid thickness.

Early in drying the surface is wet. Capillary flow and surface evaporation keep it saturated, so the rate is nearly constant and controlled by the air. This is the constant-rate period. Later the surface dries, the evaporation front moves inward, and internal diffusion controls the rate. The rate falls as X falls; this is the falling-rate period. 7.7 turns these periods into drying-time calculations.

The constant-rate period ends at the critical moisture content Xc, in kg water per kg dry solid, where the surface can no longer stay fully wet. The falling-rate period that follows often has two parts: an unsaturated-surface part, where dry patches grow between wet ones, and a part controlled entirely by internal diffusion. A thin solid in fast air may show only the second part.

Thick solids, low air velocity and high air humidity all slow drying. A high air temperature raises surface evaporation but can case-harden food or crack clay; the solid sets the allowable temperature. Surface heat and mass transfer coefficients follow the correlations in Unit 2.

7.7 Drying rate curves and estimation of drying time

Drying is not one speed. A wet solid first loses surface water at a steady rate, then slows as water must travel from inside. The drying rate curve plots rate R against moisture X and makes both stages visible. Its shape is measured in a simple batch test: weigh the sample, convert to X, differentiate, divide by drying area. The drying time is (m_s/A) times the area under the 1/R curve between the two moistures, with m_s in kg dry solid and A in m². This is how batch times, dryer capacity and the effect of air conditions are estimated before sizing equipment.

You need Chapter 7.6 for moisture content X, equilibrium moisture X* and bound and free water.

From weighings to a rate curve

A batch drying test uses a thin sample in air at fixed dry-bulb temperature, humidity and velocity. Record the sample mass m at times t. If m_s is the dry-solid mass, then X = (m − m_s)/m_s kg water per kg dry solid. Plot X against t; the slope dX/dt at each point is negative. The drying rate is R = −(m_s/A)(dX/dt), where A is the exposed drying area in m² and R is kg water evaporated per m² per hour. The dry-solid mass m_s carries units kg dry solid.

  1. Dry a known wet sample at constant air conditions.
  2. Weigh it at fixed intervals and compute X at each time.
  3. Draw X against t and find slopes dX/dt.
  4. Multiply by −m_s/A to get R at each X.
  5. Plot R against X; mark the flat part and the fall.

R = −(m_s/A) dX/dt

R is drying rate in kg/(m²·h); m_s is dry-solid mass in kg; A is drying area in m²; X is moisture in kg/kg dry solid; t is time in h.

Constant rate, critical point, falling rate

In the constant-rate period the surface is wet. Water evaporates as from a free liquid surface, so R stays at R_c. This ends at the critical moisture X_c, when the surface can no longer supply free water. Below X_c the rate falls. In a simple linear falling-rate stage the rate falls in proportion to the free moisture above X*: R = R_c(X − X*)/(X_c − X*). Here X* is the equilibrium moisture, the value at which drying stops. Many solids show a second, slower diffusion-controlled stage near dryness.

t_c = (m_s/(A R_c))(X₁ − X_c)

t_c is constant-rate time in h; X₁ is initial moisture; X_c is critical moisture; R_c is constant drying rate; other symbols as before.

t_f = (m_s (X_c − X*)/(A R_c)) ln((X_c − X*)/(X₂ − X*))

t_f is falling-rate time in h; X_c − X* is free moisture at the critical point; R_c is the constant drying rate in kg/(m²·h); A is drying area in m²; m_s is dry-solid mass in kg; X₂ is final moisture above X*; ln is the natural logarithm. Use this for one straight falling line ending at X*.

If X₂ is above X_c, only t_c applies. If X₂ is below X_c, total time is t_c + t_f. For a curved falling stage, integrate graphically: t = (m_s/A) ∫ dX/R from X₂ to X₁, which is the area under the 1/R curve.

What moves the curve

Higher air temperature and lower humidity raise R_c and often X_c, because evaporation is faster. Air velocity raises R_c by thinning the boundary layer, but it barely changes the falling-rate period, where internal diffusion controls. Doubling the bed thickness roughly doubles m_s/A and the time, but a thick bed can dry unevenly. Shrinkage reduces A, so the real rate falls faster than the ideal line. Case hardening seals the surface, trapping water inside and adding a slow second falling stage. The measured curve, not a single formula, is the safe basis for design.

  • Constant rate: air temperature, humidity and velocity all matter.
  • Falling rate: internal diffusion dominates, so velocity matters little.
  • Thickness sets the mass per area and so the time.
  • Shrinkage and case hardening bend the real curve away from the ideal.

  4. Final moisture X₂ = 0.10 is below X_c, so the falling-rate period follows.
  5. For one straight falling line to X* = 0.05: t_f = (m_s (X_c − X*)/(A R_c)) ln((X_c − X*)/(X₂ − X*)).
  6. Substitute: t_f = (50 × (0.20 − 0.05)/(1 × 1.5)) ln((0.20 − 0.05)/(0.10 − 0.05)) h = (7.5/1.5) ln(0.15/0.05) h = 5.0 ln 3 h.
  7. ln 3 = 1.0986, so t_f = 5.0 × 1.0986 h = 5.49 h.
  8. Total time t = t_c + t_f = 6.67 + 5.49 h = 12.16 h, about 12.2 h.

Answer. The constant-rate time is 6.67 h, the falling-rate time is 5.49 h and the total drying time is about 12.2 h.

Exam traps

  • Using the constant-rate formula below X_c: t_c = (m_s/(A R_c))(X₁ − X_c) stops at X_c, even when X₂ is lower.
  • Forgetting that R_c is per m² of drying area, not per kg of solid; the factor m_s/A must appear.
  • Putting X₂ = X* in the log formula divides by zero; the final moisture must be above X*.
  • Expecting air velocity to speed the falling-rate period; it mainly changes R_c.
  • Mixing X on a dry basis with wet-basis moisture; these formulas use kg water per kg dry solid.

7.8 Batch and continuous dryers; through-circulation drying

Dryers remove water from a wet solid by bringing it into contact with hot, dry air. Batch dryers dry a fixed lot; continuous dryers take a steady feed and give a steady product. The choice follows the solid: slabs, pastes, slurries and granules each need a different contact pattern. In through-circulation drying, air is forced through a bed of granular solid, so the air picks up water and cools as it climbs. That humidity rise sets the drying time. The same idea sizes tray, belt and some continuous dryers in pottery, minerals, food and pharma.

You need drying rate curves and drying time from 7.7 and psychrometry from 7.2.

Batch and continuous dryers

Dryers are grouped by how they handle solids. Batch dryers hold a lot and dry it for a set time. Tray or compartment dryers put wet solid on trays and blow hot air over them; they suit pottery, pigments and pastes. Vacuum shelf dryers do the same under low pressure, so water boils below 100 °C; they suit heat-sensitive chemicals. Freeze dryers freeze the solid and remove ice by sublimation; they suit vaccines and instant coffee.

Continuous dryers receive wet solid and deliver dry solid without stopping. Tunnel dryers move trucks of trays through zones. Belt dryers carry a layer on a perforated belt. Rotary dryers tumble granules in a sloping cylinder with hot gas. Drum dryers dry a paste on a heated rotating drum. Spray dryers atomise a slurry into hot gas and make powder, as in milk powder. Fluidised-bed dryers suspend free-flowing particles in upward air. Pneumatic or flash dryers carry fine particles in a fast gas stream for seconds. The feed form decides the contact pattern: slabs need surface air, slurries need atomisation, granules need tumbling or fluidisation.

Through-circulation drying

In through-circulation drying, air is pushed through a bed of granular solid held on a perforated tray or belt. The air enters the bottom face at dry-bulb T1 and humidity Y1. As it climbs, it picks up water. At the top face its humidity is Y2 and its temperature T2, lower because evaporation takes latent heat. The bed therefore sees air that is driest and hottest at the inlet face and wettest and coolest at the outlet face. Pressure drop rises with bed depth, air velocity and fines; it must be paid for in fan power. Cross-circulation, by contrast, blows air over the tray surface, not through the bed.

The water balance on the air gives the drying rate. If m_da kg dry air/h flows through and its humidity rises from Y1 to Y2 kg water/kg dry air, water picked up = m_da (Y2 − Y1) kg/h. For M_s kg dry solid drying from X1 to X2 kg water/kg dry solid, water to remove = M_s (X1 − X2) kg.

t = M_s (X1 − X2) / [m_da (Y2 − Y1)]

t is drying time in h; M_s is dry solid mass in kg; X1 and X2 are initial and final solid moistures in kg water/kg dry solid; m_da is dry-air mass flow in kg dry air/h; Y1 and Y2 are inlet and outlet air humidities in kg water/kg dry air.

This assumes the air leaves unsaturated and the solid surface stays wet. If Y2 reaches saturation at T2, the bed cannot pick up more water and the time rises.

Choosing a dryer

Selection rules follow from the solid and the scale. Heat-sensitive solids go to vacuum, freeze or low-temperature continuous dryers; high temperature ruins drugs, enzymes and some foods. Particle size matters: fine slurries are spray dried; free-flowing granules go to fluidised beds; lumps and minerals go to rotary dryers. Tonnage decides batch versus continuous: a few hundred kilograms per batch suits trays; steady output of many tonnes per hour suits continuous units. Direct heating mixes hot gas with the solid. It is simple and efficient, but the gas must be clean enough for food or pharma. Indirect heating uses a hot wall or steam tubes. It keeps combustion products away from the product and suits flammable solvents, but it is slower. A continuous unit is not automatically better; it needs steady feed and steady product quality. The exam favourite is to match feed form to dryer, not to memorise a list.

Worked example

Question. A through-circulation tray dryer handles 100 kg of dry solid, to be dried from X1 = 0.25 to X2 = 0.05 kg water/kg dry solid. Air enters the bed at 60 °C with Y1 = 0.010 at 500 kg dry air/h and leaves at Y2 = 0.022. Find the drying time. Check that the outlet air is not saturated.

  1. Dry solid mass M_s = 100 kg. Initial moisture X1 = 0.25 kg water/kg dry solid. Final moisture X2 = 0.05 kg water/kg dry solid.
  2. Water to remove = M_s (X1 − X2) = 100 × (0.25 − 0.05) = 20 kg.
  3. Dry air flow m_da = 500 kg dry air/h. Inlet humidity Y1 = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air. Outlet humidity Y2 = 0.022 kg water/kg dry air.
  4. Water picked up by air = m_da (Y2 − Y1) = 500 × (0.022 − 0.010) = 6 kg/h.
  5. Drying time t = water to remove / water pickup = 20 kg / 6 kg/h = 3.3 h.
  6. Check outlet: at about 30 °C the saturation humidity is near 0.027 kg/kg, above 0.022, so the air leaves unsaturated and will not condense water back onto the solid.

Answer. The drying time is about 3.3 h.

Exam traps

  • Putting tunnel dryers in the batch list: a tunnel dryer is continuous because trucks move through it without stopping.
  • Confusing through-circulation with cross-circulation: through means air passes through the bed; cross means air passes over the tray surface.
  • Using wet-basis moisture in place of dry-basis X: the formula uses kg water per kg dry solid.
  • Forgetting the saturation limit: if outlet air would be above saturation, the computed Y2 is impossible and the time is longer.
  • Mixing units: m_da in kg dry air/h and t in h; if m_da is in kg/s, t comes in seconds.

7.9 Design of dryers, including rotary dryers

A dryer design is three balances and two sizing rules. The water balance gives the kg of water to remove each hour. The air balance gives the kg of dry air that carries it. The heat balance gives the fuel or steam duty. Then the air mass velocity fixes the diameter, and a length-to-diameter ratio fixes the length. That is how rotary dryers for sand, fertiliser, detergents and clay are sized before any drawing is made.

You need: 7.1 for humidity and humid volume, 7.7 for drying time, and 7.8 for dryer types.

Water balance, air balance, heat duty

Start with the bone-dry solid S, kg/h. It does not change. The feed carries water W₁ kg/h and the product carries W₂ kg/h. The water evaporated is W = W₁ − W₂. Moisture is usually given on a wet basis, so convert: dry-basis ratio X = x/(1 − x), where x is the wet-basis mass fraction.

W = S × [x₁/(1 − x₁) − x₂/(1 − x₂)] = F·x₁ − P·x₂, or W = S(X₁ − X₂)

W is water evaporated, kg/h; S is bone-dry solid, kg/h; F and P are wet feed and product, kg/h; x₁ and x₂ are wet-basis mass fractions; X₁ and X₂ are dry-basis kg water per kg dry solid.

The dry air carries that water: mₐ = W/(Y₂ − Y₁), where mₐ is kg dry air/h and Y is kg water per kg dry air. A small humidity rise means a huge air flow and a fat shell. A large rise means the outlet air is near saturation and the solids may re-wet. Keep outlet air below about 80 % relative humidity.

Useful heat has three parts: evaporate water, heat the solids, heat the water left in the product. Total fuel duty comes from the air-side balance mₐ(h_in − h_amb)/η, which includes shell losses. Drying efficiency = W·λ/Q_fuel, typically 40 to 70 % for a direct rotary dryer.

Q_useful = W·λ + S·cₚₛ·(T₂ − T₁) + W₂·cₚw·(T₂ − T₁)

Q_useful is useful heat, kJ/h; λ is latent heat at the wet-bulb temperature, about 2400 kJ/kg; cₚₛ is specific heat of dry solid, kJ/(kg·K); cₚw is 4.18 kJ/(kg·K); T₁ and T₂ are solid inlet and outlet temperatures, °C.

Sizing the rotary shell

A rotary dryer is a slightly inclined cylinder, 0.5° to 5° to the horizontal, turning at 1 to 8 rpm. Wet feed enters one end, tumbles down the shell, meets hot air, and leaves dry at the other end. Two empirical numbers fix the geometry.

  1. Get mₐ in kg/s from the water balance and the allowed humidity rise.
  2. Choose air mass velocity G, usually 1.5 to 2.5 kg/(m²·s) on the empty shell cross-section. Higher G means smaller diameter but more dust carry-over and fan power.
  3. Cross-section A = mₐ/G, then diameter D = √(4A/π). Round up to a standard shell size.
  4. Pick L/D between 4 and 10. Take L = 6D for a typical case.
  5. Check residence time, roughly 5 to 120 min. It must exceed the drying time from the rate curve in 7.7.
  6. Compute heat duty and efficiency. Check outlet air temperature against the solid's tolerance.

Fan volumetric flow uses humid volume at the outlet: v_H = (1/29 + Y/18) × 22.4 × (T + 273)/273 m³/kg dry air. Multiply by mₐ, never by total humid air mass. Air cools and gains water, so the outlet humid volume is usually smaller than the hot inlet volume. Size the exhaust fan on outlet conditions.

Flights inside the shell, and flow arrangement

Inside the shell are longitudinal fins called flights. As the shell turns, a flight lifts a wedge of solids and showers it through the hot gas. Most heat and mass transfer happens in that shower. Spiral flights at the feed end move wet sticky solids in quickly. Straight flights in the middle give a full curtain. Short flights near discharge stop dusting.

In cocurrent, air and solids move the same way. The hottest gas meets the wettest solid, so heat-sensitive materials such as PVC and soap are safe. But the exhaust gas leaves warmer than in counter-current, so more sensible heat is lost and thermal efficiency is lower. In counter-current the dry solid meets the hottest gas, giving the best heat economy and the driest product, but the solid must tolerate the high outlet temperature. Rule: heat-sensitive solid, cocurrent; fuel cost matters, counter-current.

Use a rotary dryer for free-flowing granular solids, not for pastes that ball up. For pastes, use a drum dryer or another paste-capable dryer; spray dryers suit pumpable solutions and slurries. See 7.8.

Worked example

Question. A rotary dryer treats 1000 kg/h of wet solid at 25 % moisture (wet basis) and delivers it at 2 % moisture (wet basis). Air enters at Y = 0.010 kg water/kg dry air and leaves at Y = 0.035. The air mass velocity is 2.0 kg/(m²·s) and L/D = 6. Find the water evaporated, the dry air rate, the shell diameter and the shell length.

  1. Bone-dry solid: S = 1000 × (1 − 0.25) = 750 kg/h.
  2. Water in feed: W₁ = 1000 × 0.25 = 250 kg/h.
  3. Product is 98 % solid, so P = 750/0.98 = 765.3 kg/h and W₂ = 765.3 − 750 = 15.3 kg/h.
  4. Water evaporated: W = 250 − 15.3 = 234.7 kg/h ≈ 235 kg/h.
  5. Humidity rise: Y₂ − Y₁ = 0.035 − 0.010 = 0.025 kg water/kg dry air.
  6. Dry air rate: mₐ = 234.7/0.025 = 9388 kg dry air/h = 2.608 kg/s.

  8. Diameter: D = √(4 × 1.304/π) = 1.288 m. Use 1.3 m.
  9. Length: L = 6 × 1.3 = 7.8 m.
  10. Heat for evaporation alone: Q = 234.7 × 2400 = 5.63 × 10⁵ kJ/h.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same dryer, the solids enter at 25 °C and leave at 70 °C. The dry solid has cₚₛ = 1.2 kJ/(kg·K). Find the useful heat duty (kJ/h) and the fraction used for evaporation.

  1. Heat to evaporate: Q₁ = 234.7 × 2400 = 563 280 kJ/h.
  2. Heat to raise dry solid: Q₂ = 750 × 1.2 × (70 − 25) = 40 500 kJ/h.

Exam traps

  • Using wet-basis percentage as kg water per kg dry solid. Convert before the balance.
  • Using total humid air mass instead of dry air mass in the volumetric flow, so the fan is oversized.
  • Forgetting sensible heat for solids and remaining moisture; the duty comes out too low and the air heater is undersized.
  • Taking fan volumetric flow at inlet temperature. Air cools and gains water; the outlet humid volume is usually smaller than the hot inlet volume, so using inlet T oversizes the fan.
  • Mixing up cocurrent and counter-current: cocurrent protects heat-sensitive solids but its exhaust gas leaves warmer, so thermal efficiency is lower.

Takeaways

  1. Two numbers, the dry-bulb temperature and the humidity ratio, fix every other property of moist air; the rest are computed, not measured.
  2. The psychrometric chart is only those computations drawn once, so a state is located by any two independent properties and everything else is read off.
  3. For air and water the wet-bulb temperature and the adiabatic saturation temperature are the same because the Lewis relation holds.
  4. Humidifying air with recirculated water follows a constant wet-bulb line, so the adiabatic saturation temperature is the lowest temperature that water alone can reach.
  5. Cooling-tower design integrates an enthalpy driving force between the cold-water and hot-water temperatures, and the answer is the tower characteristic.
  6. A solid stops drying at the equilibrium moisture content of the surrounding air, so that water cannot be removed by that air at any longer time.
  7. Drying time is the area under the curve of 1/(drying rate) plotted against moisture content, taken between the starting and ending moistures.
  8. A dryer design is three balances in a row: water, dry air and heat; sizing of the shell follows from the air velocity the solid can tolerate.

Use the wet-bulb equation to get Y from t and t_wb; Read or compute Y_s at 25 °C for the saturation term; Dew point from Y by inverting the humidity equation, or from the chart; Humid heat c_s = 1.005 + 1.88 Y; Humid volume v_H = (1/29 + Y/18) × 0.08314 T, T in kelvin; Enthalpy H = c_s (t) + 2500 Y, answer in kJ per kg dry air; State units at every step and note the pressure assumed, usually 1 atm.

Apply A counter-current induced-draft cooling tower cools water from 45 °C to 30 °C. Air enters at 30 °C dry-bulb and 24 °C wet-bulb and leaves at 38 °C and 90 % relative humidity. Calculate the water-to-air ratio, the number of transfer units and the tower characteristic. 15 marks

Water balance: L(T_1 − T_2) and the evaporation loss from the humidity rise; Air-side enthalpy in and out from the chart, both in kJ per kg dry air; L/G from the overall enthalpy balance; Integrate KaV/L = ∫ dT_w/(h* − h) step by step in 3–5 °C intervals, h* from the saturation curve at the water temperature; Tabulate 1/(h* − h) against T_w and use the trapezoidal rule; State range = 15 °C and approach = 30 − 24 = 6 °C; Comment on the accuracy of the approximation that L is constant, since some water evaporates.

Apply A wet solid is dried from 0.35 to 0.05 kg water per kg dry solid in air whose equilibrium moisture content is 0.02. The critical moisture content is 0.15 and the constant drying rate is 2.5 kg/(m²·h) for 1 kg dry solid of 0.6 m² surface. The falling-rate curve is a straight line through the origin. Estimate the total drying time. 13 marks

Constant-rate period: from 0.35 to 0.15, time = (X_1 − X_c)/(rate per unit area × area per kg); Identify the falling-rate period from 0.15 down to 0.05, with free moisture X − X*; Linear falling rate in (X − X*): time = (X_c − X*) × ln[(X_c − X*)/(X_2 − X*)] ÷ constant rate; Add the two times and give the answer in hours; Check units: kg water/(kg dry solid) divided by kg water/(h·kg dry solid).

Apply A rotary dryer handles 2 tonnes per hour of wet solid, reducing moisture from 30 % to 5 % on a wet basis. Air enters at 100 °C and leaves at 60 °C. Find the water evaporated, the dry-air rate, the heat duty, and estimate the shell diameter and length for an air mass velocity of 2 kg/(m²·s). 16 marks

Convert wet-basis to dry-basis moisture: X = kg water/kg dry solid, before and after; Dry solid rate is unchanged; water evaporated = dry solid × (X_1 − X_2); Humidity of entering and leaving air from a heat and mass balance on the dryer; Dry-air rate from the water balance G(Y_2 − Y_1) = water evaporated; Heat duty from the enthalpy rise of the air plus the solid and the water vapour; Diameter from cross-sectional area = air mass rate/(mass velocity), with air density at the mean temperature; Length from a residence time or a volumetric heat-transfer coefficient; state the rule used.

Apply Air at 35 °C dry-bulb and 20 % relative humidity is blown through an air washer that recirculates its own spray water. Describe the path of the air on the psychrometric chart, find the lowest temperature the water can reach, and estimate the exit condition if the washer is 80 % effective. 13 marks

Recirculated water takes up the adiabatic saturation temperature, so the air moves along the constant wet-bulb line; Read the entering wet-bulb temperature from the chart, about 19–20 °C for this state; The lowest water temperature is the adiabatic saturation temperature; the air can approach it but not pass it; Effectiveness = (t_in − t_out)/(t_in − t_as), solve for t_out; Humidity rises as the air cools, and it stays below saturation; Explain that a chilled-water spray would move the air below the wet-bulb line and dehumidify it, which is a different operation.

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate Compare natural-draft, forced-draft and induced-draft cooling towers, and counterflow with crossflow layouts, in terms of air flow, land area, upkeep and effectiveness. Recommend a type for a large coastal power plant and justify the choice. 13 marks

Natural draft: tall hyperbolic shell, no fan, low running cost, high capital cost, poor control at part load; Forced draft: fan at the air inlet, higher power, recirculation risk; induced draft: fan at the warm air exit, more stable and easier to control; Counterflow gives better thermal performance per unit volume; crossflow is easier to build and has lower pressure drop; Compare approach, pumping head, drift and makeup water; Recommendation with reasons: induced-draft counterflow, or natural draft where land and capital allow; Mention plume and drift as environmental points.

Evaluate A heat-sensitive granular solid must be dried from a high moisture content to a low one. Compare tray, tunnel, rotary and fluidized-bed dryers on contact time, uniformity, dust, and suitability for heat-sensitive solids, and choose one with justification. 13 marks

Tray: batch, long residence time, low dust, labour heavy, poor for large tonnage; Tunnel: continuous version of tray, first-in first-out, still long times; Rotary: continuous, high throughput, tumbling causes attrition and dust, hold-up is large; Fluidized bed: excellent heat transfer and uniform temperature, short residence time, good for heat-sensitive solids, needs narrow particle size; Choose fluidized bed for heat sensitivity with uniform granules; choose rotary for large tonnage and forgiving solids; Add a note on the falling-rate period being the controlling step.

Analyze Examine the assumptions behind the Lewis relation and hence the wet-bulb thermometer. Under what conditions does the wet-bulb reading become unreliable, and what corrections or precautions are used? 13 marks

Lewis relation h_c/(k_Y c_s) ≈ 1 holds closely for air–water but not for other gas–vapour pairs; Assumes negligible radiation, negligible conduction along the stem, and air velocity in the working range; High air velocity or hot surroundings add radiation error; shield or use an aspirated psychrometer; Air near saturation gives a small wet-bulb depression, so the humidity derived is inaccurate; Water must wet the wick fully and be free of dissolved solids; Above 100 °C or with superheated vapour, the wet-bulb concept changes and the adiabatic saturation equation is used directly.

Analyze Explain what happens inside a deep bed during through-circulation drying: how the air temperature and humidity change as the air climbs, how the drying zone travels, and what this does to the total drying time compared with cross-circulation. 13 marks

Air enters hot and dry, picks up moisture in the bottom layer, leaves cooler and wetter; The bed splits into a dried zone, a drying zone and a still-wet zone; As drying proceeds the drying zone moves upward like a front, and the exit air stays near saturation for most of the run; Material near the air inlet reaches equilibrium moisture long before the top layer; Total time is set by the last layer to dry, not by the average rate; Through circulation gives better transfer coefficients and shorter times than cross-circulation, at the cost of higher pressure drop.

Glossary

Blowdown
The deliberate purging of circulating water to keep dissolved solids below a limit that would cause scaling.
Bound moisture
Water held inside the solid by chemical or cellular binding, whose vapour pressure is lower than that of free water.
Compression humidification
Humidification by compressing the gas and then cooling it, so that a controlled amount of vapour is retained.
Constant-rate period
The drying stage in which the solid surface stays wet and the drying rate stays fixed by the external heat and mass transfer.
Cooling below the dew point
A dehumidification method in which the gas is cooled so that water condenses out, leaving a saturated gas at the lower temperature.
Dehumidification
Any operation that removes water vapour from a gas stream, by cooling below the dew point, absorption or adsorption.
Drift
Fine water droplets carried out of a cooling tower by the air stream, removed by drift eliminators.
Dry-bulb depression
The difference between the dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures; it measures how much the air can still take up by evaporation.
Drying
The removal of a volatile liquid, usually water, from a wet solid by evaporation into a gas stream.
Drying rate curve
A plot of the drying rate, in kg water per (m²·h), against the moisture content, showing the warming-up, constant-rate and falling-rate periods.
Falling-rate period
The drying stage in which the surface is no longer fully wet and the drying rate drops, controlled by the movement of moisture inside the solid.
Fluidized-bed dryer
A dryer in which hot air suspends the particles of solid, giving fast, uniform drying at a nearly constant temperature.
Heat balance of a dryer
The statement that the heat supplied by the air equals the heat used to evaporate water plus the sensible heat of the solid and the losses.
Humidification
Any operation that increases the water-vapour content of a gas stream.
Humidity
The general term for the water vapour present in a gas, measured in several ways such as absolute humidity or relative humidity.
Liquid absorption
Dehumidification in which the gas contacts a hygroscopic liquid such as lithium chloride solution or glycol, which takes up water.
Makeup water
Water added to a cooling tower or washer to replace evaporation, drift and blowdown losses.
Molal humidity
Moles of water vapour per mole of vapour-free gas, which equals p_w/(P − p_w).
Percentage humidity
One hundred times the ratio of the actual humidity ratio to the saturation humidity ratio at the same dry-bulb temperature.
Percentage relative humidity
One hundred times the ratio of the actual water-vapour partial pressure to the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature.
Saturation humidity
The humidity ratio of air that is saturated with water vapour at the given dry-bulb temperature.
Solid adsorption
Dehumidification in which water vapour is held on the surface of a solid such as silica gel or activated alumina.
Steam injection
A humidification method in which live steam is sparged into the air; both the humidity and the temperature rise.
Tunnel dryer
A continuous dryer in which trucks or trays of solid move through a long chamber against a stream of hot air.
Unbound moisture
Water held in the larger pores of a solid whose vapour pressure equals that of free water at the same temperature.
Wetted-surface humidifier
A device in which air passes over a large wetted surface, picking up water vapour without any spray or liquid droplets.
