6.1 Adsorption principles and adsorbents

Adsorption takes a solute out of a gas or a liquid by holding it on the surface of a porous solid. The solid is the adsorbent, the held solute is the adsorbate, and nearly all of the surface lies inside the pores. Plants use it to decolourise sugar syrup, polish drinking water, recover solvent vapour, dry air and split oxygen from air. How much an adsorbent can hold depends on its surface area, its pore sizes and the force that holds the molecules.

What happens at the surface

A molecule at a solid surface has neighbours on one side only, so the surface attracts molecules from the fluid that touches it. When a solute molecule is held there, it has been adsorbed. Absorption is different: in gas absorption the solute dissolves into the whole body of a liquid. Adsorption stays at the surface, even though that surface is mostly the walls of pores inside each grain. When both may happen, or the mechanism is unclear, the general word is sorption.

Adsorption releases heat, the heat of adsorption, because held molecules have less energy than free ones. Two consequences follow for the plant. A bed warms as it loads, which lowers its capacity and in solvent recovery can even start a fire in a carbon bed. And a loaded adsorbent gives up its solute when it is heated or when the pressure is dropped, which is how it is regenerated and reused.

The solute reaches a site in three steps in series: across the stagnant film of fluid around the grain, along the pores into the grain, and onto the surface itself. The first two set how fast a bed works; the equilibrium of the last step sets how much it can hold.

Physical adsorption and chemisorption

Syllabi ask for the two types of adsorption side by side. Physical adsorption (van der Waals adsorption) uses the same weak forces that condense a vapour. Chemisorption forms a chemical bond between the adsorbate and the surface.

Nearly all separation adsorbers rely on physical adsorption, because the adsorbent must be regenerated thousands of times. Chemisorption is the first step of heterogeneous catalysis.

Surface area and pore size

Capacity is measured as the loading q, the mass of adsorbate held per unit mass of dry adsorbent, often mg/g or kg/kg. More surface means more sites, so good adsorbents are made highly porous. One gram of activated carbon can expose 1000 m² of surface, about four tennis courts. Pores are sorted by width: micropores below 2 nm, mesopores 2–50 nm and macropores above 50 nm.

Micropores give most of the area, and their opposite walls overlap their pull, so they hold small molecules strongly. They are also slow to enter and may be too narrow for large dye or protein molecules. Macropores add little area but act as roads that carry the fluid quickly into the grain. A good adsorbent has both: macropores and mesopores feeding the micropores. The particle size matters too: small grains are loaded faster, but they raise the pressure drop through a bed.

q = (C0 − Ce)V / W

Batch test: W of dry adsorbent shaken with volume V of solution until the concentration stops changing. C0 and Ce are the initial and equilibrium concentrations; solute lost by the liquid is gained by the solid. With C in mg/L, V in L and W in g, q is in mg/g.

Polar adsorbents (silica gel, alumina, zeolites) prefer water and other polar molecules; non-polar carbon prefers organic molecules and works in wet streams. Zeolites are named by pore opening: 3A (about 0.3 nm) admits water but not ethanol, so it dries ethanol; 4A and 5A admit larger molecules; 13X is the widest. A zeolite separates nitrogen from oxygen by equilibrium, not size: both molecules enter, but nitrogen's larger electric quadrupole is held more strongly by the cations in the cages, so oxygen leaves first and the product is oxygen-rich. A carbon molecular sieve works the other way, by rate: the slightly smaller oxygen molecule diffuses in faster, so a short cycle gives nitrogen-rich product.

Ion-exchange resins look like adsorbents (porous beads in a column) but work by swapping ions of equal charge, as described in Ion exchange principles and equipment.

Worked example

Question. 2.00 g of activated carbon is shaken with 100 mL of a dye solution of 50 mg/L until equilibrium; the liquid then holds 10 mg/L. (a) Find the loading q and the fraction of dye removed. (b) Assuming q stays the same at the same final concentration, how much carbon treats 1000 L of the same solution to 10 mg/L?

  1. Volume in litres: V = 100 mL = 0.100 L.
  2. Dye removed = (C0 − Ce)V = (50 − 10) × 0.100 = 4.0 mg.
  3. q = 4.0 mg/2.00 g = 2.0 mg/g. Fraction removed = 40/50 = 80 %.
  4. (b) Dye to remove from 1000 L = 40 mg/L × 1000 L = 40 000 mg.
  5. Carbon = 40 000/2.0 = 20 000 g = 20 kg. Batch contact uses the loading in equilibrium with the final, lowest concentration, so the carbon is used at only 2.0 mg/g.

Answer. q = 2.0 mg/g, 80 % of the dye removed; 20 kg of carbon treats 1000 L in one batch.

Worked example 2

Question. An activated carbon has a surface area of 1000 m²/g. A benzene molecule lying flat covers about 0.40 nm². Estimate the benzene held by 1 g of carbon if one complete layer covers the whole surface (benzene 78.11 g/mol).

  1. Area of one molecule = 0.40 nm² = 0.40 × 10−18 m².
  2. Molecules in the layer = 1000/(0.40 × 10−18) = 2.5 × 1021.
  3. Moles = 2.5 × 1021/(6.022 × 1023) = 4.15 × 10−3 mol.
  4. Mass = 4.15 × 10−3 × 78.11 = 0.324 g, so q ≈ 324 mg/g, about one third of the carbon's own mass.

Answer. A monolayer holds about 0.32 g of benzene per g of carbon, which is why area is the first figure quoted for an adsorbent.

Exam traps

  • Adsorption is not absorption: the solute stays on the pore walls of the solid; in absorption it dissolves into the whole liquid.
  • Using V = 100 with mg/L gives 4000 mg instead of 4.0 mg. Convert mL to L.
  • q is mass of solute per mass of dry adsorbent, not a concentration in the liquid.
  • Physical adsorption falls as temperature rises because it is exothermic; that is the basis of regeneration by heating.
  • A zeolite does not split O2 from N2 by sieving size: both enter, and N2 is held more strongly. The carbon molecular sieve is the one that separates by rate.

6.2 Adsorption equilibria: Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms

Leave an adsorbent in a fluid long enough and the loading stops changing: molecules land on the surface as fast as they leave it. The equilibrium loading q plotted against the fluid concentration C (or the partial pressure p of a gas) at one temperature is the adsorption isotherm. It tells the designer how much solute a kilogram of adsorbent can hold at the concentration it will see, and so how much adsorbent a duty needs. Two equations cover most exam questions: Langmuir and Freundlich.

You need the batch loading q = (C0 − Ce)V/W from Adsorption principles and adsorbents.

Measuring an isotherm

Flasks with the same volume V of solution but different starting concentrations C0 (or different adsorbent masses W) are shaken at constant temperature until nothing changes. Each flask gives one pair: the equilibrium concentration Ce left in the liquid and the loading q = (C0 − Ce)V/W. The isotherm is q against Ce, the equilibrium concentration, never against the feed concentration. For a gas the same is done with the partial pressure p of the adsorbate.

At very low concentration most isotherms are straight, q = KC (Henry's law region). At higher concentration they bend. An isotherm that is concave towards the C axis, rising steeply and then levelling, is favourable: a lot is held even from a dilute fluid. A straight line is linear, and one that curves upward is unfavourable: little is held until the fluid is concentrated.

Langmuir isotherm

Langmuir assumed a surface of identical sites, each holding one molecule, with no forces between held molecules, so adsorption stops at a single complete layer. Equating the rate of landing on empty sites to the rate of leaving full ones gives:

q = qmKLC / (1 + KLC)      linear form: C/q = 1/(qmKL) + C/qm

qm is the monolayer capacity (mg/g); KL is the affinity constant (L/mg when C is in mg/L); C is the equilibrium concentration. A plot of C/q against C is a straight line of slope 1/qm and intercept 1/(qmKL), so qm = 1/slope and KL = slope/intercept. For a gas replace C by p and KL by b (1/bar).

At low C, KLC ≪ 1 and q ≈ qmKLC, the straight Henry's law region. At high C, KLC ≫ 1 and q approaches qm. A large KL means the solute is held strongly and the curve reaches its plateau at a low concentration. The dimensionless separation factor RL = 1/(1 + KLC0) sums up the shape: between 0 and 1 favourable, 1 linear, above 1 unfavourable.

Freundlich isotherm

Real surfaces have strong and weak sites. Freundlich's equation is empirical and fits such surfaces over a limited range:

q = KFC1/n      linear form: log q = log KF + (1/n) log C

KF is a capacity constant whose units, (mg/g)(L/mg)1/n, depend on n; 1/n (between 0 and 1 for a favourable isotherm) measures how quickly q levels off. A plot of log q against log C has slope 1/n and intercept log KF.

The Freundlich isotherm has no plateau: q keeps rising as C rises, so it must not be extrapolated far beyond the data it was fitted to. It is the usual form for activated carbon in water treatment, where concentrations are low.

Effect of pressure and temperature

Pressure. For a gas the loading depends on the partial pressure of the adsorbate, p = yP. Raising the total pressure at a fixed mole fraction raises p and so raises q; adding an inert gas does not. Pressure-swing adsorption loads a bed at high pressure and regenerates it by dropping to low pressure.

Temperature. Physical adsorption gives out heat, so at a fixed C or p a hotter adsorbent holds less. The constants qm, KL, KF and n belong to the temperature at which they were measured. Isotherms measured at two temperatures give the heat of adsorption from the pressures p1 and p2 needed for the same loading: ln(p2/p1) = (ΔHads/R)(1/T1 − 1/T2), with ΔHads taken as a positive heat released. Temperature-swing adsorption uses this: load cold, regenerate hot.

Other shapes exist. Where several layers build up, as in nitrogen on a solid near its boiling point, the BET isotherm is used, and its fit gives the surface area of an adsorbent.

Worked example

Question. A dye on activated carbon at 25 °C follows the Langmuir isotherm with qm = 40 mg/g and KL = 1.5 L/mg. (a) Find q at C = 2.0 mg/L. (b) Find C when q = 20 mg/g. (c) Is the isotherm favourable for a feed of 10 mg/L?

  1. (a) KLC = 1.5 × 2.0 = 3.0. q = 40 × 3.0/(1 + 3.0) = 120/4.0 = 30 mg/g.
  2. (b) 20 = 60C/(1 + 1.5C), so 20 + 30C = 60C and C = 20/30 = 0.667 mg/L. Half of qm is reached at C = 1/KL, which is the same 0.667 mg/L.
  3. (c) RL = 1/(1 + 1.5 × 10) = 1/16 = 0.0625, between 0 and 1: strongly favourable.

Answer. q = 30 mg/g at 2.0 mg/L; C = 0.667 mg/L at q = 20 mg/g; RL = 0.0625, favourable.

Worked example 2

Question. Equilibrium tests for the same dye give: C (mg/L) 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0 and q (mg/g) 17.1, 24.0, 30.0, 35.3. Fit the Langmuir and the Freundlich equations by their linear plots and compare their predictions at C = 10 mg/L.

  1. Langmuir: C/q = 0.02924, 0.04167, 0.06667, 0.14164 g/L. A least-squares line through (C, C/q) has slope 0.02498 g/mg and intercept 0.01671 g/L.
  2. qm = 1/0.02498 = 40.0 mg/g; KL = 0.02498/0.01671 = 1.50 L/mg. The points lie on the line, so Langmuir fits.
  3. Freundlich: log C = −0.301, 0, 0.301, 0.699 and log q = 1.233, 1.380, 1.477, 1.548. The best line has slope 1/n = 0.311 (n = 3.22) and intercept 1.355, so KF = 101.355 = 22.7 (mg/g)(L/mg)0.311. The points bend away from this line at both ends.
  4. At C = 10 mg/L: Langmuir q = 40 × 15/16 = 37.5 mg/g; Freundlich q = 22.7 × 100.311 = 46.3 mg/g.
  5. Freundlich overshoots the monolayer capacity because it has no plateau; outside the measured range, trust the model whose straight-line plot actually fitted.

Answer. Langmuir: qm = 40.0 mg/g, KL = 1.50 L/mg. Freundlich: 1/n = 0.311, KF = 22.7. At 10 mg/L they predict 37.5 and 46.3 mg/g; the Langmuir value is the reliable one here.

Exam traps

  • In the plot of C/q against C, the slope is 1/qm and the intercept is 1/(qmKL). Reading the intercept as qm is the commonest error.
  • In the Freundlich log plot, 1/n is the slope, not n. Invert the slope to get n.
  • Plot q against the equilibrium concentration Ce, not against the starting concentration C0.
  • For a gas the loading follows the partial pressure of the adsorbate, not the total pressure.
  • An isotherm measured at 25 °C overestimates the loading of a bed run at 60 °C, since physical adsorption falls as temperature rises.

6.3 Batch and fixed-bed adsorption; breakthrough curves

An adsorbent can meet the fluid in three ways. In stagewise contact, powdered adsorbent is stirred into the liquid and filtered off. In a moving bed, granules travel down a column against the rising fluid. In a fixed bed, the fluid flows through stationary granules until the bed is full and must be regenerated. The fixed bed is the commonest; its breakthrough curve tells the operator when to switch.

You need the Freundlich and Langmuir isotherms from Adsorption equilibria: Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms.

Stagewise contact

In one stage, W kg of fresh adsorbent is mixed with V of liquid at C0 and leaves in equilibrium with the final liquid C1. The solute balance V(C0 − C1) = W q(C1) is a straight operating line of slope −V/W on the q–C diagram; where it cuts the isotherm is the result. The adsorbent ends in equilibrium with the cleanest liquid, so one stage is wasteful when C1 must be low.

Cross-current: the liquid is treated twice, each time with fresh adsorbent, and the spent adsorbent is filtered off after each stage. The first stage then works at a higher concentration, and so at a higher loading. For a Freundlich isotherm the least total adsorbent comes from choosing the middle concentration C1 to satisfy (C1/C2)1/n − (1/n)(C0/C1) = 1 − 1/n.

Counter-current: fresh adsorbent meets the cleanest liquid in the last stage, then is moved to the first stage to meet the incoming feed, where it leaves loaded almost to q(C0). The balances are V(C0 − C2) = W q1 overall and V(C1 − C2) = W q2 for the last stage, with q1 = q(C1) and q2 = q(C2).

Moving beds

A moving bed carries the counter-current idea to a column: granules enter at the top, fall slowly as a dense bed against the rising fluid, leave loaded at the bottom and go to a regenerator before returning. It acts like many counter-current stages, but the granules wear as they move. Today the same effect is obtained without moving solid in the simulated moving bed: a ring of fixed beds in which rotary valves move the feed and product points one bed along at set times, used to separate p-xylene and to purify sugars.

Fixed bed: zones and the breakthrough curve

Feed at C0 enters a bed of fresh adsorbent. The grains near the inlet load first and soon reach equilibrium with C0: the saturated zone. Ahead of it is the mass-transfer zone (MTZ), a band in which the fluid concentration falls from C0 to nearly zero and the adsorbent is partly loaded. Below it the adsorbent is still clean.

When the front of the MTZ reaches the outlet, the exit concentration C starts to rise. The plot of C/C0 against time (or volume treated) is the breakthrough curve, usually S-shaped. The break point tb is the time at which C reaches the highest level the product may contain, often C/C0 = 0.05 or 0.10; the bed is switched there. Exhaustion is at C/C0 ≈ 0.95.

ts = ∫0∞ (1 − C/C0) dt      held = C0Qts      LUB = H(1 − tb/ts)

Q is the volumetric feed rate and H the bed height. ts is the stoichiometric time: the time an ideal bed with a vertical front would last. The area above the curve up to tb gives the solute held at the break point. LUB, the length of unused bed, is the part of the bed still unused at the break point (about half the MTZ). For a new design with the same fluid velocity and particle size the LUB stays the same, so a longer bed is Hnew = Hused + LUB.

What changes the curve

  • Flow rate. A higher velocity brings solute faster and gives less contact time, so tb comes earlier, the MTZ widens and the LUB grows.
  • Particle size. Smaller grains shorten the pore path, so the curve sharpens, at the price of a higher pressure drop.
  • Bed height. The LUB stays nearly fixed, so a taller bed uses a larger fraction of its adsorbent before the break point.
  • Isotherm shape. A favourable isotherm makes the front self-sharpening: the dilute part of the front is held more strongly and moves slower than the concentrated part, so the front keeps a constant shape. An unfavourable isotherm spreads the front as it travels.

Plants run two or more beds, one adsorbing while another is regenerated by hot gas or steam (temperature swing), lower pressure (pressure swing) or a solvent wash. In lead–lag operation two beds run in series, so the first can be run to exhaustion.

Worked example

Question. 1000 L of wastewater containing 50 mg/L of phenol is to be reduced to 5 mg/L with powdered carbon whose isotherm is q = 8C0.5 (q in mg/g, C in mg/L). Find the carbon needed (a) in one stage, (b) in two cross-current stages at the best intermediate concentration, (c) in two counter-current stages.

  1. (a) The carbon leaves at q(5) = 8 × 50.5 = 17.89 mg/g. Solute to remove = 1000 × (50 − 5) = 45 000 mg. W = 45 000/17.89 = 2516 g = 2.52 kg.
  2. (b) Optimum C1 from (C1/5)0.5 − 0.5 × (50/C1) = 0.5. By trial, C1 = 17.93 mg/L: √3.587 − 25/17.93 = 1.894 − 1.394 = 0.500.
  3. Stage 1: q(17.93) = 8 × 4.235 = 33.88 mg/g; W1 = 1000 × (50 − 17.93)/33.88 = 946 g. Stage 2: W2 = 1000 × (17.93 − 5)/17.89 = 723 g. Total = 1.67 kg.
  4. (c) Counter-current: (C0 − C2)/q(C1) = (C1 − C2)/q(C2), that is 45/(8√C1) = (C1 − 5)/17.89. By trial, C1 = 25.09 mg/L: left side 45/40.07 = 1.123, right side 20.09/17.89 = 1.123.
  5. W = 1000 × 45/40.07 = 1123 g = 1.12 kg. The carbon leaves stage 1 at 40.07 mg/g, more than twice the single-stage loading.

Answer. One stage 2.52 kg; two cross-current stages 1.67 kg (946 + 723 g, C1 = 17.9 mg/L); two counter-current stages 1.12 kg, less than half the single-stage carbon.

Worked example 2

Question. A carbon bed 0.60 m high holding 1500 g of carbon treats 2 L/min of water containing 80 mg/L of a dye. The exit concentration is: t (min) 0, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110 and C/C0 0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.15, 0.35, 0.60, 0.80, 0.92, 0.97, 0.99, 1.00. Take the break point at C/C0 = 0.05. Find the loading at the break point, the saturation loading, the LUB, and the break time of a 1.2 m bed at the same velocity.

  1. Break point tb = 30 min. Area above the curve to 30 min by trapezoids: (1 + 0.99)/2 × 20 + (0.99 + 0.95)/2 × 10 = 19.9 + 9.7 = 29.6 min.
  2. Dye held at the break point = 80 mg/L × 2 L/min × 29.6 min = 4736 mg, so qb = 4736/1500 = 3.16 mg/g.
  3. Whole area: add 9.0, 7.5, 5.25, 3.0, 1.4, 0.55, 0.2 and 0.05 min for the later intervals: ts = 56.55 min. Dye held at saturation = 80 × 2 × 56.55 = 9048 mg, qsat = 6.03 mg/g.
  4. Fraction of the capacity used at the break point = 4736/9048 = 52 %. LUB = 0.60 × (1 − 30/56.55) = 0.60 × 0.469 = 0.28 m.
  5. 1.2 m bed: ts doubles to 113.1 min and the LUB stays 0.28 m, so tb = 113.1 × (1.2 − 0.28)/1.2 = 86.5 min, and 77 % of the bed is used at the break point.

Answer. qb = 3.16 mg/g, qsat = 6.03 mg/g, LUB = 0.28 m; a 1.2 m bed reaches its break point at about 86 min instead of 30 min.

Exam traps

  • The loading at the break point is not C0Qtb/W (3.20 mg/g here): that assumes nothing at all left the bed before tb. Integrate the area above the curve.
  • An early break point with a slow, spread-out curve means a long LUB and a poorly used bed, not a well-used one.
  • A favourable isotherm sharpens the front; an unfavourable one spreads it, even with fast mass transfer.

6.4 Ion exchange: principles and equipment

Ion exchange swaps ions between a solution and a solid resin. The resin carries fixed charged groups balanced by loosely held counter-ions; ions from the water take their place and the counter-ions go into the water, equivalent for equivalent. A water softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium; a demineraliser swaps every cation for H+ and every anion for OH−, which combine to water. Columns of resin run through a cycle of service and regeneration, much like a fixed-bed adsorber.

You need: The column behaviour follows Batch and fixed-bed adsorption: breakthrough curves.

Resins and the exchange reaction

Most resins are beads of polystyrene cross-linked with divinylbenzene (about 8 %) and carrying ionic groups throughout the bead, not just on its outside. The groups decide the type:

ResinFixed groupRegenerated withNote
Strong acid cation−SO3− (sulfonic)NaCl (Na form) or HCl, H2SO4 (H form)works at any pH
Weak acid cation−COO− (carboxylic)acid, very efficientlytakes hardness linked to bicarbonate

Softening on a sodium-form strong acid resin is 2R–SO3Na + Ca2+ ⇌ (R–SO3)2Ca + 2Na+. Charge is conserved, so one Ca2+ releases two Na+: the number of ions in the water rises but the equivalents do not change. That is why ion-exchange quantities are counted in equivalents (eq, or meq = eq/1000), the moles of charge, and hardness is often quoted as mg/L of CaCO3 (50.0 mg per meq). The reaction is reversible, which is what lets a strong brine push the calcium back off.

Capacity and selectivity

Total capacity is the number of exchange groups: about 4.5–5 eq per kg of dry strong acid resin, or 1.8–2.0 eq per litre of wet settled bed. Operating capacity is what a column actually delivers between regenerations, usually 0.8–1.4 eq/L, because the bed is switched at breakthrough and regeneration is never complete.

A resin prefers some ions over others. In dilute solution the rules are: higher charge wins, and among equal charges the ion with the smaller hydrated size (larger atomic number in a group) wins. For a strong acid resin: Ba2+ > Pb2+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+ > NH4+ > Na+ > H+. For a strong base resin: SO42− > NO3− > Cl− > HCO3− > OH−. Selectivity is put as a number by the selectivity coefficient KA/B = (yAxB)/(xAyB) for two ions of equal charge, with y the fraction on the resin and x in the solution.

So calcium is held in preference to sodium in a softener, yet a 10 % brine, with sodium hundreds of times more concentrated, drives it off: concentration overcomes selectivity during regeneration. In concentrated solution the preference for divalent ions also weakens, which helps.

The service cycle

  1. Service: raw water flows down through the bed; an exchange zone moves down as in a fixed-bed adsorber, and the run stops when the outlet hardness or conductivity rises.
  2. Backwash: water flows up to expand the bed, lift out trapped dirt and regrade the beads.
  3. Regeneration: regenerant (8–12 % NaCl, 4–6 % HCl or 2–4 % NaOH) passes slowly through the bed; more than the stoichiometric amount is needed, often two to three times, to push the reaction back.
  4. Rinse: water washes out the excess regenerant before the next service run.

In co-current regeneration the regenerant flows down like the water, so the bottom of the bed, the last to meet the water, is regenerated worst and some leakage results. In counter-current regeneration the regenerant flows up, so the cleanest resin sits at the outlet; leakage is lower and less regenerant is used.

Techniques, equipment and uses

  • Batch: resin stirred in a tank and filtered off, used for small lots and recovery of a valuable ion.
  • Fixed bed columns: the usual equipment, pressure vessels with a distributor at the top and a collector under the bed, run in pairs so one serves while the other regenerates.
  • Two-bed demineraliser: a hydrogen-form cation column turns salts into acids; a degasser strips the CO2 from bicarbonate; a hydroxide-form anion column removes the acids, leaving water.
  • Mixed bed: cation and anion beads mixed in one vessel act like many two-bed pairs in series and give water of very low conductivity for boilers and electronics; the beads are separated by backwashing for regeneration.
  • Continuous counter-current units: resin moved in pulses round a loop of service, regeneration and rinse sections, for large steady flows.

Applications go beyond water: recovering chromium and precious metals from plating rinses, extracting uranium from leach liquors, decolourising sugar, purifying antibiotics such as streptomycin, removing radioactive caesium in nuclear plants, and acting as solid acid catalysts.

6.5 Crystallization: solubility curves and supersaturation

Crystallization makes a pure solid from a solution in one step: sugar from syrup, salt from brine, fertilisers, most drugs. A crystal grows only from a solution that holds more solute than it can hold at equilibrium, so the solubility curve says how much product can be won and supersaturation says how fast it forms.

Crystals and their geometry

A crystal is a solid whose atoms, ions or molecules are packed in a repeating three-dimensional lattice. Because the pattern repeats, a crystal is bounded by flat faces meeting at fixed angles, and the angles between corresponding faces are the same in every crystal of a substance. Crystals are sorted by the symmetry of their lattice into seven crystal systems: cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, trigonal and hexagonal. Sodium chloride is cubic; sucrose is monoclinic.

The habit is the outward shape, needles, plates or blocks. One system can give several habits, because the relative growth rates of the faces change with solvent, supersaturation and impurities: sodium chloride grows as cubes from pure water but as octahedra with a little urea present. Habit matters to the plant, since needles break and filter slowly while compact crystals wash and flow well. Some substances also form more than one lattice, called polymorphs (calcite and aragonite are both CaCO3); for drugs the polymorph sets solubility and must be controlled.

A crystal's size is given by one characteristic length L, usually the sieve opening it just passes. Its volume and surface are then kvL³ and kaL², with shape factors kv = 1, ka = 6 for a cube and kv = π/6, ka = π for a sphere.

Solid–liquid equilibrium: solubility curves

Solubility is the concentration of a saturated solution, one in equilibrium with the solid. It is quoted per 100 parts of solvent (g per 100 g water) so that it does not change as solute leaves, and it depends mainly on temperature. The plot of solubility against temperature is the solubility curve. Most salts, such as KNO3, are much more soluble hot, so cooling crystallizes them. Sodium chloride hardly changes with temperature, so it is crystallized by evaporating water. A few salts, such as anhydrous Na2SO4 above 32.4 °C, are less soluble hot (inverse solubility) and deposit as scale on hot surfaces.

Many salts crystallize as hydrates, with water inside the lattice: Na2SO4·10H2O below 32.4 °C, MgSO4·7H2O, CuSO4·5H2O. Each hydrate has its own branch of the curve, and the break at which one form changes into another is a transition point. For a solution of one salt in water the full diagram also shows the ice line, meeting the solubility curve at the eutectic, below which ice and salt freeze out together.

Supersaturation and the metastable zone

ΔC = C − C*      S = C/C*      σ = S − 1 = ΔC/C*

C is the actual concentration and C* the solubility at the same temperature, both in the same units. ΔC is the concentration driving force, S the supersaturation ratio and σ the relative supersaturation. A saturated solution has S = 1, σ = 0.

Miers found that a clean solution can be cooled below its saturation temperature without crystals appearing. Above the solubility curve there is a metastable zone, where existing crystals grow but new ones hardly form, and beyond the supersolubility curve a labile zone, where nuclei appear on their own. The supersolubility curve is not a fixed line like the solubility curve: it depends on the cooling rate, agitation and impurities. Good crystallizers run inside the metastable zone with seed crystals, so the solute goes onto growing crystals instead of forming showers of fines.

Supersaturation is created in four ways: by cooling a solution whose solubility falls with temperature; by evaporating solvent; by adding a second solvent or a salt that lowers the solubility (antisolvent or salting-out crystallization); and by a reaction that forms a sparingly soluble product (precipitation). Vacuum crystallization combines the first two: the feed boils at low pressure, and the evaporation both removes solvent and cools the solution.

Yield by mass balance

With an ideal stage, the mother liquor leaves saturated at the final temperature. The balances are on total mass, solute and water:

F = P + M + V      solute: F xF = P xP + M s/(100 + s)

F feed, P crystals, M mother liquor, V water evaporated (kg). xF is the mass fraction of solute in the feed; xP is the mass fraction of anhydrous solute in the crystals (1 for anhydrous crystals, Msalt/Mhydrate for a hydrate); s is the final solubility in g per 100 g water. The water balance closes the set.

Evaporation and hydrate formation both raise the yield, because they leave less water to hold solute in the mother liquor. Real yields are lower if the mother liquor leaves still supersaturated.

6.6 Nucleation and crystal growth

A supersaturated solution gets rid of its excess solute in two ways: by forming new crystals, nucleation, and by depositing on crystals already present, growth. Both are driven by supersaturation, but nucleation rises far more steeply with it. The balance between the two decides the product: many small crystals when nucleation wins, few large ones when growth wins. The designer controls that balance through supersaturation, seeding, agitation and purity.

You need supersaturation S = C/C* and the metastable zone from Crystallization: solubility curves and supersaturation.

Kinds of nucleation

  • Primary homogeneous: nuclei form from the clear solution alone. It needs high supersaturation, beyond the supersolubility curve, and gives a sudden shower of fine crystals.
  • Primary heterogeneous: nuclei form on foreign surfaces, dust, the vessel wall or the stirrer. The surface lowers the energy barrier, so it starts at lower supersaturation. Industrial solutions always contain such particles.
  • Secondary: new crystals come from crystals already present, as fragments knocked off by the impeller or by other crystals (contact nucleation) or as a layer swept off a growing face. It works inside the metastable zone and is the main source of nuclei in a stirred industrial crystallizer.

Classical nucleation theory

A tiny cluster of solute molecules has two energy terms. Forming its volume releases energy, because the solute is supersaturated; forming its surface costs energy, the surface energy γ times the area. For a spherical cluster of radius r:

ΔG = 4πr²γ − (4/3)πr³ (kT ln S)/vm      r* = 2γvm/(kT ln S)      ΔG* = 16πγ³vm²/[3(kT ln S)²]

γ is the crystal–solution surface energy (J/m²), vm the volume of one molecule in the crystal (m³), k = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K, T in K, S the supersaturation ratio. ΔG rises to a maximum ΔG* at the critical radius r*: smaller clusters tend to redissolve, larger ones grow. The nucleation rate is J = A exp(−ΔG*/kT), with A of the order of 1030 nuclei/(m³ s).

Since ΔG* varies as 1/(ln S)², a modest rise in S lowers the barrier enormously and J jumps by many powers of ten. That is the meaning of the supersolubility curve: below it J is negligible, just above it nucleation is violent. It also explains why small crystals are more soluble than large ones (the Gibbs–Thomson effect): a crystal smaller than r* at a given S dissolves. In practice nucleation is fitted by a power law, B = kbσb, with b usually 2–9, larger than the growth order.

Crystal growth

Growth needs two steps in series. Solute diffuses from the bulk solution through the boundary layer to the crystal face, driven by C − Ci; it then is built into the lattice at the face, the surface integration step, driven by Ci − C*. Integration happens best at steps and kinks on the face: a screw dislocation provides a step that never runs out (the BCF spiral), and at higher supersaturation new layers start as islands on the face (birth and spread).

N = kd(C − Ci) = kr(Ci − C*)      for first-order steps: N = K(C − C*), 1/K = 1/kd + 1/kr

N is the mass deposited per unit area per unit time (kg/m² s), kd the mass-transfer coefficient (m/s), kr the surface rate constant (m/s). Like resistances in series, the larger 1/k controls. The linear growth rate is G = dL/dt; an overall fit G = kgσg with g between 1 and 2 is usual.

McCabe's ΔL law states that geometrically similar crystals of the same substance in the same solution grow by the same length ΔL in the same time, whatever their size: G does not depend on L. A seed of 0.1 mm and one of 0.3 mm both grow by 0.4 mm. Very small crystals (slower) and growth-rate dispersion between crystals of one size are known exceptions, but the law underlies seeding calculations and the MSMPR model.

6.7 Crystallizers: classification, yield and heat effects

A crystallizer is a vessel that creates supersaturation, holds the crystals in suspension while they grow, and removes or supplies the heat involved. Crystallizers are classified first by how they create supersaturation, then by how they keep the crystals moving and control their size. Their design rests on two balances: a mass balance for the yield and an energy balance for the cooling or heating duty, including the heat released as the crystals form.

You need the yield balance from Crystallization: solubility curves and supersaturation.

Classification

By the method of creating supersaturation there are four classes: cooling crystallizers, for solutes whose solubility falls steeply with temperature; evaporative crystallizers, which boil off solvent with heat supplied by steam, for flat-solubility salts such as NaCl; vacuum (adiabatic flash) crystallizers, in which hot feed enters a vessel held at low pressure, boils, and is cooled and concentrated by its own evaporation with no heat exchange surface; and precipitation crystallizers, where a reaction or an added antisolvent or salt (salting-out) lowers the solubility. Each may run batch or continuously.

The mixture of crystals and mother liquor in a crystallizer is the magma. Its solids content, often 20–40 %, is kept high enough to give a large crystal surface for growth, which keeps the supersaturation low.

Yield and purity

The yield follows from the solute and water balances with saturated mother liquor, as for one stage: crystals P = solute in feed − solute left in the mother liquor, with evaporated water and hydrate water taken out of the liquor first. In a vacuum crystallizer the water evaporated is not chosen: it is fixed by the energy balance, so the mass and energy balances must be solved together.

Crystals themselves are nearly pure, because impurities rarely fit the lattice. The product's impurity comes mostly from mother liquor left on and between the crystals: a cake holding 5 % of its mass as mother liquor with 2 % impurity carries 0.1 % impurity when dried. Large, even crystals hold less liquor and wash better, which is one reason size control matters. Washing on the filter or centrifuge, and recrystallizing when needed, raise purity.

Heat effects

Most salts release heat as they crystallize from solution (the heat of crystallization, roughly the negative of the heat of solution). In a cooling crystallizer this heat must be removed together with the sensible heat of the feed:

cooling: Q = F cp(T1 − T2) + P λc      vacuum: V λv = F cp(T1 − T2) + P λc

Q is the heat removed (kJ/h); F the feed (kg/h) of heat capacity cp, cooled from T1 to T2; P the crystals formed (kg/h); λc the heat of crystallization (kJ/kg, released); V the water evaporated and λv its latent heat at the crystallizer temperature. In an evaporative crystallizer the steam supplies V λv less the heat of crystallization and plus any sensible heating. More exact work uses an enthalpy–concentration chart.

In a cooling crystallizer the heat goes to cooling water through a jacket or coil, sized by Q = UAΔTlm. The cold wall is the most supersaturated place in the vessel and grows scale, so the temperature difference across it is kept small, often below 10 °C. In a vacuum crystallizer there is no cold surface at all: the boiling solution sets its own temperature through the pressure held by the condenser and ejector.

Design of batch and continuous crystallizers

A batch crystallizer is designed around its cooling (or evaporation) programme. Natural cooling is fastest at the start, when the solution is hottest, which drives supersaturation into the labile zone and makes fines. A controlled programme cools slowly at first and faster later, as the crystal surface grows, keeping supersaturation steady in the metastable zone; seeds are added just after saturation is reached. The batch time is set by the growth rate and the size wanted (ΔL = Gt).

A continuous crystallizer is sized by residence time: volume = volumetric feed × τ, with τ chosen so the dominant product size reaches the target, as worked out in Crystal size distribution and the MSMPR crystallizer. The heat duty sets the exchanger or condenser, and the magma density sets the circulation rate. Fines destruction (heating a stream of fines to dissolve them) and classified product removal are added when larger, narrower sizes are needed.

6.8 Crystal size distribution and the MSMPR crystallizer

A continuous crystallizer does not make crystals of one size. New nuclei appear all the time and each crystal grows for as long as it happens to stay in the vessel, so the product has a spread of sizes, the crystal size distribution (CSD). The CSD decides how the product filters, washes, flows and sells. The MSMPR model (mixed suspension, mixed product removal) turns a population balance into one straight-line plot that gives the growth rate, the nucleation rate and the whole CSD from a single sieve analysis.

You need nucleation, growth and the ΔL law from Nucleation and crystal growth.

Describing a size distribution

A sieve analysis gives the mass of crystals between pairs of sieve openings. It is plotted as the cumulative mass fraction undersize against size, or converted to numbers. The basic measure for the model is the population density n = ΔN/ΔL: the number of crystals per unit size range per unit volume of magma, in number/(m³ mm). A mass ΔM on a size band of mean size L and width ΔL contains ΔN = ΔM/(ρkvL³) crystals, so n = ΔM/(ρkvL³ΔL).

The moments of n(L) give the totals: ∫n dL is the number of crystals, ∫nL² dL times ka their surface and ∫nL³ dL times ρkv their mass. Sizes are summed up by a mean (number mean, or the mass-weighted mean), by the dominant size LD where the mass distribution peaks, and by the spread, the coefficient of variation.

The MSMPR population balance

Assumptions: steady state; the magma is perfectly mixed, so every point has the same supersaturation and CSD; the product is withdrawn with the same CSD as the vessel; the feed holds no crystals; there is no breakage or agglomeration; and growth obeys the ΔL law, G independent of L.

Take a magma volume V and the crystals between sizes L and L + ΔL. In a time Δt, crystals grow into the band across L, GnVΔt, and out of it across L + ΔL, (Gn)L+ΔLVΔt; the product stream Q removes nQΔLΔt. At steady state in = out. Dividing by ΔLΔt and letting ΔL → 0 gives V G dn/dL = −Qn. With the residence time τ = V/Q:

dn/dL = −n/(Gτ)   ⇒   n = n° e−L/(Gτ)      ln n = ln n° − L/(Gτ)

n° is the population density of nuclei (L → 0), G the linear growth rate and τ the mean residence time. A plot of ln n against L is a straight line of slope −1/(Gτ) and intercept ln n°. The nucleation rate is B° = n°G (nuclei per m³ per unit time).

Results that follow

NT = n°Gτ      MT = 6kvρ n°(Gτ)4      LD = 3Gτ

NT is the number of crystals per unit volume, MT the magma density (mass of crystals per unit volume; use n° per metre with L in metres), kv the volume shape factor and ρ the crystal density. The number mean size is Gτ, the dominant (peak-mass) size 3Gτ, the mass-weighted mean 4Gτ and the mass median 3.67Gτ.

With x = L/(Gτ), the mass fraction of product finer than L is W(x) = 1 − e−x(1 + x + x²/2 + x³/6). Every MSMPR product has the same shape of distribution, with a coefficient of variation of 50 % by mass; only Gτ, the scale, can be changed.

Plotting ln B° against ln G from runs at several residence times gives the kinetic order i in B° = kNGi, with i = b/g. If the magma density is fixed by the feed, MT ∝ Gi−1(Gτ)4, which gives G ∝ τ−4/(i+3) and LD ∝ τ(i−1)/(i+3). With i > 1, a longer residence time (lower supersaturation) gives larger crystals, but only weakly: for i = 3, doubling τ raises LD by 21/3 = 1.26.

Using and testing the model

To design, choose τ and read G and B° from the kinetics; the vessel volume is Qτ. A plot that curves upward at small sizes shows fines from breakage or size-dependent growth; one that curves downward at large sizes shows classified removal of product. Fines destruction and classified product removal are deliberately used to move away from the MSMPR distribution and narrow the product.

6.9 Membrane separations: reverse osmosis, electrodialysis and ultrafiltration

A membrane is a thin barrier that lets some species through faster than others. The feed splits into a permeate, which has passed through, and a retentate (concentrate), which has not. Pressure drives water through reverse osmosis and ultrafiltration membranes; an electric field drives ions through the charged membranes of electrodialysis. Membranes need no phase change and no added chemicals.

Membranes, terms and modules

flux J = permeate volume/(area × time)      rejection R = 1 − Cp/Cf      recovery r = Qp/Qf

J is usually in L/(m² h); Cp and Cf are the solute concentrations in permeate and feed; Qp and Qf the permeate and feed flows.

Solid membranes are dense or porous films of polymer (cellulose acetate, polyamide, polysulfone) or ceramic. Most are asymmetric: a skin 0.1–1 µm thick does the separating while a thick porous layer gives strength. RO membranes are usually thin-film composites, a polyamide skin on a polysulfone support. Liquid membranes are a thin layer of an organic liquid between two aqueous phases. In a supported liquid membrane the liquid fills the pores of a porous film; in an emulsion liquid membrane it forms the skin of small droplets. A carrier dissolved in the liquid picks up a metal ion on one side and releases it on the other (facilitated transport), so copper or phenol can be pumped out of a dilute stream into a strong one.

Membranes are packed into modules: plate-and-frame, tubular (for dirty feeds), spiral-wound (flat sheets with spacers rolled round a permeate tube, the usual RO module) and hollow fibre (thousands of fine tubes, the largest area per volume). Pressure-driven processes are ranked by pore size: microfiltration (0.1–10 µm, bacteria and suspended solids), ultrafiltration (about 1–100 nm, proteins and colloids), nanofiltration (divalent ions, sugars) and reverse osmosis (nearly everything except water).

Osmosis and reverse osmosis

If a membrane permeable only to water separates pure water from a salt solution, water flows into the solution: osmosis. The pressure that would have to be applied to the solution to stop that flow is its osmotic pressure π. Applying more than π reverses the flow and pushes water out of the solution: reverse osmosis (RO).

π = iCRT      Jw = Aw(ΔP − Δπ)      Js = B(Cf − Cp)

π (Pa) for a dilute solution: i ions per formula unit (2 for NaCl), C in mol/m³, R = 8.314 J/(mol K), T in K. Jw is the water flux, Aw the water permeability, ΔP the pressure difference across the membrane and Δπ the osmotic pressure difference between the feed side and the permeate. Js is the salt flux with salt permeability B. Raising ΔP raises Jw but not Js, so a higher pressure gives purer permeate.

As water leaves, the salt left behind piles up at the membrane surface: concentration polarisation. The surface concentration exceeds the bulk, raising the local π and the salt leakage and encouraging scale; spacers and high cross-flow velocity keep it down. Brackish water (1000–5000 mg/L) is treated at 10–20 bar; seawater (about 35 000 mg/L, π near 27 bar) needs 55–70 bar.

Ultrafiltration

Ultrafiltration membranes have real pores and separate by size, at 1–10 bar. They are rated by their molecular weight cut-off (MWCO): the molar mass of a solute that is 90 % rejected, from 1 to 500 kDa. Salts and sugars pass; proteins, colloids, oil droplets, bacteria and viruses are held. The osmotic pressure of large molecules is small, so ultrafiltration works at low pressure.

The held macromolecules collect at the surface until they form a gel layer of fixed concentration Cg. Beyond that, extra pressure only thickens the gel, and the flux levels off at J = k ln(Cg/Cb), where k is the mass-transfer coefficient and Cb the bulk concentration; flux is raised by stirring or faster cross-flow, not by pressure. Uses: whey protein concentration, juice clarification, electrocoat paint recovery and pretreatment before RO.

Electrodialysis

An electrodialysis (ED) stack has many thin compartments between two electrodes, separated alternately by cation-exchange membranes (pass only cations) and anion-exchange membranes (pass only anions). Under the applied voltage cations move towards the cathode and anions towards the anode. In every second compartment the ions leave and cannot be replaced, so it becomes the diluate (product water); in the neighbours they arrive and are trapped, so those become the concentrate. A cation membrane, a diluate, an anion membrane and a concentrate together make one cell pair.

I = zFQΔC/(εN) energy = VIt, per m³: VI/Q

I is the current (A); z the charge of the ion (1 for NaCl); F = 96 485 C/mol; Q the diluate flow (m³/s); ΔC the drop in concentration (mol/m³); ε the current efficiency; N the number of cell pairs; V the stack voltage. The current is limited by the limiting current density, at which the ion concentration at the membrane falls to zero and water starts to split.

ED removes only ions, so uncharged matter stays in the product. Its energy rises with the salt removed, so it suits brackish water and is used to desalt whey and to concentrate brine for salt. Reversing the polarity every few minutes (EDR) cleans scale off the membranes.

Worked example

Question. An RO unit treats 1000 L/h of brackish water with 2000 mg/L NaCl at 25 °C and 75 % recovery; the permeate holds 100 mg/L. The membrane has Aw = 3.0 L/(m² h bar) and runs at ΔP = 15 bar.   4. Mean feed-side π = (1.70 + 6.53)/2 = 4.11 bar; Δπ = 4.11 − 0.085 = 4.03 bar. Net = 15 − 4.03 = 10.97 bar.
  5. Jw = 3.0 × 10.97 = 32.9 L/(m² h). Area = 750/32.9 = 22.8 m².

Takeaways

  1. Adsorption holds solute on pore walls; area, pore size and the type of force (physical or chemical) set how much a solid holds and how it is regenerated.
  2. An isotherm is q against the equilibrium C at one temperature; Langmuir has a plateau qm, Freundlich does not, and both are fitted from straight-line plots.
  3. Counter-current contact loads the adsorbent near equilibrium with the feed, so it needs far less adsorbent than one stage or cross-current stages.
  4. A fixed bed is switched at its break point; the area above the breakthrough curve gives the solute held and the length of unused bed sets the scale-up.
  5. Ion exchange swaps equivalents: the bed is sized on operating capacity, and regeneration needs two to three times the stoichiometric chemical.
  6. Crystallization yield comes from a solute and water balance with saturated mother liquor; evaporation and hydrate water raise it.
  7. Nucleation rises far more steeply with supersaturation than growth does, so low, steady supersaturation with seeds gives large crystals.
  8. Crystallizer duty includes the heat of crystallization; a vacuum crystallizer finds its own evaporation from the energy balance.
  9. In an MSMPR crystallizer ln n is linear in L with slope −1/(Gτ), and the dominant product size is 3Gτ.
  10. RO pushes water against Δπ, UF sieves macromolecules and stops gaining flux at the gel point, and ED pulls ions through charged membranes with a current set by the salt removed.

Understand Distinguish physical adsorption from chemisorption. Describe the commonly used industrial adsorbents, and explain how surface area and pore structure decide the capacity and rate of an adsorbent. 10 marks

Points to cover. Definition of adsorption, adsorbent, adsorbate; Physical adsorption and chemisorption compared; Surface area and pore classes; Activated carbon, silica gel, alumina, zeolites, carbon molecular sieve, polymer adsorbents; Choice of adsorbent for a duty.

Model answer.

  1. Adsorption. Adsorption is the holding of a solute (the adsorbate) from a gas or a liquid on the surface of a solid (the adsorbent). Unlike absorption, the solute does not enter the bulk of the solid; it stays on the walls of the pores. Adsorption releases heat, the heat of adsorption, and is reversed by heating or by lowering the pressure or concentration, which is how adsorbents are regenerated.
  2. Two types of adsorption.
    PropertyPhysical adsorptionChemisorption
    Forcevan der Waalschemical bond
    Heat20–40 kJ/mol, near the heat of condensation80–400 kJ/mol, like a reaction
    Specificitylowhigh
    Layersseveral possibleone at most
    Temperaturefalls as T risesneeds activation; may rise with T at first
    Reversibilityeasyoften difficult
    Usedrying, purification, solvent recovery, air separationcatalysis, H2S removal on ZnO
  3. Why separations use physical adsorption. An adsorbent in a plant is loaded and regenerated thousands of times, so the force must be weak enough to reverse by a modest change in temperature or pressure. Chemisorption is the first step of heterogeneous catalysis rather than a separation tool.
  4. Surface area. The loading q (mass held per mass of adsorbent) grows with the area available, so adsorbents are made with huge internal area: activated carbon 500–1500 m²/g. As a check of scale, 1000 m²/g covered by a single layer of benzene molecules (0.40 nm² each) holds 2.5 × 1021 molecules, 0.32 g per g of carbon.
  5. Pore structure. Micropores (under 2 nm) give most of the area and hold small molecules strongly but are slow to enter and may exclude large molecules; mesopores (2–50 nm) give access and some area; macropores (over 50 nm) are the roads into the grain. The rate of uptake is set by film diffusion round the grain and pore diffusion inside it, so a good adsorbent combines micropores with a network of larger pores, and smaller grains are faster but give a higher pressure drop.
  6. Activated carbon: charred coal, wood or coconut shell activated with steam; non-polar, so it holds organic molecules even from wet streams; used for colour, taste and odour, trace organics in water, and solvent vapour recovery.
  7. Silica gel and activated alumina: porous SiO2 (300–800 m²/g) and Al2O3 (200–400 m²/g); polar, so they hold water and are used to dry air and gases; alumina also removes fluoride from water.
  8. Zeolites (molecular sieves): crystalline aluminosilicates with uniform openings of 0.3–1 nm. 3A admits water but not ethanol and dries ethanol; 4A, 5A and 13X admit larger molecules. Zeolite 5A or 13X separates air by equilibrium: N2, with its larger quadrupole, is held more strongly by the cations, so O2-rich gas leaves the bed.
  9. Carbon molecular sieve: very narrow uniform pores in which O2 diffuses faster than N2; a short pressure-swing cycle gives N2-rich product, a rate separation.
  10. Polymeric adsorbents: porous resin beads without ionic groups, used for antibiotics and phenols; ion-exchange resins look similar but exchange ions instead.
  11. Choosing. Polar solids for water and polar molecules, carbon for organics, zeolites where molecular size or a strong electric field decides the separation. Check: every adsorbent named above is regenerated by heat or pressure swing, which is possible only because the holding is physical.
Understand Derive the Langmuir isotherm, stating its assumptions. Give the Freundlich isotherm, show how the constants of each are found from data, and explain the effect of pressure and temperature on adsorption equilibrium. 12 marks

Points to cover. Assumptions; Rate balance and derivation; Limits at low and high C; Linear plot and constants; Freundlich equation and log plot; Effect of partial pressure and temperature; Favourable and unfavourable shapes.

Model answer.

  1. Assumptions (Langmuir). The surface has a fixed number of identical sites; each site holds one molecule; held molecules do not interact; adsorption stops when a single layer is complete; at equilibrium the rate of landing equals the rate of leaving.
  2. Derivation for a gas at partial pressure p. Let θ be the fraction of sites occupied.
    rate of adsorption  = ka p (1 − θ)      (molecules land only on empty sites)
rate of desorption  = kd θ             (molecules leave only from full sites)
equilibrium: ka p (1 − θ) = kd θ
θ = b p/(1 + b p),   b = ka/kd
q = qm θ = qm b p/(1 + b p)
for a liquid: q = qm KL C/(1 + KL C)
  3. Limits. At low concentration KLC ≪ 1 and q ≈ qmKLC, a straight Henry's law line. At high concentration KLC ≫ 1 and q → qm, the monolayer. Half the sites are full at C = 1/KL.
  4. Constants from data. Rearranged, C/q = 1/(qmKL) + C/qm. Plot C/q against C: slope 1/qm, intercept 1/(qmKL). Example: data C = 0.5, 1, 2, 5 mg/L, q = 17.1, 24.0, 30.0, 35.3 mg/g give C/q = 0.0292, 0.0417, 0.0667, 0.1416 g/L; the line has slope 0.02498 and intercept 0.01671, so qm = 40.0 mg/g and KL = 0.02498/0.01671 = 1.50 L/mg. A straight plot confirms the model.
  5. Freundlich. q = KFC1/n, an empirical equation that fits heterogeneous surfaces whose sites differ in strength. Taking logs, log q = log KF + (1/n) log C: a plot of log q against log C has slope 1/n and intercept log KF. For the data above, 1/n = 0.311 and KF = 22.7; the points curve slightly, so Langmuir fits better. Freundlich has no plateau and must not be extrapolated (it predicts 46.3 mg/g at 10 mg/L against Langmuir's 37.5).
  6. Shape. An isotherm concave to the C axis (1/n < 1, or RL = 1/(1 + KLC0) between 0 and 1) is favourable: much is held even from dilute fluid, and fixed-bed fronts stay sharp. A linear isotherm is borderline; a concave-up one is unfavourable.
  7. Pressure. For a gas, q depends on the partial pressure of the adsorbate p = yP, not the total pressure. Raising the total pressure at a fixed mole fraction raises p and q; adding inert gas does not. Pressure-swing adsorption loads at high pressure and regenerates at low pressure.
  8. Temperature. Physical adsorption is exothermic, so at a fixed p or C a hotter bed holds less; constants belong to the temperature at which they were measured. Two isotherms give the heat of adsorption from the pressures needed for the same loading: if 0.10 bar at 25 °C and 0.40 bar at 60 °C give the same q, ΔHads = R ln 4/(1/298.15 − 1/333.15) = 11.53/3.523 × 10−4 = 32.7 kJ/mol, typical of physical adsorption. Temperature-swing adsorption loads cold and regenerates hot.
  9. Check. With the fitted constants, q at C = 2 mg/L is 40 × 3/4 = 30.0 mg/g, the measured value.
Understand Explain the principle of ion exchange. Describe cation and anion exchange resins, selectivity, the service and regeneration cycle, and the techniques and equipment used, with applications. 12 marks

Points to cover. Exchange reaction and equivalents; Resin structure and types; Capacity and selectivity; Service, backwash, regeneration, rinse; Co- and counter-current regeneration; Batch, fixed bed, two-bed, mixed bed, continuous; Applications.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. An ion-exchange resin carries fixed ionic groups throughout its beads, balanced by mobile counter-ions. Ions from the solution replace the counter-ions, which go into the solution, equivalent for equivalent, so the solution stays electrically neutral. Softening on a sodium-form resin: 2R–SO3Na + Ca2+ ⇌ (R–SO3)2Ca + 2Na+. One Ca2+ releases two Na+: the ion count doubles, the equivalents stay equal. The reaction is reversible, which allows regeneration.
  2. Resins. Beads of polystyrene cross-linked with about 8 % divinylbenzene. Strong acid cation resins carry −SO3− and work at any pH; weak acid resins carry −COO− and regenerate very efficiently. Strong base anion resins carry quaternary ammonium groups and remove even silica and CO2; weak base resins carry tertiary amines and take only strong acids.
  3. Capacity. Total capacity is the number of groups (about 1.8–2.0 eq per litre of strong acid resin); the operating capacity actually used between regenerations is lower, 0.8–1.4 eq/L, and is what sizes a bed.
  4. Selectivity. In dilute solution a resin prefers ions of higher charge and, at equal charge, smaller hydrated size: Ba2+ > Pb2+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+ > NH4+ > Na+ > H+ for cation resins and SO42− > NO3− > Cl− > HCO3− > OH− for strong base resins. The selectivity coefficient is KA/B = yAxB/(xAyB). A concentrated regenerant overcomes the preference, which is why 10 % brine strips calcium.
  5. Cycle. (1) Service: water flows down; an exchange zone moves down the bed as in fixed-bed adsorption, and the run ends when hardness or conductivity at the outlet rises. (2) Backwash: upflow lifts the bed, removes dirt and regrades the beads. (3) Regeneration: 8–12 % NaCl, 4–6 % HCl or 2–4 % NaOH passed slowly, usually two to three times the stoichiometric amount. (4) Rinse: excess regenerant washed out.
  6. Co- and counter-current regeneration. In co-current regeneration the regenerant flows down like the water and the outlet end of the bed is regenerated worst, causing leakage. In counter-current regeneration the regenerant enters at the outlet end, so the cleanest resin meets the treated water last; leakage and chemical use fall.
  7. Techniques and equipment. Batch contact in a stirred tank for small lots. Fixed-bed pressure columns with distributors and collectors, run in pairs so one serves while the other regenerates. Two-bed demineraliser: H-form cation column, degasser to strip CO2, OH-form anion column. Mixed bed: cation and anion beads mixed in one column act as many exchangers in series and give water of very low conductivity; the beads are separated by backwashing for regeneration. Continuous counter-current units move resin in pulses round service, regeneration and rinse zones.
  8. Applications. Softening and demineralising boiler feed and process water; ultrapure water for electronics; recovery of chromium and precious metals from plating rinses; uranium from leach liquors; sugar decolourising; purification of antibiotics such as streptomycin; removal of radioactive caesium; solid acid catalysts.
  9. Check with numbers. 500 L/h of water with 4.0 meq/L hardness for 8 h is 16 eq; at 1.0 eq/L this needs 16 L of resin, and 150 g/L of salt is 2.4 kg, 2.57 times the stoichiometric 935 g.
Understand Explain solubility curves, supersaturation and Miers' supersaturation theory. List the methods of creating supersaturation and describe the kinds of nucleation. 10 marks

Points to cover. Solubility curve and its forms; Hydrates and transition points; ΔC, S, σ; Metastable and labile zones; Four methods of creating supersaturation; Primary homogeneous, heterogeneous and secondary nucleation.

Model answer.

  1. Solubility curve. Solubility is the concentration of a solution in equilibrium with the solid, usually in g of solute per 100 g of water, plotted against temperature. Most salts (KNO3) are much more soluble hot; NaCl hardly changes (about 36 to 39 g per 100 g water from 20 to 100 °C); a few, such as anhydrous Na2SO4 above 32.4 °C, are less soluble hot. A salt that forms hydrates has one branch per hydrate, meeting at transition points (Na2SO4·10H2O changes to Na2SO4 at 32.4 °C).
  2. Supersaturation. A solution holding more solute than the solubility C* is supersaturated. It is measured by ΔC = C − C*, the ratio S = C/C* or the relative value σ = S − 1. For example 45 g per 100 g water where C* = 30 gives ΔC = 15, S = 1.5, σ = 0.5. Supersaturation is the driving force for both nucleation and growth.
  3. Miers' theory. Miers showed that a clean solution can be cooled below its saturation temperature without crystallizing. He drew a supersolubility curve roughly parallel to the solubility curve. Between them lies the metastable zone, where crystals present grow but new nuclei rarely form; beyond the supersolubility curve lies the labile zone, where nuclei form on their own. Below the solubility curve the solution is unsaturated and crystals dissolve. The supersolubility curve is not fixed: faster cooling, stronger agitation and impurities move it.
  4. Example. A solution saturated at 60 °C at 40 g per 100 g water, cooled to 20 °C where the solubility is 20 g, would reach S = 2.0 if no crystals formed: deep in the labile zone, giving a shower of fines. With seeds added just after it becomes saturated, solute deposits on the seeds and the path stays in the metastable zone. At the end, 1000 kg of feed gives 285.7 − 142.9 = 142.9 kg of crystals.
  5. Creating supersaturation. (1) Cooling, for salts whose solubility falls with temperature. (2) Evaporating solvent, for flat-solubility salts such as NaCl. (3) Adding an antisolvent or a common-ion salt that lowers the solubility (salting-out). (4) A chemical reaction that forms a sparingly soluble product (precipitation). Vacuum crystallization combines cooling and evaporation by flashing hot feed at low pressure.
  6. Primary homogeneous nucleation. Nuclei form from the clear solution alone. A cluster must reach the critical radius r* = 2γvm/(kT ln S) to grow; the energy barrier ΔG* falls as 1/(ln S)², so the rate is negligible until S is high and then rises steeply.
  7. Primary heterogeneous nucleation. Nuclei form on dust, walls or stirrers, which lower the barrier, so it starts at lower supersaturation. Real solutions always contain such surfaces.
  8. Secondary nucleation. New crystals come from crystals already present: fragments from collisions with the impeller, walls or other crystals, or layers sheared off growing faces. It works within the metastable zone and supplies most of the nuclei in stirred industrial crystallizers.
  9. Check. In the example, S = 40/20 = 2.0 and the mass balance closes: 142.9 kg crystals + 857.1 kg mother liquor (714.3 water + 142.9 solute) = 1000 kg.

Analyze A continuous crystallizer makes a product that is too fine. Analyse how supersaturation, residence time, seeding, agitation, fines destruction and the type of crystallizer affect the crystal size, and recommend changes, using the MSMPR relations where you can. 13 marks

Points to cover. Why fines form: B and G orders; Effect of τ at fixed magma density; Seeding and supersaturation control; Agitation and secondary nucleation; Fines destruction and classified removal; Crystallizer choice; Recommendation.

Model answer.

  1. Starting point. The product size is set by the competition between nucleation, B° = kNσb, and growth, G = kgσg. Since b (2–9) is usually larger than g (1–2), a higher supersaturation raises nucleation more than growth, so the solute is shared among more crystals and each stays small. A fine product means either too high a supersaturation or too much secondary nucleation.
  2. Residence time. In an MSMPR crystallizer LD = 3Gτ. Writing B° = kNGi (i = b/g) and holding the magma density fixed gives G ∝ τ−4/(i+3) and LD ∝ τ(i−1)/(i+3). For i = 3, doubling τ (a vessel twice the size) raises LD by 21/3 = 1.26; for i = 2 only by 21/5 = 1.15. A longer τ helps, but it is an expensive way to gain size. Lowering supersaturation at a fixed τ by itself lowers G and does not enlarge the crystals unless nucleation falls further.
  3. Seeding and supersaturation control. In batch work, seeding just inside the metastable zone and cooling slowly at first keeps σ low and lets the seeds take up the solute; by the ΔL law, 1 kg of 0.1 mm seed grown to 0.5 mm becomes 125 kg of product. In continuous work, a high magma density (20–40 % solids) gives a large crystal surface that consumes supersaturation quickly and keeps σ low.
  4. Agitation. Agitation speeds the diffusion step of growth but also causes contact nucleation and breakage, which are the main source of nuclei in stirred vessels. A slow, large impeller, lower tip speed and a pump that does not crush crystals cut secondary nucleation; the stirring needs to be just enough to suspend the crystals.
  5. Fines destruction. Drawing liquor from a quiet zone where only small crystals are carried, heating it to dissolve them and returning it removes surplus nuclei. Fewer crystals share the same solute, so they grow larger. This is what a draft-tube baffle (DTB) crystallizer does. Classified product removal (withdrawing only the larger crystals, as in an Oslo unit) raises the size and narrows the spread further; both make the ln n plot depart from the MSMPR straight line.
  6. Crystallizer type. A forced-circulation unit with a high circulation rate through a pump and heater gives fine to medium crystals and high capacity; a DTB with fines destruction gives coarser crystals; an Oslo (classifying) crystallizer grows crystals in a fluidised bed with gentle supersaturated flow and gives the largest, most even product.
  7. Heat transfer and scaling. In cooling crystallizers a large wall temperature difference makes local high supersaturation, nucleation and scale. Keeping the wall ΔT below about 10 °C, or using vacuum cooling with no cold surface, helps.
  8. Recommendation. For a continuous duty now in a forced-circulation unit: convert to a DTB with fines destruction, keep magma density high, reduce impeller or pump tip speed, and only then consider a longer τ. Check: with i = 3 the MSMPR prediction for doubling τ (0.45 to 0.57 mm in the bank's MSMPR example) shows that size gains from τ alone are small, so nuclei removal is the stronger lever.

Evaluate A dye house discharges 20 m³/h of wastewater with 30 mg/L of dye, hardness 300 mg/L as CaCO3 and 1500 mg/L of dissolved salts. The plant wants to reuse it as low-pressure boiler feed. Propose and evaluate a treatment train using adsorption, ion exchange and membranes: choose the adsorbent and contactor, size the carbon use and the softener, decide where RO fits, and state what must be checked by tests. 16 marks

Points to cover. Targets; Pretreatment; Dye removal: adsorbent and contactor; Carbon use; Softening: resin and size; Salt removal: RO or demineraliser; Order of units; Wastes; Tests.

Model answer.

  1. Targets. Low-pressure boiler feed needs nearly zero hardness, low dissolved solids and no colour or organics that foam or foul. The water has three problems: dye (organic, coloured), hardness (scale) and dissolved salt.
  2. Pretreatment. Settling or filtration removes suspended solids that would blind carbon and resin beds; a sand or cartridge filter (or UF) protects the later units.
  3. Dye removal. Dyes are large organic molecules, so a non-polar, mesoporous activated carbon is chosen: its mesopores admit the dye and its area gives high capacity. Granular carbon in two fixed beds in lead–lag series is preferred to powdered carbon in stirred tanks: the first bed can be run to exhaustion while the second polishes, so the carbon is used nearly to its saturation loading and the effluent stays below 1 mg/L.
  4. Carbon use. Suppose column tests give a loading at the break point of 60 mg/g for this dye (to be measured).
    dye removed = 20 m³/h × (30 − 1) g/m³ = 580 g/h = 13.9 kg/day
carbon used = 13.9 kg/day/0.060 kg/kg = 232 kg/day
with lead–lag running each bed nearer saturation, less (perhaps 150–180 kg/day)
  5. Spent carbon is regenerated thermally off site; dye does not desorb easily with steam.
  6. Softening. Hardness 300 mg/L as CaCO3 = 300/50.04 = 6.0 meq/L. A sodium-form strong acid resin, regenerated with brine, two columns.
    load = 20 m³/h × 6.0 eq/m³ = 120 eq/h = 2880 eq/day
8 h run: 960 eq; at 1.0 eq/L operating capacity: 960 L of resin per column
salt at 2.5 × stoichiometric: 2.5 × 960 × 58.44 g = 140 kg per regeneration
  7. Salt removal. 1500 mg/L of dissolved solids is too high for boiler feed even after softening, which only swaps calcium for sodium. Two options: (i) RO after softening, at 75 % recovery giving 15 m³/h of permeate with about 2–3 % of the salt; softening first prevents CaCO3 and CaSO4 scale in the RO concentrate; (ii) a two-bed demineraliser on the full flow, which at 1500 mg/L (about 25 meq/L) would need very large resin volumes and regenerant, so it is used only to polish the RO permeate if a higher-pressure boiler is later added.
  8. Order. Filter → carbon (lead–lag) → softener → RO → (optional mixed bed). Carbon goes before the resin and the membrane because dye and other organics foul both; softening goes before RO to prevent scale.
  9. Wastes. Spent carbon (to regeneration), brine from the softener (about 140 kg of salt per regeneration plus hardness), and RO concentrate, 5 m³/h at about 6000 mg/L. These must be discharged or evaporated; their cost often decides between RO and a demineraliser.
  10. Evaluation. The train meets the target with mature equipment; carbon cost and concentrate disposal are the largest running costs. An alternative is nanofiltration in place of softener plus RO, which removes hardness and colour together but passes much of the monovalent salt; it would suit a less strict reuse.
  11. Tests needed. Isotherm and small-column breakthrough tests for the dye on the chosen carbon (to fix the 60 mg/g assumption and the LUB); the resin's operating capacity at this water's hardness and sodium; RO scaling indices and a pilot run for fouling; and the boiler's actual feedwater limits.
  12. Check. Carbon removes 13.9 kg/day of dye; the softener removes 2880 eq/day of hardness; RO delivers 15 m³/h, enough for a boiler raising about 14 t/h of steam.

Glossary

Adsorbate
The solute held on the surface of the adsorbent.
Adsorbent
The porous solid that holds the solute, such as activated carbon or a zeolite.
Break point
The time at which the outlet concentration reaches the allowed limit and the bed is switched.
Breakthrough curve
Outlet concentration against time (or volume) for a fixed bed.
Cell pair
In electrodialysis, one cation membrane, diluate, anion membrane and concentrate compartment.
Concentration polarisation
Build-up of rejected solute at the membrane surface.
Critical nucleus
The cluster size at the top of the free-energy barrier; smaller clusters dissolve.
Crystal habit
The outward shape of a crystal, which changes with growth conditions.
Dominant size
The size at which the mass distribution peaks; 3Gτ in an MSMPR crystallizer.
Isotherm
Equilibrium loading against fluid concentration or partial pressure at one temperature.
Labile zone
Region beyond the supersolubility curve where nuclei form spontaneously.
Length of unused bed
LUB = H(1 − tb/ts), the part of a bed not yet used at the break point.
Magma
The suspension of crystals in mother liquor inside a crystallizer.
Mass-transfer zone
The band of a fixed bed in which the fluid concentration falls from the feed value to nearly zero.
Metastable zone
Region above the solubility curve where crystals grow but few nuclei form.
Mixed bed
Cation and anion resins mixed in one column to make very pure water.
Molecular sieve
A zeolite or carbon with uniform pores close to molecular size.
Molecular weight cut-off
The molar mass of solute that an ultrafiltration membrane rejects to 90 %.
Monolayer capacity
qm, the Langmuir loading when every site holds one molecule.
Mother liquor
The saturated solution left after crystals are removed.
Operating capacity
The equivalents an ion-exchange bed delivers between regenerations.
Permeate, retentate
The stream that passes a membrane and the stream held back.
Polymorph
One of two or more crystal lattices of the same substance.
Population density
Number of crystals per unit size range per unit volume, n = dN/dL.
Regeneration
Restoring an adsorbent or resin by heat, pressure drop or a chemical (brine, acid, alkali).
Secondary nucleation
Formation of new crystals from crystals already present, by contact or shear.
Selectivity coefficient
KA/B = yAxB/(xAyB), the preference of a resin for ion A over B.
Supersaturation
Concentration above the solubility; ΔC = C − C*, S = C/C*.
