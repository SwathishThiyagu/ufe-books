6.1 Adsorption principles and adsorbents

Adsorption is a surface operation. Molecules of a solute leave a fluid and stick to the outside and inside surface of a porous solid. The fluid is the feed; the solid is the adsorbent; the stuck solute is the adsorbate. The solid is not dissolved and its structure barely changes. Industries use adsorption to remove colour from sugar, polish drinking water, recover solvent vapour, dry air and separate oxygen from air. The solid's surface area and pore size decide how much it can hold and how fast it works.

You need diffusion ideas from Unit 1, Molecular Diffusion and Diffusivity, and interphase transfer from Unit 2, Mass Transfer Coefficients and Interphase Transfer; 6.2 adds equilibrium isotherms.

The surface step

Adsorption happens at an interface. The solid offers a surface; the fluid touches it. A solute molecule that sticks is the adsorbate. The solid that holds it is the adsorbent. The word adsorption is not absorption: absorption takes solute into the bulk of another phase, as in gas absorption (see Unit 3). Adsorption keeps solute on the surface, though the surface may be inside pores.

Physical adsorption, also called van der Waals adsorption in some syllabi, uses weak forces. It is reversible, gives low heat, and gets weaker as temperature rises. Chemical adsorption, or chemisorption, forms a chemical bond. It is stronger, gives high heat, and may need activation energy. A real adsorbent can show both, depending on solute and conditions.

The path has three resistances. Solute first crosses the film of fluid around the particle: film diffusion. It then moves through pore mouths and along pore walls: pore diffusion. Finally it attaches to an active site: surface adsorption. Any step can be slow, but the last step supplies the capacity.

Area, pores and capacity

Capacity is reported as q, mass of adsorbate per mass of dry adsorbent, usually mg/g. A gram of good activated carbon can expose more than 1000 m² of surface; that is roughly a football field. The area is mostly inside pores. More area means more sites and higher q at the same fluid concentration.

Pore size sets a trade-off. Micropores are under 2 nm, mesopores are 2–50 nm, macropores are above 50 nm. Micropores give high area and hold small molecules strongly, but diffusion is slow. Macropores give fast access but little area. A useful solid has a pore network: large pores as roads, small pores as storage rooms.

Pressure and temperature change the equilibrium qualitatively. For a gas, higher pressure pushes more molecules onto the surface. For physical adsorption, higher temperature gives molecules more energy to escape, so loading falls. Chemisorption can behave differently because a bond must form. The full q against C curve is the isotherm; 6.2 treats its equations.

Common adsorbents

Activated carbon is made by heating carbon-rich material such as coconut shell or coal without air, then activating with steam or chemicals. It has high area, is non-polar, and adsorbs organic vapours, dyes, colour and many trace pollutants. It is used in water treatment and solvent recovery.

Silica gel is porous silicon dioxide. It is polar and holds water strongly, so it dries air and gases. Activated alumina is porous aluminium oxide; it also dries gases and removes fluoride and some ions. Zeolites are crystalline aluminosilicates with uniform pores. They act as molecular sieves: a molecule that is too large cannot enter. They separate oxygen from nitrogen and dry gases.

Ion-exchange resins are polymer beads with charged groups. They take ions from solution and release other ions of the same charge. The surface holds the ions, so the unit links to 6.4. Resins are not high-area carbons, but they are selective. Softening hard water and demineralising water use them.

Worked example

Question. 2.00 g activated carbon is shaken with 100 mL of dye solution. The initial dye concentration is 50 mg/L and the final equilibrium concentration is 10 mg/L. Find the adsorption capacity q in mg/g.

  1. List the data: C0 = 50 mg/L, Ce = 10 mg/L, V = 100 mL, W = 2.00 g.
  2. Convert the volume: V = 100 mL × (1 L / 1000 mL) = 0.100 L.
  3. Dye removed = (C0 − Ce)V = (50 − 10) mg/L × 0.100 L = 40 × 0.100 = 4.0 mg.
  4. q = removed mass ÷ adsorbent mass = 4.0 mg ÷ 2.00 g = 2.0 mg/g.

Answer. The adsorption capacity is q = 2.0 mg/g.

Exam traps

  • Using V = 100 directly with mg/L gives 4000 mg, not 4.0 mg; convert mL to L first.
  • Writing q as mg per litre of solution; q is mg per gram of dry adsorbent.
  • Mixing adsorption with absorption; adsorption is surface-held, absorption enters the bulk.
  • Assuming chemisorption is always reversible like physisorption; chemisorption forms bonds and may need heat to reverse.
  • Using the initial concentration as the equilibrium value; q needs the final equilibrium concentration Ce.

6.2 Adsorption equilibria: Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms

At a fixed temperature, a solid adsorbent in contact with a fluid reaches equilibrium when the rate of molecules sticking to the surface equals the rate leaving it. The equilibrium solid loading q (mg/g) plotted against fluid concentration C (mg/L) is the adsorption isotherm. It answers a practical question: for a given feed concentration, how much solute will a gram of carbon, silica or resin hold? Designers use it for drinking-water treatment, dye removal, sugar decolourisation and solvent recovery.

You need: 6.1 Adsorption principles and adsorbents for surface area, pores and capacity.

Langmuir isotherm

An isotherm is measured by shaking known masses of adsorbent with solutions of different initial concentration until equilibrium. q is the mass of solute adsorbed per mass of dry adsorbent (mg/g). C, often written Ce, is the remaining solute concentration in the fluid (mg/L). Each point is one equilibrium pair (C, q). The same idea applies to a gas; then use partial pressure p (Pa or bar) in place of C.

q = q_max K_L C / (1 + K_L C)

q is solid loading (mg solute per g dry solid); C is fluid concentration at equilibrium (mg/L); q_max is monolayer capacity (mg/g); K_L is affinity constant (L/mg).

Langmuir model assumes adsorption stops at one monolayer. Sites are identical, each holds one molecule, and adsorbed molecules do not interact. At low C many sites are free, so q rises almost linearly with C. At high C the monolayer fills and q approaches q_max. K_L measures how strongly the solute binds: a larger K_L reaches the plateau at a lower C.

C / q = 1 / (q_max K_L) + C / q_max

Plot C/q against C: slope = 1/q_max; intercept = 1/(q_max K_L).

To find the constants, plot C/q against C. The line has slope 1/q_max and intercept 1/(q_max K_L). Invert the slope to get q_max; divide intercept into 1/q_max to get K_L. If the data do not form a straight line, Langmuir is only an approximation.

Freundlich isotherm

Many real surfaces are heterogeneous: some sites bind strongly, others weakly. The Freundlich isotherm is empirical and allows that spread. It does not assume a monolayer and has no finite plateau.

q = K_F C^(1/n)

K_F is capacity constant ((mg/g)(L/mg)^(1/n)); n is dimensionless heterogeneity index; C in mg/L.

K_F is a capacity constant; its units are (mg/g)(L/mg)^(1/n) and depend on 1/n. n is a dimensionless number that describes how steeply loading rises with concentration. For favourable adsorption, 1/n lies between 0 and 1, so n > 1; the surface is easily loaded at low C and more C gives diminishing extra loading.

log q = log K_F + (1/n) log C

Plot log q against log C: slope = 1/n; intercept = log K_F.

Plot log q against log C. Slope = 1/n, intercept = log K_F. Use base 10 or natural logs consistently. Freundlich fits a limited concentration range; it cannot predict a true saturation capacity, so do not use it far outside the measured range.

Temperature, pressure and favourable shape

Physisorption is exothermic. At fixed C, raising temperature shifts equilibrium toward desorption, so q usually falls. The constants q_max, K_L, K_F and n are therefore tied to the temperature at which they were measured. Chemisorption can behave differently because a chemical bond must form; it may need heat and can increase with temperature, but it is not simple physical adsorption.

For a gas, loading depends on the partial pressure of the adsorbate, not total pressure. Raising total pressure at fixed mole fraction raises partial pressure and hence q. Raising only an inert gas pressure has little effect. A pressure-swing adsorber uses this: high pressure loads the bed, low pressure strips it.

An isotherm is favourable when it is concave downward: a large q is reached at small C. Such a curve gives a good driving force in a fixed bed and a sharp breakthrough, discussed in 6.3. A linear isotherm is intermediate; a concave-up isotherm is unfavourable and needs much more adsorbent.

Worked example

Question. At 25 °C, a dye on activated carbon follows the Langmuir isotherm with q_max = 40 mg/g and K_L = 1.5 L/mg. (a) Find q at Ce = 2.0 mg/L. (b) Find Ce when q = 20 mg/g.

  1. Langmuir equation: q = q_max K_L C / (1 + K_L C).
  2. (a) Put q_max = 40 mg/g, K_L = 1.5 L/mg, C = 2.0 mg/L. K_L C = 3.0 (dimensionless). Numerator = 40 × 3.0 = 120 mg/g. Denominator = 1 + 3.0 = 4.0. q = 120 / 4.0 = 30 mg/g.
  3. (b) Put q = 20 mg/g. 20 = 40 × 1.5 × C / (1 + 1.5 C) = 60 C / (1 + 1.5 C).
  4. 20(1 + 1.5 C) = 60 C, so 20 + 30 C = 60 C. Then 30 C = 20 and C = 0.667 mg/L.

Answer. At 25 °C, q = 30 mg/g at Ce = 2.0 mg/L; Ce = 0.667 mg/L when q = 20 mg/g.

Exam traps

  • Reading the intercept of the Langmuir plot C/q versus C as q_max. It is 1/q_max; invert it.
  • Mixing units: q in mg/g uses mass solute per mass dry solid, C in mg/L, and K_L in L/mg. Convert before multiplying.
  • Treating Freundlich n as q_max. Freundlich has no plateau; n only sets the exponent.
  • Applying a 25 °C isotherm to a 60 °C stream. Constants change with temperature; physisorption usually falls on heating.
  • For a gas, using total pressure in place of adsorbate partial pressure. Loading follows the partial pressure.

6.3 Batch and fixed-bed adsorption; breakthrough curves

Batch adsorption is a fixed amount of solid stirred with a fixed volume of liquid until equilibrium. A fixed bed is a column packed with adsorbent through which fluid flows continuously. The bed cleans the fluid well until the adsorption zone reaches the exit. The breakthrough curve records that arrival. Industry uses batch tests to rank adsorbents and fixed beds to polish water, recover solvents and clean air. The curve tells the operator when to stop feeding and switch or regenerate the bed.

You need: 6.1 and 6.2 for adsorbent capacity and isotherms, and Unit 2, Mass Transfer Coefficients and Interphase Transfer, for the idea of a mass-transfer zone.

Batch adsorption: one mass balance

In a batch test, W grams of dry adsorbent are added to V litres of solution at initial concentration C0. The mixture is shaken until the liquid concentration stops changing at Ce. Solute lost from the liquid equals solute gained by the solid. No reaction and no volume change are assumed. The loading q is the mass of solute held per mass of dry adsorbent.

q = (C0 - Ce)V / W

q is the loading (mg/g); C0 and Ce are initial and equilibrium liquid concentrations (mg/L); V is solution volume (L); W is dry adsorbent mass (g); if W is given in kg, convert to g before dividing.

A high q at low Ce means a good adsorbent. The pair (Ce, q) must also sit on the isotherm from 6.2. Batch tests are quick, but they give equilibrium capacity, not how fast a column works.

Fixed bed: zones and breakthrough

Feed with solute concentration C0 enters the top of a packed column. Clean adsorbent starts at the bottom. Three regions form. Near the inlet, the adsorbent is saturated and in equilibrium with C0; this is the saturated zone. Ahead of it is the mass-transfer zone (MTZ), a moving band where concentration falls from C0 to nearly zero. Below the MTZ the adsorbent is still fresh. The MTZ moves down much slower than the fluid.

At first the exit concentration C is almost zero. When the front of the MTZ reaches the exit, C rises. The plot of C/C0 against time is the breakthrough curve. By custom, breakthrough is often C/C0 = 0.05 and exhaustion is C/C0 = 0.95. The exact limits are a design choice; state them when you use them.

An early breakthrough means a long unused bed (much of the capacity is still fresh) or poor kinetics. Confirm with LUB ≈ H(1 − q_b/q_e): a small q_b gives a large LUB. A sharp curve means a narrow MTZ and good use of capacity. A long tail means slow intraparticle diffusion, axial dispersion or channeling, or an unfavourable (near-linear) isotherm; a favourable isotherm sharpens the front. Some syllabi cover stagewise and moving-bed adsorbers as separate contactors; only the fixed bed is treated here. Ion-exchange columns use the same curve; see 6.4.

Capacity from the curve and unused bed

For constant feed flow Q, solute fed up to time t is C0Qt. Solute leaving is Q∫C dt. Solute held by the bed is the difference. The exact loading on the bed at time t is the area between C0 and the breakthrough curve.

q(t) = (Q/W) ∫ (C0 - C) dt, from 0 to t

q(t) is bed loading at time t (mg/g); Q is feed flow (L/min); W is dry adsorbent mass (g); C0 is feed concentration (mg/L); C is exit concentration at time t (mg/L).

If C is near zero up to breakthrough tb, then q_b ≈ C0Qtb/W. This is the breakthrough capacity, not the full capacity. The shortcut q_e ≈ C0Qte/W with exhaustion time te is an upper estimate because solute leaves after breakthrough. The exact q_e integrates the whole curve.

f_unused = (q_e - q_b) / q_e    and    LUB ≈ H(1 - q_b/q_e)

f_unused is the fraction of bed capacity unused at breakthrough; q_b and q_e are loadings at breakthrough and exhaustion (mg/g); LUB is length of unused bed; H is bed depth (m).

A short LUB means the bed is well used at breakthrough. A long LUB means part of the column is still fresh when the exit begins to rise. Some syllabi cover stagewise and moving-bed adsorbers as separate contactors; only the fixed bed is treated here, and fixed-bed design depends on the breakthrough curve.

Worked example

Question. A column contains W = 1.5 kg adsorbent. Feed concentration C0 = 80 mg/L and flow Q = 2 L/min. Breakthrough (C/C0 = 0.05) is at tb = 30 min; exhaustion (C/C0 = 0.95) is at te = 90 min. Take exit concentration as negligible up to breakthrough, and use the zero-exit shortcut for the exhaustion capacity. Find q at breakthrough in mg/g and the fraction of bed capacity unused at breakthrough if capacity at exhaustion is used.

  1. Convert adsorbent mass: W = 1.5 kg = 1500 g.
  2. Solute fed up to breakthrough: C0Qtb = 80 mg/L × 2 L/min × 30 min = 4800 mg.
  3. Breakthrough loading: q_b = 4800 mg / 1500 g = 3.2 mg/g.
  4. Solute fed up to exhaustion by the shortcut: C0Qte = 80 × 2 × 90 = 14400 mg.
  5. Exhaustion loading estimate: q_e = 14400 mg / 1500 g = 9.6 mg/g.
  6. Unused fraction: (q_e - q_b)/q_e = (9.6 - 3.2)/9.6 = 6.4/9.6 = 0.6667.
  7. Note: 0.667 equals 1 − tb/te. Since the shortcut overestimates q_e, the true unused fraction is somewhat below 66.7%; the exact value needs the integral of C0 − C.

Answer. q_b = 3.2 mg/g; q_e = 9.6 mg/g; unused fraction ≈ 0.667, about 66.7%. This is an upper estimate: the shortcut overestimates q_e, so the true unused fraction is somewhat below 66.7%.

Exam traps

  • Using exhaustion time te in q_b: breakthrough capacity uses tb, not te.
  • Treating C/C0 = 0.05 as exactly zero without saying so; the shortcut is an approximation.
  • Reporting q in mg/g when concentrations are mg/L and W is in kg without converting kg to g.
  • Confusing the breakthrough curve (C/C0 against time) with the isotherm (q against C).
  • Calling q_e from C0Qte/W exact after breakthrough; after breakthrough solute leaves, so exact q_e needs the integral of C0 - C.

6.4 Ion exchange: principles and equipment

Ion exchange is a reversible swap of ions between a liquid and a solid resin. The resin holds fixed charged groups. A mobile ion from solution replaces a mobile ion on the resin, and the total charge stays balanced. The idea exists because some ions are unwanted: hardness in boiler feed, salts in laboratory water, or heavy metals in effluent. Softening, demineralisation and deionisation use it at city and plant scale. Unlike adsorption (6.1), the resin does not hold molecules on its surface; it trades one ion for another.

You need: adsorption on solids and the interphase transfer ideas of Unit 2.

Resin chemistry and the exchange step

A resin is a polymer bead with fixed charged groups and mobile counter-ions. A cation resin has negative fixed groups and mobile cations. An anion resin has positive fixed groups and mobile anions. Strong acid cation resin carries sulfonate groups and works over a wide pH range. Weak acid resin carries carboxylate groups and works best near neutral pH. Strong base anion resin carries quaternary ammonium groups; weak base resin carries tertiary amine groups. Here strong and weak describe acid or base strength, not mechanical strength.

The exchange is stoichiometric. On a sodium-form cation resin: 2 R-Na + Ca²⁺ → R₂-Ca + 2 Na⁺. One calcium ion takes two sites and two sodium ions leave. On a chloride-form anion resin: R-Cl + NO₃⁻ → R-NO₃ + Cl⁻. The total charge stays the same. The bead swells or shrinks slightly because the mobile ions carry different amounts of water.

Selectivity is the resin's preference for one ion over another. Higher-charge ions are held more strongly. For cation resins, the ion with the smaller hydrated radius is held more strongly (Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺). Strong-base anion resins run the other way: Cl⁻ > OH⁻ > F⁻. Concentration also matters: a concentrated acid or alkali can reverse the exchange during regeneration.

Capacity, selectivity and units

Exchange capacity is the number of fixed charges per amount of resin. Report it as eq/L of wet bed or meq/g of dry resin. Total capacity uses every site. Operating capacity is the part of the total capacity actually used in service between two regenerations. It is lower than the total capacity because not every site is regenerated, and design uses it.

n = F × t × c and V = n ÷ Q

n is equivalents exchanged (eq); F is feed flow (L/h); t is run time (h); c is ionic concentration (eq/L); V is resin volume (L); Q is operating capacity (eq/L).

Use equivalents, not moles. One mole of Ca²⁺ is 2 eq; one mole of Na⁺ is 1 eq. A concentration of 4 meq/L is 0.004 eq/L because 1 eq = 1000 meq.

Softening exchanges Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ for Na⁺. Hardness falls, but total dissolved solids stay about the same because each divalent ion is replaced by two sodium ions. Demineralisation uses a cation bed in H⁺ form and an anion bed in OH⁻ form. The replaced H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to water: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Deionisation is the same duty; the product is checked by conductivity.

The service cycle and equipment

  1. Service: feed passes down through the bed and the wanted exchange happens.
  2. Exhaustion: the exchange zone reaches the bottom and leakage rises, so feed stops before the quality limit is crossed.
  3. Backwash: water flows upward and expands the bed, removing fines, broken beads and gas pockets.
  4. Regeneration: acid or alkali restores the mobile ions; counter-current flow passes regenerant opposite to service and uses less chemical.
  5. Rinse: water removes leftover regenerant before the next service run.

A simple softener is a pressure vessel with a bed of cation resin, inlet distributor, outlet collector and freeboard for expansion. A mixed bed holds cation and anion resins in one vessel; it gives very pure water but needs careful separation before regeneration. Counter-current regeneration gives a sharper regenerated zone near the outlet, so leakage is lower. Some syllabi add ion-exchange chromatography, where different ions migrate at different speeds. For membrane-based ion removal, see 6.9.

Worked example

Question. A cation resin has exchange capacity 1.2 eq/L. Feed is 500 L/h with 4 meq/L Ca²⁺. Find the resin volume needed for an 8 h run.

  1. Convert concentration: 4 meq/L ÷ 1000 meq/eq = 0.004 eq/L.
  2. Volume of feed in 8 h: 500 L/h × 8 h = 4000 L.
  3. Equivalents to remove: 4000 L × 0.004 eq/L = 16 eq.
  4. Resin volume: 16 eq ÷ 1.2 eq/L = 13.333 L, about 13.3 L.

Answer. The resin volume is 13.3 L (13.33 L).

Exam traps

  • Using mg/L as meq/L. Convert mass concentration to equivalents with the ion's equivalent weight first.
  • Forgetting that 1 eq = 1000 meq. Divide meq/L by 1000 to get eq/L.
  • Using total capacity instead of operating capacity when finding run time or resin volume.
  • Thinking regeneration creates new resin. It only restores the original mobile ions on the fixed sites.

6.5 Crystallization: solubility curves and supersaturation

Crystallization separates a solute from solution by turning it into an ordered solid. For that to happen the solution must first hold more solute than its equilibrium solubility allows. That excess, called supersaturation, is the driving force, and the solubility curve tells you how much the solution can hold at any temperature. Every crystallizer problem therefore starts with two points on that curve: where the feed sits and where the final liquor must end. This chapter covers the curves, the supersaturation ratio, and the yield you get by cooling.

You need: the equilibrium ideas of Unit 4 and the separation context of 6.1; nucleation and growth follow in 6.6.

Solubility curves

A solubility curve gives the concentration of a saturated solution at each temperature. The usual unit is grams of solute per 100 g of water, written g/100 g water. Read the axis label first: some curves use g per 100 g of solution, and the two numbers differ. A point on the curve is saturated. Below the curve the solution is unsaturated and can dissolve more solid. Above the curve the solution is supersaturated.

Most inorganic salts dissolve more as the solution warms. Potassium nitrate rises steeply. Sodium chloride is almost flat, roughly 36 g per 100 g water between 20 °C and 60 °C. A few salts, such as lithium carbonate, fall. The figure uses a straight-line example: 20 g/100 g water at 20 °C and 40 g/100 g water at 60 °C.

Many salts crystallise as hydrates: copper sulfate as CuSO₄·5H₂O, washing soda as Na₂CO₃·10H₂O. The curve is normally quoted for the anhydrous solute per 100 g of water, but the solid leaving the crystallizer carries water of crystallisation with it. That water must appear in the balance. Polymorphs add a second complication. The same compound can form two lattice structures with different solubility curves, so a curve belongs to a solid form, not just to a chemical formula. The less soluble polymorph is the stable one at that temperature.

Supersaturation and the metastable zone

Solubility tells you the most solute a solution can hold. Supersaturation tells you how far past that limit the solution actually is. Write C for the actual solute concentration and C* for the saturation concentration at the same temperature, both in the same units (g/100 g water, or kg/m³).

S = C / C* σ = (C − C*) / C* = S − 1

S is the supersaturation ratio (dimensionless); σ is the relative supersaturation (dimensionless); C is the actual solute concentration; C* is the saturation concentration at the same temperature, in the same units as C.

S = 1 means saturated, S > 1 supersaturated, S < 1 unsaturated. A solution with S = 1.5 has σ = 0.5. Supersaturation is needed because a new crystal must build a surface, and that surface costs energy. Tiny nuclei therefore redissolve unless the solution is pushed some distance above the curve. The band between the curve and the point where nuclei start to survive is the metastable zone. Its width depends on cooling rate, agitation and impurities, and it is measured for each system.

Three routes create supersaturation:

  • Cooling: C* falls while C stays, so S rises. On the figure this is a horizontal move leftwards at constant concentration, taking the solution above the curve.
  • Evaporation: water leaves, C rises at constant temperature.
  • Salting out: add a second solvent or a common-ion salt so C* falls.

Yield by mass balance

With a solubility curve and a water balance you can predict the crystal yield of a simple cooling or evaporative crystallizer.

  1. Take a basis, say 1000 kg of feed solution.
  2. Split the feed into solute and water using the solubility at the feed temperature.
  3. Balance the water. In a cooling crystallizer water is neither created nor destroyed; it leaves only by evaporation or inside hydrate crystals.
  4. Solute left in the mother liquor = (kg of water at the end) × (solubility at the final temperature, g/100 g water) ÷ 100.
  5. Crystals = solute in the feed − solute left in the mother liquor, on an anhydrous basis.
  6. Yield = crystals ÷ solute in the feed.

crystals = W (C₁ − C₂) / 100

W is the mass of water in the system in kg; C₁ is the solubility at the feed temperature in g solute per 100 g water; C₂ is the solubility at the final temperature in the same unit; the result is kg of anhydrous crystals. The formula assumes no evaporation and anhydrous crystals. It is valid when the feed is saturated at the feed temperature; for an unsaturated feed use the actual feed concentration in place of C₁.

The same balance works when water is evaporated: subtract the evaporated water in step 3. Cooling is limited by how steeply the curve falls. A salt with a flat curve, like sodium chloride, gives little by cooling and is worked evaporatively; see 6.7 for the equipment and heat duty.

Worked example

Question. A salt has solubility 40 g per 100 g water at 60 °C and 20 g per 100 g water at 20 °C. 1000 kg of saturated solution at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C with no evaporation, and the crystals are anhydrous. Find the mass of crystals formed and the yield based on the original solute.

  1. Basis: 1000 kg of saturated solution at 60 °C.
  2. Mass fraction of solute at 60 °C = 40 ÷ (40 + 100) = 40/140 = 0.2857. Solute = 1000 × 0.2857 = 285.7 kg.
  3. Water in the feed = 1000 − 285.7 = 714.3 kg. Check: 1000 × 100/140 = 714.3 kg.
  4. No evaporation and anhydrous crystals, so all 714.3 kg of water stays in the mother liquor.
  5. At 20 °C, 100 g water holds 20 g solute. Solute left = 714.3 × 20/100 = 142.9 kg.
  6. Crystals = 285.7 − 142.9 = 142.9 kg.
  7. Yield = 142.9 ÷ 285.7 = 0.50, that is 50%.

Answer. 142.9 kg of anhydrous crystals form, a yield of 50% on the original solute.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same salt, 1000 kg of saturated solution at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C while 100 kg of water is evaporated. Anhydrous crystals. Find the crystals formed.

  1. Solute in feed = 285.7 kg; water in feed = 714.3 kg.
  2. Water left after evaporation = 714.3 − 100 = 614.3 kg.
  3. Solute held at 20 °C = 614.3 × 20/100 = 122.9 kg.
  4. Crystals = 285.7 − 122.9 = 162.8 kg.

Answer. 162.8 kg of crystals, more than the 142.9 kg from cooling alone.

Exam traps

  • Solubility is grams of solute per 100 g of water, not per 100 g of solution. Using 40 g in 100 g of solution gives 400 kg in a 1000 kg feed instead of 285.7 kg.
  • Water is conserved in a cooling crystallizer. Skipping the water balance and taking 20% of 1000 kg as the final solute gives 200 kg, and a wrong 85.7 kg of crystals.
  • Hydrate crystals carry water of crystallisation. The solid mass is larger than the anhydrous mass removed, and the mother liquor also loses that water, so both sides of the balance change.
  • S and σ are different numbers. S = 1.5 corresponds to σ = 0.5; mixing them up costs marks.
  • A point above the curve is supersaturated, not saturated. Examiners often ask you to label the unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated regions on a solubility plot.

6.6 Nucleation and crystal growth

Crystallization does not begin the moment a solution becomes saturated. It needs supersaturation: the solution must hold more solute than its equilibrium solubility. That extra solute can form new nuclei or deposit on crystals already present. Nucleation sets how many crystals appear; growth sets how large they become. The balance between them decides crystal number and size. Industries use this balance for salt, sugar, urea, pharmaceutical crystals and water-treatment softening. Too much nucleation gives a fine, cloudy product; controlled growth gives large, filterable crystals.

You need Chapter 6.5: solubility, saturation and supersaturation.

Two paths from supersaturation

Supersaturation is the driving force. Write absolute supersaturation ΔC = C − C* and relative supersaturation σ = (C − C*)/C*. C is actual solute concentration (kg solute per m³ solution or mol/m³); C* is equilibrium solubility at the same temperature; σ is dimensionless. Many correlations write S for σ. Some syllabi call σ the relative supersaturation. Keep the definition with the correlation: changing from ΔC to σ changes the numerical value and the constant.

Supersaturation feeds two competing paths. Nucleation creates new crystals. Growth adds solute to existing crystals. A clear solution with no crystals can stay metastable for some time: it has supersaturation but no nuclei. A speck of dust, a scratched vessel wall or a seed crystal can start nucleation.

Primary nucleation starts from a clear solution. Homogeneous primary nucleation is cluster formation in the bulk liquid with no foreign surface. A cluster must reach the critical nucleus size; smaller clusters redissolve. Heterogeneous primary nucleation starts on a foreign surface, so it needs less supersaturation. Secondary nucleation starts from crystals already present: contact with an impeller, pump, wall or other crystals breaks off small particles or creates ordered layers that become nuclei. In an agitated seeded crystallizer, secondary nucleation often dominates.

Higher supersaturation raises both rates, but not equally. Nucleation is steeply dependent on supersaturation. Growth is milder. A sudden high supersaturation therefore makes many small crystals; controlled low supersaturation favours growth of fewer, larger crystals.

Nucleation rate and critical nucleus

Classical nucleation theory treats clusters as forming and disappearing by molecule attachment and detachment. The free energy has an unfavourable surface term and a favourable volume term. The critical nucleus is the size where adding one more molecule starts to lower free energy. Above it the nucleus grows; below it the cluster dissolves. The critical size falls as supersaturation rises, so high supersaturation makes nucleation easier.

B = k_n Sn   or   B = k_n σn

B is nucleation rate (number of new crystals per m³ suspension per second, # m⁻³ s⁻¹); k_n is the nucleation rate constant for the chosen supersaturation definition; S is supersaturation (use σ if relative); n is the empirical nucleation order, often 3 to 8.

For engineering use, nucleation is written as a power law. If S means σ, B = k_n σⁿ and k_n carries the units. If S means ΔC, the same symbol k_n has different units and a different numerical value. Record which definition you use.

  1. Solute molecules collide and form loose clusters.
  2. Clusters gain or lose molecules by attachment and detachment.
  3. Clusters below the critical size dissolve; those above survive.
  4. Surviving nuclei grow and may trigger secondary nucleation.

Secondary nucleation needs parent crystals. Its rate rises with supersaturation, magma density (kg crystals per m³ suspension) and agitation. It is often the main source of new particles in a seeded, stirred crystallizer.

Growth: diffusion then integration

Growth has two resistances in series. Solute must diffuse from bulk solution through a boundary layer to the crystal surface. Then it must integrate into the lattice at a step, kink or screw dislocation. Either step can control the overall rate.

G = k_g Sg

G is linear growth rate (m/s), the increase in crystal size per time; k_g is the overall growth coefficient (units depend on how S is defined; m/s when S is dimensionless); S is supersaturation; g is the growth order, often 1 to 2.

FeatureDiffusion controlSurface integration control
Growth order g≈1Often >1
AgitationRaises GLittle effect
ImpuritiesUsually minorCan block growth
TemperatureRaises diffusivityRaises rate strongly

Temperature raises both diffusion and surface integration, but the surface step more strongly. Agitation shrinks the boundary layer and raises growth, but also raises secondary nucleation. Impurities can adsorb on faces, slow growth and change crystal habit; some act as nucleation sites. Seeds provide growth surface and reduce primary nucleation, but hard agitation breaks them into fines. For large crystals, keep supersaturation low and uniform, seed, and agitate enough for mass transfer without making secondary nucleation dominate.

Worked example

Question. A salt crystallizes with G = k_g S^g. k_g = 5.0×10⁻⁵ m/s when S is relative supersaturation, and g = 1.5. (a) Find G at S = 0.04. (b) By what factor must S rise to double G? (c) State the new S.

  1. S is dimensionless relative supersaturation. k_g is in m/s, so G comes out in m/s.

  5. 2^(1/1.5) = 2^0.6667 = 1.587.
  6. S_new = 0.04 × 1.587 = 0.0635.
  7. The factor is 1.59.

Answer. G = 4.0×10⁻⁷ m/s; S must rise by a factor 1.59 to S_new = 0.0635 (dimensionless relative supersaturation).

  • Assuming higher supersaturation gives bigger crystals. It usually raises nucleation more steeply than growth, giving more, smaller crystals.
  • Ignoring secondary nucleation in a stirred, seeded crystallizer. It often dominates over primary nucleation.

6.7 Crystallizers: classification, yield and heat effects

A crystallizer turns dissolved solute into solid crystals by creating supersaturation and then controlling nucleation and growth. It must also remove or supply heat. The way a unit creates supersaturation names its class: cooling, evaporative/vacuum, reaction or salting-out. Yield follows a solute balance against solubility. Heat duty follows a heat balance that includes sensible cooling, evaporation and heat of crystallization. These ideas size crystallizers for sugar, salt, urea, pharmaceuticals and water treatment.

You need: solubility and supersaturation (6.5) and nucleation and growth (6.6).

Classification by supersaturation and heat removal

Every crystallizer must create supersaturation. The class name comes from how it does that. Cooling crystallizers cool the solution; solubility falls and solute crystallizes. Evaporative crystallizers boil off solvent; the solution becomes more concentrated. A vacuum crystallizer flashes solvent at low pressure, so evaporation removes sensible heat and causes cooling. Reaction crystallizers form a sparingly soluble product by mixing reactants. Salting-out units add a foreign substance that lowers solubility.

Operation is batch or continuous. Batch units suit small volumes, seasonal products or recipe changes. Continuous units suit large steady production and give steadier product size. The stirred tank is the simple batch cooling unit. The Swenson-Walker is a continuous cooling trough with scrapers that move crystals and keep heat-transfer surfaces clean. A forced-circulation crystallizer pumps magma through an external heat exchanger; this gives good heat transfer with low supersaturation at the crystal bed. The draft-tube-baffle unit uses an internal propeller and a baffle to suspend crystals. The Oslo crystallizer grows crystals in a fluidized bed and withdraws a classified product.

Yield and purity from a solute balance

Yield comes from the solute left dissolved in the mother liquor at the final temperature. Use solubility as mass of solute per 100 mass of water, not per 100 mass of solution. For feed F kg/h with solute mass fraction xF, solute in feed is SF = F xF. Water in feed is W = F - SF. At the final temperature, mother liquor holds SL = W s/100, where s is solubility in g solute per 100 g water. For anhydrous crystals, crystal mass P = SF - SL. Keep all masses in the same unit, usually kg/h.

P = F xF - (F - F xF) s/100 = F[xF - (1 - xF) s/100]

P is crystal mass flow (kg/h); F is feed mass flow (kg/h); xF is solute mass fraction in feed; s is solubility (kg solute per 100 kg water).

Purity differs from yield. Yield counts solute recovered. Purity counts the wanted solid as a fraction of product mass. Mother liquor film, other soluble salts and hydrate water lower purity. Washing removes the film. If crystals are a hydrate, crystal mass includes water of crystallization: P = (SF - SL)/yc, where yc is solute mass fraction in the hydrate. That water must also enter the water balance. Saturated mother liquor always carries solute, so 100% yield needs recycle or further treatment.

Heat effects and design of batch and continuous units

The heat balance depends on the crystallizer type. In a cooling crystallizer with no evaporation, heat leaves through the wall.

Q = mf cp,f (Tf - T) + P ΔHc

Q is heat removed through the wall (kJ/h); mf is feed mass flow (kg/h); cp,f is feed specific heat (kJ/(kg K)); Tf and T are initial and final temperatures (K or °C); P is crystal mass flow (kg/h); ΔHc is heat of crystallization (kJ/kg).

Cooling water flow is mw = Q/(cp,w ΔTw).

mw = Q/(cp,w ΔTw)

mw is cooling water flow (kg/h); cp,w is water specific heat (kJ/(kg K)); ΔTw is water temperature rise (K).

For an evaporative crystallizer heated by steam, steam supplies heat to raise feed to boiling, to crystallize and to evaporate solvent.

Qsteam = mf cp,f (Tb - Tf) + P ΔHc + mv ΔHvap

Qsteam is steam duty (kJ/h); Tb is boiling temperature (K or °C); mv is evaporated solvent (kg/h); ΔHvap is latent heat of vaporization (kJ/kg); other symbols are as above.

For a vacuum crystallizer with no external heat, solvent flashes and carries latent heat from the liquor.

mv ΔHvap = mf cp,f (Tf - T) + P ΔHc

mv is flashed solvent (kg/h); ΔHvap is latent heat (kJ/kg); other symbols are as above. Solve this for mv. External cooling water is small or zero.

Design keeps supersaturation inside the metastable zone. Too high a supersaturation gives many nuclei and fine crystals; too low gives little growth and low yield. Key variables are residence time, magma density (mass of crystals per volume of slurry), circulation rate, temperature difference across heat-transfer surfaces and heat-transfer area. Magma is the slurry of crystals in mother liquor. A cooling crystallizer needs Q = U A ΔTlm to size area, where U is overall heat-transfer coefficient (kJ/(h m² K)) and A is heat-transfer area (m²). ΔTlm is the log-mean temperature difference between the magma and the coolant, the log-mean of the two end differences. A small ΔT needs large area but gives gentler supersaturation and less encrustation. Batch design fixes the cooling curve and batch time. Continuous design fixes residence time and withdrawal rate. High-circulation units such as forced-circulation and draft-tube reduce local supersaturation. Crystal size distribution is covered in 6.8.

Worked example

Question. A feed of 1000 kg/h contains 30 mass% solute at 50 °C and is cooled to 20 °C. Solubility at 20 °C is 20 g solute per 100 g water. The feed solution has cp = 3.8 kJ/(kg K).   5. Anhydrous crystals P = 300 - 140 = 160 kg/h.
  6. Sensible heat Qsens = 1000 × 3.8 × (50 - 20) = 114000 kJ/h.
  7. Crystallization heat Qcryst = 160 × 120 = 19200 kJ/h.
  8. Total heat Qtotal = 114000 + 19200 = 133200 kJ/h.
  9. Cooling water mw = 133200/(4.18 × 10) = 3186.6 kg/h.

6.8 Crystal size distribution and the MSMPR crystallizer

A continuous crystallizer makes not one size of crystal but a spread. The crystal size distribution (CSD) records how many crystals lie in each size band. The MSMPR model — mixed suspension, mixed product removal — links that spread to just two quantities: growth rate G and residence time τ. Its population density falls exponentially with size. Industry uses the CSD to control filtration, drying, caking and dosage, from sugar and salt to pharmaceutical crystals and battery materials.

You need: nucleation and growth rates from 6.6, and crystallizer types and yield from 6.7.

The crystal size distribution

Crystal size distribution (CSD) is the amount of crystal in each size band. A real crystallizer discharges crystals from fine nuclei to large product. Sieve analysis or laser diffraction gives the mass in each band. Customers see mass, but the MSMPR model counts crystals.

Let L be the characteristic crystal size in mm, for example the sieve opening. Define the population density n(L) as the number of crystals per unit volume of suspension per unit size interval. Its units are per m³ per mm. The number of crystals in a narrow band from L to L + dL is n dL per m³ of slurry.

The cumulative number N(L) is the number per m³ larger than L. The CSD can be plotted as n against L, or as mass fraction against L. The dominant size is the size holding the most mass; it is not the largest crystal.

CSD matters because it sets filtration rate, washing, drying, caking, flow and tablet strength. Sugar, salt, urea and pharmaceutical crystals are sold on size. A narrow CSD is easier to filter and dry than a broad one.

The MSMPR population balance

MSMPR means mixed suspension, mixed product removal. Feed enters a perfectly mixed vessel, crystals grow, and the slurry leaves with the same CSD as the vessel. The model rests on these assumptions:

  • Perfect mixing at uniform supersaturation.
  • Constant volume V with feed flow Q, so τ = V/Q.
  • Product removal without classification.
  • Growth rate G independent of crystal size.
  • No agglomeration or breakage.
  • Steady state.

Take a size band L to L + dL. Crystals enter the band by growth from below, leave by growth to above, and leave in the product stream. At steady state the balance gives:

G dn/dL + n/τ = 0

n is population density (per m³ per mm); L is crystal size (mm); G is linear growth rate (mm/h); τ = V/Q is mean residence time (h); V is suspension volume (m³); Q is product slurry flow (m³/h).

Integrate from L = 0, where n = n°, to size L:

n = n° exp(−L/(Gτ))

n° is the nuclei population density at L = 0 (per m³ per mm); exp is the exponential function.

Taking logs:

ln n = ln n° − L/(Gτ)

ln is natural logarithm; the intercept is ln n°; the slope is −1/(Gτ).

A plot of ln n against L is a straight line. The nucleation rate follows from the same n°:

B° = n° G

B° is the number of new nuclei per m³ of suspension per hour (per m³ per h) when G is in mm/h and n° is per m³ per mm.

To use plant data: tabulate L and n; take ln n; fit a straight line; read intercept and slope; convert n° = exp(intercept); find G or τ from slope if the other is known; compute B°. Some syllabi call this the population balance.

Reading the line: design and limits

The line gives quick design numbers. The dominant size of the mass distribution is approximately:

L_D ≈ 3 G τ

L_D is the size with the largest mass fraction (mm); G is growth rate (mm/h); τ is residence time (h).

Doubling τ or G moves the product coarser. But n is highest at L = 0, so an MSMPR always makes many fines. That is why real plants add fines dissolution, seeding or a classified product draw. Such tricks break the no-classification assumption, so the simple straight line no longer fits.

A batch crystallizer has no continuous product removal. Its supersaturation, nucleation and CSD change with time. Seeded batch cooling often gives a narrower CSD than an MSMPR. Continuous MSMPR operation gives a broad exponential distribution, which is predictable but needs good fines handling.

Use the model to size a crystallizer: pick τ from the target dominant size and the growth rate you can achieve at the chosen supersaturation. Then B° tells you the nuclei count, which sets the load on fines removal. If the ln n plot bends, the assumptions are failing. Size-dependent growth, agglomeration, attrition, imperfect mixing or classification all bend it. Then use a full population balance with these terms.

Worked example

Question. An MSMPR crystallizer has mean residence time τ = 5.0 h, growth rate G = 0.20 mm/h, and nuclei population density n° = 2.0×10⁸ per m³ per mm. Find the population density n at L = 1.0 mm, the slope of ln n against L, and the nucleation rate B°.

  1. List the data: τ = 5.0 h, G = 0.20 mm/h, n° = 2.0×10⁸ per m³ per mm, L = 1.0 mm.
  2. Compute the product Gτ = 0.20 mm/h × 5.0 h = 1.0 mm. This is the size-scale constant.
  3. Compute the exponent −L/(Gτ) = −1.0 mm ÷ 1.0 mm = −1.0. The exponent is dimensionless.
  4. Compute exp(−1.0) = 0.3679.
  5. Compute n = n° exp(−L/(Gτ)) = 2.0×10⁸ × 0.3679 = 7.36×10⁷ per m³ per mm.
  6. Compute slope = −1/(Gτ) = −1 ÷ 1.0 mm = −1.0 mm⁻¹.
  7. Compute B° = n°G = 2.0×10⁸ × 0.20 = 4.0×10⁷ nuclei per m³ per hour.

Answer. n = 7.36×10⁷ per m³ per mm, the slope of ln n against L is −1.0 mm⁻¹, and B° = 4.0×10⁷ nuclei per m³ per hour.

Exam traps

  • Mixing time units: putting τ in minutes while G is in mm/h. Convert τ to hours or G to mm/min before forming Gτ.
  • Treating n° as the population density at the first sieve or the largest crystal. n° is the value at L = 0, and the intercept of ln n against L is ln n°, not n°.
  • Writing B° = n°G/τ. The correct relation is B° = n°G; the residence time is already inside n° for a given run.
  • Fitting a straight line to n against L or thinking the slope of n against L is constant. The straight line is ln n against L, and its slope is −1/(Gτ), not −Gτ.
  • Forcing the MSMPR line onto a classified product or a fines-dissolution crystallizer. Classification bends the line; use a population balance with that term instead.

Understand State four assumptions of the MSMPR model and give one industrial reason they fail. 4 marks

Analyze A product from an MSMPR crystallizer has too many fines. Using the model, what changes would you consider, and what is the risk? 5 marks

Increase the residence time τ: the dominant size is about 3Gτ, so a larger τ raises it directly. 6.9 Membrane separations: reverse osmosis, electrodialysis and ultrafiltration

Membranes are thin barriers that let some molecules pass and hold others back. The driving force is either pressure or an electric field. Reverse osmosis pushes water through against osmotic pressure and leaves salt behind. Ultrafiltration holds macromolecules and colloids while water and small solutes pass. Electrodialysis uses an electric field and ion-exchange membranes to move ions out of a stream. These units make drinking water, concentrate fruit juice, purify antibiotics and treat industrial wastewater.

You need Unit 2 interphase transfer and 6.4 Ion exchange for charged membranes and resins.

Osmosis, flux and rejection

Osmosis is the flow of solvent through a membrane that passes solvent but not solute. The equilibrium pressure that stops this flow is the osmotic pressure π.

π = i C R T

π is osmotic pressure in Pa; i is the van't Hoff factor, the number of ions per formula unit, 2 for NaCl; C is solute concentration in mol/m³; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K.

Reverse osmosis applies pressure greater than π. The pump raises the feed pressure, water passes through the membrane, and salt leaves in the retentate. A seawater plant and a home water purifier use the same principle.

Production is measured by flux, rejection and recovery.

J = Qp / A,   Y = Qp / Qf

J is permeate flux in L m⁻² h⁻¹; Qp is permeate flow in L/h; A is membrane area in m²; Y is recovery; Qf is feed flow in L/h.

R = 1 - Cp / Cf

R is rejection as a fraction; Cp is permeate solute concentration; Cf is feed solute concentration; both in the same units, usually mg/L. Multiply R by 100 for percent.

Reverse osmosis and ultrafiltration

In reverse osmosis, a high-pressure pump raises the feed pressure above π. The module holds salt back. Water passes as permeate; the salt-rich retentate leaves. The figure uses 1000 L/h feed at 2000 mg/L. At 75% recovery, permeate is 750 L/h and retentate is 250 L/h. If permeate is 100 mg/L, rejection is 95% and retentate reaches 7700 mg/L.

Ultrafiltration uses a porous membrane and lower pressure. It holds macromolecules, proteins, colloids, oil droplets and bacteria while water and small dissolved solutes pass. The pore size or molecular weight cut-off sets the separation. Ultrafiltration does not remove dissolved salts well. Flux falls over time as a gel layer or fouling builds near the membrane.

Some syllabi add solid and liquid membranes. Only a few universities teach module design and concentration polarisation in detail.

Electrodialysis

Electrodialysis uses an electric field and ion-exchange membranes. Cation-exchange membranes pass cations and block anions; anion-exchange membranes do the reverse. Alternating membranes make diluate and concentrate compartments. Feed flows through; cations move toward the cathode and anions toward the anode. Ions leave the diluate and collect in the concentrate. The membranes resemble the charged resins of 6.4 Ion exchange.

Electrodialysis desalts brackish water, demineralises whey and recovers acids. Energy use scales with current and salt removed. Current efficiency is the fraction of current carried by the target ions; water splitting and leaks lower it. No high pressure is needed.

Worked example

Question. An RO unit treats 1000 L/h feed with 2000 mg/L salt at 75% recovery. Permeate salt is 100 mg/L. Find permeate flow, retentate flow, retentate concentration and salt rejection.

Python program.

Qf = 1000.0
Cf = 2000.0
rec = 0.75
Cp = 100.0
Qp = rec * Qf
Qr = Qf - Qp
Cr = (Qf * Cf - Qp * Cp) / Qr

print(Qp)
print(Qr)
print(Cr)

  1. Permeate flow Qp = recovery × feed flow = 0.75 × 1000 = 750 L/h.
  2. Retentate flow Qr = feed flow - permeate flow = 1000 - 750 = 250 L/h.
  3. Feed salt rate = 1000 L/h × 2000 mg/L = 2.00×10⁶ mg/h.
  4. Permeate salt rate = 750 L/h × 100 mg/L = 7.50×10⁴ mg/h.
  5. Retentate salt rate = 2.00×10⁶ - 7.50×10⁴ = 1.925×10⁶ mg/h.
  6. Retentate concentration = 1.925×10⁶ mg/h ÷ 250 L/h = 7700 mg/L.
  7. Rejection R = (1 - Cp/Cf) × 100 = (1 - 100/2000) × 100 = 95%.

Exam traps

  • Recovery is the permeate fraction, not the retained fraction: 75% recovery means 75% permeate and 25% retentate.
  • Rejection uses permeate concentration: R = 1 - Cp/Cf. Using retentate concentration gives a wrong answer.
  • Osmotic pressure is not the applied pressure. RO needs an applied pressure above π plus pressure losses.
  • Ultrafiltration separates by size and does not remove dissolved ions like reverse osmosis. Electrodialysis removes ions but not neutral molecules.
  • Salt balance uses flow × concentration. Do not average feed and retentate concentrations without a mass balance.

Takeaways

  1. Adsorption capacity comes from surface area and pore structure, so adsorbent choice is as important as isotherm choice.
  2. Langmuir and Freundlich equations turn equilibrium data into constants that can be used for batch and column calculations.
  3. A fixed bed works until the mass-transfer zone reaches the exit; the breakthrough curve is the design record of that arrival.
  4. Ion exchange is a reversible swapping of ions on a resin, and regeneration is part of every practical cycle.
  5. Supersaturation is the driving force for crystallization; without it, neither nuclei nor crystal growth can occur.
  6. Nucleation and growth compete for the same supersaturation, so the balance between them controls crystal number and size.
  7. Crystallizer yield follows from a solute balance, while the heat balance fixes the cooling or evaporation duty.
  8. The MSMPR model gives an exponential population-density plot whose slope and intercept yield growth and nucleation rates.
  9. Membranes separate by pressure or electric driving force, and the choice among reverse osmosis, electrodialysis and ultrafiltration depends on the species to be removed.

Glossary

Adsorbate
The solute species that is adsorbed from the fluid onto the solid.

An empirical adsorption model written q = K·C^(1/n).

Reversible exchange of ions between a solution and a solid resin that keeps total charge balanced.
Langmuir isotherm
A monolayer adsorption model written q = qmax·K·C/(1 + K·C).
Mass-transfer zone
The region in a fixed bed where the fluid concentration changes from feed value to nearly zero.

The formation of new crystal particles from a supersaturated solution.

The fluid stream that passes through a membrane.

Weak, reversible adsorption caused by van der Waals forces, with low heat release.

The number of crystals per unit size interval per unit volume of crystal suspension.
