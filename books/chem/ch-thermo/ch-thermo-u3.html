3.1 Statements of the second law: Kelvin-Planck and Clausius

The first law says energy is conserved. It does not say which way a process may go. The second law adds direction. It forbids a cyclic engine from turning heat from one reservoir completely into work, and forbids a refrigerator from moving heat from cold to hot without work input. These are the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements. They are equivalent. They explain why a power plant needs a condenser and why 100% thermal efficiency is impossible. This chapter states both forms and proves their equivalence by contradiction.

You need: the first law energy balance for a cyclic process, and the definitions of heat, work and a system from Unit 1.

Reservoirs, cycles and efficiency

A heat reservoir is a large body whose temperature stays constant when heat enters or leaves it. A river, the atmosphere or a large condenser can act as one over a short time. A cyclic device returns to its initial state after each cycle, so its internal energy change is zero. For an engine between a hot reservoir at TH and a cold reservoir at TC, let QH be heat taken from the hot reservoir, QC be heat rejected to the cold reservoir, and W be work delivered. Use positive magnitudes in kJ.

The first law for one cycle gives W = QH − QC. Thermal efficiency is the work obtained per unit heat supplied: η = W/QH. η is a fraction; multiply by 100 for percent. The first law allows QC = 0, so η = 100%. The second law forbids that case.

W = QH − QC,   η = W/QH = (QH − QC)/QH

QH is heat taken from the hot reservoir (kJ); QC is heat rejected to the cold reservoir (kJ); W is work delivered per cycle (kJ); η is thermal efficiency (dimensionless).

Engine, refrigerator and heat-pump hardware is in 3.2.

Kelvin–Planck statement

Kelvin–Planck statement: no cyclic device can take heat from a single reservoir and convert all of it into work. It may take QH from a hot reservoir, but it must reject some heat QC > 0 to a colder reservoir. Therefore W < QH and η < 1. Some syllabi call this the Kelvin–Planck statement. The word cyclic matters. A single expansion of gas can produce work while cooling; it is not a cycle. A device that claims to violate this statement is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM2. It does not violate the first law; it takes heat from air or sea and produces work forever in a cycle. The statement says PMM2 is impossible. Work can become heat fully: a paddle wheel stirred in water converts work into internal energy. The restriction is one-way.

Clausius statement and COP

Clausius statement: no cyclic device can move heat from a colder body to a hotter body without work input. A refrigerator takes QC from the cold space, receives work Win, and rejects QH = QC + Win to the warm surroundings. Its coefficient of performance is COPR = QC/Win. A heat pump has COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1. COP can be greater than 1; it is not efficiency. COP = 5 means 5 kJ of cold-side heat removed per 1 kJ of work. The statement applies to the net effect of a complete cycle, not to a single process. It forbids COPR = ∞ for a cyclic refrigerator with no work. Practical cycles are in 3.9.

COPR = QC/Win,   COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1

QC is heat removed from the cold space (kJ); QH is heat rejected to the warm space (kJ); Win is work input per cycle (kJ); COPR and COPHP are dimensionless coefficients of performance.

The two statements are equivalent

Kelvin–Planck and Clausius are equivalent: if one is false, the other is false. Suppose Kelvin–Planck is false. A cyclic engine E takes QH from the hot reservoir and delivers W = QH, rejecting no heat. Use W to drive a normal refrigerator R between the same reservoirs. R takes QC from the cold reservoir and rejects QC + W to the hot reservoir. Combine E and R. The hot reservoir loses QH to E and gains QC + QH from R; net gain is QC. The cold reservoir loses QC. The composite moves QC from cold to hot with no net work input, because E supplies W and R consumes W. That violates Clausius. So not-KP implies not-Clausius.

Now suppose Clausius is false. A device R moves QC from the cold reservoir to the hot reservoir with no work input. Take a normal engine E that removes QH from the hot reservoir, rejects QC to the cold reservoir, and delivers W = QH − QC. The cold reservoir gains QC from E and loses QC to R; net change is zero. The hot reservoir loses QH and gains QC; net loss is QH − QC = W. The composite takes heat W from the hot reservoir only and delivers work W in a cycle. That violates Kelvin–Planck. So not-Clausius implies not-KP. The two statements stand or fall together.

Worked example

Question. A cyclic engine takes 100 kJ from a 600 K reservoir and rejects 60 kJ to a 300 K reservoir in each cycle. Find W and η. Does it violate the Kelvin–Planck statement?

  1. Data: QH = 100 kJ, QC = 60 kJ, TH = 600 K, TC = 300 K.
  2. First law for one cycle: W = QH − QC = 100 kJ − 60 kJ = 40 kJ.
  3. Thermal efficiency: η = W/QH = 40 kJ / 100 kJ = 0.40 = 40%.
  4. Kelvin–Planck violation would require QC = 0, so W = QH. Here QC = 60 kJ > 0 and W = 40 kJ < 100 kJ.
  5. The engine is allowed by the Kelvin–Planck statement. The temperatures do not decide this point; they set the maximum possible η in 3.3.

Answer. W = 40 kJ and η = 40%; the engine does not violate the Kelvin–Planck statement because it rejects 60 kJ to the cold reservoir.

Exam traps

  • Thinking η = 100% is only inefficient. The first law allows it; the second law forbids it for a cyclic engine.
  • Applying Kelvin–Planck to a single process. It is a statement about a complete cycle; a gas can produce work while cooling in one expansion.
  • Treating COP > 1 as a violation. COP is heat moved per work input, not efficiency, so values like 4 are normal.
  • Confusing the two statements. Kelvin–Planck limits heat→work; Clausius limits cold→hot heat transfer without work.
  • Assuming Clausius says heat never flows cold to hot. It says a cyclic device cannot do so with no work input; a refrigerator does it with work.

3.2 Heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps

A heat engine takes heat from a hot body, converts part of it into work, and rejects the rest to a cold body. A refrigerator or heat pump runs the same energy flow backwards: work input moves heat from a cold space to a warm space. The three devices share one balance, QH = QC + W. Efficiency and coefficient of performance (COP) compare what you want with what you pay. Room air conditioners, kitchen refrigerators and heat-pump water heaters use this balance every day.

You need: the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements from 3.1, and the steady-flow energy balance from Unit 1.

The energy balance with consistent signs

Take every heat flow and work flow as a positive magnitude, and fix the direction on a sketch. QH is the heat flow at the hot side, QC is the heat flow at the cold side, and W is the work flow. For a heat engine, QH enters from a hot source, W leaves as useful work, and QC leaves to a cold sink. Energy balance gives QH = W + QC. The engine pays QH and receives W; the second law forces QC > 0, so W < QH.

For a refrigerator, the wanted result is QC removed from a cold space. Work W enters through a compressor, and QH leaves to the kitchen or outdoor air. The balance is QC + W = QH, the same equation. A heat pump is the same hardware with the wanted result changed to QH delivered to a warm space. Some syllabi call a heat pump a reversed heat engine; the phrase means the arrows of heat flow are reversed by work input, not that the device is a different machine. Keep the magnitudes positive in all three cases. The sketch below uses the worked example: 5 kW enters from the cold space, 2 kW of compressor work enters, and 7 kW leaves to the warm space.

Heat engine: efficiency

A heat engine converts heat into work. Its thermal efficiency η is the work you get divided by the heat you pay for.

η = W/QH = 1 − QC/QH

η is thermal efficiency (dimensionless); W is net work output; QH is heat taken from the hot source; QC is heat rejected to the cold sink; W, QH and QC use the same unit (kW or MW).

For the engine balance QH = W + QC, so W = QH − QC. Substitute: η = (QH − QC)/QH = 1 − QC/QH. The second law says QC cannot be zero for a cyclic engine that takes heat from one hot source, so η < 1. A steam power plant is a heat engine: fuel or nuclear heat enters the boiler, work leaves at the turbine shaft, and waste heat leaves at the condenser, as in 3.8. Do not insert Celsius temperatures into η. The ratio QC/QH comes from the actual cycle; for a reversible cycle between two reservoirs, the Carnot result links it to Kelvin temperatures, covered in 3.3.

Refrigerator and heat pump: COP

A refrigerator is rated by its coefficient of performance, COPR, defined as heat removed from the cold space divided by work input.

COPR = QC/W

COPR is the refrigerator coefficient of performance (dimensionless); QC is heat removed from the cold space; W is work input; both in kW or both in MW.

A heat pump is rated by COPHP = QH/W, because the wanted output is heat delivered to the warm space. Both COPs are dimensionless and both can be greater than 1. A room air conditioner is a refrigerator: it removes QC from the room and rejects QH to the outdoor air. A domestic refrigerator does the same and rejects QH to the kitchen. A heat pump for space heating or hot water wants QH, so it uses the same cycle but a different rating. Divide the balance QH = QC + W by W.

COPHP = QH/W = (QC + W)/W = COPR + 1

COPHP is the heat-pump coefficient of performance; QH is heat delivered to the warm space; W is work input.

The difference of 1 is not a small correction; it follows from energy conservation alone. If a refrigerator has COPR = 2.5, the same cycle used as a heat pump has COPHP = 3.5. That is why a heat pump can deliver more heat than the electrical work it consumes.

Worked example

Question. A refrigerator removes 5 kW of heat from a cold space at 4 °C and rejects heat to a kitchen at 32 °C. Its compressor draws 2 kW of electrical power. Find the heat rejected to the kitchen, QH, and the coefficient of performance, COPR.

  1. Take the refrigerator as the system at steady state. All heat and work magnitudes are positive: QC = 5 kW into the device, W = 2 kW into the device, and QH leaves to the kitchen.
  2. Apply the energy balance: QH = QC + W = 5 kW + 2 kW = 7 kW.
  3. Apply the definition: COPR = QC/W = 5 kW / 2 kW = 2.5.

Answer. The refrigerator rejects QH = 7 kW to the kitchen, and its COPR is 2.5.

Exam traps

  • Do not swap QH and QC: QH is the larger heat flow to the hot side, QC is the smaller flow from the cold side, and W is their difference.
  • COP is not W/QC. It is QC/W for a refrigerator and QH/W for a heat pump.
  • For a refrigerator or heat pump, work input W is positive in the magnitude balance QH = QC + W. Do not enter it as negative and then add signs again.
  • A heat pump and a refrigerator are the same device with a different wanted output; COPHP = COPR + 1, not a separate cycle.
  • Do not use Celsius temperatures in η or COP. These are ratios of energies; Celsius enters only through temperature limits such as the Carnot limit in 3.3.

3.3 Carnot cycle, Carnot theorems and thermodynamic temperature scale

A Carnot engine is a reversible heat engine that takes heat Q_H from a hot reservoir at T_H, rejects heat Q_C to a cold reservoir at T_C, and returns to its starting state. Every step is reversible, so it gives the best efficiency possible between those two temperatures. Real power plants, refrigerators and heat pumps are compared with this limit. The Carnot theorems and the thermodynamic temperature scale turn that comparison into a precise tool for calculating temperatures and efficiencies.

You need: 3.1 and 3.2 for the second law and engine energy balance, Unit 2 for ideal-gas isothermal and adiabatic processes, and Unit 1 for the meaning of a reversible process.

The four Carnot steps on an ideal gas

Take one mole of ideal gas in a piston–cylinder. The gas is the working fluid. The cycle has two isothermal steps at the reservoir temperatures and two adiabatic steps that change temperature. All steps are reversible.

  1. 1 → 2: isothermal expansion at T_H. The gas takes Q_H from the hot reservoir and does work. For an ideal gas, Q_H = n R T_H ln(V₂/V₁); n is moles, R is the gas constant, V is volume.
  2. 2 → 3: adiabatic expansion from T_H to T_C. Q = 0, so the gas cools while doing work.
  3. 3 → 4: isothermal compression at T_C. Work is done on the gas and it rejects Q_C to the cold reservoir. Q_C = n R T_C ln(V₃/V₄) as a positive magnitude.
  4. 4 → 1: adiabatic compression from T_C back to T_H. Q = 0, and the gas returns to its starting state.

The two adiabatic steps connect the same two temperatures, so (V₂/V₁) = (V₃/V₄). Divide the heat magnitudes: Q_H/Q_C = T_H/T_C. This ratio is the key result; it does not depend on n or R.

Q_H/Q_C = T_H/T_C

Q_H is heat taken from the hot reservoir; Q_C is heat rejected to the cold reservoir, both in kJ; T_H and T_C are absolute temperatures in kelvin.

Carnot theorems and the thermodynamic temperature scale

First Carnot theorem: no engine working between two fixed reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine between the same two reservoirs. Second Carnot theorem: all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever the working fluid. The proof idea is a pair of coupled engines. If a claimed engine were better, reverse the reversible one and the pair would move heat from cold to hot with no net work, which the Kelvin–Planck statement forbids (see 3.1).

For any reversible engine, the ratio Q_H/Q_C depends only on the two reservoir temperatures. This lets us define the thermodynamic temperature scale: T_H/T_C = Q_H/Q_C for a reversible engine. The kelvin is fixed by one reference point; the triple point of water is 273.16 K. Then the Carnot efficiency follows from the first law, W = Q_H − Q_C, and the definition.

η_C = W/Q_H = 1 − Q_C/Q_H = 1 − T_C/T_H

η_C is the Carnot efficiency; W is net work output; Q_H is heat taken from the hot reservoir; Q_C is heat rejected; T_H and T_C are absolute temperatures in kelvin.

For the reversed cycle, the same engine becomes a refrigerator or heat pump. Its coefficient of performance (COP) is the useful heat divided by work input.

COP_R = Q_C/W = T_C/(T_H − T_C)

COP_R is the refrigerator COP; Q_C is heat removed from the cold space; W is work input; T_H and T_C are absolute temperatures.

COP_HP = Q_H/W = T_H/(T_H − T_C)

COP_HP is the heat-pump COP; Q_H is heat delivered to the hot space.

What the efficiency limit tells you

The formula η_C = 1 − T_C/T_H has three practical readings. Raising T_H raises η_C. Lowering T_C raises η_C. Making T_C approach 0 K makes η_C approach 1, but no real cold sink is at absolute zero, so 100 % is impossible.

A real steam plant has irreversibilities: friction, finite temperature differences, and heat leaks. Its efficiency is below the Carnot value between the same boiler and condenser temperatures; the gap is lost work (see 3.7). Real Rankine cycles are treated in 3.8, and refrigeration cycles in 3.9.

The same limit applies to refrigerators. To get a large COP_R, keep T_H − T_C small. A domestic refrigerator in a hot room therefore works harder than the same refrigerator in a cool room. This is the Carnot limit in everyday use.

Worked example

Question. A Carnot engine works between a hot reservoir at 600 K and a cold reservoir at 300 K. It takes 1000 kJ from the hot reservoir per cycle. Find the Carnot efficiency η, the net work W, and the heat rejected Q_C.

  1. T_H = 600 K, T_C = 300 K, Q_H = 1000 kJ.
  2. η_C = 1 − T_C/T_H = 1 − 300/600 = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 = 50 %.
  3. W = η_C Q_H = 0.5 × 1000 kJ = 500 kJ.
  4. Energy balance: Q_H = W + Q_C, so Q_C = 1000 − 500 = 500 kJ. Check: Q_C/Q_H = 0.5 = T_C/T_H.

Answer. η = 50 %, W = 500 kJ, and Q_C = 500 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Using Celsius temperatures in η = 1 − T_C/T_H. Use kelvin; 27 °C and 327 °C are 300 K and 600 K, not 27 and 327.
  • Using η = 1 − T_C/T_H for a real engine. That formula is for a reversible Carnot engine; a real engine needs its actual Q_H and Q_C.
  • Treating Q_C/Q_H = T_C/T_H as true for every engine. The ratio is fixed only for a reversible cycle between those reservoirs.
  • Thinking a COP above 1 breaks the second law. A refrigerator COP of 9 or a heat-pump COP of 15 is allowed; efficiency η is the quantity that cannot exceed 1.
  • Writing W = Q_C − Q_H for a refrigerator. Work input is positive when W = Q_H − Q_C, because heat delivered to the hot side is larger than heat removed from the cold side.

3.4 Entropy, its calculation and the Clausius inequality

Entropy is the property that turns the second law into arithmetic. The Carnot cycle shows that δQ_rev/T sums to zero around any reversible loop, so that ratio depends only on the end states — it defines a state function S. Once you can compute ΔS, you can decide whether a process is possible, because the entropy of an isolated system never falls. Chemical engineers use this to test whether a proposed separation, heat-exchanger network or liquefaction train can work at all, and to measure how much work a real plant throws away.

You need: 3.3 Carnot cycle, the first law and the idea of a state function from Unit 1, and ideal-gas relations from Unit 2.

Entropy is a state function built from δQ_rev/T

In the Carnot cycle (see 3.3), heat Q_H enters at T_H and Q_C leaves at T_C. For a reversible cycle the efficiency is 1 − T_C/T_H, and the energy balance then gives Q_H/T_H = Q_C/T_C. Taking heat leaving as negative, the sum around the cycle is zero: Σ δQ_rev/T = 0.

Any reversible cycle can be sliced into thin Carnot strips, and each strip obeys the same result. So ∮δQ_rev/T = 0 for every reversible cycle. In Unit 1 you met the test: a quantity whose cyclic integral is zero is a state function, like internal energy. This one is called entropy, S.

dS = δQ_rev/T

dS is the change in entropy of the system (J/K); δQ_rev is the small heat added along a reversible path (J); T is the absolute temperature of the system in kelvin at which that heat crosses.

For a finite change, ΔS = ∫₁² δQ_rev/T, and the value depends only on states 1 and 2, never on the route. S is extensive, measured in J/K; per mole it is J/(mol·K), per kg J/(kg·K). Reversible heat divided by temperature is the whole definition — nothing about 'disorder' is needed at this level.

Calculating ΔS for the cases you meet

Because S is a state function, choose any convenient reversible path between the same two states. If the real path is irreversible, use a substitute path made of simple pieces.

ΔS = ∫(C_V/T)dT + R ln(V₂/V₁)

C_V is the molar heat capacity at constant volume, J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant; V₁ and V₂ are initial and final volumes; T is in kelvin.

ΔS = ∫(C_P/T)dT − R ln(P₂/P₁)

C_P is the molar heat capacity at constant pressure, J/(mol·K); P₁ and P₂ are initial and final pressures; T is in kelvin. The minus sign comes from the relation T dS = dH − V dP. For an ideal gas dH = C_P dT, so dividing by T gives dS = C_P dT/T − R dP/P; raising the pressure at constant T therefore lowers the entropy.

With constant heat capacity these integrate to ΔS = C_V ln(T₂/T₁) + R ln(V₂/V₁) and ΔS = C_P ln(T₂/T₁) − R ln(P₂/P₁). For an isothermal ideal-gas expansion the temperature terms vanish and only the volume or pressure term survives.

ΔS = ΔH/T

ΔH is the latent heat of the phase change in J/mol at temperature T in K (melting, boiling or sublimation). The sign of ΔH carries the sign of ΔS.

ΔS = ∫(C_P/T)dT = C_P ln(T₂/T₁)

the second form holds for a solid or liquid heated with constant C_P and no phase change; T₁ and T₂ are in kelvin.

  1. Fix the end states and write down their T, P and V.
  2. Break the path into constant-T, constant-P or constant-V pieces.
  3. Apply the matching formula to each piece.
  4. Add the pieces, keeping signs: melting is positive, condensation negative.

The Clausius inequality

Real cycles are irreversible. Compare any such cycle with a reversible one working between the same two reservoirs at T_H and T_C. The real efficiency is lower, so Q_C/Q_H > T_C/T_H, and with rejected heat counted negative the cyclic integral drops below zero.

∮δQ/T ≤ 0

δQ is the heat crossing the system boundary (J); T is the absolute temperature of the reservoir or boundary where that heat crosses, in K; the integral runs over the whole cycle. Zero means a reversible cycle, negative means irreversible, positive is impossible.

This is the Clausius inequality. Some syllabi call it the mathematical statement of the second law, because it converts the words of 3.1 into a number you can evaluate for any proposed device.

ΔS_system ≥ ∫₁² δQ/T

the process form, for a change from state 1 to state 2; T is the temperature of the boundary where δQ crosses, in K. Equality holds only for a reversible path. When the boundary temperature is a constant T₀ this reduces to ΔS_system ≥ Q/T₀.

Now apply it to a system plus its surroundings at T₀. The surroundings exchange heat reversibly, so their entropy change is −Q/T₀, and the inequality rearranges to ΔS_system − Q/T₀ ≥ 0, that is, ΔS_universe ≥ 0. For an isolated system, ΔS ≥ 0, with equality only when everything inside is reversible.

S_gen = ΔS_sys + ΔS_sur ≥ 0

S_gen is the entropy generated by irreversibility, J/K; it is zero for a reversible process and positive for every real one. The work lost because of it is the theme of 3.7.

Worked example

Question. One mole of an ideal gas at 300 K expands reversibly and isothermally from 10 L to 20 L. Find the heat added, the entropy change of the gas, and the entropy change of the surroundings.

  1. Reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas: ΔU = 0, so Q_rev = W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  2. Volume ratio V₂/V₁ = 20/10 = 2, and ln 2 = 0.6931.
  3. Q_rev = 1 mol × 8.314 J/(mol·K) × 300 K × 0.6931 = 1728.8 J.
  4. ΔS_gas = Q_rev/T = 1728.8 J / 300 K = 5.76 J/K. Cross-check with R ln(V₂/V₁) = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K.
  5. The gas takes this heat from the reservoir, so ΔS_sur = −1728.8/300 = −5.76 J/K, and ΔS_universe = 0, as it must be for a reversible process.

Answer. Q_rev = 1728.8 J, ΔS of the gas = 5.76 J/K, and ΔS of the surroundings = −5.76 J/K.

Exam traps

  • Using the real irreversible heat in dS = δQ/T. The actual Q/T is not the entropy change; compute ΔS along a reversible substitute between the same end states.
  • Putting the system temperature instead of the boundary temperature in ∮δQ/T. For an irreversible process the T belongs to the reservoir where the heat crosses.
  • Swapping the signs of the two ideal-gas forms: the volume term is +R ln(V₂/V₁), the pressure term is −R ln(P₂/P₁). Using plus with pressure gives the wrong sign in compression.
  • Forgetting that freezing and condensation have negative ΔS, because their ΔH is negative and ΔS = ΔH/T keeps that sign.
  • Unit slip: R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) with T in kelvin. Writing 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K) leaves the answer in L·bar/K, not J/K.

3.5 Entropy balance for open systems (control volume)

An open control volume has streams crossing its boundary. Each stream carries entropy at rate m s. Heat crossing the boundary also carries entropy at rate Q/T. Inside, friction, mixing and finite gradients create extra entropy. The entropy balance adds these terms and says the entropy stored in the control volume changes by what flows in minus what flows out plus what is generated. At steady state the stored entropy does not change, so generation equals outlet entropy flow minus inlet entropy flow minus heat entropy flow. Engineers use it to test turbines, compressors, nozzles and heat exchangers for irreversibility.

You need: 3.4 Entropy and the steady-flow mass and energy balances from Unit 1.

The control-volume entropy balance

Take a control volume (CV), a fixed region in space. Let m_i be mass flow at inlet i in kg/s and s_i its specific entropy in kJ/(kg·K). Let m_e and s_e be the same at outlet e. Let Q_j be heat rate into the CV across boundary j in kW, and T_j the absolute temperature of that boundary in K. Let S_CV be total entropy inside the CV in kJ/K, and S_gen the entropy generation rate in kW/K. The balance is:

dS_CV/dt = Σ m_i s_i − Σ m_e s_e + Σ Q_j/T_j + S_gen

S_CV is total entropy inside the CV in kJ/K; t is time in s; m_i is inlet mass flow in kg/s; s_i is inlet specific entropy in kJ/(kg·K); m_e and s_e are outlet mass flow and specific entropy; Q_j is heat rate into the CV in kW; T_j is the uniform absolute temperature of boundary j in K; if the boundary temperature varies, replace Q_j/T_j by the integral ∫ δQ/T over that boundary; S_gen is entropy generation rate in kW/K.

The first term is accumulation. The next two are entropy carried by flow. The heat term is entropy carried by heat; use T_j in kelvin. S_gen comes from irreversibility inside: friction, mixing, chemical reaction, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference. The second law gives S_gen ≥ 0, with equality only for a reversible process. S_gen is not a state property; it is fixed by the process. At steady state dS_CV/dt = 0, so S_gen = Σ m_e s_e − Σ m_i s_i − Σ Q_j/T_j. A negative computed S_gen means the data or sign convention is wrong.

Adiabatic devices and heat exchangers

For one inlet and one outlet, steady, adiabatic (Q_j = 0), the balance reduces to:

S_gen = m(s_out − s_in)

m is mass flow in kg/s; s_in and s_out are inlet and outlet specific entropies in kJ/(kg·K); S_gen is entropy generation rate in kW/K.

Work and kinetic energy do not appear: entropy crosses only with mass and heat. A real adiabatic turbine has s_out > s_in because friction and losses generate entropy; the ideal isentropic turbine sets s_out = s_in and S_gen = 0. A compressor needs work; if adiabatic, the same formula applies and real s_out > s_in. If the compressor is cooled, heat leaves, so Q_j is negative; use the full balance. A nozzle has no work and is often adiabatic: S_gen = m(s_out − s_in), and an ideal nozzle is isentropic.

For a two-stream heat exchanger with no external heat, enclose both streams in the CV. Then S_gen = m_h(s_h,out − s_h,in) + m_c(s_c,out − s_c,in). Internal heat from hot to cold stays inside the CV and does not appear as Q_j. If the exchanger also loses heat to the room, the leak raises the generation: S_gen = m_h(s_h,out − s_h,in) + m_c(s_c,out − s_c,in) − Q_room/T_room, with Q_room negative when heat leaves, so the last term is positive. Some syllabi call the steady balance the second law for a control volume.

A short procedure

  1. Draw the CV and mark every inlet, outlet and heat crossing.
  2. Decide steady or transient.
  3. Write m and s for each stream, using kJ/(kg·K).
  4. Write each Q_j as positive into the CV and T_j in K.
  5. Put terms in the balance and solve for S_gen. Check units and sign.

Check units: kg/s × kJ/(kg·K) = kW/K; kW divided by K = kW/K. Check sign: S_gen cannot be negative. For a reversible adiabatic device, S_gen = 0. For a real adiabatic device, outlet specific entropy is greater than inlet. The same S_gen becomes lost work when multiplied by T₀; see 3.7 Irreversibility, lost work and ideal work.

Worked example

Question. Steam enters an adiabatic turbine. Take s_in = 7.0 kJ/(kg·K) and s_out = 7.4 kJ/(kg·K) as the given data (rounded for arithmetic). The mass flow is 2 kg/s. The turbine is steady and adiabatic. Find the entropy generation rate. State whether the turbine is reversible.

  1. Choose a CV around the turbine. Steady flow: dS_CV/dt = 0. One inlet, one outlet, adiabatic: Q_j = 0.
  2. Write the balance: 0 = m s_in − m s_out + S_gen.
  3. Solve: S_gen = m(s_out − s_in).
  4. Substitute: S_gen = 2 kg/s × (7.4 − 7.0) kJ/(kg·K) = 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 kJ/(s·K).
  5. Convert units: 1 kJ/s = 1 kW, so S_gen = 0.8 kW/K.

Answer. S_gen = 0.8 kW/K. The positive value shows the turbine is irreversible, not isentropic.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting S_gen and treating an adiabatic real turbine as isentropic. A real adiabatic turbine has s_out > s_in.
  • Reversing the order: for an adiabatic device, S_gen = m(s_out − s_in), not m(s_in − s_out). A negative answer is impossible.
  • Using the fluid temperature instead of the boundary temperature in Q_j/T_j. The temperature is the absolute temperature where heat crosses the CV boundary, in K.
  • Mixing up mass flow and total mass. m s has units kg/s × kJ/(kg·K) = kW/K; using kg instead of kg/s gives the wrong unit.
  • Setting S_gen = 0 for a two-stream heat exchanger because there is no external heat. Internal heat exchange can still generate entropy; add the entropy changes of both streams.

3.6 Third law of thermodynamics

The third law gives entropy a fixed zero. For a perfect ordered crystal at absolute zero, S = 0. Then every absolute entropy can be found by heating the substance from 0 K and summing C_p/T. This sounds abstract, but it is how entropy tables are built. It also explains why some solids keep a small leftover entropy after cooling, and why no laboratory reaches 0 K exactly. Engineers use it to compare absolute entropies in power cycles, refrigerators and chemical reactions.

You need entropy from Chapter 3.4 and heat capacity from Unit 2.

The third law and the Nernst theorem

Absolute zero is 0 K, about −273.15 °C. A perfect crystal is a solid with every atom or molecule in one ordered arrangement, with no frozen-in disorder. The Nernst heat theorem says that for condensed phases near absolute zero, the entropy change of a reversible isothermal process tends to zero as T → 0. In plain form, the third law states: the entropy of a perfect ordered crystal is zero at 0 K.

This is a reference point, not a calculation trick. Before the third law, entropy changes could be measured but absolute entropy had no origin. The law chooses S(0 K) = 0 for the perfect crystal. Then S at any T is an absolute entropy. Tables of standard molar entropy, S°, use this choice. The value at 298.15 K is not zero, and 0 °C is not the zero either. Only 0 K is.

The law applies to a perfect crystal. A real crystal can have defects, mixed orientations or frozen disorder. Those solids may have residual entropy at 0 K. The next sections separate the ideal case from these real cases.

Absolute entropy from heat capacity

For reversible heating at constant pressure, dS = C_p dT / T. Start at 0 K with S(0) = 0 and integrate to T:

S(T) = ∫₀ᵀ (C_p / T) dT + Σ (ΔH_tr / T_tr)

S(T) is absolute molar entropy at T in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; C_p is heat capacity at constant pressure in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; ΔH_tr is enthalpy of a phase transition in J mol⁻¹; T_tr is the transition temperature in K. The sum adds each solid-solid or melting transition between 0 K and T.

The integral cannot start at exactly 0 K in a real measurement, because C_p/T is hard to measure and 0 K is unattainable. Experimenters measure C_p down to a low temperature, often a few kelvin. Below that they fit the low-temperature law C_p = aT³. Then the integral from 0 K to that low temperature is finite and easy: ∫₀ᵀ aT² dT = aT³/3. Here a is a constant with units J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴. The cubic form is the Debye limit for non-metals; metals add an electronic term.

Procedure to build an absolute entropy:

  1. Measure C_p at many temperatures above the lowest measured point.
  2. Fit C_p = aT³ below that point and add aT³/3.
  3. Add ΔH_tr/T_tr for every phase transition on the path.
  4. Add ∫(C_p/T)dT over each single-phase range.
  5. Report S(T) with the third-law zero at 0 K.

This is why entropy tables need heat-capacity data, not just one calorimeter reading.

Residual entropy and the unattainable zero

Residual entropy is entropy that remains at 0 K when a solid is not a perfect ordered crystal. Carbon monoxide is the standard example. The molecules CO are almost symmetric in size, so in a crystal they can point CO or OC with little energy difference. Cooling freezes a random arrangement. The crystal has S(0) > 0, roughly R ln 2 per mole if two orientations are equally likely. Glasses are another case: a liquid's disorder is frozen in before it can crystallize, so the glass keeps extra entropy.

The third law needs the words perfect crystal. For CO, glass or a crystal with frozen defects, S(0) is not zero. The law still sets the zero for the perfectly ordered reference, and the measured entropy of the real solid is larger by the residual amount.

The third law also says absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. As a system is cooled toward 0 K, each step removes less entropy and the cooling power falls. A real cryostat can reach very low temperatures, but it cannot cross to 0 K. This is the unattainability statement. It is consistent with S → 0: at 0 K the entropy would be fixed, and no finite process can take a system there. In engineering, the practical message is that reaching lower temperatures costs more work and better insulation; see Chapter 3.9.

Worked example

Question. A crystal obeys C_p = aT³ at low temperature with a = 0.001 J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴. Using S(0) = 0, find S at 10 K.

  1. Identify the low-temperature heat capacity: C_p = aT³, with a = 0.001 J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴.
  2. Use the third-law integral: S(10 K) − S(0 K) = ∫₀¹⁰ (C_p/T) dT.
  3. Set S(0 K) = 0 for the perfect crystal.
  4. Substitute C_p: C_p/T = aT³/T = aT².
  5. Integrate: S(10 K) = ∫₀¹⁰ aT² dT = a[T³/3]₀¹⁰ = a(1000/3) K³.
  6. Insert a: S = 0.001 × 1000/3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.3333 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Answer. S(10 K) = 0.333 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Using S = 0 at 0 °C or at 298 K. The third-law zero is 0 K, not the ice point or room temperature.
  • Forgetting to divide C_p by T. The entropy integral is ∫(C_p/T)dT, not ∫C_p dT.
  • Assuming every solid has S(0) = 0. Only a perfect ordered crystal does; CO and glasses keep residual entropy.
  • Omitting phase-transition terms. A complete absolute entropy adds ΔH_tr/T_tr at each transition between 0 K and T.
  • Thinking enough cooling steps can reach exactly 0 K. The third law says 0 K is unattainable in a finite number of steps.

3.7 Irreversibility, lost work and ideal work

Every real process generates entropy, and that entropy generation carries a work penalty. With surroundings at temperature T₀, take work input as positive. The ideal work of a steady-flow unit is W_ideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS. When W_ideal is negative the unit produces work, and the best work it can give is −W_ideal = T₀ΔS − ΔH. The difference W_actual − W_ideal is the lost work, W_lost = T₀S_gen. Power plants, refrigerators and heat-recovery networks use this number to see where useful work is thrown away.

You need entropy, entropy generation and the entropy balance from 3.4 and 3.5, plus the steady-flow energy balance from Unit 1.

Ideal work and the dead state

Take the surroundings as a large reservoir at temperature T₀. T₀ is the dead-state temperature: heat exchanged with the environment at T₀ has zero work potential. A process can be reversible only if heat crosses its boundary at T₀, that is, with no finite temperature difference. Exchanging heat with the environment at T₀ is the necessary condition for the ideal work formula to apply.

For a steady-flow unit with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes, take work input as positive and write W_ideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS. Here ΔH is outlet minus inlet enthalpy in kJ, ΔS is outlet minus inlet entropy in kJ/K, and T₀ is in K. A negative W_ideal means the unit produces work; the ideal output is −W_ideal, the maximum possible. A positive W_ideal is the minimum work input.

Only a few universities teach this as exergy or availability. Do not confuse W_ideal with the Gibbs free energy from Unit 4; that uses the system temperature, not T₀.

W_ideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS

W_ideal is reversible work input in kJ (negative means work output); ΔH is outlet minus inlet enthalpy in kJ; ΔS is outlet minus inlet entropy in kJ/K; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K.

Gouy–Stodola: entropy generation becomes lost work

The second law gives the price of irreversibility. For any process, the total entropy generation is S_gen = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings, with both changes in kJ/K. For an isolated composite system the surroundings term is already inside the system boundary and S_gen is just the total entropy change. The Gouy–Stodola theorem states that W_lost = T₀S_gen. S_gen is in kJ/K and T₀ is in K, so W_lost is in kJ. A reversible process has S_gen = 0 and loses no work.

Lost work is also called exergy destruction. With work input positive, W_lost = W_actual − W_ideal; this stays positive for turbines and compressors. For a turbine W_ideal is more negative than W_actual, so subtracting gives a positive loss. The lost work is always positive. Adding it to actual work is wrong; it is already gone.

Use T₀, not the hot or cold stream temperature, because the environment is the only free heat reservoir. If a plant rejects heat at 450 K but the outside air is at 300 K, the 300 K value sets the work value of that heat.

W_lost = T₀ S_gen

W_lost is lost work in kJ; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K; S_gen is total entropy generation in kJ/K.

Worked example

Question. Heat of 1000 kJ passes from a reservoir at 500 K to a reservoir at 450 K. The surroundings are at 300 K. Find the entropy generated and the lost work. Treat the two reservoirs and the heat path as the system.

  1. Hot reservoir: ΔS_hot = −Q/T_hot = −1000 kJ / 500 K = −2.000 kJ/K.
  2. Cold reservoir: ΔS_cold = +Q/T_cold = +1000 kJ / 450 K = +2.222 kJ/K.
  3. The composite system is isolated, so S_gen = ΔS_hot + ΔS_cold = −2.000 + 2.222 = 0.222 kJ/K.
  4. Lost work: W_lost = T₀ S_gen = 300 K × 0.222 kJ/K = 66.7 kJ.

Answer. S_gen = 0.222 kJ/K and W_lost = 66.7 kJ; the heat transfer destroys 66.7 kJ of potential work.

Exam traps

  • Do not add lost work to actual work. With work input positive, use W_lost = W_actual − W_ideal; for a turbine this gives a positive loss because W_actual is less negative than W_ideal.
  • Use T₀, the environment temperature, not the hot reservoir or cold reservoir temperature, inside W_lost = T₀S_gen.
  • S_gen is total entropy generation for system plus surroundings. Using only the steam or gas entropy change misses the surroundings and gives the wrong lost work.
  • Check units: S_gen in kJ/K times T₀ in K gives kJ. Mixing J/K with K gives a factor of 1000 error.

3.8 Steam power plant cycles

A steam power plant burns coal or gas to boil water, spins a turbine with the steam, and condenses the exhaust back to liquid so the loop can repeat. The four devices — pump, boiler, turbine, condenser — form the Rankine cycle. Almost all thermal electricity in India, whether from coal, nuclear or solar-thermal heat, comes from this cycle, so its efficiency decides how much fuel a unit burns for every kWh it sends out.

You need: Carnot cycle, entropy, the steady-flow energy balance from Unit 1, and steam-table reading from Unit 4 (property tables).

Four devices in a closed loop

Water circulates. The pump takes saturated liquid from the condenser and raises its pressure to boiler pressure; the liquid temperature rises only a little because liquid water is nearly incompressible. The boiler adds heat at almost constant pressure: the water warms to saturation, boils, and is usually superheated. The turbine expands the high-pressure steam and delivers shaft work, so pressure and temperature fall. The condenser rejects heat at constant pressure and temperature, turning the exhaust back into saturated liquid.

For each device, the steady-flow energy balance with negligible kinetic and potential energy gives q − w = Δh. With the state numbers of the figure: turbine work w_t = h₃ − h₄, pump work w_p = h₂ − h₁, boiler heat q_in = h₃ − h₂, condenser heat q_out = h₄ − h₁. Here h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg, q and w are per kg of water. w_t and w_p are both taken as positive magnitudes — w_t is work delivered by the turbine, w_p is work absorbed by the pump. In the sign convention of q − w = Δh, the pump work is w = −w_p.

A Carnot cycle between the same temperature limits is more efficient, but it cannot be built in steam: compressing a wet mixture needs a huge two-phase compressor running at turbine speed. The Rankine cycle finishes condensation in the condenser and compresses liquid only, which needs a small pump. The price is a lower mean temperature at which heat is added, so efficiency stays well below the Carnot value.

On a T–s diagram the saturated liquid and vapour lines form the dome. Pump work is a short near-vertical line on the liquid side. Boiler heating runs right along a constant-pressure line to superheated steam. Turbine expansion drops down and to the right, often into the wet region. Condensation is a horizontal line at condenser temperature.

Efficiency from steam tables

The thermal efficiency compares net work out with heat paid for in the boiler. The pump work is small compared with turbine work, but it is real work bought from the same shaft, so it must be subtracted.

η = (w_t − w_p) / q_in = [(h₃ − h₄) − (h₂ − h₁)] / (h₃ − h₂)

η is the cycle thermal efficiency (dimensionless); h₃ is turbine-inlet enthalpy, h₄ turbine-exit enthalpy, h₁ condenser-exit (pump-inlet) enthalpy, h₂ pump-exit (boiler-inlet) enthalpy, all in kJ/kg.

  1. Fix the boiler pressure p_b and the condenser pressure p_c.
  2. At the turbine inlet (state 3) take the known pressure and temperature, read h₃ and s₃ from the superheated steam table.
  3. For an ideal turbine, s₄ = s₃ at pressure p_c; find quality x₄ = (s₃ − s_f)/s_fg, then h₄ = h_f + x₄ h_fg.
  4. Pump work for liquid: w_p ≈ v_f (p_b − p_c), with v_f in m³/kg and pressures in kPa, giving kJ/kg. Then h₂ = h₁ + w_p.
  5. Boiler heat q_in = h₃ − h₂, net work w_net = (h₃ − h₄) − w_p, and η = w_net/q_in.

Real machines fall short. Turbine isentropic efficiency η_t = (h₃ − h₄)/(h₃ − h₄s) is about 0.85 to 0.92, so the actual exit has higher entropy, higher enthalpy and more moisture than the ideal one. Pump isentropic efficiency is 0.70 to 0.85, but because w_p is only 1 to 3 percent of turbine work, its uncertainty barely moves the cycle efficiency.

Typical large utility values: boiler at 100 bar and 540 °C, condenser at 0.1 bar where saturation temperature is about 46 °C. The ideal cycle gives roughly 40 percent; with real turbine and pump it drops to about 35 to 38 percent.

Reheat, regeneration and condenser pressure

  • Reheat: expand in the turbine to an intermediate pressure, pipe the steam back to the boiler to reheat at constant pressure, then expand again to the condenser. The average temperature of heat addition rises, and the exhaust stays drier — quality above about 0.88, which protects the last rows of blades. Efficiency gain is modest, a few percent.
  • Regenerative feed heating: bleed a fraction of steam from the turbine at several points and use it to warm the feedwater in closed heaters. The boiler then receives hotter water, so q_in falls for the same turbine output. Bleeding removes steam that would have made work, so the gain is real but bounded; practical plants use four to seven heaters.
  • Condenser pressure: a lower p_c means a lower temperature of heat rejection, which raises efficiency by the Carnot argument. The limit is the cooling water available — river or cooling-tower water near 30 °C — plus air leakage into the vacuum and the length of the last turbine blades.

Raising boiler pressure also raises efficiency, but it pushes the turbine exhaust deeper into the wet region, so higher pressure goes together with superheat and reheat. Supercritical units run above the critical pressure of water, 220.6 bar, where there is no boiling stage at all.

Worked example

Question. In an ideal Rankine cycle, steam enters the turbine with h = 3200 kJ/kg and leaves with h = 2100 kJ/kg. Saturated liquid leaves the condenser with h = 200 kJ/kg, and the pump work is 20 kJ/kg. Find the net work, the heat added in the boiler, the cycle efficiency and the heat rejected in the condenser.

  1. Turbine work: w_t = h₃ − h₄ = 3200 − 2100 = 1100 kJ/kg.
  2. Feedwater enthalpy after the pump: h₂ = h₁ + w_p = 200 + 20 = 220 kJ/kg.
  3. Net work: w_net = w_t − w_p = 1100 − 20 = 1080 kJ/kg.
  4. Heat added in the boiler: q_in = h₃ − h₂ = 3200 − 220 = 2980 kJ/kg.
  5. Efficiency: η = w_net/q_in = 1080/2980 = 0.3624, that is 36.2 percent.
  6. Heat rejected: q_out = h₄ − h₁ = 2100 − 200 = 1900 kJ/kg.
  7. Check the energy balance: q_in − q_out = 2980 − 1900 = 1080 kJ/kg, equal to w_net.

Answer. Net work is 1080 kJ/kg, heat added is 2980 kJ/kg, cycle efficiency is 36.2 percent, and heat rejected is 1900 kJ/kg.

Exam traps

  • Using turbine work alone as net work. Subtract the pump work: w_net = w_t − w_p.
  • Taking q_in from the condenser exit enthalpy h₁. The boiler receives feedwater after the pump, so use h₂ = h₁ + w_p.
  • Mixing bar, kPa and MPa in w_p = v_f Δp. Convert pressure difference to kPa so that v_f in m³/kg gives kJ/kg directly.
  • Confusing turbine isentropic efficiency with cycle efficiency. One compares actual with ideal expansion; the other compares net work with boiler heat.
  • Forgetting that in the condenser the temperature stays constant only while the steam is wet. Once fully condensed, further cooling drops the temperature below saturation.

3.9 Refrigeration and liquefaction processes

A refrigerator does not create cold; it moves heat from a cold space to a warmer one, and that costs work. The same cycle with a different purpose is a heat pump: it is the condenser heat Q_out, not the evaporator heat Q_in, that we want. Industry uses these cycles in kitchen refrigerators, air conditioners, cold stores, server rooms and vaccine storage. Liquefaction of gases such as nitrogen and natural gas pushes the same idea to very low temperatures. This chapter builds the vapour-compression cycle, defines its coefficient of performance, compares it with the Carnot limit, and shows how liquefaction cycles are arranged.

You need the steady-flow device analysis from 3.2, the Carnot COP in 3.3, and refrigerant property tables from Unit 4, Property tables and diagrams.

The four devices of the vapour-compression cycle

Refrigeration keeps a space colder than its surroundings. Heat leaks in, so a device must continuously pump that heat to a warmer place. The vapour-compression cycle does this by making a fluid boil at low pressure, absorbing heat, and condense at high pressure, releasing heat.

A liquid boils at a temperature fixed by its pressure. Lower the pressure, and the boiling point falls. Pick a refrigerant whose boiling point at a few atmospheres is below the cold-space temperature; the cold space then supplies the heat of boiling. The cycle runs in four steps.

  1. Evaporator. A low-pressure liquid–vapour mixture boils at constant pressure and temperature, taking heat Q_in from the cold space.
  2. Compressor. Low-pressure vapour is squeezed to high pressure. Work W_in goes in, so the vapour leaves superheated and hotter.
  3. Condenser. Hot high-pressure vapour gives heat Q_out to surroundings and condenses to liquid.
  4. Expansion valve (throttle). Liquid drops to low pressure through a restriction. No work is produced, enthalpy stays nearly constant, temperature falls, and a little liquid flashes to vapour. The cold mixture returns to the evaporator.

For steady flow, neglecting kinetic and potential energy, Q_in + W_in = Q_out. The condenser duty is always larger than the refrigerating effect by the compressor work.

Coefficient of performance and the Carnot limit

Efficiency is a poor word for a refrigerator, because we want heat removed, not work. The yardstick is the coefficient of performance (COP).

COP_R = Q_in / W_in = Q_in / (Q_out − Q_in)

COP_R is the coefficient of performance of the refrigerator (dimensionless); Q_in is the heat taken from the cold space in kW; Q_out is the heat rejected at the condenser in kW; W_in is the compressor work in kW.

COP_HP = Q_out / W_in = COP_R + 1

COP_HP is the heat-pump COP; Q_out is heat delivered to the warm room in kW. COP_HP = COP_R + 1 because the work also ends up in the room.

No cycle between cold at T_C and warm at T_H can beat the reversed Carnot cycle. Its COP depends only on the two absolute temperatures.

COP_R,Carnot = T_C / (T_H − T_C)

T_C is cold-region temperature in kelvin; T_H is warm-region temperature in kelvin. Use kelvin, never Celsius, because the ratio must stay positive and correct.

Real machines reach roughly half the Carnot value: compression is not isentropic, the throttle wastes pressure energy, and heat exchangers need a finite temperature difference. That gap is destroyed work, as in 3.7.

Real cycles, Joule–Thomson expansion and liquefaction

The ideal cycle assumes saturated vapour entering the compressor and saturated liquid leaving the condenser. Practical cycles differ in three ways.

  • The vapour is superheated a few degrees before compression. Liquid droplets damage a compressor, and superheat guarantees dry vapour.
  • The condensate is subcooled, raising refrigerating effect per kilogram.
  • Compression is not isentropic; isentropic efficiency is typically 0.7 to 0.85. Use the actual compressor exit enthalpy, not the isentropic one.

The expansion valve is a throttling device: enthalpy is constant across it and entropy rises, so it is irreversible. For most gases below their inversion temperature, throttling also lowers temperature. This is the Joule–Thomson effect, and it makes gas liquefaction possible without moving cold parts.

Liquefaction needs cooling below the critical temperature of the gas, the point beyond which no pressure can condense a vapour, as 3.2 shows.

  • Linde cycle. One compressor, a counter-current heat exchanger and a Joule–Thomson valve. Part of the gas liquefies; cold unliquefied gas returns through the exchanger to pre-cool the incoming stream.
  • Claude cycle. Adds an expansion engine, so part of the gas expands while doing work. Work removal gives far more cooling per kilogram, so Claude suits large tonnages.

Worked example

Question. A refrigerator removes 5 kW from a cold space at 250 K and rejects heat to surroundings at 300 K. Its actual COP is 2.5. Find (a) compressor work, (b) Carnot COP between the same temperatures, and (c) minimum work for the same 5 kW.

  1. COP_R = Q_in / W_in, with Q_in = 5 kW and COP_R = 2.5.
  2. W_in = Q_in / COP_R = 5 / 2.5 = 2 kW.
  3. Energy balance: Q_out = Q_in + W_in = 5 + 2 = 7 kW, the condenser duty.
  4. Temperatures are already absolute: T_C = 250 K, T_H = 300 K.
  5. COP_Carnot = T_C / (T_H − T_C) = 250 / (300 − 250) = 5.
  6. W_min = Q_in / COP_Carnot = 5 / 5 = 1 kW.
  7. The real machine uses 2 kW where 1 kW would suffice reversibly; lost work is 1 kW and second-law efficiency is 1/2 = 0.50.

Answer. Compressor work is 2 kW, the Carnot COP is 5, and the minimum work for the same duty is 1 kW.

Exam traps

  • Putting Celsius temperatures into COP_Carnot = T_C/(T_H − T_C). Take a cold space at −23 °C (250 K) and surroundings at 27 °C (300 K). The correct COP is 250/(300 − 250) = 5. Using Celsius gives −23/(27 − (−23)) = −23/50 = −0.46, a negative nonsense value. The difference 27 − (−23) survives in either scale, but the numerator must be absolute, so T_C = 250 K, not −23.
  • Forgetting COP_HP = COP_R + 1; a heat pump shows a larger number than the refrigerator it is built from.
  • Treating the expansion valve as a turbine. It is a throttle: h is constant, no work, and entropy rises.
  • Using isentropic compressor exit enthalpy when isentropic efficiency is given; the real exit state has higher enthalpy and temperature.
  • Writing evaporator heat as Q_out. The evaporator takes heat in; the condenser rejects heat out.

3.10 Entropy from a microscopic point of view

Entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements fit the same macroscopic state. Two samples can have the same energy, volume and number of particles yet very different numbers of ways to arrange those particles. Boltzmann turned that count into a number: S = kB ln Ω. The idea exists because the Clausius definition from 3.4 tells you how entropy changes, but not what entropy is counting. Engineers use it to judge disorder in alloys, glasses and crystals, and to connect the third law to a real solid.

You need entropy as a state property from 3.4 and the third law from 3.6.

Microstates and macrostates

A microstate is one exact arrangement of the particles in a system. For a gas, it fixes the position and momentum of every molecule. For a simple model, it can be as coarse as naming which box each labelled particle occupies. A macrostate is what you measure on the bench: total energy U, volume V, number of particles N, pressure, temperature. Many microstates can give the same macrostate.

Take two distinguishable particles A and B and two boxes, left and right. The four microstates are: both left, A left and B right, A right and B left, both right. If all four have the same energy, the macrostate 'any arrangement' has Ω = 4 microstates. The macrostate 'one particle on each side' has only 2 of those microstates.

The basic assumption of statistical thermodynamics is that, at fixed U, V and N, every accessible microstate is equally likely. Entropy is then fixed by how many microstates are accessible. Liquid water has many more ways to share its thermal energy than ice, which is why the liquid has higher entropy.

The Boltzmann relation

S = kB ln Ω

S is entropy in J/K; kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; ln is the natural logarithm; Ω is the number of accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N, dimensionless and at least 1.

The logarithm makes entropy additive. Two independent systems have Ω = Ω₁ Ω₂, so S = kB ln(Ω₁ Ω₂) = kB ln Ω₁ + kB ln Ω₂ = S₁ + S₂. Entropy of the pair is the sum, as thermodynamics demands.

When Ω = 1, ln 1 = 0 and S = 0. A single possible arrangement carries no entropy. Doubling Ω adds kB ln 2 = 0.693 kB, about 9.57 × 10⁻²⁴ J/K, whatever the starting count. Entropy therefore grows slowly with Ω; the figure shows S/kB against Ω.

Third law and residual entropy

At absolute zero a perfect crystal has every atom in its lowest-energy position. There is one way to build that crystal, so Ω = 1 and S = kB ln 1 = 0. This is the microscopic reason for the third law: perfect crystals have zero entropy at 0 K.

Some real solids never reach that single arrangement. Carbon monoxide molecules can point in two directions in the crystal, and cooling locks in a random mixture. Ice has protons arranged with many equivalent hydrogen-bond patterns. A glass is frozen in a liquid-like arrangement. Each has Ω > 1 even at 0 K, so S = kB ln Ω gives a positive residual entropy. The third law is not broken; the crystal is simply not perfect.

Entropy is not merely 'disorder'. A system can look messy but have few accessible microstates, or look ordered but have many. The correct statement is: entropy counts accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N. Some syllabi call this the statistical interpretation of entropy.

Worked example

Question. Two distinguishable particles A and B can each sit in one of two boxes, left or right. Every arrangement has the same energy. (a) List the microstates. (b) Find Ω. (c) Find S/kB. (d) Find S using kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K.

  1. Label the boxes L and R. Particle A has two choices, particle B has two choices. Independent choices give 2 × 2 = 4 microstates.
  2. List them: (A L, B L), (A L, B R), (A R, B L), (A R, B R). Each is one microstate. So Ω = 4.
  3. S/kB = ln Ω = ln 4 = 1.386.
  4. S = kB × 1.386 = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 1.386 = 1.914 × 10⁻²³ J/K, which rounds to 1.91 × 10⁻²³ J/K.

Answer. Ω = 4; S/kB = 1.386; S = 1.91 × 10⁻²³ J/K.

Exam traps

  • Entropy is not just 'disorder'; it is the count of accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N. A visually messy system can have low entropy if few arrangements are accessible.
  • Ω is a count of microstates, not a probability and not work. It is dimensionless and cannot be less than 1.
  • Using R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) instead of kB gives molar entropy in J/(mol·K). The formula S = kB ln Ω is for one system of N particles, not per mole unless you use R and count per mole.
  • S = 0 at 0 K only for a perfect crystal with a unique ground state. Glasses, CO and ice can keep residual entropy.
  • Do not say 'entropy is the number of microstates'; entropy is proportional to ln Ω, so it is not Ω itself. Doubling Ω does not double S.

MicrostateOne specific microscopic arrangement of the molecules of a system; a macrostate is a set of microstates with the same measured properties.3.10
Residual entropyEntropy still present at 0 K when a crystal is not perfect, for example because molecules freeze in random orientations.3.10

Takeaways

  1. The second law is a statement about direction: work can turn completely into heat, but heat cannot turn completely into work in a cycle.
  2. A heat engine, a refrigerator and a heat pump are the same energy balance read in three different directions.
  3. The Carnot efficiency 1 − T_C/T_H is a ceiling set only by reservoir temperatures, not by the fluid or the machine.
  4. Entropy is a state function; ΔS = ∫δQ_rev/T over any convenient reversible path between the same two states.
  5. The Clausius inequality ∮δQ/T ≤ 0 is the seed from which the whole entropy concept grows.
  6. For a control volume, entropy carried in by flow and heat plus entropy generated equals entropy carried out plus accumulation.
  7. Every real process destroys work equal to T₀ S_gen, and that gap is what a second-law (exergy) analysis measures.
  8. Real power plants and refrigerators are engineered by pushing their cycles closer to the reversible ideal — reheat, regeneration, and careful choice of boiler, condenser and evaporator pressures.

Exam question bank

Long answers usually ask for a statement, a labelled sketch and a derivation, so show the equivalence of the two second-law statements and derive efficiency or COP from first principles before plugging in numbers.

Remember and understand

Remember State the Kelvin–Planck and the Clausius statements of the second law and show that each one implies the other. 10 marks

Write both statements exactly, naming the reservoirs and the cycle; Assume a Kelvin–Planck violator, couple it to a reversible refrigerator, and show heat is pumped from cold to hot with no work — a Clausius violation; Assume a Clausius violator, couple it to a reversible heat engine, and show a single reservoir driving a cycle to give work — a Kelvin–Planck violation; Conclude both statements are equivalent forms of the same law.

Understand Define entropy as a state function. Show that ∫δQ_rev/T between two states is independent of the reversible path. 10 marks

Show that ∮δQ_rev/T = 0 for a reversible cycle using the Carnot result Q_C/Q_H = T_C/T_H; Split a reversible cycle into two paths A and B between states 1 and 2; Conclude that ∫δQ_rev/T is the same along A and B, so dS = δQ_rev/T is exact; State that S is a property, and compare with the path functions Q and W.

Understand State the third law of thermodynamics and explain why absolute entropies can be tabulated and why residual entropy can still appear at 0 K. 8 marks

Perfect crystal at 0 K has one microstate, so S = 0; Entropy integrals ∫(C_p/T)dT are finite from 0 K upward, so a reference zero is usable; Real crystals may freeze in disordered orientations (CO, glassy solids), leaving residual entropy; Mention that the third law lets tables report absolute S values rather than differences.

Understand Explain the microscopic meaning of entropy using S = k_B ln Ω, with the example of gas molecules in two halves of a box. 8 marks

Define microstate and macrostate; k_B = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K; Number of ways to place N molecules so that n are in one half: Ω = N!/[n!(N−n)!]; All-in-one-half has Ω = 1, S = 0; even split has the largest Ω and the largest S; This explains why gas expands to fill both halves — the system drifts to the most probable macrostate.

Apply

Apply A Carnot engine works between 800 K and 300 K and receives 500 kJ from the hot reservoir. Find the efficiency, the work output and the heat rejected. Then find the COP of a Carnot refrigerator working between the same two temperatures. 10 marks

η = 1 − T_C/T_H = 1 − 300/800 = 0.625; W = ηQ_H = 0.625 × 500 = 312.5 kJ; Q_C = 187.5 kJ; COP_R = T_C/(T_H − T_C) = 300/500 = 0.6; COP_HP = COP_R + 1 = 1.6; keep units kJ and note temperatures must be in K.

Apply Calculate the entropy change when 2 kg of water is heated from 300 K to 350 K at constant pressure given c_p = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K), and when the same water is frozen at 273 K with latent heat 334 kJ/kg. 12 marks

Heating: ΔS = m c_p ln(T₂/T₁) = 2 × 4.18 × ln(350/300) = 1.29 kJ/K; Freezing: ΔS = −m λ/T_f = −(2 × 334)/273 = −2.45 kJ/K; State clearly that the path chosen must be reversible, e.g. a series of reversible heat additions; Sign convention and units kJ/K throughout.

Apply Steam enters an insulated turbine at 4 MPa and 500 °C and leaves at 10 kPa with a quality of 0.90. Take surroundings at 298 K. Find the entropy generated per kg of steam and the lost work. 13 marks

Energy balance for the adiabatic turbine: w = h₁ − h₂ from steam tables; Entropy balance: s₂ − s₁ = s_gen/q, so s_gen = s₂ − s₁ per kg; Compute s₂ = s_f + x s_fg at 10 kPa; Lost work = T₀ s_gen with T₀ = 298 K; units kJ/kg.

Apply An ideal Rankine cycle operates between boiler pressure 10 MPa and condenser pressure 10 kPa. Sketch the cycle on T–s coordinates and compute its thermal efficiency. Then explain how reheat changes the answer. 13 marks

Four steps: pump, boiler, turbine, condenser; sketch the dome and the four lines; η = (w_T − w_P)/q_in, with q_in = h₃ − h₂ and pump work v(P₂ − P₁); Use saturated liquid at condenser pressure for state 1 and saturated or superheated steam at state 3; Reheat: expand to an intermediate pressure, reheat at constant pressure, expand again — raises the mean temperature of heat addition and reduces turbine wetness.

Apply A vapour-compression refrigerator uses R-134a between evaporator at −10 °C and condenser at 40 °C. Compute the COP for the ideal cycle and explain why the real COP is lower. 12 marks

Ideal cycle: isentropic compression, condensation, throttling to evaporator, evaporation; COP = (h₁ − h₄)/[(h₂ − h₁)] using saturation and superheat tables; Real losses: throttling is irreversible, compressor is not isentropic, pressure drops and heat leaks; Compare with Carnot COP between the same temperatures to show the gap.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Two streams of water, 1 kg/s at 350 K and 1 kg/s at 290 K, are mixed adiabatically in a steady mixer. Calculate the entropy generated and the lost work at T₀ = 290 K, and comment on how the answer would change if the streams were brought together through a reversible heat engine. 13 marks

Energy balance gives the outlet temperature ≈ 320 K; ΔS_total = m[c_p ln(320/350)] + m[c_p ln(320/290)], evaluated for each stream, plus no heat term; S_gen > 0 confirms mixing is irreversible; lost work = T₀ S_gen; With a reversible engine between the streams, some of the temperature difference is converted to work and S_gen falls to zero.

Analyze A salesman claims a cyclic device that takes 100 kW of heat from a reservoir at 500 K, rejects heat at 300 K and delivers 60 kW of work. Check the claim using the first law and the second law. 10 marks

First law: Q_C = 100 − 60 = 40 kW, so the energy balance itself is satisfied; Carnot limit: η_max = 1 − 300/500 = 0.40, so maximum work is 40 kW; Alternatively check ΔS of the reservoirs: −100/500 + 40/300 = −0.067 kW/K < 0, impossible; Conclude the claim violates the second law; a real device must have even lower work output.

Evaluate Compare the Carnot and Rankine cycles for steam power generation. Explain why practical plants do not use the Carnot cycle and how superheat, reheat and regeneration each improve performance. 14 marks

Carnot cycle needs two-phase compression in the pump, which is impractical; the turbine would run in the wet region; Rankine uses complete condensation and a cheap liquid pump; Superheat raises the average temperature of heat addition and dries the turbine exhaust; Reheat limits exhaust wetness and adds work at high temperature; regeneration raises feed-water temperature and lowers boiler heat input; Evaluate: each modification moves the real cycle closer to the reversible ceiling between the same source and sink.

Evaluate Explain the Linde and Claude cycles for liquefaction. Why does the Claude cycle give a higher liquid yield, and what role does the Joule–Thomson coefficient play? 13 marks

Linde: simple throttling after counter-current cooling; yield depends on μ_JT being positive, that is, working below the inversion temperature; Claude: part of the gas is expanded in an engine doing work, so it cools more and needs no throttling for that portion; Claude recovers work and reaches lower temperatures, so the fraction liquefied per unit compression work is larger; Discuss why hydrogen and helium need pre-cooling before throttling (negative μ_JT at room temperature).

Evaluate Define second-law (exergy) efficiency for a steady-flow device and use the entropy balance to discuss where irreversibility arises in a heat exchanger and in a throttling valve. 13 marks

Entropy balance: Σṁ_in s_in + Σ(Q̇/T) + Ṡ_gen = Σṁ_out s_out for steady state; Second-law efficiency = ideal work / actual work (or the reverse for work-consuming devices); Heat exchanger: irreversibility comes from finite temperature difference across the wall, S_gen = ṁ_h Δs_h + ṁ_c Δs_c; Throttling: h constant, s rises, S_gen > 0, all potential work destroyed; no work recovered.

Glossary

Absolute entropy
Entropy measured from the third-law zero at 0 K, as listed in steam and property tables.
Boiler
Heat exchanger in a power plant that adds Q_in to pressurised water to make steam.
Boltzmann constant
k_B = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K, the entropy per unit of ln Ω.
Clausius inequality
∮δQ/T ≤ 0 for any thermodynamic cycle.
Condenser
Heat exchanger that rejects Q_out and condenses turbine exhaust back to liquid.
Control volume
A fixed region in space with mass and energy crossing its boundary; used for turbines, compressors and heat exchangers.
Entropy balance
Accounting statement: entropy in by flow and heat, plus generated entropy, equals entropy out plus accumulation.
Evaporator
The cold-side heat exchanger of a refrigerator where the refrigerant boils and absorbs Q_C.
Heat reservoir
A body large enough that taking or giving heat does not change its temperature.
Ideal work
The work a process would need or deliver if it were fully reversible, W_ideal = W_actual − W_lost.
Inversion temperature
Temperature at which μ_JT changes sign; above it a gas warms on throttling.
Irreversibility
Any effect such as friction, unrestrained expansion or finite-temperature heat transfer that makes a process non-reversible and creates entropy.
Isentropic
A process of constant entropy, the ideal limit for an adiabatic reversible turbine or compressor.
Joule–Thomson coefficient
μ_JT = (∂T/∂P)_H; positive means the gas cools on throttling.
Linde cycle
Liquefaction cycle that cools the gas in a counter-current exchanger and then expands it through a throttle valve.
Macrostate
The measured, macroscopic description of a system (P, V, T, n) that many microstates can produce.
Reversible adiabatic
An isentropic process, drawn as a vertical line on T–s coordinates.
Reversible process
An ideal process whose direction can be reversed by an infinitesimal change, leaving no trace on the surroundings.
Second-law efficiency
Ratio of actual performance to the best reversible performance under the same conditions.
Steady state
Condition where properties inside the control volume do not change with time, so accumulation terms vanish.
Superheat
Heating steam above its saturation temperature at the same pressure.
Thermal efficiency
Work output divided by heat input of a heat engine, η = W/Q_H.
Throttling
Flow through a restriction with no work and negligible heat transfer, so enthalpy stays constant and pressure falls.
