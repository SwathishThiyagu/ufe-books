3.1 Statements of the second law: Kelvin-Planck and Clausius

The first law says energy is conserved. It does not say which way a process may go. The second law adds direction. It forbids a cyclic engine from turning heat from one reservoir completely into work, and forbids a refrigerator from moving heat from cold to hot without work input. These are the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements. They are equivalent. They explain why a power plant needs a condenser and why 100% thermal efficiency is impossible. This chapter states both forms and proves their equivalence by contradiction.

You need: the first-law energy balance for a cyclic process, and the meaning of heat, work and a system from the first-law chapters.

Reservoirs, cycles and efficiency

A heat reservoir is a large body whose temperature stays constant when heat enters or leaves it. A river, the atmosphere or a large condenser can act as one over a short time. A cyclic device returns to its initial state after each cycle, so its internal energy change is zero. For an engine between a hot reservoir at TH and a cold reservoir at TC, let QH be heat taken from the hot reservoir, QC be heat rejected to the cold reservoir, and W be work delivered. Use positive magnitudes in kJ.

The first law for one cycle gives W = QH − QC. Thermal efficiency is the work obtained per unit heat supplied: η = W/QH. η is a fraction; multiply by 100 for percent. The first law allows QC = 0, so η = 100%. The second law forbids that case.

W = QH − QC,   η = W/QH = (QH − QC)/QH

QH is heat taken from the hot reservoir (kJ); QC is heat rejected to the cold reservoir (kJ); W is work delivered per cycle (kJ); η is thermal efficiency (dimensionless).

Engine, refrigerator and heat-pump hardware is in heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps.

Kelvin–Planck statement

Kelvin–Planck statement: no cyclic device can take heat from a single reservoir and convert all of it into work. It may take QH from a hot reservoir, but it must reject some heat QC > 0 to a colder reservoir. Therefore W < QH and η < 1. The word cyclic matters. A single expansion of gas can produce work while cooling; it is not a cycle. A device that claims to violate this statement is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM2. It would not break the first law: it would take heat from the air or the sea and turn all of it into work, cycle after cycle. The statement says PMM2 is impossible. Work can become heat fully: a paddle wheel stirred in water converts work into internal energy. The restriction is one-way.

Clausius statement and COP

Clausius statement: no cyclic device can move heat from a colder body to a hotter body without work input. A refrigerator takes QC from the cold space, receives work Win, and rejects QH = QC + Win to the warm surroundings. Its coefficient of performance is COPR = QC/Win. A heat pump has COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1. COP can be greater than 1; it is not efficiency. COP = 5 means 5 kJ of cold-side heat removed per 1 kJ of work. The statement applies to the net effect of a complete cycle, not to a single process. It forbids COPR = ∞ for a cyclic refrigerator with no work. Practical cycles are in refrigeration and liquefaction.

COPR = QC/Win,   COPHP = QH/Win = COPR + 1

QC is heat removed from the cold space (kJ); QH is heat rejected to the warm space (kJ); Win is work input per cycle (kJ); COPR and COPHP are dimensionless coefficients of performance.

The two statements are equivalent

Kelvin–Planck and Clausius are equivalent: if one is false, the other is false. Suppose Kelvin–Planck is false. A cyclic engine E takes QH from the hot reservoir and delivers W = QH, rejecting no heat. Use W to drive a normal refrigerator R between the same reservoirs. R takes QC from the cold reservoir and rejects QC + W to the hot reservoir. Combine E and R. The hot reservoir loses QH to E and gains QC + QH from R; net gain is QC. The cold reservoir loses QC. The composite moves QC from cold to hot with no net work input, because E supplies W and R consumes W. That violates Clausius. So not-KP implies not-Clausius.

Now suppose Clausius is false. A device R moves QC from the cold reservoir to the hot reservoir with no work input. Take a normal engine E that removes QH from the hot reservoir, rejects QC to the cold reservoir, and delivers W = QH − QC. The cold reservoir gains QC from E and loses QC to R; net change is zero. The hot reservoir loses QH and gains QC; net loss is QH − QC = W. The composite takes heat W from the hot reservoir only and delivers work W in a cycle. That violates Kelvin–Planck. So not-Clausius implies not-KP. The two statements stand or fall together.

Worked example

Question. A cyclic engine takes 100 kJ from a 600 K reservoir and rejects 60 kJ to a 300 K reservoir in each cycle. Find W and η. Does it violate the Kelvin–Planck statement?

  1. Data: QH = 100 kJ, QC = 60 kJ, TH = 600 K, TC = 300 K.
  2. First law for one cycle: W = QH − QC = 100 kJ − 60 kJ = 40 kJ.
  3. Thermal efficiency: η = W/QH = 40 kJ / 100 kJ = 0.40 = 40%.
  4. Kelvin–Planck violation would require QC = 0, so W = QH. Here QC = 60 kJ > 0 and W = 40 kJ < 100 kJ.
  5. The engine is allowed by the Kelvin–Planck statement. The temperatures do not decide this point; they set the maximum possible η in Carnot cycle.

Answer. W = 40 kJ and η = 40%; the engine does not violate the Kelvin–Planck statement because it rejects 60 kJ to the cold reservoir.

Worked example 2

Question. Suppose a "perfect" engine E took 100 kJ per cycle from a hot reservoir and gave 100 kJ of work. Its work drives an ordinary refrigerator R with COPR = 3 between the same two reservoirs. Find the net heat each reservoir gains or loses and the net work of the pair, and say which statement is broken.

  1. Engine E: takes QH,E = 100 kJ from the hot reservoir, rejects nothing, gives W = 100 kJ.
  2. Refrigerator R: receives W = 100 kJ and removes QC = COPR × W = 3 × 100 = 300 kJ from the cold reservoir.
  3. R rejects QH,R = QC + W = 300 + 100 = 400 kJ to the hot reservoir.
  4. Hot reservoir: −100 kJ + 400 kJ = +300 kJ. Cold reservoir: −300 kJ. Net work of the pair: 100 − 100 = 0 kJ.
  5. The pair moves 300 kJ from cold to hot with no work input. That breaks the Clausius statement, so the perfect engine (a Kelvin–Planck violator) cannot exist.

Answer. Hot reservoir gains 300 kJ, cold reservoir loses 300 kJ, net work 0 kJ: a Clausius violation built from a Kelvin–Planck violator.

Exam traps

  • Thinking η = 100% is only inefficient. The first law allows it; the second law forbids it for a cyclic engine.
  • Applying Kelvin–Planck to a single process. It is a statement about a complete cycle; a gas can produce work while cooling in one expansion.
  • Treating COP > 1 as a violation. COP is heat moved per work input, not efficiency, so values like 4 are normal.
  • Confusing the two statements. Kelvin–Planck limits heat→work; Clausius limits cold→hot heat transfer without work.
  • Assuming Clausius says heat never flows cold to hot. It says a cyclic device cannot do so with no work input; a refrigerator does it with work.

3.2 Heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps

A heat engine takes heat from a hot body, converts part of it into work, and rejects the rest to a cold body. A refrigerator or heat pump runs the same energy flow backwards: work input moves heat from a cold space to a warm space. The three devices share one balance, QH = QC + W. Efficiency and coefficient of performance (COP) compare what you want with what you pay. Room air conditioners, kitchen refrigerators and heat-pump water heaters use this balance every day.

You need: the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements from second-law statements, and the steady-flow energy balance from the first-law chapters.

The energy balance with consistent signs

Take every heat flow and work flow as a positive magnitude, and fix the direction on a sketch. QH is the heat flow at the hot side, QC is the heat flow at the cold side, and W is the work flow. For a heat engine, QH enters from a hot source, W leaves as useful work, and QC leaves to a cold sink. Energy balance gives QH = W + QC. The engine pays QH and receives W; the second law forces QC > 0, so W < QH.

For a refrigerator, the wanted result is QC removed from a cold space. Work W enters through a compressor, and QH leaves to the kitchen or outdoor air. The balance is QC + W = QH, the same equation. A heat pump is the same hardware with the wanted result changed to QH delivered to a warm space. Some syllabi call a heat pump a reversed heat engine; the phrase means the arrows of heat flow are reversed by work input, not that the device is a different machine. Keep the magnitudes positive in all three cases. The sketch below uses the worked example: 5 kW enters from the cold space, 2 kW of compressor work enters, and 7 kW leaves to the warm space.

Heat engine: efficiency

A heat engine converts heat into work. Its thermal efficiency η is the work you get divided by the heat you pay for.

η = W/QH = 1 − QC/QH

η is thermal efficiency (dimensionless); W is net work output; QH is heat taken from the hot source; QC is heat rejected to the cold sink; W, QH and QC use the same unit (kW or MW).

For the engine balance QH = W + QC, so W = QH − QC. Substitute: η = (QH − QC)/QH = 1 − QC/QH. The second law says QC cannot be zero for a cyclic engine that takes heat from one hot source, so η < 1. A steam power plant is a heat engine: fuel or nuclear heat enters the boiler, work leaves at the turbine shaft, and waste heat leaves at the condenser, as in steam power plant cycles. The ratio QC/QH comes from the actual cycle; for a reversible cycle between two reservoirs, the Carnot result links it to Kelvin temperatures, covered in Carnot cycle.

Refrigerator and heat pump: COP

A refrigerator is rated by its coefficient of performance, COPR, defined as heat removed from the cold space divided by work input.

COPR = QC/W

COPR is the refrigerator coefficient of performance (dimensionless); QC is heat removed from the cold space; W is work input; both in kW or both in MW.

A heat pump is rated by COPHP = QH/W, because the wanted output is heat delivered to the warm space. Both COPs are dimensionless and both can be greater than 1. A room air conditioner is a refrigerator: it removes QC from the room and rejects QH to the outdoor air. A domestic refrigerator does the same and rejects QH to the kitchen. A heat pump for space heating or hot water wants QH, so it uses the same cycle but a different rating. Divide the balance QH = QC + W by W.

COPHP = QH/W = (QC + W)/W = COPR + 1

COPHP is the heat-pump coefficient of performance; QH is heat delivered to the warm space; W is work input.

The difference of 1 is not a small correction; it follows from energy conservation alone. If a refrigerator has COPR = 2.5, the same cycle used as a heat pump has COPHP = 3.5. That is why a heat pump can deliver more heat than the electrical work it consumes.

Worked example

Question. A refrigerator removes 5 kW of heat from a cold space at 4 °C and rejects heat to a kitchen at 32 °C. Its compressor draws 2 kW of electrical power. Find the heat rejected to the kitchen, QH, the coefficient of performance COPR, the COP of the same unit used as a heat pump, and compare COPR with the reversible limit between the two temperatures.

  1. Take the refrigerator as the system at steady state. All heat and work magnitudes are positive: QC = 5 kW into the device, W = 2 kW into the device, and QH leaves to the kitchen.
  2. Apply the energy balance: QH = QC + W = 5 kW + 2 kW = 7 kW.
  3. Apply the definition: COPR = QC/W = 5 kW / 2 kW = 2.5.
  4. As a heat pump the wanted output is QH: COPHP = QH/W = 7/2 = 3.5 = COPR + 1.
  5. Reversible limit (proved in the Carnot cycle chapter): TC = 4 + 273.15 = 277.15 K, TH = 32 + 273.15 = 305.15 K, so COPR,max = TC/(TH − TC) = 277.15/28 = 9.90.
  6. The real unit reaches 2.5/9.90 = 0.25 of the limit; the rest is lost to irreversibility in the compressor, valve and heat exchangers.

Answer. QH = 7 kW, COPR = 2.5 and COPHP = 3.5; the reversible limit is 9.90, so the unit works at about a quarter of it.

Exam traps

  • Do not swap QH and QC: QH is the larger heat flow to the hot side, QC is the smaller flow from the cold side, and W is their difference.
  • COP is not W/QC. It is QC/W for a refrigerator and QH/W for a heat pump.
  • For a refrigerator or heat pump, work input W is positive in the magnitude balance QH = QC + W. Do not enter it as negative and then add signs again.
  • A heat pump and a refrigerator are the same device with a different wanted output; COPHP = COPR + 1, not a separate cycle.
  • η and COP are ratios of energies, so no temperature enters them. Temperatures appear only in the Carnot limits, and there they must be in kelvin.

3.3 Carnot cycle, Carnot theorems and thermodynamic temperature scale

A Carnot engine is a reversible heat engine that takes heat QH from a hot reservoir at TH, rejects heat QC to a cold reservoir at TC, and returns to its starting state. Every step is reversible, so it gives the best efficiency possible between those two temperatures. Real power plants, refrigerators and heat pumps are compared with this limit. The Carnot theorems and the thermodynamic temperature scale turn that comparison into a precise tool for calculating temperatures and efficiencies.

You need: the second-law statements and the engine energy balance of heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps, the ideal-gas isothermal and adiabatic relations from PVT behaviour and equations of state, and the meaning of a reversible process from the first-law chapters.

The four Carnot steps on an ideal gas

Take n moles of ideal gas in a piston–cylinder. The gas is the working fluid. The cycle has two isothermal steps at the reservoir temperatures and two adiabatic steps that change temperature. All steps are reversible.

  1. 1 → 2: isothermal expansion at TH. The gas takes QH from the hot reservoir and does work. For an ideal gas, QH = n R TH ln(V₂/V₁); n is moles, R is the gas constant, V is volume.
  2. 2 → 3: adiabatic expansion from TH to TC. Q = 0, so the gas cools while doing work.
  3. 3 → 4: isothermal compression at TC. Work is done on the gas and it rejects QC to the cold reservoir. QC = n R TC ln(V₃/V₄) as a positive magnitude.
  4. 4 → 1: adiabatic compression from TC back to TH. Q = 0, and the gas returns to its starting state.

For a reversible adiabatic step of an ideal gas TVγ−1 is constant (γ = CP/CV), so TH V₂γ−1 = TC V₃γ−1 and TH V₁γ−1 = TC V₄γ−1. Dividing one by the other gives (V₂/V₁) = (V₃/V₄), so the two logarithms are equal. Divide the heat magnitudes: QH/QC = TH/TC. This ratio is the key result; it does not depend on n or R.

QH/QC = TH/TC

QH is heat taken from the hot reservoir; QC is heat rejected to the cold reservoir, both in kJ; TH and TC are absolute temperatures in kelvin.

Carnot theorems and the thermodynamic temperature scale

First Carnot theorem: no engine working between two fixed reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine between the same two reservoirs. Second Carnot theorem: all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever the working fluid. The proof idea is a pair of coupled engines. If a claimed engine were better, reverse the reversible one and the pair would move heat from cold to hot with no net work input, which the Clausius statement forbids (see the second-law statements).

For any reversible engine, the ratio QH/QC depends only on the two reservoir temperatures. This lets us define the thermodynamic temperature scale: TH/TC = QH/QC for a reversible engine. The scale needs one fixed value. It was fixed by giving the triple point of water exactly 273.16 K; since 2019 the kelvin is defined by fixing the Boltzmann constant, kB = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K, and the triple point stays at 273.16 K within measurement error. Then the Carnot efficiency follows from the first law, W = QH − QC, and the definition.

ηC = W/QH = 1 − QC/QH = 1 − TC/TH

ηC is the Carnot efficiency; W is net work output; QH is heat taken from the hot reservoir; QC is heat rejected; TH and TC are absolute temperatures in kelvin.

For the reversed cycle, the same engine becomes a refrigerator or heat pump. Its coefficient of performance (COP) is the useful heat divided by work input.

COPR = QC/W = TC/(TH − TC)

COPR is the refrigerator COP; QC is heat removed from the cold space; W is work input; TH and TC are absolute temperatures.

COPHP = QH/W = TH/(TH − TC)

COPHP is the heat-pump COP; QH is heat delivered to the hot space.

What the efficiency limit tells you

The formula ηC = 1 − TC/TH has three practical readings. Raising TH raises ηC. Lowering TC raises ηC. Making TC approach 0 K makes ηC approach 1, but no real cold sink is at absolute zero, so 100 % is impossible.

A real steam plant has irreversibilities: friction, finite temperature differences, and heat leaks. Its efficiency is below the Carnot value between the same boiler and condenser temperatures; the gap is lost work (see lost work and ideal work). Real Rankine cycles are treated in steam power plant cycles, and vapour-compression cycles in refrigeration and liquefaction.

The same limit applies to refrigerators. To get a large COPR, keep TH − TC small. A domestic refrigerator in a hot room therefore works harder than the same refrigerator in a cool room. This is the Carnot limit in everyday use.

Worked example

Question. A Carnot engine works between a hot reservoir at 600 K and a cold reservoir at 300 K. It takes 1000 kJ from the hot reservoir per cycle. Find the Carnot efficiency η, the net work W, and the heat rejected QC.

  1. TH = 600 K, TC = 300 K, QH = 1000 kJ.
  2. ηC = 1 − TC/TH = 1 − 300/600 = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 = 50 %.
  3. W = ηC QH = 0.5 × 1000 kJ = 500 kJ.
  4. Energy balance: QH = W + QC, so QC = 1000 − 500 = 500 kJ. Check: QC/QH = 0.5 = TC/TH.

Answer. η = 50 %, W = 500 kJ, and QC = 500 kJ.

Worked example 2

Question. A reversible engine works between a hot body at an unknown temperature T and a bath at the triple point of water (273.16 K). Per cycle it takes 500 kJ from the hot body and rejects 250 kJ to the bath. Find T on the thermodynamic scale and the engine efficiency.

  1. For a reversible engine the thermodynamic scale gives T/Ttp = QH/QC.
  2. T = 273.16 K × (500/250) = 273.16 × 2 = 546.32 K, that is 273.17 °C.
  3. η = 1 − QC/QH = 1 − 250/500 = 0.50; the same value as 1 − 273.16/546.32.
  4. No thermometer fluid was used: the temperature comes from heat ratios alone.

Answer. T = 546.32 K and η = 50 %.

Exam traps

  • Using Celsius temperatures in η = 1 − TC/TH. Use kelvin; 27 °C and 327 °C are 300 K and 600 K, not 27 and 327.
  • Using η = 1 − TC/TH for a real engine. That formula is for a reversible Carnot engine; a real engine needs its actual QH and QC.
  • Treating QC/QH = TC/TH as true for every engine. The ratio is fixed only for a reversible cycle between those reservoirs.
  • Thinking a COP above 1 breaks the second law. A refrigerator COP of 9 or a heat-pump COP of 15 is allowed; efficiency η is the quantity that cannot exceed 1.
  • Writing W = QC − QH for a refrigerator. Work input is positive when W = QH − QC, because heat delivered to the hot side is larger than heat removed from the cold side.

3.4 Entropy, its calculation and the Clausius inequality

Entropy is the property that turns the second law into arithmetic. The Carnot cycle shows that δQrev/T sums to zero around any reversible loop, so that ratio depends only on the end states — it defines a state function S. Once you can compute ΔS, you can decide whether a process is possible, because the entropy of an isolated system never falls. Chemical engineers use this to test whether a proposed separation, heat-exchanger network or liquefaction train can work at all, and to measure how much work a real plant throws away.

You need: the Carnot cycle, the first law and the idea of a state function from the first-law chapters, and the ideal-gas relations from PVT behaviour and equations of state.

Entropy is a state function built from δQrev/T

In the Carnot cycle (see Carnot cycle), heat QH enters at TH and QC leaves at TC. For a reversible cycle the efficiency is 1 − TC/TH, and the energy balance then gives QH/TH = QC/TC. Taking heat leaving as negative, the sum around the cycle is zero: Σ δQrev/T = 0.

Any reversible cycle can be sliced into thin Carnot strips, and each strip obeys the same result. So ∮δQrev/T = 0 for every reversible cycle. A quantity whose cyclic integral is zero is a state function, the same test that makes internal energy a property. This one is called entropy, S.

dS = δQrev/T

dS is the change in entropy of the system (J/K); δQrev is the small heat added along a reversible path (J); T is the absolute temperature of the system in kelvin at which that heat crosses.

For a finite change, ΔS = ∫₁² δQrev/T, and the value depends only on states 1 and 2, never on the route. S is extensive, measured in J/K; per mole it is J/(mol·K), per kg J/(kg·K). Reversible heat divided by temperature is the whole definition — nothing about 'disorder' is needed at this level.

Calculating ΔS for the cases you meet

Because S is a state function, choose any convenient reversible path between the same two states. If the real path is irreversible, use a substitute path made of simple pieces.

ΔS = ∫(CV/T)dT + R ln(V₂/V₁)

CV is the molar heat capacity at constant volume, J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant; V₁ and V₂ are initial and final volumes; T is in kelvin.

ΔS = ∫(CP/T)dT − R ln(P₂/P₁)

CP is the molar heat capacity at constant pressure, J/(mol·K); P₁ and P₂ are initial and final pressures; T is in kelvin. The minus sign comes from the relation T dS = dH − V dP. For an ideal gas dH = CP dT, so dividing by T gives dS = CP dT/T − R dP/P; raising the pressure at constant T therefore lowers the entropy.

With constant heat capacity these integrate to ΔS = CV ln(T₂/T₁) + R ln(V₂/V₁) and ΔS = CP ln(T₂/T₁) − R ln(P₂/P₁). For an isothermal ideal-gas expansion the temperature terms vanish and only the volume or pressure term survives.

ΔS = ΔH/T

ΔH is the latent heat of the phase change in J/mol at temperature T in K (melting, boiling or sublimation). The sign of ΔH carries the sign of ΔS.

ΔS = ∫(CP/T)dT = CP ln(T₂/T₁)

the second form holds for a solid or liquid heated with constant CP and no phase change; T₁ and T₂ are in kelvin.

  1. Fix the end states and write down their T, P and V.
  2. Break the path into constant-T, constant-P or constant-V pieces.
  3. Apply the matching formula to each piece.
  4. Add the pieces, keeping signs: melting is positive, condensation negative.

The Clausius inequality

Real cycles are irreversible. Compare any such cycle with a reversible one working between the same two reservoirs at TH and TC. The real efficiency is lower, so QC/QH > TC/TH, and with rejected heat counted negative the cyclic integral drops below zero.

∮δQ/T ≤ 0

δQ is the heat crossing the system boundary (J); T is the absolute temperature of the reservoir or boundary where that heat crosses, in K; the integral runs over the whole cycle. Zero means a reversible cycle, negative means irreversible, positive is impossible.

This is the Clausius inequality. Some syllabi call it the mathematical statement of the second law, because it converts the words of second-law statements into a number you can evaluate for any proposed device.

ΔSsystem ≥ ∫₁² δQ/T

the process form, for a change from state 1 to state 2; T is the temperature of the boundary where δQ crosses, in K. Equality holds only for a reversible path. When the boundary temperature is a constant T₀ this reduces to ΔSsystem ≥ Q/T₀.

Now apply it to a system plus its surroundings at T₀. The surroundings exchange heat reversibly, so their entropy change is −Q/T₀, and the inequality rearranges to ΔSsystem − Q/T₀ ≥ 0, that is, ΔSuniverse ≥ 0. For an isolated system, ΔS ≥ 0, with equality only when everything inside is reversible.

Sgen = ΔSsys + ΔSsur ≥ 0

Sgen is the entropy generated by irreversibility, J/K; it is zero for a reversible process and positive for every real one. The work lost because of it is the theme of lost work and ideal work.

Worked example

Question. One mole of an ideal gas at 300 K expands reversibly and isothermally from 10 L to 20 L. Find the heat added, the entropy change of the gas, and the entropy change of the surroundings.

  1. Reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas: ΔU = 0, so Qrev = W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  2. Volume ratio V₂/V₁ = 20/10 = 2, and ln 2 = 0.6931.
  3. Qrev = 1 mol × 8.314 J/(mol·K) × 300 K × 0.6931 = 1728.8 J.
  4. ΔSgas = Qrev/T = 1728.8 J / 300 K = 5.76 J/K. Cross-check with R ln(V₂/V₁) = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K.
  5. The gas takes this heat from the reservoir, so ΔSsur = −1728.8/300 = −5.76 J/K, and ΔSuniverse = 0, as it must be for a reversible process.

Answer. Qrev = 1728.8 J, ΔS of the gas = 5.76 J/K, and ΔS of the surroundings = −5.76 J/K.

Worked example 2

Question. The same 1 mol of ideal gas at 300 K now expands from 10 L to 20 L freely into an evacuated, insulated space (a free or Joule expansion). Find Q, W, ΔS of the gas, ΔS of the surroundings and Sgen.

  1. Insulated, so Q = 0; expansion into vacuum, so W = 0; then ΔU = 0 and the ideal gas stays at 300 K.
  2. The end states (300 K, 10 L) and (300 K, 20 L) are the same as in the first example. S is a state function, so use that reversible isothermal path as the substitute: ΔSgas = nR ln(V₂/V₁) = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K.
  3. The real path exchanges no heat, so ΔSsur = 0.
  4. Sgen = ΔSgas + ΔSsur = 5.76 J/K > 0: the free expansion is irreversible. Note that the real ∫δQ/T = 0 is less than ΔS = 5.76 J/K, as the Clausius inequality demands.

Answer. Q = W = 0, ΔSgas = 5.76 J/K, ΔSsur = 0 and Sgen = 5.76 J/K.

Exam traps

  • Using the real irreversible heat in dS = δQ/T. The actual Q/T is not the entropy change; compute ΔS along a reversible substitute between the same end states.
  • Putting the system temperature instead of the boundary temperature in ∮δQ/T. For an irreversible process the T belongs to the reservoir where the heat crosses.
  • Swapping the signs of the two ideal-gas forms: the volume term is +R ln(V₂/V₁), the pressure term is −R ln(P₂/P₁). Using plus with pressure gives the wrong sign in compression.
  • Forgetting that freezing and condensation have negative ΔS, because their ΔH is negative and ΔS = ΔH/T keeps that sign.
  • Unit slip: R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) gives ΔS in J/K. With R = 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K) the answer would be in L·bar/K, which is not an entropy unit you can add to J/K.

3.5 Entropy balance for open systems (control volume)

An open control volume has streams crossing its boundary. Each stream carries entropy at the rate ṁs, heat carries entropy at the rate Q/T, and friction, mixing and finite temperature gaps inside create more. The entropy balance adds these terms. At steady state nothing accumulates, so the generation equals entropy out minus entropy in. Engineers use it to test turbines, compressors, nozzles and heat exchangers.

You need: entropy and the Clausius inequality and the steady-flow mass and energy balances from the first-law chapters.

The control-volume entropy balance

Take a control volume (CV), a fixed region in space. Let mi be mass flow at inlet i in kg/s and si its specific entropy in kJ/(kg·K). Let me and se be the same at outlet e. Let Qj be heat rate into the CV across boundary j in kW, and Tj the absolute temperature of that boundary in K. Let SCV be total entropy inside the CV in kJ/K, and Sgen the entropy generation rate in kW/K. The balance is:

dSCV/dt = Σ mi si − Σ me se + Σ Qj/Tj + Sgen

SCV is total entropy inside the CV in kJ/K; t is time in s; mi is inlet mass flow in kg/s; si is inlet specific entropy in kJ/(kg·K); me and se are outlet mass flow and specific entropy; Qj is heat rate into the CV in kW; Tj is the uniform absolute temperature of boundary j in K; if the boundary temperature varies, replace Qj/Tj by the integral ∫ δQ/T over that boundary; Sgen is entropy generation rate in kW/K.

The first term is accumulation. The next two are entropy carried by flow. The heat term is entropy carried by heat; use Tj in kelvin. Sgen comes from irreversibility inside: friction, mixing, chemical reaction, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference. The second law gives Sgen ≥ 0, with equality only for a reversible process. Sgen is not a state property; it is fixed by the process. At steady state dSCV/dt = 0, so Sgen = Σ me se − Σ mi si − Σ Qj/Tj. A negative computed Sgen means the data or sign convention is wrong.

Adiabatic devices and heat exchangers

For one inlet and one outlet, steady, adiabatic (Qj = 0), the balance reduces to:

Sgen = m(sout − sin)

m is mass flow in kg/s; sin and sout are inlet and outlet specific entropies in kJ/(kg·K); Sgen is entropy generation rate in kW/K.

Work and kinetic energy do not appear: entropy crosses only with mass and heat. A real adiabatic turbine has sout > sin because friction and losses generate entropy; the ideal isentropic turbine sets sout = sin and Sgen = 0. A compressor needs work; if adiabatic, the same formula applies and real sout > sin. If the compressor is cooled, heat leaves, so Qj is negative; use the full balance. A nozzle has no work and is often adiabatic: Sgen = m(sout − sin), and an ideal nozzle is isentropic.

For a two-stream heat exchanger with no external heat, enclose both streams in the CV. Then Sgen = mh(sh,out − sh,in) + mc(sc,out − sc,in). Internal heat from hot to cold stays inside the CV and does not appear as Qj. If the exchanger also loses heat to the room, the leak raises the generation: Sgen = mh(sh,out − sh,in) + mc(sc,out − sc,in) − Qroom/Troom, with Qroom negative when heat leaves, so the last term is positive. Some syllabi call the steady balance the second law for a control volume.

A short procedure

  1. Draw the CV and mark every inlet, outlet and heat crossing.
  2. Decide steady or transient.
  3. Write m and s for each stream, using kJ/(kg·K).
  4. Write each Qj as positive into the CV and Tj in K.
  5. Put terms in the balance and solve for Sgen. Check units and sign.

Check units: kg/s × kJ/(kg·K) = kW/K; kW divided by K = kW/K. Check sign: Sgen cannot be negative. For a reversible adiabatic device, Sgen = 0. For a real adiabatic device, outlet specific entropy is greater than inlet. The same Sgen becomes lost work when multiplied by T₀; see lost work and ideal work.

Worked example

Question. Steam at 2 kg/s enters an adiabatic turbine at 3 MPa and 400 °C (sin = 6.9233 kJ/(kg·K), hin = 3231.6 kJ/kg) and leaves at 50 kPa with quality 0.95 (at 50 kPa: sf = 1.0910, sg = 7.5930 kJ/(kg·K), hf = 340.5, hg = 2645.2 kJ/kg).   1. Choose a CV around the turbine. Steady flow: dSCV/dt = 0. One inlet, one outlet, adiabatic: Qj = 0, so 0 = m sin − m sout + Sgen.
  2. Exit entropy: sout = sf + x(sg − sf) = 1.0910 + 0.95 × 6.5020 = 7.2679 kJ/(kg·K).
  3. Sgen = m(sout − sin) = 2 × (7.2679 − 6.9233) = 2 × 0.3446 = 0.689 kW/K (1 kJ/s = 1 kW).
  4. Exit enthalpy: hout = hf + x(hg − hf) = 340.5 + 0.95 × 2304.7 = 2530.0 kJ/kg. Power = m(hin − hout) = 2 × 701.6 = 1403 kW.
  5. For comparison, an isentropic exit at 50 kPa has x = (6.9233 − 1.0910)/6.5020 = 0.897 and h = 2407.8 kJ/kg, so the turbine efficiency is 701.6/823.8 = 0.85.

Answer. Sgen = 0.689 kW/K and the turbine gives 1403 kW. Sgen > 0, so it is irreversible; its isentropic efficiency is about 85 %.

  • Setting Sgen = 0 for a two-stream heat exchanger because there is no external heat. Internal heat exchange can still generate entropy; add the entropy changes of both streams.

3.6 Third law of thermodynamics

The third law gives entropy a fixed zero. For a perfect ordered crystal at absolute zero, S = 0. Then every absolute entropy can be found by heating the substance from 0 K and summing Cp/T. This sounds abstract, but it is how entropy tables are built. It also explains why some solids keep a small leftover entropy after cooling, and why no laboratory reaches 0 K exactly. Engineers use it to compare absolute entropies in power cycles, refrigerators and chemical reactions.

You need: entropy and the Clausius inequality and heat capacity from the first-law chapters.

The third law and the Nernst theorem

Absolute zero is 0 K, about −273.15 °C. A perfect crystal is a solid with every atom or molecule in one ordered arrangement, with no frozen-in disorder. The Nernst heat theorem says that for condensed phases near absolute zero, the entropy change of a reversible isothermal process tends to zero as T → 0. In plain form, the third law states: the entropy of a perfect ordered crystal is zero at 0 K.

This is a reference point, not a calculation trick. Before the third law, entropy changes could be measured but absolute entropy had no origin. The law chooses S(0 K) = 0 for the perfect crystal. Then S at any T is an absolute entropy. Tables of standard molar entropy, S°, use this choice. The value at 298.15 K is not zero, and 0 °C is not the zero either. Only 0 K is.

The law applies to a perfect crystal. A real crystal can have defects, mixed orientations or frozen disorder. Those solids may have residual entropy at 0 K. The next sections separate the ideal case from these real cases.

Absolute entropy from heat capacity

For reversible heating at constant pressure, dS = Cp dT / T. Start at 0 K with S(0) = 0 and integrate to T:

S(T) = ∫₀T (Cp/T′) dT′ + Σ (ΔHtr/Ttr)

S(T) is absolute molar entropy at T in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T′ is the integration variable, running from 0 K up to the final temperature T in K; Cp is heat capacity at constant pressure in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; ΔHtr is the enthalpy of a phase transition in J mol⁻¹ and Ttr its temperature in K. The sum adds each solid–solid, melting or boiling transition between 0 K and T, and the integral is taken piece by piece over each single-phase range.

The integral cannot start at exactly 0 K in a real measurement, because Cp/T is hard to measure and 0 K is unattainable. Experimenters measure Cp down to a low temperature, often a few kelvin. Below that they fit the low-temperature law Cp = aT³. Then the integral from 0 K to that low temperature T is finite and easy: ∫₀T aT′² dT′ = aT³/3, which is one third of Cp at T. Here a is a constant with units J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴. The cubic form is the Debye T³ law. Strictly it is a law for CV, but at a few kelvin CP and CV of a solid differ by far less than the measurement error, so CP = aT³ is used. Metals add an electronic term: CP = aT³ + γT, which integrates to S = aT³/3 + γT.

Procedure to build an absolute entropy:

  1. Measure Cp at many temperatures above the lowest measured point.
  2. Fit Cp = aT³ below that point and add aT³/3.
  3. Add ΔHtr/Ttr for every phase transition on the path.
  4. Add ∫(Cp/T)dT over each single-phase range.
  5. Report S(T) with the third-law zero at 0 K.

This is why entropy tables need heat-capacity data, not just one calorimeter reading.

Residual entropy and the unattainable zero

Residual entropy is entropy that remains at 0 K when a solid is not a perfect ordered crystal. Carbon monoxide is the standard example. The molecules CO are almost symmetric in size, so in a crystal they can point CO or OC with little energy difference. Cooling freezes a random arrangement. The crystal has S(0) > 0: if the two orientations were equally likely it would be R ln 2 = 8.314 × 0.693 = 5.76 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; the measured residual entropy of CO is a little smaller, about 4.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, because the freezing is not perfectly random. Glasses are another case: a liquid's disorder is frozen in before it can crystallize, so the glass keeps extra entropy.

The third law needs the words perfect crystal. For CO, glass or a crystal with frozen defects, S(0) is not zero. The law still sets the zero for the perfectly ordered reference, and the measured entropy of the real solid is larger by the residual amount.

The third law also says absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. As a system is cooled toward 0 K, each step removes less entropy and the cooling power falls. A real cryostat can reach very low temperatures, but it cannot cross to 0 K. This is the unattainability statement. It is consistent with S → 0: at 0 K the entropy would be fixed, and no finite process can take a system there. In engineering, the practical message is that reaching lower temperatures costs more work and better insulation; see refrigeration and liquefaction.

Worked example

Question. A crystal obeys Cp = aT³ at low temperature with a = 0.001 J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴. Using S(0) = 0, find S at 10 K.

  1. Identify the low-temperature heat capacity: Cp = aT³, with a = 0.001 J mol⁻¹ K⁻⁴.
  2. Use the third-law integral: S(10 K) − S(0 K) = ∫₀¹⁰ (Cp/T) dT.
  3. Set S(0 K) = 0 for the perfect crystal.
  4. Substitute Cp: Cp/T = aT³/T = aT².
  5. Integrate: S(10 K) = ∫₀¹⁰ aT² dT = a[T³/3]₀¹⁰ = a(1000/3) K³.
  6. Insert a: S = 0.001 × 1000/3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.3333 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Answer. S(10 K) = 0.333 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Using S = 0 at 0 °C or at 298 K. The third-law zero is 0 K, not the ice point or room temperature.
  • Forgetting to divide Cp by T. The entropy integral is ∫(Cp/T)dT, not ∫Cp dT.
  • Assuming every solid has S(0) = 0. Only a perfect ordered crystal does; CO and glasses keep residual entropy.
  • Omitting phase-transition terms. A complete absolute entropy adds ΔHtr/Ttr at each transition between 0 K and T.
  • Thinking enough cooling steps can reach exactly 0 K. The third law says 0 K is unattainable in a finite number of steps.

3.7 Irreversibility, lost work and ideal work

Every real process generates entropy, and that entropy generation carries a work penalty. With surroundings at temperature T₀, take work input as positive. The ideal work of a steady-flow unit is Wideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS. When Wideal is negative the unit produces work, and the best work it can give is −Wideal = T₀ΔS − ΔH. The difference Wactual − Wideal is the lost work, Wlost = T₀Sgen. Power plants, refrigerators and heat-recovery networks use this number to see where useful work is thrown away.

You need entropy, entropy generation and the entropy balance from entropy and the Clausius inequality and control-volume entropy balance, plus the steady-flow energy balance from the first-law chapters.

Ideal work and the dead state

Take the surroundings as a large reservoir at temperature T₀. T₀ is the dead-state temperature: heat exchanged with the environment at T₀ has zero work potential. The ideal work imagines the same change of state done with no entropy generation: any heat the stream must gain or lose is passed through reversible engines or heat pumps that work against the surroundings at T₀.

For a steady-flow unit with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes, take work input as positive and write Wideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS. Here ΔH is outlet minus inlet enthalpy in kJ, ΔS is outlet minus inlet entropy in kJ/K, and T₀ is in K. A negative Wideal means the unit produces work; the ideal output is −Wideal, the maximum possible. A positive Wideal is the minimum work input.

The same ideas are also called exergy or availability analysis. Do not confuse Wideal with a change in Gibbs free energy (thermodynamic property relations): ΔG uses the system temperature, not T₀.

Wideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS

Wideal is reversible work input in kJ (negative means work output); ΔH is outlet minus inlet enthalpy in kJ; ΔS is outlet minus inlet entropy in kJ/K; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K.

Gouy–Stodola: entropy generation becomes lost work

The second law gives the price of irreversibility. For any process, the total entropy generation is Sgen = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings, with both changes in kJ/K. For an isolated composite system the surroundings term is already inside the system boundary and Sgen is just the total entropy change. The Gouy–Stodola theorem states that Wlost = T₀Sgen. Sgen is in kJ/K and T₀ is in K, so Wlost is in kJ. A reversible process has Sgen = 0 and loses no work.

Lost work is also called exergy destruction. With work input positive, Wlost = Wactual − Wideal; this stays positive for turbines and compressors. For a turbine Wideal is more negative than Wactual, so subtracting gives a positive loss. The lost work is always positive. Adding it to actual work is wrong; it is already gone.

Use T₀, not the hot or cold stream temperature, because the environment is the only free heat reservoir. If a plant rejects heat at 450 K but the outside air is at 300 K, the 300 K value sets the work value of that heat.

Wlost = T₀ Sgen

Wlost is lost work in kJ; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K; Sgen is total entropy generation in kJ/K.

Worked example

Question. Heat of 1000 kJ passes from a reservoir at 500 K to a reservoir at 450 K. The surroundings are at 300 K. Find the entropy generated and the lost work. Treat the two reservoirs and the heat path as the system.

  1. Hot reservoir: ΔShot = −Q/Thot = −1000 kJ / 500 K = −2.000 kJ/K.
  2. Cold reservoir: ΔScold = +Q/Tcold = +1000 kJ / 450 K = +2.222 kJ/K.
  3. The composite system is isolated, so Sgen = ΔShot + ΔScold = −2.000 + 2.222 = 0.222 kJ/K.
  4. Lost work: Wlost = T₀ Sgen = 300 K × 0.222 kJ/K = 66.7 kJ.

Answer. Sgen = 0.222 kJ/K and Wlost = 66.7 kJ; the heat transfer destroys 66.7 kJ of potential work.

Worked example 2

Question. Use the steam turbine of the control-volume entropy balance: per kg, h falls from 3231.6 to 2530.0 kJ/kg and s rises from 6.9233 to 7.2679 kJ/(kg·K). Take T₀ = 300 K. Find Wideal, Wactual, Wlost and the second-law efficiency.

  1. Work input positive. ΔH = 2530.0 − 3231.6 = −701.6 kJ/kg; ΔS = 7.2679 − 6.9233 = +0.3446 kJ/(kg·K).
  2. Wideal = ΔH − T₀ΔS = −701.6 − 300 × 0.3446 = −701.6 − 103.4 = −805.0 kJ/kg: at best the steam could give 805.0 kJ/kg.
  3. Adiabatic turbine: Wactual = ΔH = −701.6 kJ/kg (it gives 701.6 kJ/kg).
  4. Wlost = Wactual − Wideal = −701.6 − (−805.0) = 103.4 kJ/kg. Check: T₀Sgen = 300 × 0.3446 = 103.4 kJ/kg.
  5. Second-law efficiency = actual output / ideal output = 701.6/805.0 = 0.872.

Exam traps

  • Do not add lost work to actual work. With work input positive, use Wlost = Wactual − Wideal; for a turbine this gives a positive loss because Wactual is less negative than Wideal.
  • Use T₀, the environment temperature, not the hot reservoir or cold reservoir temperature, inside Wlost = T₀Sgen.
  • Sgen is total entropy generation for system plus surroundings. Using only the steam or gas entropy change misses the surroundings and gives the wrong lost work.
  • Check units: Sgen in kJ/K times T₀ in K gives kJ. Mixing J/K with K gives a factor of 1000 error.

3.8 Steam power plant cycles

A steam power plant burns coal or gas to boil water, spins a turbine with the steam, and condenses the exhaust back to liquid so the loop can repeat. The four devices — pump, boiler, turbine, condenser — form the Rankine cycle. Almost all thermal electricity in India, whether from coal, nuclear or solar-thermal heat, comes from this cycle, so its efficiency decides how much fuel a unit burns for every kWh it sends out.

You need: the Carnot cycle, entropy, the steady-flow energy balance from the first-law chapters, and steam-table reading (thermodynamic property tables and diagrams).

Four devices in a closed loop

Water circulates. The pump takes saturated liquid from the condenser and raises its pressure to boiler pressure; the liquid temperature rises only a little because liquid water is nearly incompressible. The boiler adds heat at almost constant pressure: the water warms to saturation, boils, and is usually superheated. The turbine expands the high-pressure steam and delivers shaft work, so pressure and temperature fall. The condenser rejects heat at constant pressure and temperature, turning the exhaust back into saturated liquid.

For each device the steady-flow energy balance, with kinetic and potential energy neglected, gives q − w = Δh. With the state numbers of the figure: turbine work wt = h₃ − h₄, pump work wp = h₂ − h₁, boiler heat qin = h₃ − h₂ and condenser heat qout = h₄ − h₁, all in kJ per kg of water. Here wt and wp are positive magnitudes: wt is delivered, wp is absorbed (in q − w = Δh the pump has w = −wp).

A Carnot cycle between the same temperatures would be more efficient, but it cannot be built for steam: it would have to compress a wet mixture. The Rankine cycle condenses fully and pumps only liquid, which needs a small pump; the price is a lower mean temperature of heat addition.

On the T–s diagram of the figure the saturated liquid and vapour lines form the dome. Pump work 1→2 is a short, nearly vertical line on the liquid side. Boiler heating 2→3 runs along the 4 MPa line to superheated steam. Isentropic expansion 3→4 is a vertical drop into the wet region. Condensation 4→1 is a horizontal line at 45.8 °C.

Efficiency from steam tables

The thermal efficiency compares net work out with heat paid for in the boiler. The pump work is small compared with turbine work, but it is real work bought from the same shaft, so it must be subtracted.

η = (wt − wp) / qin = [(h₃ − h₄) − (h₂ − h₁)] / (h₃ − h₂)

η is the cycle thermal efficiency (dimensionless); h₃ is turbine-inlet enthalpy, h₄ turbine-exit enthalpy, h₁ condenser-exit (pump-inlet) enthalpy, h₂ pump-exit (boiler-inlet) enthalpy, all in kJ/kg.

  1. Fix the boiler pressure pb and the condenser pressure pc.
  2. At the turbine inlet (state 3) take the known pressure and temperature, read h₃ and s₃ from the superheated steam table.
  3. For an ideal turbine, s₄ = s₃ at pressure pc; find quality x₄ = (s₃ − sf)/sfg, then h₄ = hf + x₄ hfg.
  4. Pump work for liquid: wp ≈ vf (pb − pc), with vf in m³/kg and pressures in kPa, giving kJ/kg. Then h₂ = h₁ + wp.
  5. Boiler heat qin = h₃ − h₂, net work wnet = (h₃ − h₄) − wp, and η = wnet/qin.

Real machines fall short. Turbine isentropic efficiency ηt = (h₃ − h₄)/(h₃ − h₄s) is about 0.85 to 0.92, so the actual exit has higher entropy, higher enthalpy and more moisture than the ideal one. Pump isentropic efficiency is 0.70 to 0.85, but because wp is only 1 to 3 percent of turbine work, its uncertainty barely moves the cycle efficiency.

Typical large utility values: boiler at 100 bar and 540 °C, condenser at 0.1 bar, where the saturation temperature is 45.8 °C. The ideal cycle gives about 41 percent; with an 85 percent turbine it drops to about 35 percent, and reheat and feed heating win part of the loss back.

Reheat, regeneration and condenser pressure

  • Reheat: expand to an intermediate pressure, send the steam back to the boiler to reheat at constant pressure, then expand again. The mean temperature of heat addition rises a little, and the exhaust stays drier (quality above about 0.88), which protects the last blades.
  • Regenerative feed heating: bleed a fraction of steam from the turbine at several points and use it to warm the feedwater in open (mixing) or closed heaters. The boiler then receives hotter water, so qin falls for the same turbine output. Bleeding removes steam that would have made work, so the gain is real but bounded; large units use several heaters in series.
  • Condenser pressure: a lower pc lowers the temperature of heat rejection and raises efficiency. The limit is the cooling water available (river or cooling-tower water near 30 °C), air leaking into the vacuum, and the length of the last blades.

Raising boiler pressure also raises efficiency, but it pushes the turbine exhaust deeper into the wet region, so higher pressure goes together with superheat and reheat. Supercritical units run above the critical pressure of water, 220.6 bar, where there is no boiling stage at all.

Worked example

Question. An ideal Rankine cycle has steam at 4 MPa and 400 °C entering the turbine and a condenser at 10 kPa.   • Using turbine work alone as net work. Subtract the pump work: wnet = wt − wp.
  • Taking qin from the condenser exit enthalpy h₁. The boiler receives feedwater after the pump, so use h₂ = h₁ + wp.

3.9 Refrigeration and liquefaction processes

A refrigerator does not create cold; it moves heat from a cold space to a warmer one, and that costs work. The same cycle used for the heat it rejects is a heat pump. Kitchen refrigerators, air conditioners, cold stores and vaccine storage all use the vapour-compression cycle, and gas liquefaction pushes the same idea to very low temperatures.

You need: the device balances of heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps, the Carnot COP from the Carnot cycle, and refrigerant property tables (thermodynamic property tables and diagrams).

The four devices of the vapour-compression cycle

Refrigeration keeps a space colder than its surroundings. Heat leaks in, so a device must continuously pump that heat to a warmer place. The vapour-compression cycle does this by making a fluid boil at low pressure, absorbing heat, and condense at high pressure, releasing heat.

A liquid boils at a temperature fixed by its pressure. Lower the pressure, and the boiling point falls. Pick a refrigerant whose boiling point at a few atmospheres is below the cold-space temperature; the cold space then supplies the heat of boiling. The cycle runs in four steps.

  1. Evaporator. A low-pressure liquid–vapour mixture boils at constant pressure and temperature, taking heat Qin from the cold space.
  2. Compressor. Low-pressure vapour is squeezed to high pressure. Work Win goes in, so the vapour leaves superheated and hotter.
  3. Condenser. Hot high-pressure vapour gives heat Qout to surroundings and condenses to liquid.
  4. Expansion valve (throttle). Liquid drops to low pressure through a restriction. No work is produced, enthalpy stays nearly constant, temperature falls, and a little liquid flashes to vapour. The cold mixture returns to the evaporator.

For steady flow, neglecting kinetic and potential energy, Qin + Win = Qout. The condenser duty is always larger than the refrigerating effect by the compressor work.

Coefficient of performance and the Carnot limit

Efficiency is a poor word for a refrigerator, because we want heat removed, not work. The yardstick is the coefficient of performance (COP).

COPR = Qin / Win = Qin / (Qout − Qin)

COPR is the coefficient of performance of the refrigerator (dimensionless); Qin is the heat taken from the cold space in kW; Qout is the heat rejected at the condenser in kW; Win is the compressor work in kW.

COPHP = Qout / Win = COPR + 1

COPHP is the heat-pump COP; Qout is heat delivered to the warm room in kW. COPHP = COPR + 1 because the work also ends up in the room.

No cycle between cold at TC and warm at TH can beat the reversed Carnot cycle. Its COP depends only on the two absolute temperatures.

COPR,Carnot = TC / (TH − TC)

TC is cold-region temperature in kelvin; TH is warm-region temperature in kelvin. Use kelvin, never Celsius, because the ratio must stay positive and correct.

Real machines reach roughly half the Carnot value: compression is not isentropic, the throttle wastes pressure energy, and heat exchangers need a finite temperature difference. That gap is destroyed work, as in lost work and ideal work.

Real cycles, Joule–Thomson expansion and liquefaction

The ideal cycle assumes saturated vapour entering the compressor and saturated liquid leaving the condenser. Practical cycles differ:

  • The vapour is superheated a few degrees before compression. Liquid droplets damage a compressor, and superheat guarantees dry vapour.
  • The condensate is subcooled, raising refrigerating effect per kilogram.
  • Compression is not isentropic; isentropic efficiency is typically 0.7 to 0.85. Use the actual compressor exit enthalpy, not the isentropic one.

The expansion valve is a throttling device: enthalpy is constant across it and entropy rises, so it is irreversible. For most gases below their inversion temperature, throttling also lowers temperature. This is the Joule–Thomson effect, and it makes gas liquefaction possible without moving cold parts.

Liquefaction needs cooling below the critical temperature of the gas, above which no pressure can condense the vapour (126 K for nitrogen). The two classic cycles are:

  • Linde cycle. Compress the gas at room temperature, cool it in a counter-current heat exchanger, and throttle it. Part of the gas liquefies; the cold gas left over returns through the exchanger to pre-cool the incoming stream. With a perfect exchanger the liquid fraction is y = (h₁ − h₂)/(h₁ − hf), where h₁ is the low-pressure gas at room temperature, h₂ the high-pressure gas at room temperature and hf the saturated liquid.
  • Claude cycle. Adds an expansion engine, so part of the gas expands while doing work. Work removal gives far more cooling per kilogram, so Claude suits large tonnages.

3.10 Entropy from a microscopic point of view

Entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements fit the same macroscopic state. Two samples can have the same energy, volume and number of particles yet very different numbers of ways to arrange those particles. Boltzmann turned that count into a number: S = kB ln Ω. The idea exists because the Clausius definition from entropy and the Clausius inequality tells you how entropy changes, but not what entropy is counting. Engineers use it to judge disorder in alloys, glasses and crystals, and to connect the third law to a real solid.

You need entropy as a state property from entropy and the Clausius inequality and the third law from third law.

Microstates and macrostates

A microstate is one exact arrangement of the particles in a system. For a gas, it fixes the position and momentum of every molecule. For a simple model, it can be as coarse as naming which half of a box each labelled molecule occupies. A macrostate is what you can measure: U, V and N, or in the box model just how many molecules are in the left half. Many microstates can give the same macrostate.

Take four labelled molecules and a box split into two equal halves. Each molecule can be left or right, so there are 2⁴ = 16 microstates. The macrostate "n molecules on the left" can be made in Ω = 4!/[n!(4 − n)!] ways: 1, 4, 6, 4 and 1 ways for n = 0 to 4. The even split has the most microstates, which is why a gas left to itself spreads evenly.

The basic assumption of statistical thermodynamics is that, at fixed U, V and N, every accessible microstate is equally likely. Entropy is then fixed by how many microstates are accessible. Liquid water has many more ways to share its thermal energy than ice, which is why the liquid has higher entropy.

The Boltzmann relation

S = kB ln Ω

S is entropy in J/K; kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; ln is the natural logarithm; Ω is the number of accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N, dimensionless and at least 1.

The logarithm makes entropy additive. Two independent systems have Ω = Ω₁ Ω₂, so S = kB ln(Ω₁ Ω₂) = kB ln Ω₁ + kB ln Ω₂ = S₁ + S₂. Entropy of the pair is the sum, as thermodynamics demands.

When Ω = 1, ln 1 = 0 and S = 0. A single possible arrangement carries no entropy. Doubling Ω adds kB ln 2 = 0.693 kB, about 9.57 × 10⁻²⁴ J/K, whatever the starting count. For 1 mol of gas whose volume doubles, each of the NA molecules gets twice as many places, Ω rises by 2NA, and ΔS = NA kB ln 2 = R ln 2 = 5.76 J/K: the same number the Clausius route gives in the chapter on entropy and the Clausius inequality.

Third law and residual entropy

At absolute zero a perfect crystal has every atom in its lowest-energy position. There is one way to build that crystal, so Ω = 1 and S = kB ln 1 = 0. This is the microscopic reason for the third law: perfect crystals have zero entropy at 0 K.

Some real solids never reach that single arrangement. Carbon monoxide molecules can point in two directions in the crystal, and cooling locks in a random mixture. Ice has protons arranged with many equivalent hydrogen-bond patterns; Pauling counted them and found S(0) = R ln(3/2) = 3.37 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, close to the measured value. A glass is frozen in a liquid-like arrangement. Each has Ω > 1 even at 0 K, so S = kB ln Ω gives a positive residual entropy. The third law is not broken; the crystal is simply not perfect.

Entropy is not merely 'disorder'. A system can look messy but have few accessible microstates, or look ordered but have many. The correct statement is: entropy counts accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N. Some syllabi call this the statistical interpretation of entropy.

Worked example

Question. Four labelled gas molecules move in a box with two equal halves. (a) Find Ω for each macrostate n = 0 to 4 (n molecules on the left). (b) Find S/kB for the all-left macrostate, the even split and the whole box. (c) Find ΔS when 1 mol of ideal gas doubles its volume at constant temperature.

  1. Each molecule has 2 choices, so the whole box has 2⁴ = 16 microstates.
  2. Ω(n) = 4!/[n!(4 − n)!]: Ω(0) = 1, Ω(1) = 4, Ω(2) = 6, Ω(3) = 4, Ω(4) = 1. Sum = 16.
  3. All on the left: S/kB = ln 1 = 0. Even split: S/kB = ln 6 = 1.792. Whole box: S/kB = ln 16 = 2.773.
  4. One mole doubling its volume: Ω₂/Ω₁ = 2NA, so ΔS = kB NA ln 2 = R ln 2 = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K.

Answer. Ω = 1, 4, 6, 4, 1; S/kB = 0, 1.792 and 2.773; ΔS = R ln 2 = 5.76 J/K for 1 mol, matching the thermodynamic result.

Exam traps

  • Entropy is not just 'disorder'; it is the count of accessible microstates at fixed U, V and N. A visually messy system can have low entropy if few arrangements are accessible.
  • Ω is a count of microstates, not a probability and not work. It is dimensionless and cannot be less than 1.
  • R = NA kB. Use S = kB ln Ω for the whole system; per mole, S = R ln w only when each molecule independently has w arrangements (CO in a crystal, w = 2), because then Ω = wNA.
  • S = 0 at 0 K only for a perfect crystal with a unique ground state. Glasses, CO and ice can keep residual entropy.
  • Do not say 'entropy is the number of microstates'; entropy is proportional to ln Ω, so it is not Ω itself. Doubling Ω does not double S.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Statistical mechanics builds the whole of thermodynamics from Ω: the partition function, Boltzmann distribution and quantum gases all start here. Materials science uses configurational entropy to model alloys and solid solutions; cryogenics uses residual entropy to check whether a solid has truly ordered. Search next for 'configurational entropy', 'residual entropy of ice' and 'Boltzmann distribution'. A later course derives S = kB ln Ω from probability rather than stating it.

Takeaways

  1. The second law is a statement about direction: work can turn completely into heat, but heat cannot turn completely into work in a cycle.
  2. A heat engine, a refrigerator and a heat pump are the same energy balance read in three different directions.
  3. The Carnot efficiency 1 − TC/TH is a ceiling set only by reservoir temperatures, not by the fluid or the machine.
  4. Entropy is a state function; ΔS = ∫δQrev/T over any convenient reversible path between the same two states.
  5. The Clausius inequality ∮δQ/T ≤ 0 is the seed from which the whole entropy concept grows.
  6. For a control volume, entropy carried in by flow and heat plus entropy generated equals entropy carried out plus accumulation.
  7. Every real process destroys work equal to T₀ Sgen, and that gap is what a second-law (exergy) analysis measures.
  8. Real power plants and refrigerators are engineered by pushing their cycles closer to the reversible ideal — reheat, regeneration, and careful choice of boiler, condenser and evaporator pressures.
  9. Entropy counts microstates: S = kB ln Ω, which is zero for a perfect crystal at 0 K and gives R ln 2 when a mole of gas doubles its volume.

Remember State the Kelvin–Planck and Clausius statements of the second law and prove that they are equivalent. 10 marks

Points to cover. Both statements exactly, with the word cyclic; A Kelvin–Planck violator driving a refrigerator breaks Clausius; A Clausius violator paired with an engine breaks Kelvin–Planck; Conclusion: the two statements stand or fall together.

Model answer.

  1. Kelvin–Planck statement. No device working in a cycle can take heat from a single reservoir and convert all of it into work. Some heat must be rejected to a colder reservoir, so for every heat engine W < QH and η < 1.
  2. Clausius statement. No device working in a cycle can transfer heat from a colder body to a hotter body without work input. A refrigerator moves heat upward only because a compressor supplies work.
  3. Both statements speak of a cyclic device. A single process can do what they forbid: a gas expanding isothermally turns all the heat it takes into work, but it ends in a different state, so it is not a cycle.
  4. To prove equivalence, show that breaking either statement lets you build a device that breaks the other. Then "not KP ⇒ not Clausius" and "not Clausius ⇒ not KP", so KP ⇔ Clausius.
  5. Part 1: a Kelvin–Planck violator makes a Clausius violator. Let engine E take QH from the hot reservoir and deliver W = QH, rejecting nothing. Use W to drive an ordinary refrigerator R between the same reservoirs; R takes QC from the cold reservoir and rejects QC + W to the hot reservoir. Treat E + R as one device:
    ItemHot reservoirCold reservoirWork
    E−QH0+W = +QH
    R+(QC + W)−QC−W
    E + R+QC−QC0
  6. The pair moves QC from cold to hot with zero net work. That is exactly what the Clausius statement forbids. With numbers: if E takes 100 kJ and gives 100 kJ, and R has COPR = 3, then R lifts 300 kJ and rejects 400 kJ; the hot reservoir gains 300 kJ, the cold one loses 300 kJ, and no work is used.
  7. Part 2: a Clausius violator makes a Kelvin–Planck violator. Let device C move QC from the cold to the hot reservoir with no work. Run an ordinary engine E between the same reservoirs, adjusted so that it rejects exactly QC; it takes QH and gives W = QH − QC.
    ItemHot reservoirCold reservoirWork
    C+QC−QC0
    E−QH+QC+(QH − QC)
    C + E−(QH − QC)0+(QH − QC)
  8. The cold reservoir is unchanged, so the pair takes heat QH − QC from the hot reservoir alone and turns all of it into work in a cycle. That breaks the Kelvin–Planck statement.
  9. Sketch for each part: two reservoirs as horizontal boxes, the two devices as circles between them, arrows for every heat and work flow with its label, and a dashed outline round the pair to show the combined device.
  10. Conclusion: each statement implies the other, so they are two forms of one law. Kelvin–Planck is the form used for engines (η < 1), Clausius the form used for refrigerators (COP is finite). Check of the numbers in Part 1: energy is conserved, since +300 − 300 + 0 = 0, so only the second law is broken.
Understand Starting from the Carnot cycle, show that ∮δQrev/T = 0 for any reversible cycle and hence that entropy is a state function. Explain how ΔS is calculated for an irreversible process. 10 marks

Points to cover. QH/TH = QC/TC for a Carnot cycle; Any reversible cycle as a set of thin Carnot cycles; Zero cyclic integral means path independence; Irreversible process: use a reversible path between the same end states.

Model answer.

  1. For a reversible (Carnot) engine between TH and TC, the thermodynamic temperature scale gives QH/QC = TH/TC, so QH/TH = QC/TC.
  2. Count heat into the system as positive. Over one Carnot cycle the heat crosses only at TH (+QH) and TC (−QC), so Σ Q/T = QH/TH − QC/TC = 0.
  3. Any reversible cycle drawn on P–V axes can be covered by a large number of thin Carnot cycles made of isotherms and adiabats. Neighbouring adiabats are traversed in opposite directions, so their contributions cancel, and the outer zig-zag path approaches the real cycle as the strips get thinner.
  4. Each strip gives δQH/TH − δQC/TC = 0, so adding them all: ∮δQrev/T = 0 for every reversible cycle.
  5. Path independence: take two states 1 and 2 and two reversible paths A and B between them. Going 1→2 along A and back 2→1 along B makes a reversible cycle:
    ∫(1→2, A) δQrev/T + ∫(2→1, B) δQrev/T = 0
∫(1→2, A) δQrev/T = ∫(1→2, B) δQrev/T
  6. The integral depends only on the end states, so it is the change of a property. Define dS = δQrev/T and ΔS = ∫₁² δQrev/T. Q and W are path functions; S, like U, is a state function.
  7. Irreversible process: the actual δQ/T does not give ΔS. Because S is a state function, pick any reversible path between the same end states and integrate along it. The Clausius inequality then says ΔS ≥ ∫δQ/T for the real path.
  8. Example: 1 mol of ideal gas at 300 K expands freely from 10 L to 20 L (Q = 0, W = 0, T unchanged). The reversible isotherm between the same states gives ΔS = R ln(V₂/V₁) = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K. The real ∫δQ/T is 0, which is less than 5.76 J/K, as the inequality requires; Sgen = 5.76 J/K.
  9. Two paths, one ΔS. Check path independence with the same end states (1 mol of a diatomic ideal gas, 300 K, 10 L → 20 L, CV = 5R/2 = 20.785 J/K, γ = 1.4). Path A, reversible isotherm: ΔS = 5.76 J/K as above. Path B: a reversible adiabatic expansion from 10 L to 20 L (ΔS = 0) cools the gas to T′ = 300 × (10/20)0.4 = 227.36 K; then reversible heating at constant volume back to 300 K gives ΔS = CV ln(300/227.36) = 20.785 × 0.27726 = 5.76 J/K.
  10. Both paths give 5.76 J/K even though their heat flows differ (1728.8 J on path A; CV × 72.64 = 1509.9 J on path B). Q depends on the path; Qrev/T summed along any reversible path does not, which is what a state function means.
  11. Check with the reversible isotherm itself: Qrev = RT ln 2 = 8.314 × 300 × 0.6931 = 1728.8 J, and Qrev/T = 1728.8/300 = 5.76 J/K, the same ΔS.
Understand State the third law of thermodynamics and explain, using S = kB ln Ω, why a perfect crystal has zero entropy at 0 K while carbon monoxide and ice keep a residual entropy. Show how absolute entropies are built from heat-capacity data. 10 marks

Points to cover. Statement and the perfect-crystal condition; Ω = 1 gives S = 0; frozen-in orientations give Ω > 1; Residual entropy of CO and ice with numbers; S(T) from the Debye part, ∫Cp/T dT and transition terms.

Model answer.

  1. Third law. The entropy of a perfect, ordered crystal is zero at 0 K. A consequence is that 0 K cannot be reached in a finite number of steps.
  2. Microscopic meaning. Boltzmann: S = kB ln Ω, where Ω is the number of microstates that give the same macrostate and kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K. In a perfect crystal at 0 K every molecule sits in its lowest-energy position in one arrangement, so Ω = 1 and S = kB ln 1 = 0.
  3. Carbon monoxide. CO is nearly symmetric, so each molecule can sit as CO or OC with almost the same energy, and cooling freezes a random mixture. If each of the NA molecules in a mole had 2 equally likely orientations, Ω = 2NA and S = kB NA ln 2 = R ln 2 = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The measured value is about 4.6 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, a little smaller because the freezing is not perfectly random.
  4. Ice. The hydrogen atoms can be arranged in many ways that all obey the hydrogen-bond rules. Pauling counted about (3/2)NA arrangements per mole, giving S(0) = R ln 1.5 = 8.314 × 0.4055 = 3.37 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, close to the measured value. Glasses also keep frozen disorder. None of this breaks the third law: these solids are not perfect crystals.
  5. Absolute entropy from data. With S(0) = 0, heat reversibly at constant pressure and add every piece:
    S(T) = aT₁³/3 + ∫(T₁→Tm) Cp,s/T dT + ΔHfus/Tm + ∫(Tm→T) Cp,l/T dT
(below T₁ ≈ 10 K, Cp = aT³, so that piece is Cp(T₁)/3)
  6. Worked numbers (the third-law chapter data): Cp(10 K) = 0.80, solid integral 60.0, ΔHfus = 8.00 kJ/mol at 200 K, liquid Cp = 120 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

With S(0) = 0, tables can list absolute entropies S° that are used directly in reaction-equilibrium calculations. Understand Explain the microscopic meaning of entropy using S = kB ln Ω for N labelled gas molecules in a box with two equal halves. Show that the result for one mole doubling its volume agrees with the classical ΔS = R ln(V₂/V₁). 8 marks

Points to cover. Microstate and macrostate; Ω(n) = N!/[n!(N − n)!]; Even split is the most probable macrostate; ΔS = kB ln 2NA = R ln 2.

Model answer.

  1. A microstate says where every labelled molecule is (here: left or right half). A macrostate is what we measure, here n, the number of molecules in the left half.
  2. Each molecule has 2 choices, so the box has 2N microstates in all. The number giving n molecules on the left is Ω(n) = N!/[n!(N − n)!]. At fixed U, V and N every microstate is equally likely, so the macrostate with the largest Ω is the one we observe.
  3. N = 4: Ω = 1, 4, 6, 4, 1 for n = 0 to 4 (total 16). N = 10: the all-left macrostate has Ω = 1, the even split has Ω = 252, out of 1024.
    MacrostateΩS/kB = ln Ω
    N = 4, all left10
    N = 4, 2 and 261.792
    N = 4, whole box162.773
    N = 10, all left10
    N = 10, 5 and 52525.529
  4. As N grows, the even split takes an ever larger share and the chance that all molecules gather in one half, (1/2)N, becomes vanishingly small. This is why a gas spreads to fill its container and never un-mixes: the process goes towards larger Ω, that is larger S.
  5. One mole doubling its volume. Each of the NA molecules now has twice as many places, so Ω₂/Ω₁ = 2NA. ΔS = kB ln(Ω₂/Ω₁) = kB NA ln 2 = R ln 2.
  6. Classical result for an isothermal ideal gas: ΔS = R ln(V₂/V₁) = R ln 2 = 8.314 × 0.6931 = 5.76 J/K. The two routes agree, because R = NA kB = 6.022 × 10²³ × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  7. Probability of un-mixing. With N = 10 the chance of finding all molecules on the left is 1/1024 = 0.00098; with N = 100 it is (1/2)100 = 7.9 × 10⁻³¹. For a mole it is never seen.
  8. For large N, Stirling's formula ln N! ≈ N ln N − N gives ln Ω(N/2) ≈ N ln 2, the same as ln 2N for the whole box. For N = 100 the exact values are ln Ω(50) = 66.78 and 100 ln 2 = 69.31, already within 4 %. So the even-split macrostate carries almost all the entropy of the gas.

Apply A Carnot engine works between 800 K and 300 K and receives 500 kJ from the hot reservoir per cycle. Find the efficiency, the work and the heat rejected. Then find the COP of a Carnot refrigerator and of a Carnot heat pump working between the same two temperatures, and comment. 10 marks

Points to cover. η = 1 − TC/TH; W and QC with an energy check; COPR = TC/(TH − TC), COPHP = COPR + 1; Entropy check: QH/TH = QC/TC.

Model answer.

  1. Data: TH = 800 K, TC = 300 K, QH = 500 kJ. Temperatures are already in kelvin.
  2. Carnot efficiency: η = 1 − TC/TH = 1 − 300/800 = 1 − 0.375 = 0.625, that is 62.5 %.
  3. Work: W = η QH = 0.625 × 500 = 312.5 kJ.
  4. Heat rejected: QC = QH − W = 500 − 312.5 = 187.5 kJ. Ratio check: QC/QH = 187.5/500 = 0.375 = TC/TH.
  5. Entropy check: the hot reservoir loses 500/800 = 0.625 kJ/K and the cold one gains 187.5/300 = 0.625 kJ/K, so the total entropy change is zero, as it must be for a reversible cycle.
  6. Carnot refrigerator between the same temperatures: COPR = TC/(TH − TC) = 300/(800 − 300) = 300/500 = 0.60.
  7. Carnot heat pump: COPHP = TH/(TH − TC) = 800/500 = 1.60 = COPR + 1.
  8. Comment: COPR is below 1 here because the lift from 300 K to 800 K is very large; every kJ moved costs 1/0.6 = 1.67 kJ of work. COP is not an efficiency and can be above or below 1. With the reversed engine, 312.5 kJ of work would lift 187.5 kJ from 300 K and deliver 500 kJ at 800 K, exactly undoing the engine.
  9. Which change helps more? Lowering TC by 10 K gives η = 1 − 290/800 = 0.6375; raising TH by 10 K gives η = 1 − 300/810 = 0.6296. Lowering the cold temperature helps more, because η depends on the ratio TC/TH.
  10. Why the result holds for any working fluid: the Carnot theorem says every reversible engine between the same two reservoirs has the same efficiency, so 62.5 % is the limit whether the engine uses steam, air or an ideal gas. Only the reservoir temperatures, in kelvin, enter the answer.
  11. A real engine for comparison. If a real engine gave 250 kJ from the same 500 kJ, η = 0.50 and the second-law efficiency is 0.50/0.625 = 0.80. Analyze Two water streams, 1 kg/s at 350 K and 1 kg/s at 290 K, mix adiabatically at steady state. Take cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K) and T₀ = 290 K. Find the exit temperature, the entropy generated and the lost work. Then find the work a reversible engine could produce if it brought the two streams to a common temperature instead, and explain the difference. 13 marks

Points to cover. Energy balance gives 320 K; Sgen from the two stream entropy changes; Wlost = T₀ Sgen; Reversible engine: common temperature √(T₁T₂) and its work.

Model answer.

  1. Energy balance: equal flows and equal cp, so Tout = (350 + 290)/2 = 320 K.
  2. Steady, adiabatic mixer: Sgen = Σṁ_out sout − Σṁ_in sin = ṁcp ln(Tout/T₁) + ṁcp ln(Tout/T₂).
  3. Hot stream: 4.18 × ln(320/350) = 4.18 × (−0.08961) = −0.3746 kW/K. Cold stream: 4.18 × ln(320/290) = 4.18 × 0.09844 = 0.4115 kW/K.
  4. Sgen = −0.3746 + 0.4115 = 0.0369 kW/K > 0: mixing is irreversible.
  5. Lost work: Wlost = T₀Sgen = 290 × 0.0369 = 10.7 kW.
  6. Reversible alternative: let the hot stream feed a reversible engine whose cold side is the cold stream. With no entropy generated, ln(Tf/350) + ln(Tf/290) = 0, so Tf = √(350 × 290) = 318.6 K.
  7. Work from the energy balance: W = ṁcp(350 − Tf) − ṁcp(Tf − 290) = 4.18 × (640 − 2 × 318.6) = 4.18 × 2.82 = 11.8 kW.
  8. Why 11.8 kW and not 10.7 kW: the two answers use different end states. The reversible engine ends with both streams at 318.6 K, not 320 K, because it has turned part of the energy into work. The lost work 10.7 kW is measured against a dead state at T₀ = 290 K for the mixer's own end state. Both say the same thing: mixing at a 60 K difference throws away about 11 kW of work potential.
  9. Summary:
    RouteExit T (K)Sgen (kW/K)Work (kW)
    adiabatic mixing320.00.03690 (10.7 lost)
    reversible engine318.6011.8
  10. Exergy check. Flow exergy of liquid water relative to T₀ = 290 K: ṁcp[(T − T₀) − T₀ ln(T/T₀)]. Hot inlet: 4.18 × [60 − 290 × ln(350/290)] = 4.18 × (60 − 54.535) = 22.84 kW. Cold inlet at T₀: 0. Exit, 2 kg/s at 320 K: 8.36 × [30 − 290 × ln(320/290)] = 8.36 × (30 − 28.548) = 12.14 kW.
  11. Exergy destroyed = 22.84 + 0 − 12.14 = 10.70 kW, the same as T₀Sgen. So almost half of the hot stream's work potential is lost in the mixer.
  12. Evaluation: in plant design this is why hot and cold streams are matched in heat exchangers with small temperature differences rather than mixed, and why waste heat at 350 K still has some value. Check: Sgen small but positive; Wlost/(ṁcpΔT of the hot stream, 125.4 kW) is under 10 %, sensible for a 60 K gap near ambient.
Analyze A salesman claims a cyclic device takes 100 kW of heat from a reservoir at 500 K, rejects heat to a reservoir at 300 K and delivers 60 kW of work. Test the claim with the first law, the Carnot limit and the Clausius inequality, and find the largest work the device could give. 10 marks

Points to cover. First law gives QC = 40 kW; Carnot limit 40 %; ∮δQ/T > 0 means impossible; Maximum work 40 kW.

Model answer.

  1. First law for a cycle: QC = QH − W = 100 − 60 = 40 kW. Energy balances, so the first law alone cannot reject the claim.
  2. Claimed efficiency: η = W/QH = 60/100 = 0.60.
  3. Carnot limit between the same reservoirs: ηmax = 1 − 300/500 = 0.40. The claim (0.60) is above the limit, so it breaks the Carnot theorem and therefore the second law.
  4. Clausius inequality: ∮δQ/T = QH/TH − QC/TC = 100/500 − 40/300 = 0.2000 − 0.1333 = +0.0667 kW/K. A positive value is impossible (it must be ≤ 0).
  5. Same result as reservoir entropy: the hot reservoir changes by −100/500 = −0.2000 kW/K and the cold one by +40/300 = +0.1333 kW/K; total −0.0667 kW/K, a decrease of the entropy of an isolated system, which is impossible.
  6. Largest possible work: a reversible device gives Wmax = 0.40 × 100 = 40 kW and rejects 60 kW; then ∮δQ/T = 100/500 − 60/300 = 0. Any real device gives less than 40 kW.
  7. What would make 60 kW possible: a hotter source or a colder sink. With TC = 300 K, η = 0.60 needs TH ≥ 300/0.40 = 750 K.
  8. Rating three possible claims for the same 100 kW at 500 K:
    Claimed W (kW)QC (kW)Sgen = QC/300 − 100/500 (kW/K)Verdict
    3070+0.0333possible, irreversible
    40600reversible limit
    6040−0.0667impossible
  9. The test that needs no Carnot formula is the entropy of the two reservoirs: any proposed device can be judged from its three numbers QH, QC and W and the two temperatures. That is why the Clausius inequality is the general form of the second law.
  10. For the 30 kW device: second-law efficiency 30/40 = 0.75, and lost work TC Sgen = 300 × 0.0333 = 10 kW = 40 − 30. The entropy balance and the work balance tell the same story.
  11. Sketch: hot reservoir at 500 K above, cold reservoir at 300 K below, the device as a circle between them with 100 kW in, the claimed 60 kW out as work and 40 kW rejected; mark the reversible case (40 kW work, 60 kW rejected) beside it for comparison.

Evaluate Compare the Carnot and Rankine cycles for a steam power plant. Using the plant with steam at 4 MPa and 400 °C and a condenser at 10 kPa (η = 35.3 %), explain with the mean temperature of heat addition why the Rankine efficiency is lower, and evaluate how superheat, reheat, regeneration and condenser pressure each change it. 14 marks

Points to cover. Why a Carnot steam cycle is impractical; η = 1 − Tlow/Tmean with numbers; Effect of each modification; Practical limits.

Model answer.

  1. A Carnot cycle needs all heat added at one top temperature and all heat rejected at one bottom temperature, with isentropic compression and expansion. With steam inside the dome that means compressing a wet mixture back to saturated liquid, which needs a large two-phase compressor, and a turbine running wet. Superheating at constant temperature is not practical either.
  2. The Rankine cycle condenses fully and pumps liquid only (4.0 kJ/kg in the example, about 0.4 % of the turbine work), and adds heat at constant pressure. Heat is therefore added over a range of temperatures, from 45.9 °C feedwater up to 400 °C steam.
  3. Mean temperature of heat addition: Tm = qin/(s₃ − s₂) = 3018.6/(6.7712 − 0.6492) = 3018.6/6.1220 = 493.1 K. Heat is rejected at Tlow = 318.96 K (45.8 °C). Then η = 1 − Tlow/Tm = 1 − 318.96/493.1 = 0.353, exactly the cycle efficiency.
  4. Comparison with Carnot cycles:
    CycleTop temperatureη
    Rankine (example)Tm = 493.1 K35.3 %
    Carnot, boiling to condensing523.5 K (250.4 °C)39.1 %
    Carnot, peak to condensing673.15 K (400 °C)52.6 %
  5. So the Rankine cycle loses mainly because much of its heat goes in at low temperature (warming the feedwater from 46 °C to 250 °C). Every improvement either raises Tm or lowers Tlow.
  6. Superheat: raises the top temperature and so Tm, and dries the exhaust. Limited by metal strength (about 540–600 °C in conventional plants).
  7. Reheat: adds a second slice of heat at high temperature. For 10 MPa, 500 °C with reheat at 1 MPa to 500 °C, η rises from 40.2 % to 41.3 % and the exhaust quality from 0.79 to 0.95; the drier exhaust is the main reason to reheat.
  8. Regeneration: bleed steam heats the feedwater, so the boiler no longer adds the low-temperature heat. Tm rises, qin falls, and η rises by several percentage points in large plants; the cost is less work per kg of steam and the heaters.
  9. Condenser pressure: lowering pc lowers Tlow. It is limited by the cooling-water temperature (near 30 °C in India), air leakage and the size of the last-stage blades.
  10. Evaluation: superheat, reheat and regeneration all move the Rankine cycle toward the Carnot cycle between the same extremes while keeping its practical advantages (liquid pumping, dry turbine). Check: the Tm method reproduces the computed 35.3 %, so the explanation is quantitative, not only qualitative.

Glossary

Absolute entropy
Entropy measured from the third-law zero at 0 K, as listed in steam and property tables.
Boiler
Heat exchanger in a power plant that adds Qin to pressurised water to make steam.
Boltzmann constant
kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K, the entropy per unit of ln Ω.
Clausius inequality
∮δQ/T ≤ 0 for any thermodynamic cycle.
Condenser
Heat exchanger that rejects Qout and condenses turbine exhaust back to liquid.
Control volume
A fixed region in space with mass and energy crossing its boundary; used for turbines, compressors and heat exchangers.
Entropy balance
Accounting statement: entropy in by flow and heat, plus generated entropy, equals entropy out plus accumulation.
Evaporator
The cold-side heat exchanger of a refrigerator where the refrigerant boils and absorbs QC.
Heat reservoir
A body large enough that taking or giving heat does not change its temperature.
Ideal work
The work a process would need or deliver if it were fully reversible, Wideal = Wactual − Wlost.
Inversion temperature
Temperature at which μJT changes sign; above it a gas warms on throttling.
Irreversibility
Any effect such as friction, unrestrained expansion or finite-temperature heat transfer that makes a process non-reversible and creates entropy.
Isentropic
A process of constant entropy, the ideal limit for an adiabatic reversible turbine or compressor.
Joule–Thomson coefficient
μJT = (∂T/∂P)H; positive means the gas cools on throttling.
Linde cycle
Liquefaction cycle that cools the gas in a counter-current exchanger and then expands it through a throttle valve.
Macrostate
The measured, macroscopic description of a system (for example U, V and N) that many microstates can produce.
Reversible adiabatic
An isentropic process, drawn as a vertical line on T–s coordinates.
Reversible process
An ideal process whose direction can be reversed by an infinitesimal change, leaving no trace on the surroundings.
Second-law efficiency
Ratio of actual performance to the best reversible performance under the same conditions.
Steady state
Condition where properties inside the control volume do not change with time, so accumulation terms vanish.
Superheat
Heating steam above its saturation temperature at the same pressure.
Thermal efficiency
Work output divided by heat input of a heat engine, η = W/QH.
Throttling
Flow through a restriction with no work and negligible heat transfer, so enthalpy stays constant and pressure falls.
