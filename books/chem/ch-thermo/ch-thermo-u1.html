1.1 Systems, variables, intensive and extensive properties

Every thermodynamics problem starts with a decision: what is inside the boundary and what is outside. The stuff inside is the system; the rest is the surroundings. Once the boundary is drawn, we describe the system by its properties — temperature, pressure, volume, mass. These properties split into two classes. Extensive properties grow with the size of the system; intensive properties do not. The split decides what you may add, what you may look up in a table, and what you may hold constant. Plant design, steam tables and simulation software all rest on it.

You need: nothing beyond school physics and chemistry — mass, volume, temperature and pressure as measured quantities. The next chapter, 1.2, uses these terms for state and path functions.

System, surroundings and boundary

Thermodynamics begins with a choice. You draw a real or imaginary surface and declare that everything inside is the system and everything outside is the surroundings. That surface is the boundary. It can be the inner wall of a reactor, the skin of a potato in an oven, or a bubble of steam in a pipe. It can be fixed or moving, real or imagined.

The boundary decides the type of system. A closed system (some syllabi call it a control mass) has no mass crossing the boundary, though heat and work may cross. A sealed cylinder of gas cooled in a fridge is closed. An open system (a control volume) has mass crossing the boundary: a pump, a turbine, a length of pipe, a cup of chai while you sip it. An isolated system has neither mass nor energy crossing. A perfectly insulated, rigid, sealed flask is the ideal case; no real flask is perfect.

  1. Sketch the equipment and mark the boundary with a dashed line.
  2. Ask: does mass cross the boundary? If yes, the system is open.
  3. If no mass crosses, ask: does heat or work cross? If yes, the system is closed.
  4. If neither mass nor energy crosses, the system is isolated — an ideal limit.

Phase, component, homogeneous and heterogeneous

A phase is a region with uniform chemical composition and uniform physical properties. Ice and liquid water in a glass are two phases. Air is one phase even though it is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen and argon, because it is mixed at the molecular level.

A component is a chemically independent species. The number of components is the smallest number of species needed to describe the composition of every phase present. Pure water is one component. Water plus ethanol is two. Salt dissolved in water is still two components (salt and water), even though the salt splits into ions.

  • A homogeneous system has one phase throughout: superheated steam, brine, dry air.
  • A heterogeneous system has two or more phases with boundaries between them: wet steam in a boiler drum, oil floating on water.
  • One phase may contain many components; many phases may share one component.

Intensive and extensive properties

Any measurable feature of the system is a property. The test for the two classes is a thought experiment: cut the system into two equal halves. An extensive property halves with the system. Mass m, volume V, amount of substance n, internal energy U, enthalpy H and entropy S are extensive. Two identical vessels joined together hold twice the volume and twice the mass of one. An intensive property does not change with size. Temperature T, pressure P, density ρ and molar volume do not halve; each half of a gas at 300 K is still at 300 K.

A ratio of two extensive properties is intensive. Density divides mass by volume, so the size cancels. Specific volume is volume per unit mass. Molar volume is volume per mole. Specific properties are per kilogram (lower-case symbols v, u, h, s). Molar properties are per mole. Both are intensive, which is why a steam table can list them without knowing the mass of the plant.

ρ = m/V,   v = V/m = 1/ρ,   Vm = V/n

ρ is density in kg/m³; m is mass in kg; V is volume in m³; v is specific volume in m³/kg; Vm is molar volume in m³/mol; n is amount of substance in mol.

The rule that catches students: extensive properties add, intensive properties never do. Join two 1 m³ tanks of water at 300 K and you get 2 m³ at 300 K, not 2 m³ at 600 K. Write m = m₁ + m₂ for masses, but never T = T₁ + T₂ for temperatures.

Worked example

Question. A closed rigid vessel holds 2.0 kg of gas at 300 K and 150 kPa. Its volume is 1.2 m³. Classify m, V, T, P, density and specific volume as intensive or extensive. Compute the density and the specific volume.

  1. Data: mass m = 2.0 kg, volume V = 1.2 m³, temperature T = 300 K, pressure P = 150 kPa. The vessel is closed and rigid, so m is fixed.
  2. Classify the simple variables: m and V grow when you take more gas, so both are extensive. T and P are the same everywhere inside and do not depend on how much gas is present, so both are intensive.
  3. Density: ρ = m/V = 2.0 kg ÷ 1.2 m³ = 1.667 kg/m³. Mass per unit volume is a ratio of two extensives, so ρ is intensive.
  4. Specific volume: v = V/m = 1.2 m³ ÷ 2.0 kg = 0.6 m³/kg. Volume per unit mass is also intensive.
  5. Consistency check: v = 1/ρ = 1 ÷ 1.667 = 0.6 m³/kg. The two answers agree.

Exam traps

  • Density ρ = m/V and specific volume v = V/m are intensive even though they are built from the extensive m and V. A ratio of two extensives is intensive; do not label the ratio extensive because a big letter appears in the formula.
  • Intensive properties are not additive. Joining two vessels at 300 K and 400 K does not give 350 K; the final temperature depends on the masses and heat capacities, and the volumes add while the temperatures do not.
  • Closed means no mass crosses, not no energy. A closed vessel can still be heated or stirred. An insulated vessel is not automatically isolated unless no work crosses either.
  • The system is not the equipment. In a cylinder, the gas may be the system and the metal wall part of the surroundings; choose and state the boundary before naming anything, because the same gas can be closed in one problem and open in another.

1.2 Point and path functions; reversible and irreversible processes

A state function depends only on where the system is, not how it got there. Pressure, volume and internal energy are state functions. Work and heat are path functions: their values depend on the route between the same two states. Engineers use this distinction to compute work in pistons, compressors and turbines, and to test whether a proposed process is reversible. The same start and end can give different work and different heat, and the first law ties the difference to the path.

You need 1.1 for system, boundary and properties.

State functions, path functions and differentials

A state function has a fixed value once the state is fixed by properties such as P, V and T. Its value does not depend on history. Some syllabi call it a point function because it belongs to one point on a property diagram. Pressure P, volume V, temperature T and internal energy U are state functions. The change ΔU = U₂ − U₁ depends only on the two end states.

A path function depends on the route between the states. Work W and heat Q are path functions. They are energy in transit across the boundary, not stored properties; see 1.3. A small amount of work is written δW and a small amount of heat as δQ. The symbol δ is used because there is no function W(P,V) or Q(P,V) whose difference gives δW or δQ. Write W for total work, not ΔW.

∮ dX = 0

X is any state function; ∮ means integration around a closed path that returns to the same state; dX is an exact differential.

dX = M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy is exact when ∂M/∂y = ∂N/∂x

M and N are functions of x and y; ∂M/∂y is the partial derivative of M with respect to y; the test is done at constant other variable. Some syllabi call this the exact/inexact differential test.

  • Exact differential: value depends only on endpoints; the quantity is a state function.
  • Inexact differential: value depends on the path; the quantity is a path function such as W or Q.
  • For a state function, the cyclic integral is zero. For work, ∮ δW is not zero in general.

Reversible processes

A reversible process is an ideal limit. The system moves through a quasi-static path: it stays infinitesimally close to equilibrium, so P and T are nearly uniform inside it. There is no friction and no finite driving force. The external pressure differs from the system pressure by only dP, and the external temperature differs from the system temperature by only dT. An infinitesimal change in the driving force can reverse the direction.

For a reversible expansion, the external pressure equals the system pressure at every step. The work done by the gas is the area under the P–V curve: W = ∫ P dV. This path can be drawn on a P–V diagram because P is defined at each step. Reversible paths are useful limits: they give the maximum work in expansion and the minimum work in compression.

δW = P_ext dV

δW is small work done by the system in J; P_ext is the external pressure in Pa or kPa; dV is the small volume change in m³ or L. For a reversible path, P_ext = P, the system pressure.

Real processes are irreversible to some degree. Friction, stirring and finite temperature differences add dissipation. A reversible process is not a claim that real equipment is reversible; it is a benchmark for calculations and for the second-law limits studied later.

Irreversible processes and the same endpoints

An irreversible process cannot be reversed by an infinitesimal change. It has finite driving forces, gradients or friction, and reversing it leaves a net change in the surroundings. Common examples are sudden expansion of a gas, paddle-wheel stirring of a liquid, and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference.

In a sudden expansion, the gas pushes a piston against a finite external pressure. The work is W = P_ext(V₂ − V₁), not ∫ P dV, because the gas pressure inside may not be uniform and is not equal to P_ext. In free expansion into vacuum, P_ext = 0, so the work is zero. Stirring puts work into the liquid and raises its temperature; the liquid cannot spontaneously turn that heat back into shaft work.

The same two end states can give different work along different paths. On a P–V plot, work done by the gas is the area under the path. Two routes between the same endpoints have different areas, so W differs. This is the clearest proof that work is a path function, not a state function.

W = ∫ P dV (reversible path)

W is work done by the gas in J; P is absolute pressure in Pa or kPa; V is volume in m³ or L. Use consistent units; 1 kPa·L = 1 J.

Worked example

Question. A gas changes from A (P = 3 kPa, V = 1 L) to B (P = 1 kPa, V = 3 L). Path A passes through (2 L, 1.5 kPa); path B passes through (2 L, 2 kPa). Compute the work done by the gas along each path. Use 1 kPa·L = 1 J.

  1. For a straight segment on a P–V plot, area = average pressure × volume change = 0.5(P₁ + P₂)(V₂ − V₁).
  2. Path A, first segment: 0.5(3 + 1.5)(2 − 1) = 0.5 × 4.5 × 1 = 2.25 J.
  3. Path A, second segment: 0.5(1.5 + 1)(3 − 2) = 0.5 × 2.5 × 1 = 1.25 J.
  4. Path A total: W_A = 2.25 + 1.25 = 3.50 J.
  5. Path B, first segment: 0.5(3 + 2)(2 − 1) = 0.5 × 5 × 1 = 2.50 J.
  6. Path B, second segment: 0.5(2 + 1)(3 − 2) = 0.5 × 3 × 1 = 1.50 J.
  7. Path B total: W_B = 2.50 + 1.50 = 4.00 J.
  8. The endpoints are the same, but W_A ≠ W_B. Work is a path function.

Answer. Work along path A is 3.5 J and along path B is 4.0 J; the same endpoints give different work, so work is a path function.

Exam traps

  • Treating work as a state function because a reversible path lets you calculate it from P and V. Reversible only means P is defined along the path; it does not make work path-independent.
  • Thinking all reversible paths between two states give the same work. Different reversible P–V routes have different areas under them.
  • Writing dW or ΔW for a small or total amount of work. Use δW for a small path-dependent amount and W for total work.
  • Using W = ∫ P dV for an irreversible sudden expansion. Use W = P_ext(V₂ − V₁) when P_ext is fixed and finite, and remember P_ext may not equal the gas pressure.
  • Mixing up exact and inexact differentials: ∮ dX = 0 for a state function X, but ∮ δW is generally not zero.

1.3 Energy classifications; work and heat

Energy in a closed system is either stored inside it or crossing its boundary. Stored energy is internal, kinetic and potential; crossing energy is work or heat. The first law balances only what crosses against what the system keeps. A charger, a turbine shaft and a cup of chai ask the same question: how much crossed, which way, and what stayed. This chapter fixes classifications, work and heat modes, and the sign rule.

You need 1.1 for system and boundary, and 1.2 for state versus path functions, because work and heat are path functions.

Stored energy: total energy E = U + KE + PE

A closed system stores energy in three forms. Total energy E is their sum in joules (J). Internal energy U is microscopic: molecular translation, rotation, vibration and intermolecular pull. Kinetic energy KE and potential energy PE are macroscopic: whole mass moving or at a height.

E = U + ½mV² + mgz

E is total energy in J; U is internal energy in J; m is mass in kg; V is speed in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s²; z is height in m above a chosen datum.

U has no measurable zero. Tables set U = 0 at a reference state and report changes, so the first law uses ΔU, never U alone (see 1.5). For z, pick a datum and stay with it; only changes matter.

In a rigid closed tank with no shaft, ΔKE and ΔPE are zero, so ΔE = ΔU. In a nozzle or falling stream they are not, and dropping them gives a wrong answer (see 1.8). E is extensive; specific energy e = E/m in J/kg is intensive (see 1.1).

Work: energy crossing as force times distance

Work crosses a boundary when a force moves its point of application: W = ∫F dx, force in newtons, distance in metres, work in joules. Work is energy in transit, not stored. It is a path function, so the route matters (see 1.2). Four modes cover most problems.

  • Boundary (P-V) work: W = ∫P dV, for a gas pushing a piston. At constant pressure W = P(V₂ − V₁), P in Pa, V in m³.
  • Shaft power: 2πNτ, N in revolutions per second, torque τ in N·m, giving watts.
  • Electrical power: VI, V in volts, I in amperes, giving watts; energy is ∫VI dt.
  • Surface work: ∫γ dA, surface tension γ in N/m, area A in m². Small but real in droplets.

Sign rule: work done by the system on the surroundings is positive. An expanding gas pushes the piston out and does positive work. Work done on the system, by a stirrer or compressed spring, is negative.

1 J = 1 N·m; 1 kJ = 1000 J. Power is rate: 1 W = 1 J/s, so 1 kW = 1 kJ/s. A 5 kW pump for 10 min (600 s) delivers 5 × 600 = 3000 kJ of shaft work.

Heat: energy crossing because of a temperature difference

Heat is energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference. It flows on its own from hot to cold and stops when temperatures match. Like work, it is a path function and is never stored: a body contains internal energy, not heat.

  • Conduction: Q̇ = kA ΔT / L. k is thermal conductivity in W/(m·K), A area in m², L thickness in m, ΔT in K. A steel spoon left in hot chai.
  • Convection: Q̇ = hA ΔT, h the convection coefficient in W/(m²·K). Air blowing over a hot radiator plate.
  • Radiation: Q̇ = εσA(T_s⁴ − T_surr⁴), emissivity ε from 0 to 1, σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). Temperatures in kelvin.

The dot means rate in W. Total heat is Q = ∫Q̇ dt. A constant-power heater P for t seconds delivers Q = Pt.

For a plain temperature change, Q = mCΔT, C specific heat in J/(kg·K) (see 1.7). Two litres of water (m ≈ 2 kg, C = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K)) heated by 25 K takes Q ≈ 2 × 4.18 × 25 ≈ 209 kJ.

One sign convention for the whole book

Every balance here uses one convention. Heat into the system is positive. Work done by the system is positive.

ΔE = Q − W   and, with KE and PE negligible,   ΔU = Q − W

Q is net heat entering the system in J; W is net work done by the system in J; ΔU = U₂ − U₁ is the change in internal energy in J.

Read wording carefully: 'receives 50 kJ of heat' gives Q = +50 kJ; 'rejects 15 kJ' gives Q = −15 kJ; 'does 20 kJ of work' gives W = +20 kJ; '10 kJ of work is done on it' gives W = −10 kJ.

This is the whole first law for a closed system; 1.5 builds on it. Open systems add flow work and use enthalpy instead of internal energy (see 1.6).

Question. A closed system receives 50 kJ of heat and does 20 kJ of work. Find ΔU. In a second step it loses 15 kJ of heat while 10 kJ of work is done on it. Find ΔU for the second step and the net ΔU over both steps.

1.4 Zeroth law and temperature scales

The zeroth law makes temperature measurable. If body A has no net heat exchange with body B, and B with C, then A and C also have no net heat exchange. This transitive property lets us build a thermometer: put it in contact with a body, wait for balance, and read a number. That number is temperature. Industry uses it to check steam headers, chilled-water lines and reactor jackets. The definitions needed are thermal walls, empirical temperature, fixed points and the four common scales.

You need: 1.1 for system, boundary and intensive properties; 1.3 for heat as energy crossing a boundary.

Thermal contact and the law

Two bodies are in thermal contact when energy can cross the wall between them as heat. A wall that blocks heat is adiabatic; a wall that lets heat pass is diathermal. Put A and B in contact through a diathermal wall. After some time the net heat flow stops. They are then in thermal equilibrium. The zeroth law states: if A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B with C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with C. This transitivity is what makes a thermometer work: thermometer B reads body A, then reads body C; if the reading is unchanged, A and C have the same temperature. Some syllabi call this the law of thermal equilibrium.

  • Adiabatic wall: no heat transfer. Use it to define an isolated system.
  • Diathermal wall: heat transfer allowed until equilibrium.
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net heat flow, and all bodies would show the same temperature on a common thermometer.

Empirical temperature and scales

An empirical temperature is a number assigned by a chosen thermometer. Pick a property X that changes with hotness, for example the length of a mercury column or the resistance of platinum. Two fixed points fix a linear scale.

θ = aX + b

θ is the empirical temperature; X is the thermometric property, such as mercury length or platinum resistance; a and b are constants fixed by two fixed points.

Reading a thermometer

A thermometer must reach thermal equilibrium before you read it. Tap the bulb, wait, and check that two successive readings agree. A Celsius weather app, a Kelvin steam table and a Fahrenheit datasheet all carry the same physical temperature; only the numbers differ. Convert to one scale before you compare or calculate. Absolute zero is 0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F = 0 R. No body reaches it. For temperature differences, use ΔT(K) = Δt(°C) and Δt(°F) = 1.8Δt(°C).

  1. Write the given value and its scale, for example 27 °C.
  2. For kelvin, add 273.15 to the Celsius value.
  3. For Fahrenheit, multiply the Celsius value by 1.8 and add 32.
  4. For Rankine, add 459.67 to the Fahrenheit value, or multiply the kelvin value by 1.8.
  5. Check the interval: a 10 °C rise is a 10 K rise and an 18 °F rise.

Worked example

Question. A thermometer reads 27 °C. Convert this temperature to K, °F and R.

  • Using 273 instead of 273.15 in Kelvin conversions. For 27 °C that gives 300 K, an error of 0.15 K.

  • Reading a thermometer before thermal equilibrium. The reading drifts; wait until two successive readings agree.
  • Confusing Rankine with Kelvin. Rankine uses Fahrenheit-sized degrees, so 300.15 K equals 540.27 R, not 300.15 R.

1.5 First law for closed systems; Joule's experiment

A closed system keeps a fixed mass, so energy can cross its boundary only as heat or as work. The first law turns that into one line: the rise in internal energy equals the heat added minus the work done. It exists because heat and work are path functions, yet their difference is not — it depends only on the two end states. Joule's paddle-wheel experiment put numbers on this by showing that falling masses and heat are the same currency. Every batch calorimeter, stirred tank and non-flow energy balance uses this form.

You need 1.3 for the work and heat sign convention, and 1.2 for state versus path functions.

The first law for a closed system

A closed system holds a fixed mass. No matter crosses its boundary; only heat and work do. The first law is the energy balance written for that fixed mass, and for a non-flow process it reads as follows.

ΔU = Q − W

ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system (kJ); Q is the heat added to the system across the boundary (kJ); W is the work done by the system on the surroundings (kJ).

The signs come from the convention in 1.3: Q is positive when heat flows in, W is positive when the system does work outside. Some books write ΔU = Q + W and mean work done on the system. Then every W flips sign. Check the convention before you substitute numbers.

Internal energy U is a state function. Its change depends only on the end states, so ΔU is the same for every route between them. Q and W are path functions; only their difference is fixed.

When the system as a whole moves or changes height, add those terms: ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE = Q − W. In most process equipment the last two are negligible and the short form is enough.

∮δQ = ∮δW

the closed loop means a sum over a complete cycle; δQ is the small heat added in one step (kJ) and δW the small work done in that step (kJ).

For a cycle the system returns to its starting state, so ΔU = 0 and the net heat equals the net work. This cyclic form drives every power and refrigeration cycle in Unit 3. Only a few syllabi name the closed-system equation the non-flow energy equation.

Joule's paddle-wheel experiment

In the 1840s James Joule wanted to prove that mechanical work and heat are the same thing measured in different units. He hung a known mass on a string wound round a spindle. As the mass fell, it turned a paddle wheel inside an insulated vessel of water.

The insulation gives Q = 0. The falling mass loses potential energy mgh and delivers it to the water as stirring work. The water does no work outward, so under the convention above W = −mgh. The first law then gives ΔU = 0 − (−mgh) = +mgh, and the water warms.

ΔT = m g h / (1000 m_w c)

m is the falling mass (kg); g = 9.81 m/s²; h is the fall height (m); m_w is the mass of water (kg); c is its specific heat capacity, about 4.18 kJ/(kg·K) for water; ΔT is the temperature rise (K). The product mgh is in joules, and the factor 1000 converts it to kilojoules so it matches c in kJ/(kg·K).

  1. Check the vessel is insulated: set Q = 0.
  2. Compute the stirring work W_shaft = m g h in joules, then divide by 1000 to get kJ.
  3. Apply the sign: this work is done on the system, so W = −W_shaft.
  4. Write ΔU = Q − W = +W_shaft for the water.
  5. Find the rise from ΔU = m_w c ΔT, keeping every term in kJ and kg.

Repeating with different masses and heights, Joule got the same temperature rise per unit of work. That is the mechanical equivalent of heat: roughly 4.18 kJ of work raises 1 kg of water by 1 K, the same effect as 1 kcal. Heat and work are both energy in transit, and the joule measures both.

Joule expansion: the ideal-gas result U = U(T)

Joule ran a second, different experiment. A rigid insulated container is split in two: one side holds gas under pressure, the other is vacuum. A valve is opened and the gas fills the whole vessel.

The vessel is rigid, so there is no boundary work; there is no shaft or electrical work either. Insulation gives Q = 0. So W = 0 and Q = 0, and the first law leaves ΔU = 0. This is free expansion, and it is not the paddle-wheel test.

Joule saw no measurable temperature change in air. If ΔU = 0 and ΔT = 0 for any drop in pressure, then U of that gas depends on temperature alone: U = U(T). That statement defines the ideal gas. Real gases do show a small change, and this experiment is too insensitive to measure it well; throttling tests do better (see Unit 4, Thermodynamic Property Relations). The slopes of U(T) and the heat capacities wait for 1.7.

Worked example

Question. In a Joule paddle-wheel experiment, a 10.0 kg mass falls 2.00 m and stirs 1.00 kg of insulated water. Find the work done on the water and the temperature rise. Take c = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K) and g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. The vessel is insulated, so the heat transfer is Q = 0.
  2. Stirring work from the falling mass: W_shaft = m g h = 10.0 kg × 9.81 m/s² × 2.00 m = 196.2 J.
  3. Convert to kilojoules: 196.2 J ÷ 1000 = 0.1962 kJ.
  4. This work is done on the water, so in ΔU = Q − W it enters as W = −0.1962 kJ.
  5. First law: ΔU = Q − W = 0 − (−0.1962 kJ) = +0.1962 kJ. The internal energy of the water rises.
  6. Temperature rise: ΔT = ΔU / (m_w c) = 0.1962 kJ ÷ (1.00 kg × 4.18 kJ/(kg·K)) = 0.0469 K.

Answer. The mass does 196.2 J (0.1962 kJ) of stirring work on the water, and the water warms by about 0.0469 K.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting Q = 0 in an insulated paddle-wheel test and adding a heat term that is not there.
  • Mixing sign conventions: ΔU = Q − W uses W done by the system. If your book writes ΔU = Q + W, then W is work done on the system and every sign flips.
  • Leaving mgh in joules while c is in kJ/(kg·K). Divide the work by 1000 first, or the temperature rise comes out a thousand times too large.
  • Treating Joule expansion as the same test as the paddle wheel. In free expansion W = 0 and Q = 0; in the paddle-wheel test only Q = 0.
  • Writing Q = W for a single process. That holds only for a complete cycle, where ΔU = 0.

1.6 First law for open systems and steady-flow processes

A pump, turbine or pipe rarely holds a fixed mass. Fluid crosses its boundary all the time. For an open system at steady state, mass in equals mass out, and energy in equals energy out plus shaft work. The bookkeeping must include the work that pushes each stream across the boundary. That push work turns internal energy into enthalpy. The result is the steady-flow energy equation, used for steam turbines, compressors, nozzles and any control volume in a process plant.

You need: 1.3 for work and heat crossing a boundary, and 1.5 for ΔU = Q − W in a closed system.

Control volume and steady flow

A control volume is a chosen region of space. Its boundary may be a real casing or an imaginary surface in a pipe. Fluid crosses the boundary, so the control volume is an open system. At steady flow, properties at each fixed point inside and on the boundary do not change with time. The mass inside stays constant even though fluid keeps moving through. Mass entering must equal mass leaving. For several streams the balance is Σṁ_in = Σṁ_out. Here ṁ is mass flow rate in kg/s; in means entering the control volume, out means leaving. For one inlet 1 and one outlet 2, ṁ1 = ṁ2 = ṁ. Steady flow is not the same as no flow: a turbine at constant load is steady, while its start-up is not. The steady-flow energy equation applies only after this steady state is reached. This mass balance is the first equation for every open system.

Σṁ_in = Σṁ_out

ṁ is mass flow rate in kg/s; in and out mark streams entering and leaving the control volume.

Where enthalpy comes from

A stream entering carries energy in three forms: internal energy u, kinetic energy V²/2 and potential energy gz, all per unit mass. It also carries flow work. To push fluid into the control volume, the upstream fluid does work Pv per unit mass; at the outlet the control volume pushes fluid out. The two push works combine with u into enthalpy. Define h = u + Pv. Here h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg; u is specific internal energy in kJ/kg; P is pressure in kPa; v is specific volume in m³/kg, so Pv has units kJ/kg. Pv is called flow work, not shaft work. With Q positive into the system and W_s positive by the system, the steady-flow energy equation for any number of streams is Q − W_s = Σṁ_out(h + V²/2 + gz) − Σṁ_in(h + V²/2 + gz). Q is heat rate into the control volume in kW; W_s is shaft power by the control volume in kW; V is velocity in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s²; z is elevation in m. Each bracket is energy per kg, so multiply by ṁ in kg/s to get kW. Use consistent units: 1 kJ/kg = 1000 m²/s². Some syllabi call this the control-volume energy balance.

h = u + Pv

One stream, per kilogram

For one inlet and one outlet with the same ṁ, divide by ṁ. The per-unit-mass form is q − w_s = (h2 − h1) + (V2² − V1²)/2 + g(z2 − z1). Here q = Q/ṁ is heat per unit mass in kJ/kg; w_s = W_s/ṁ is shaft work per unit mass in kJ/kg; subscript 1 is inlet and 2 is outlet. The equation says heat in minus shaft work out equals the rise in enthalpy plus kinetic plus potential energy. Many exam problems neglect Δpe or Δke. Neglect only after you estimate the term. A velocity change from 60 to 120 m/s gives Δke = 5.4 kJ/kg, small beside a steam enthalpy change of hundreds of kJ/kg. In a nozzle, however, Δke is the main output, so it cannot be dropped. In a turbine, h falls and W_s leaves; in a compressor, W_s enters and h rises. Sign errors are common: heat loss makes Q negative. The first law does not say Q = W_s; it says Q − W_s equals the net energy carried by the flow. Nozzles and compressors are worked out in 1.8.

Question. Steam enters a turbine at h1 = 3200 kJ/kg and V1 = 60 m/s. It leaves at h2 = 2600 kJ/kg and V2 = 120 m/s. The mass flow rate is 2.0 kg/s. Heat loss from the casing is 50 kW. Neglect potential energy. Find the shaft power.

  2. Convert kinetic energy to kJ/kg: (V2² − V1²)/2 = (120² − 60²)/2 = 5400 J/kg = 5.4 kJ/kg.
  3. Find the enthalpy change: h2 − h1 = 2600 − 3200 = −600 kJ/kg.
  4. Add the specific energy change: −600 + 5.4 = −594.6 kJ/kg.
  5. Multiply by mass flow: ṁ × (−594.6) = 2.0 × (−594.6) = −1189.2 kW.

1.7 Internal energy, enthalpy and heat capacities

A gas heated in a sealed rigid vessel stores energy; the same gas heated under a moving piston stores energy and also pushes the piston. The stored part is internal energy U. Adding the push work PV gives enthalpy H = U + PV. Both are state functions, fixed once the state is fixed. Their slopes against temperature, at constant volume and at constant pressure, are the heat capacities Cv and Cp. These two numbers give the heat in most closed-system heating steps and the work in most flow devices.

You need: the first law for closed systems (1.5), flow work in the steady-flow balance (1.6), and the state-function idea (1.2).

Internal energy U and enthalpy H

Internal energy U is the energy stored inside the boundary: the kinetic and potential energy of the molecules themselves, with the system at rest as a whole. U is a state function (1.2) and it is extensive (1.1), so doubling the gas doubles U. We quote the molar internal energy u = U/n in J/mol, where n is the number of moles, or the specific internal energy u = U/m in J/kg, where m is the mass.

Enthalpy is defined as H = U + PV, where P is pressure in Pa and V is volume in m³. Since 1 Pa = 1 N/m², the product PV carries units of N·m, that is J, and adds cleanly to U. The molar form is h = H/n = u + Pv, with v the molar volume in m³/mol. Because U, P and V are all state variables, H is also a state function, and it is extensive like U.

Why add PV? In an open system the entering fluid must be pushed in and the leaving fluid pushes out; that push work is PV per mole (1.6). Grouping it with U gives one property, h, that appears naturally in nozzles, throttling valves, compressors and heat exchangers. Steam tables list h rather than u for exactly this reason.

Heat capacities Cv and Cp

Heat capacity is the heat needed to raise a unit amount by one kelvin. Which heat it is depends on what is held fixed. At constant volume the slope of u against T is Cv = (∂u/∂T)_v, in J/(mol·K). The subscript v means volume is held constant while T changes. At constant pressure the slope of h against T is Cp = (∂h/∂T)_p. The specific forms use small letters, cv and cp, in J/(kg·K).

Two slopes exist because the paths differ. Heat a gas in a rigid sealed vessel: no expansion work happens, so all the heat lands in internal energy and Q = ΔU. This rule, Qv = ΔU, holds for a closed system with only P-V work. Heat the same gas under a freely moving piston and the gas also pushes the piston, doing PΔV work.

Q_p = ΔU + PΔV = Δ(U + PV) = ΔH

Q_p is the heat added at constant pressure, in J; ΔU is the internal energy change, in J; P is the constant pressure, in Pa; ΔV is the volume change, in m³; ΔH is the enthalpy change, in J.

So for the same kind of process, Qp = ΔH. Read the two rules together: Qv = ΔU measures the change at constant volume, Qp = ΔH measures it at constant pressure. Some syllabi call Cv and Cp specific heats; the name is loose but common.

Ideal gas: u and h depend only on T

Joule's experiment (1.5) showed that a gas expanding into vacuum with no work and no heat shows no temperature change. For an ideal gas this means u depends on T alone, not on P or V. Then h = u + Pv = u + RT is also a function of T alone, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant.

Differentiate h = u + RT with respect to T at constant pressure: the R term contributes R, so Cp = Cv + R.

Cp − Cv = R and γ = Cp/Cv

Cp is the molar heat capacity at constant pressure, in J/(mol·K); Cv is the molar heat capacity at constant volume, in J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); γ (gamma) is the heat capacity ratio, dimensionless.

Exam traps

  • Using Cp for a constant-volume step. In a rigid vessel only Cv applies; at constant pressure only Cp applies.
  • Forgetting the moles. Cv in J/(mol·K) must be multiplied by n; if the value is in J/(kg·K), multiply by mass instead. The unit tells you which.
  • Treating h as heat. h is a state property in J/mol; Q is path-dependent in J. They are numerically equal only for a closed system at constant P with only P-V work.
  • Writing Cp − Cv = R after converting to specific heats. In J/(kg·K) the difference is R/M, with M the molar mass in kg/mol.
  • Applying Q = ΔU to an open steady-flow device. There the balance carries Δh plus kinetic and potential changes (chapter 1.8).

1.8 Flow processes: duct flow, nozzles and compressors

A nozzle, a duct and a compressor all have fluid crossing their boundaries continuously, so the first law must be written for an open system. Because flow work is packed inside enthalpy, the energy equation stays short: enthalpy trades against kinetic energy, or against shaft work. This one equation sizes steam-turbine nozzles, jet-engine intakes, air compressors and gas pipelines. The numbers are simple; the unit slip between kJ/kg and m²/s² is what costs marks.

You need the open-system energy balance from 1.6 and the meaning of enthalpy from 1.7.

The steady-flow energy equation for one kilogram

Take the energy balance of 1.6 and divide it by the mass flow rate. What is left is energy per kilogram passing through a steady device:

h₁ + v₁²/2 + g z₁ + q = h₂ + v₂²/2 + g z₂ + w_s

h is specific enthalpy (J/kg or kJ/kg); v is velocity (m/s); z is height (m); g = 9.81 m/s²; q is heat added to the fluid per kg; w_s is shaft work done by the fluid per kg. Subscript 1 is the inlet, 2 the outlet.

Every term is energy per kilogram. The kinetic term v²/2 comes out in m²/s², which is the same as J/kg. One kJ/kg equals 1000 m²/s², so divide v²/2 by 1000 before adding it to an enthalpy in kJ/kg. This one conversion causes most of the errors in this chapter.

Most devices let you delete terms.

  • Adiabatic nozzle, diffuser or duct: q = 0 and w_s = 0, so h₁ + v₁²/2 = h₂ + v₂²/2 when the height change is negligible.
  • Adiabatic compressor or turbine with small velocity change: w_s = h₁ − h₂ per kg. This is positive for a turbine (work leaves) and negative for a compressor (work enters). The work put into a compressor is h₂ − h₁.
  • Heat exchanger with no moving parts: q = h₂ − h₁ per kg.

Nozzles and diffusers

A nozzle is a duct that speeds the fluid up; a diffuser slows it down. In a gas nozzle the flow speeds up in the converging part and, once the throat is sonic, also in a diverging part; the simple picture that a nozzle narrows holds for liquids and for slow gas flow. Neither device has moving parts, so w_s = 0. If the walls are insulated, q = 0. With height change negligible, the energy equation reduces to:

h₁ + v₁²/2 = h₂ + v₂²/2

h₁, h₂ are inlet and outlet specific enthalpies; v₁, v₂ are inlet and outlet velocities. The quantity h + v²/2 is called the stagnation enthalpy and stays constant.

Read it as a trade. Whatever kinetic energy the fluid gains comes out of its own enthalpy. In a nozzle the velocity rises and the enthalpy falls, so the fluid cools. In a diffuser the fluid slows and its enthalpy and temperature rise. This is why the air entering a jet engine is first slowed in an intake diffuser, and why steam in a turbine nozzle leaves the stage at high speed and low enthalpy.

The equation fixes only the sum, not the split. A measured inlet and exit enthalpy gives the exit velocity; a measured inlet and exit velocity gives the exit enthalpy.

Compressors and the shaft work

In a compressor the inlet and outlet pipes are large, so the velocities are small and nearly equal. The kinetic terms cancel, and height change is small. For an adiabatic compressor the work input per kilogram is h₂ − h₁, so the shaft power is:

P = ṁ (h₂ − h₁)

P is shaft power into the fluid (kW); ṁ is mass flow rate (kg/s); h₁ and h₂ are inlet and outlet specific enthalpies (kJ/kg). Since kg/s × kJ/kg = kW, no further conversion is needed.

For compression h₂ > h₁, so w_s = h₁ − h₂ is negative: work is done on the fluid. The input per kg is the positive quantity h₂ − h₁, which is what P measures. A turbine reverses this: h₂ < h₁, w_s is positive, and work leaves with the shaft. If the compressor is cooled, q is not zero and must be carried in the equation.

Both ends of the calculation need enthalpies. For a gas close to ideal, h₂ − h₁ = C_p (T₂ − T₁) using the heat capacity of 1.7. For steam or a real gas, read h from a property table; tables and equations of state are the subject of Unit 4.

Why compressible flow is different

In a gas nozzle the velocity often rises past 200 m/s, and pressure and temperature fall sharply along the way. Density then falls too, so the gas cannot be treated as incompressible. Mass conservation ṁ = ρ A v still holds, but ρ changes from point to point, so you cannot use a single density to relate area and velocity.

The energy equation itself does not care: h + v²/2 is still constant along an adiabatic nozzle. What changes is where the properties come from. A constant density would give a wrong velocity for a given area, and the enthalpy must come from a property table or an equation of state rather than from a single C_p value over a large temperature drop.

Near the speed of sound the area–velocity relation reverses: mass conservation, with ρ falling faster than v rises, means the accelerating part of the nozzle must widen. The second law decides which exit states are possible, so the ideal limit is an isentropic expansion. Isentropic efficiency, real compression cycles and refrigeration belong to Unit 3; density changes and property tables belong to Unit 2 and Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. Air enters an adiabatic nozzle at h₁ = 300 kJ/kg and v₁ = 50 m/s. It leaves at h₂ = 280 kJ/kg. Neglect potential energy change and shaft work. Find the exit velocity v₂ in m/s.

  1. Write the steady-flow energy equation for the nozzle: h₁ + v₁²/2 = h₂ + v₂²/2. Here q = 0 is adiabatic, w_s = 0 because a nozzle has no shaft, and g z₁ = g z₂ is dropped.
  2. Rearrange for the unknown term: v₂²/2 = (h₁ − h₂) + v₁²/2.
  3. Enthalpy drop: h₁ − h₂ = 300 − 280 = 20 kJ/kg. Convert to J/kg: 20 × 1000 = 20 000 J/kg, which is 20 000 m²/s².
  4. Inlet kinetic term: v₁²/2 = 50²/2 = 2500/2 = 1250 m²/s² = 1.25 kJ/kg.
  5. Add: v₂²/2 = 20 000 + 1250 = 21 250 m²/s².
  6. Multiply by 2: v₂² = 42 500 m²/s².
  7. Take the square root: v₂ = 206.16 m/s, about 206 m/s.

Answer. The exit velocity is v₂ = 206.2 m/s (about 206 m/s).

Exam traps

  • Adding v²/2 in m²/s² straight to h in kJ/kg. Divide the kinetic term by 1000 first; 1 kJ/kg = 1000 m²/s².
  • Adding shaft work to a nozzle or diffuser. They have no shaft, so w_s = 0 always, whatever the fluid and however fast it flows.
  • Reversing the compressor sign. In the equation w_s is work done by the fluid, so for compression h₂ > h₁ and w_s = h₁ − h₂ is negative; the work input per kg is h₂ − h₁. For a turbine h₂ < h₁, w_s is positive and work leaves.
  • Assuming that falling enthalpy always means rising velocity. That trade holds only when q = 0 and w_s = 0; with heat loss or shaft work the balance shifts.
  • Using a single constant density for a gas nozzle. Density and velocity change together, so area cannot be found from one ρ value.

Takeaways

  1. A thermodynamic system is defined only after you draw the boundary; every balance is written for what crosses that boundary.
  2. Intensive properties locate the state; extensive properties scale with amount and become specific or molar properties after dividing by mass or moles.
  3. State functions depend only on where you start and end; work and heat depend on the road taken between them.
  4. Energy is stored as internal, kinetic or potential energy, or is in transit as heat or work; only transit terms cross the boundary.
  5. The zeroth law is what makes temperature measurable, and the ideal-gas scale lets any gas thermometer agree with the rest.
  6. ΔU = Q − W for a closed system is the whole first law; Joule's paddle-wheel experiment is what tells us U is a state function.
  7. Enthalpy is not a new kind of energy, only U + PV, and it enters open-system balances because the fluid carries flow work in and out.
  8. Cv and Cp convert temperature changes into internal-energy and enthalpy changes; for an ideal gas Cp − Cv = R.
  9. In a nozzle the energy of the flow is still conserved, but enthalpy converts into kinetic energy, so pressure and temperature both fall.

An extensive property divided by the number of moles, in units per mole.
Nozzle
A duct of decreasing area in which pressure falls and velocity rises, with enthalpy converting to kinetic energy.
Open system
A system in which mass crosses the boundary, also called a control volume.
Path function
A quantity whose value between two states depends on the route, such as heat and work.
Reversible process
An idealised process through continuous equilibrium states that can be reversed exactly by an infinitesimal change in the surroundings.
Specific property
An extensive property divided by mass, in units per kilogram.
State function
A property fixed by the state of the system, so its change depends only on the end states.
Steady flow
A flow in which properties at any point do not change with time, so mass and energy within the control volume stay constant.
Zeroth law
If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other, which justifies temperature as a property.
