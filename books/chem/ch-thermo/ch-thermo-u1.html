1.1 Systems, variables, intensive and extensive properties

Every thermodynamics problem starts with a decision: what is inside the boundary and what is outside. The stuff inside is the system; the rest is the surroundings. Once the boundary is drawn, we describe the system by its properties — temperature, pressure, volume, mass. Properties are also called state variables, and they split into two classes: extensive properties grow with the size of the system, intensive properties do not. The split decides what you may add and what you may look up in a table.

You need: nothing beyond school physics and chemistry — mass, volume, temperature and pressure as measured quantities. The next chapter, 1.2, uses these terms for state and path functions.

System, surroundings and boundary

Thermodynamics begins with a choice. You draw a real or imaginary surface and declare that everything inside is the system and everything outside is the surroundings. That surface is the boundary. It can be the inner wall of a reactor, the skin of a potato in an oven, or a bubble of steam in a pipe. It can be fixed or moving.

The boundary decides the type of system. A closed system (some syllabi call it a control mass) has no mass crossing the boundary, though heat and work may cross. A sealed cylinder of gas cooled in a fridge is closed. An open system (a control volume) has mass crossing the boundary: a pump, a turbine, a length of pipe, a cup of chai while you sip it. An isolated system has neither mass nor energy crossing. A perfectly insulated, rigid, sealed flask is the ideal case; no real flask is perfect.

  1. Sketch the equipment and mark the boundary with a dashed line.
  2. Ask: does mass cross the boundary? If yes, the system is open.
  3. If no mass crosses, ask: does heat or work cross? If yes, the system is closed.
  4. If neither mass nor energy crosses, the system is isolated — an ideal limit.

Phases, components, states and processes

A phase is a region with uniform chemical composition and uniform physical properties. Ice and liquid water in a glass are two phases. Air is one phase even though it is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen and argon, because it is mixed at the molecular level.

A component is a chemically independent species. The number of components is the smallest number of species needed to describe the composition of every phase present. Pure water is one component. Water plus ethanol is two. Salt dissolved in water is still two components (salt and water), even though the salt splits into ions.

  • A homogeneous system has one phase throughout: superheated steam, brine, dry air.
  • A heterogeneous system has two or more phases with boundaries between them: wet steam in a boiler drum, oil floating on water.
  • One phase may contain many components; many phases may share one component.

The state of a system is the set of values of all its properties. When nothing changes with time and nothing would change if the system were cut off from the surroundings, the system is in equilibrium. A process is any change from one state to another; it is named by what stays fixed: isothermal (T), isobaric (P), isochoric (V) or adiabatic (no heat). A cycle is a series of processes that returns the system to its first state. Two kinds of quantity appear in every problem: variables of state such as P, V, T and U, which belong to one state, and quantities exchanged during a process, heat Q and work W, which do not (see point and path functions).

Intensive and extensive properties

Any measurable feature of the system is a property (a state variable). The test for the two classes is a thought experiment: cut the system into two equal halves. An extensive property halves with the system. Mass m, volume V, amount of substance n, internal energy U, enthalpy H and entropy S are extensive. Two identical vessels joined together hold twice the volume and twice the mass of one. An intensive property does not change with size. Temperature T, pressure P, density ρ and molar volume do not halve; each half of a gas at 300 K is still at 300 K.

A ratio of two extensive properties is intensive. Density divides mass by volume, so the size cancels. Specific volume is volume per unit mass. Molar volume is volume per mole. Specific properties are per kilogram (lower-case symbols v, u, h, s). Molar properties are per mole. Both are intensive, which is why a steam table can list them without knowing the mass of the plant.

ρ = m/V,   v = V/m = 1/ρ,   Vm = V/n

ρ is density in kg/m³; m is mass in kg; V is volume in m³; v is specific volume in m³/kg; Vm is molar volume in m³/mol; n is amount of substance in mol.

The rule that catches students: extensive properties add, intensive properties never do. Join two 1 m³ tanks of water at 300 K and you get 2 m³ at 300 K, not 2 m³ at 600 K. Write m = m₁ + m₂ for masses, but never T = T₁ + T₂ for temperatures.

Worked example

Question. A closed rigid vessel holds 2.00 kg of gas at 300 K and 150 kPa. Its volume is 1.20 m³. Classify m, V, T, P, density and specific volume as intensive or extensive, and compute the density and the specific volume. A thin wall is then slid in that cuts the vessel into two equal halves: give all six values for one half.

  1. Data: m = 2.00 kg, V = 1.20 m³, T = 300 K, P = 150 kPa. The vessel is closed and rigid, so m and V are fixed.
  2. Classify: m and V grow when you take more gas, so both are extensive. T and P are the same everywhere inside and do not depend on how much gas is present, so both are intensive.
  3. Density: ρ = m/V = 2.00 kg ÷ 1.20 m³ = 1.67 kg/m³.
  4. Specific volume: v = V/m = 1.20 m³ ÷ 2.00 kg = 0.600 m³/kg. Check: 1/ρ = 1/1.667 = 0.600 m³/kg.
  5. One half: m = 1.00 kg and V = 0.600 m³ (both halved), while T = 300 K, P = 150 kPa, ρ = 1.00/0.600 = 1.67 kg/m³ and v = 0.600 m³/kg are unchanged. The halving test confirms the classes.

Exam traps

  • Density ρ = m/V and specific volume v = V/m are intensive even though they are built from the extensive m and V. A ratio of two extensives is intensive; do not label the ratio extensive because a big letter appears in the formula.
  • Intensive properties are not additive, and a mixture temperature is not a plain average either. Mixing 1 kg at 300 K with 3 kg at 400 K of the same gas gives 375 K, not 350 K; only equal masses of the same gas with constant heat capacity give the midpoint.
  • Closed means no mass crosses, not no energy. A closed vessel can still be heated or stirred. An insulated vessel is not automatically isolated unless no work crosses either.
  • The system is not the equipment. In a cylinder, the gas may be the system and the metal wall part of the surroundings; choose and state the boundary before naming anything, because the same gas can be closed in one problem and open in another.

1.2 Point and path functions; reversible and irreversible processes

A state function depends only on where the system is, not on how it got there. Pressure, volume and internal energy are state functions. Work and heat are path functions: their values depend on the route between the same two states. Engineers use this split to compute work in pistons, compressors and turbines, and to judge how close a process comes to the reversible ideal. Q and W both change with the path, yet the first law says their difference Q − W is the same for every path.

You need 1.1 for system, boundary and properties.

State functions, path functions and differentials

A state function has a fixed value once the state is fixed by properties such as P, V and T. Its value does not depend on history. Some syllabi call it a point function because it belongs to one point on a property diagram. Pressure P, volume V, temperature T and internal energy U are state functions. The change ΔU = U₂ − U₁ depends only on the two end states.

A path function depends on the route between the states. Work W and heat Q are path functions. They are energy in transit across the boundary, not stored properties; see 1.3. A small amount of work is written δW and a small amount of heat as δQ. The symbol δ is used because there is no function W(P,V) or Q(P,V) whose difference gives δW or δQ. Write W for total work, not ΔW.

∮ dX = 0

X is any state function; ∮ means integration around a closed path that returns to the same state; dX is an exact differential.

dX = M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy is exact when ∂M/∂y = ∂N/∂x

M and N are functions of x and y; ∂M/∂y is the partial derivative of M with respect to y; ∂M/∂y is taken with x held fixed and ∂N/∂x with y held fixed. Some syllabi call this the exactness (Euler) test.

Try the test on an ideal gas, with T and V as the two variables. Internal energy gives dU = Cv dT + 0·dV: here ∂Cv/∂V = 0 and ∂(0)/∂T = 0, so dU is exact. Reversible work gives δW = P dV = 0·dT + (RT/V) dV: now ∂(0)/∂V = 0 but ∂(RT/V)/∂T = R/V, which is not zero, so δW is inexact. The test proves what the next section shows with areas.

  • Exact differential: value depends only on endpoints; the quantity is a state function.
  • Inexact differential: value depends on the path; the quantity is a path function such as W or Q.
  • For a state function, the cyclic integral is zero. For work, ∮ δW is not zero in general.

Reversible processes

A reversible process is an ideal limit. The system moves through a quasi-static path: it stays infinitesimally close to equilibrium, so P and T are nearly uniform inside it. There is no friction and no finite driving force. The external pressure differs from the system pressure by only dP, and the external temperature differs from the system temperature by only dT. An infinitesimal change in the driving force can reverse the direction.

Sign rule for the whole unit: W is work done by the system and Q is heat added to it, so the first law reads ΔU = Q − W (set out in work and heat). For a reversible expansion, the external pressure equals the system pressure at every step. The work done by the gas is the area under the P–V curve: W = ∫ P dV. This path can be drawn on a P–V diagram because P is defined at each step. Reversible paths are useful limits: they give the maximum work in expansion and the minimum work in compression.

δW = Pext dV

δW is small work done by the system in J; Pext is the external pressure in Pa or kPa; dV is the small volume change in m³ or L. For a reversible path, Pext = P, the system pressure.

Real processes are irreversible to some degree. Friction, stirring and finite temperature differences add dissipation. A reversible process is not a claim that real equipment is reversible; it is a benchmark for calculations and for the second-law limits studied later.

Irreversible processes and the same endpoints

An irreversible process cannot be reversed by an infinitesimal change. It has finite driving forces, gradients or friction, and reversing it leaves a net change in the surroundings. Common examples are sudden expansion of a gas, paddle-wheel stirring of a liquid, and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference.

In a sudden expansion, the gas pushes a piston against a finite external pressure. The work is W = Pext(V₂ − V₁), not ∫ P dV, because the gas pressure inside may not be uniform and is not equal to Pext. In free expansion into vacuum, Pext = 0, so the work is zero. Stirring puts work into the liquid and raises its temperature; the liquid cannot spontaneously turn that heat back into shaft work.

The same two end states can give different work along different paths. On a P–V plot, work done by the gas is the area under the path. Two routes between the same endpoints have different areas, so W differs. This is the clearest proof that work is a path function, not a state function.

W = ∫ P dV (reversible path)

W is work done by the gas in J; P is absolute pressure in Pa or kPa; V is volume in m³ or L. Use consistent units; 1 kPa·L = 1 J.

Worked example

Question. A gas changes from A (P = 3 kPa, V = 1 L) to B (P = 1 kPa, V = 3 L) by two slow (quasi-static) paths, each made of two straight segments on the P–V plot. Path A passes through (2 L, 1.5 kPa); path B passes through (2 L, 2 kPa). Because both paths are quasi-static, the gas pressure is defined at every step and the work equals the area under each path. Compute the work done by the gas along each path. Use 1 kPa·L = 1 J.

  1. For a straight segment on a P–V plot, area = average pressure × volume change = 0.5(P₁ + P₂)(V₂ − V₁).
  2. Path A, first segment: 0.5(3 + 1.5)(2 − 1) = 0.5 × 4.5 × 1 = 2.25 J.
  3. Path A, second segment: 0.5(1.5 + 1)(3 − 2) = 0.5 × 2.5 × 1 = 1.25 J.
  4. Path A total: WA = 2.25 + 1.25 = 3.50 J.
  5. Path B, first segment: 0.5(3 + 2)(2 − 1) = 0.5 × 5 × 1 = 2.50 J.
  6. Path B, second segment: 0.5(2 + 1)(3 − 2) = 0.5 × 3 × 1 = 1.50 J.
  7. Path B total: WB = 2.50 + 1.50 = 4.00 J.
  8. The endpoints are the same, but WA ≠ WB. Work is a path function.

Answer. Work along path A is 3.5 J and along path B is 4.0 J; the same endpoints give different work, so work is a path function.

Exam traps

  • Treating work as a state function because a reversible path lets you calculate it from P and V. Reversible only means P is defined along the path; it does not make work path-independent.
  • Thinking all reversible paths between two states give the same work. Different reversible P–V routes have different areas under them.
  • Writing dW or ΔW for a small or total amount of work. Use δW for a small path-dependent amount and W for total work.
  • Using W = ∫ P dV for an irreversible sudden expansion. Use W = Pext(V₂ − V₁) when Pext is fixed and finite, and remember Pext may not equal the gas pressure.
  • Mixing up exact and inexact differentials: ∮ dX = 0 for a state function X, but ∮ δW is generally not zero.

1.3 Energy classifications; work and heat

Energy in a closed system is either stored inside it or crossing its boundary. Stored energy is internal, kinetic and potential; crossing energy is work or heat. The first law balances only what crosses against what the system keeps. A phone charger, a turbine shaft and a cup of chai ask the same question: how much crossed, which way, and what stayed. This chapter fixes the energy classes, the modes of work and heat, and the sign rule used in the whole unit.

You need 1.1 for system and boundary, and 1.2 for state versus path functions, because work and heat are path functions.

Stored energy: total energy E = U + KE + PE

A closed system stores energy in three forms. Total energy E is their sum in joules (J). Internal energy U is microscopic: molecular translation, rotation, vibration and intermolecular pull. Kinetic energy KE and potential energy PE are macroscopic: whole mass moving or at a height.

E = U + ½mc² + mgz

E is total energy in J; U is internal energy in J; m is mass in kg; c is the speed of the system as a whole in m/s (this unit writes speed as c so that V always means volume); g = 9.81 m/s²; z is height in m above a chosen datum.

U has no measurable zero. Tables set U = 0 at a reference state and report changes, so the first law uses ΔU, never U alone (see 1.5). For z, pick a datum and stay with it; only changes matter.

In a rigid closed tank with no shaft, ΔKE and ΔPE are zero, so ΔE = ΔU. In a nozzle or falling stream they are not, and dropping them gives a wrong answer (see 1.8). E is extensive; specific energy e = E/m in J/kg is intensive (see 1.1).

Work: energy crossing as force times distance

Work crosses a boundary when a force moves its point of application: W = ∫F dx, force in newtons, distance in metres, work in joules. Work is energy in transit, not stored. It is a path function, so the route matters (see 1.2). Four modes cover most problems.

  • Boundary (P-V) work: W = ∫P dV for a slow (quasi-equilibrium) expansion, where the gas pressure equals the pressure on the piston; for a fast expansion use the external pressure, W = ∫Pext dV. At constant pressure W = P(V₂ − V₁), P in Pa, V in m³.
  • Shaft power: 2πNτ, N in revolutions per second, torque τ in N·m, giving watts.
  • Electrical power: voltage × current, in volts, I in amperes, giving watts; energy is ∫VI dt.
  • Surface work: ∫γ dA, surface tension γ in N/m, area A in m². Small but real in droplets.

Sign rule: work done by the system on the surroundings is positive. An expanding gas pushes the piston out and does positive work. Work done on the system, by a stirrer or compressed spring, is negative.

1 J = 1 N·m; 1 kJ = 1000 J. Power is rate: 1 W = 1 J/s, so 1 kW = 1 kJ/s. A 5 kW motor driving a pump for 10 min (600 s) supplies 5 × 600 = 3000 kJ of shaft work to the pump shaft.

Heat: energy crossing because of a temperature difference

Heat is energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference. It flows on its own from hot to cold and stops when temperatures match. Like work, it is a path function and is never stored: a body contains internal energy, not heat.

  • Conduction: Q̇ = kA ΔT / L. k is thermal conductivity in W/(m·K), A area in m², L thickness in m, and ΔT = Thot − Tcold across the wall in K, so Q̇ is the rate from the hot side to the cold side. A steel spoon left in hot chai.
  • Convection: Q̇ = hA ΔT, h the convection coefficient in W/(m²·K). Air blowing over a hot radiator plate.
  • Radiation: Q̇ = εσA(Ts⁴ − Tsurr⁴), emissivity ε from 0 to 1, σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). Temperatures in kelvin.

The dot means rate in W. Total heat is Q = ∫Q̇ dt. A constant-power heater P for t seconds delivers Q = Pt.

For a plain temperature change of a liquid or solid, Q = m cp ΔT, with cp the specific heat in J/(kg·K) (lower-case c for per kilogram; capital C for the heat capacity of a whole body in J/K) (see 1.7). Two litres of water (m ≈ 2 kg, cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K)) heated by 25 K takes Q ≈ 2 × 4.18 × 25 ≈ 209 kJ.

One sign convention for the whole book

Every balance here uses one convention. Heat into the system is positive. Work done by the system is positive.

ΔE = Q − W   and, with KE and PE negligible,   ΔU = Q − W

Q is net heat entering the system in J; W is net work done by the system in J; ΔU = U₂ − U₁ is the change in internal energy in J.

Read wording carefully: 'receives 50 kJ of heat' gives Q = +50 kJ; 'rejects 15 kJ' gives Q = −15 kJ; 'does 20 kJ of work' gives W = +20 kJ; '10 kJ of work is done on it' gives W = −10 kJ.

This is the whole first law for a closed system; 1.5 builds on it. Open systems add flow work and use enthalpy instead of internal energy (see 1.6).

Worked example

Question. A closed system receives 50 kJ of heat and does 20 kJ of work. Find ΔU. In a second step it loses 15 kJ of heat while 10 kJ of work is done on it. Find ΔU for the second step and the net ΔU over both steps.

  1. Step 1 signs: Q₁ = +50 kJ, W₁ = +20 kJ.
  2. ΔU₁ = Q₁ − W₁ = 50 − 20 = +30 kJ.
  3. Step 2 signs: Q₂ = −15 kJ, W₂ = −10 kJ.
  4. ΔU₂ = (−15) − (−10) = −5 kJ.
  5. Net: ΔU = 30 + (−5) = +25 kJ. Check: net Q = 35 kJ, net W = 10 kJ, 35 − 10 = 25 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Mixing conventions: using ΔU = Q + W while calling work positive out. This book uses ΔU = Q − W.
  • Treating heat as stored. Chai has internal energy, not 'heat content'. Heat exists only while crossing a boundary.
  • Missing work-on-system sign: 'work is done on it' means W is negative, so −W raises ΔU.
  • Dropping KE or PE because they were zero in an earlier tank. In a fast stream or falling mass, ½mV² and mgz are real.
  • Confusing power and energy. A 2 kW heater for 30 s supplies 60 kJ, not 2 kJ; multiply by seconds.

1.4 Zeroth law and temperature scales

The zeroth law makes temperature measurable. If body A has no net heat exchange with body B, and B with C, then A and C also have no net heat exchange. This transitive property lets us build a thermometer: put it in contact with a body, wait for balance, and read a number. That number is temperature. Industry uses it to check steam headers, chilled-water lines and reactor jackets. The definitions needed are thermal walls, empirical temperature, fixed points and the four common scales.

You need: 1.1 for system, boundary and intensive properties; 1.3 for heat as energy crossing a boundary.

Thermal contact and the law

Two bodies are in thermal contact when energy can cross the wall between them as heat. A wall that blocks heat is adiabatic; a wall that lets heat pass is diathermal. Put A and B in contact through a diathermal wall. After some time the net heat flow stops. They are then in thermal equilibrium. The zeroth law states: if A is in thermal equilibrium with B, and B with C, then A is in thermal equilibrium with C. This transitivity is what makes a thermometer work: thermometer B reads body A, then reads body C; if the reading is unchanged, A and C have the same temperature. Some syllabi call this the law of thermal equilibrium.

  • Adiabatic wall: no heat transfer. A system inside one is thermally insulated; it is isolated only if no work and no mass cross either.
  • Diathermal wall: heat transfer allowed until equilibrium.
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net heat flow, and all bodies would show the same temperature on a common thermometer.

Empirical temperature and scales

An empirical temperature is a number assigned by a chosen thermometer. Pick a property X that changes with hotness, for example the length of a mercury column or the resistance of platinum. Two fixed points fix a linear scale.

θ = aX + b

θ is the empirical temperature; X is the thermometric property, such as mercury length or platinum resistance; a and b are constants fixed by two fixed points.

Older scales use the ice point (0 °C, 32 °F) and steam point (100 °C, 212 °F) at 1 atm. The Kelvin and Rankine scales start at absolute zero, the lowest temperature. The size of a kelvin equals a Celsius degree; the size of a Rankine degree equals a Fahrenheit degree.

The constant-volume gas thermometer and the ideal-gas scale

Liquid-in-glass and resistance thermometers each give a slightly different number between their fixed points, because mercury, alcohol and platinum do not change in exactly the same way. A gas thermometer removes that choice. A fixed volume of gas sits in a bulb; a mercury column or gauge reads its pressure P. The bulb is first held at the triple point of water, 273.16 K, where the gas pressure is Ptp. Then T = 273.16 K × P/Ptp.

T = 273.16 K × lim (P/Ptp) as Ptp → 0

T is the ideal-gas temperature in K; P is the gas pressure at the unknown temperature; Ptp is the gas pressure at the triple point of water, both at the same fixed volume.

Different gases (hydrogen, nitrogen, helium) give slightly different readings when the bulb is well filled. Repeat with less and less gas in the bulb, so that Ptp falls, and the readings of every gas converge on one value. In that limit every gas behaves as an ideal gas, so the result does not depend on which gas was used. That limit defines the ideal-gas temperature scale, and it matches the thermodynamic kelvin scale. One fixed point is enough because the scale is proportional, with zero at absolute zero.

Reading a thermometer

A thermometer must reach thermal equilibrium before you read it. Wait for the reading to settle and check that two readings a minute apart agree. A Celsius weather app, a Kelvin steam table and a Fahrenheit datasheet all carry the same physical temperature; only the numbers differ. Convert to one scale before you compare or calculate. Absolute zero is 0 K = −273.15 °C = −459.67 °F = 0 R. No body reaches it. For temperature differences, use ΔT(K) = Δt(°C) and Δt(°F) = 1.8Δt(°C).

  1. Write the given value and its scale, for example 27 °C.
  2. For kelvin, add 273.15 to the Celsius value.
  3. For Fahrenheit, multiply the Celsius value by 1.8 and add 32.
  4. For Rankine, add 459.67 to the Fahrenheit value, or multiply the kelvin value by 1.8.
  5. Check the interval: a 10 °C rise is a 10 K rise and an 18 °F rise.

1.5 First law for closed systems; Joule's experiment

A closed system keeps a fixed mass, so energy can cross its boundary only as heat or as work. The first law turns that into one line: the rise in internal energy equals the heat added minus the work done. Heat and work are path functions, yet their difference depends only on the two end states. Joule's paddle-wheel experiment showed that falling masses and heat are the same currency. Every batch calorimeter and stirred-tank energy balance uses this form.

You need 1.3 for the work and heat sign convention, and 1.2 for state versus path functions.

The first law for a closed system

A closed system holds a fixed mass. No matter crosses its boundary; only heat and work do. The first law is the energy balance written for that fixed mass, and for a non-flow process it reads as follows.

ΔU = Q − W

ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system (kJ); Q is the heat added to the system across the boundary (kJ); W is the work done by the system on the surroundings (kJ).

The signs come from the convention in 1.3: Q is positive when heat flows in, W is positive when the system does work outside. Some books write ΔU = Q + W with W done on the system; then every W flips sign.

Internal energy U is a state function, so ΔU is the same for every route between two states. Q and W are path functions; only their difference is fixed.

When the system as a whole moves or changes height, add those terms: ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE = Q − W. In most process equipment the last two are negligible and the short form is enough.

∮δQ = ∮δW

the closed loop means a sum over a complete cycle; δQ is the small heat added in one step (kJ) and δW the small work done in that step (kJ).

For a cycle the system returns to its starting state, so ΔU = 0 and the net heat equals the net work. Every power and refrigeration cycle studied with the second law starts from this cyclic form. Some syllabi call the closed-system equation the non-flow energy equation.

Joule's paddle-wheel experiment

In the 1840s James Joule wanted to prove that mechanical work and heat are the same thing measured in different units. He hung a known mass on a string wound round a spindle. As the mass fell, it turned a paddle wheel inside an insulated vessel of water.

The insulation gives Q = 0. The falling mass loses potential energy mgh and delivers it to the water as stirring work. The water does no work outward, so under the convention above W = −mgh. The first law then gives ΔU = 0 − (−mgh) = +mgh, and the water warms.

ΔT = m g h / (1000 mw c)

m is the falling mass (kg); g = 9.81 m/s²; h is the fall height (m); mw is the mass of water (kg); c is its specific heat capacity, about 4.18 kJ/(kg·K) for water; ΔT is the temperature rise (K). The product mgh is in joules, and the factor 1000 converts it to kilojoules so it matches c in kJ/(kg·K).

Repeating with different masses and heights, Joule got the same temperature rise per unit of work. That is the mechanical equivalent of heat: roughly 4.18 kJ of work raises 1 kg of water by 1 K, the same effect as 1 kcal. Heat and work are both energy in transit, and the joule measures both.

Joule expansion: the ideal-gas result U = U(T)

Joule ran a second, different experiment. A rigid insulated container is split in two: one side holds gas under pressure, the other is vacuum. A valve is opened and the gas fills the whole vessel.

The vessel is rigid, so there is no boundary work; there is no shaft or electrical work either. Insulation gives Q = 0. So W = 0 and Q = 0, and the first law leaves ΔU = 0. This is free expansion, and it is not the paddle-wheel test.

Joule saw no measurable temperature change in air. If ΔU = 0 and ΔT = 0 for any drop in pressure, then U of that gas depends on temperature alone: U = U(T). That statement defines the ideal gas. Real gases do show a small change, and this experiment is too insensitive to measure it well; throttling tests do better (the Joule–Thomson coefficient, met again in thermodynamic property relations). The slopes of U(T) and the heat capacities wait for 1.7.

1.6 First law for open systems and steady-flow processes

A pump, turbine or pipe rarely holds a fixed mass: fluid crosses its boundary all the time. For an open system at steady state, mass in equals mass out, and the energy carried in by the streams plus the heat added equals the energy carried out plus the shaft work delivered. The bookkeeping must include the work that pushes each stream across the boundary, and that push work turns internal energy into enthalpy. The result is the steady-flow energy equation, used for turbines, compressors, nozzles and every control volume in a process plant.

You need: 1.3 for work and heat crossing a boundary, and 1.5 for ΔU = Q − W in a closed system.

Control volume and steady flow

A control volume is a chosen region of space. Its boundary may be a real casing or an imaginary surface across a pipe. Fluid crosses the boundary, so the control volume is an open system. In steady flow, properties at each fixed point inside and on the boundary do not change with time, so the mass inside stays constant even though fluid keeps moving through, and mass entering equals mass leaving. For one inlet 1 and one outlet 2, ṁ₁ = ṁ₂ = ṁ. Steady flow is not the same as no flow: a turbine at constant load is steady, while its start-up is not.

Σṁin = Σṁout

ṁ is mass flow rate in kg/s; in and out mark streams entering and leaving the control volume.

Where enthalpy comes from

A stream carries energy in three forms per kilogram: internal energy u, kinetic energy c²/2 and potential energy gz, where c is the stream speed in m/s (this unit writes speed as c so that v stays specific volume). It also carries flow work. To push one kilogram into the control volume, the fluid behind it does work Pv, with P and v the pressure and specific volume of that stream where it enters. At the outlet the control volume does the work P₂v₂ to push its kilogram out. Each stream's flow work joins that same stream's internal energy, so every stream carries its own enthalpy h = u + Pv, worked out at its own pressure and specific volume.

h = u + Pv

h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg; u is specific internal energy in kJ/kg; P is pressure in kPa; v is specific volume in m³/kg, so Pv is in kJ/kg. Pv is flow work, not shaft work.

With Q positive into the control volume and Ws (shaft power) positive out of it, the steady-flow energy equation for any number of streams is:

Q − Ws = Σṁout(h + c²/2 + gz) − Σṁin(h + c²/2 + gz)

Q is the heat rate into the control volume in kW; Ws is the shaft power delivered by it in kW; ṁ is mass flow rate in kg/s; c is speed in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s²; z is elevation in m. Each bracket is energy per kg; multiply by ṁ in kg/s to get kW, after converting c²/2 with 1 kJ/kg = 1000 m²/s². Some syllabi call this the control-volume energy balance.

One stream, per kilogram

For one inlet and one outlet with the same ṁ, divide by ṁ. Here q = Q/ṁ and ws = Ws/ṁ are heat in and shaft work out per kilogram, and subscript 1 is the inlet, 2 the outlet. The equation says: heat in minus shaft work out equals the rise in enthalpy, kinetic energy and potential energy of each kilogram between inlet and outlet.

q − ws = (h₂ − h₁) + (c₂² − c₁²)/2 + g(z₂ − z₁)

q and ws are in kJ/kg; h in kJ/kg; c in m/s (divide the c² term by 1000 for kJ/kg); z in m.

Neglect Δke or Δpe only after you estimate them. A speed change from 60 to 120 m/s gives Δke = 5.4 kJ/kg, small beside a steam enthalpy drop of hundreds of kJ/kg; in a nozzle, however, Δke is the main output. In a turbine h falls and Ws leaves; in a compressor Ws enters (negative) and h rises. A heat loss makes Q negative. The first law does not say Q = Ws: it says Q − Ws equals the energy carried out by the flow minus the energy carried in. Nozzles, compressors and throttling are worked out in 1.8.

Filling and emptying: unsteady flow

When a tank fills or empties, the mass and energy inside change with time, so the storage term stays in. With kinetic and potential energy neglected, the mass and energy balances of the control volume are:

dmcv/dt = Σṁin − Σṁout;   d(mu)cv/dt = Q − W + Σṁin hin − Σṁout hout

mcv and u are the mass and specific internal energy inside the control volume; the streams still carry enthalpy h; Q and W are rates in kW.

Fill an evacuated, insulated, rigid tank from a supply line whose state stays fixed: Q = 0, W = 0, nothing leaves, so integrating gives m₂u₂ = m₂hline, that is u₂ = hline. For an ideal gas with constant heat capacities, cv T₂ = cp Tline, so T₂ = γTline: the gas in the tank ends hotter than the line, because the flow work of every kilogram pushed in is stored as internal energy.

1.7 Internal energy, enthalpy and heat capacities

A gas heated in a sealed rigid vessel stores energy; the same gas heated under a moving piston stores energy and also pushes the piston. The stored part is internal energy U. Adding the push work PV gives enthalpy H = U + PV. Both are state functions, fixed once the state is fixed. Their slopes against temperature, at constant volume and at constant pressure, are the heat capacities Cv and Cp. These two numbers give the heat in most closed-system heating steps and the work in most flow devices.

You need: the first law for closed systems (1.5), flow work in the steady-flow balance (1.6), and the state-function idea (1.2).

Internal energy U and enthalpy H

Internal energy U is the energy stored inside the boundary: the kinetic and potential energy of the molecules themselves, with the system at rest as a whole. U is a state function (1.2) and it is extensive (1.1), so doubling the gas doubles U. In this chapter small letters u, h and v are molar values, per mole, in J/mol and m³/mol; the same letters per kilogram (specific values, J/kg) appear in the flow chapters, and the unit tells you which is meant.

Enthalpy is defined as H = U + PV, where P is pressure in Pa and V is volume in m³. Since 1 Pa = 1 N/m², the product PV carries units of N·m, that is J, and adds cleanly to U. The molar form is h = H/n = u + Pv, with v the molar volume in m³/mol. Because U, P and V are all state variables, H is also a state function, and it is extensive like U.

Why add PV? In an open system the entering fluid must be pushed in and the leaving fluid pushes out; that push work is PV per mole (1.6). Grouping it with U gives one property, h, that appears naturally in nozzles, throttling valves, compressors and heat exchangers. Steam tables list h rather than u for exactly this reason.

Heat capacities Cv and Cp

Heat capacity is the heat needed to raise a unit amount by one kelvin. Which heat it is depends on what is held fixed. At constant volume the slope of u against T is Cv = (∂u/∂T)_v, in J/(mol·K). The subscript v means volume is held constant while T changes. At constant pressure the slope of h against T is Cp = (∂h/∂T)_p. The specific forms use small letters, cv and cp, in J/(kg·K).

Two slopes exist because the paths differ. Heat a gas in a rigid sealed vessel: no expansion work happens, so all the heat lands in internal energy and Q = ΔU. This rule, Qv = ΔU, holds for a closed system with only P-V work. Heat the same gas under a freely moving piston and the gas also pushes the piston, doing PΔV work.

Qp = ΔU + PΔV = Δ(U + PV) = ΔH

Qp is the heat added at constant pressure, in J; ΔU is the internal energy change, in J; P is the constant pressure, in Pa; ΔV is the volume change, in m³; ΔH is the enthalpy change, in J.

So for the same kind of process, Qp = ΔH. Read the two rules together: Qv = ΔU measures the change at constant volume, Qp = ΔH measures it at constant pressure. Some syllabi call Cv and Cp specific heats; the name is loose but common.

Ideal gas: u and h depend only on T

Joule's experiment (1.5) found no measurable temperature change when air at low pressure expanded into vacuum with no work and no heat. A real gas does change a little; for an ideal gas the change is exactly zero, so u depends on T alone, not on P or V. Then h = u + Pv = u + RT is also a function of T alone, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant.

Differentiate h = u + RT with respect to T at constant pressure: the R term contributes R, so Cp = Cv + R.

Cp − Cv = R and γ = Cp/Cv

Cp is the molar heat capacity at constant pressure, in J/(mol·K); Cv is the molar heat capacity at constant volume, in J/(mol·K); R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); γ (gamma) is the heat capacity ratio, dimensionless.

Worked example

Question. 2.0 mol of an ideal gas with Cv = 20.8 J/(mol·K) is heated in a rigid closed vessel from 300 K to 400 K. Find ΔU, Q and W. The same gas is then heated from 300 K to 400 K under a constant pressure piston. Find ΔH, Q and W. Take R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).

  1. Temperature change: ΔT = 400 − 300 = 100 K.
  2. Constant volume, internal energy: ΔU = n Cv ΔT = 2.0 mol × 20.8 J/(mol·K) × 100 K = 4160 J.
  3. Rigid vessel means no volume change, so W = 0 J. First law ΔU = Q − W gives Q = ΔU = 4160 J, added as heat.
  4. Constant-pressure capacity: Cp = Cv + R = 20.8 + 8.314 = 29.114 J/(mol·K).
  5. Enthalpy change: ΔH = n Cp ΔT = 2.0 mol × 29.114 J/(mol·K) × 100 K = 5822.8 J.
  6. Cross-check: ΔH = ΔU + nRΔT = 4160 J + 2.0 × 8.314 × 100 J = 4160 + 1662.8 = 5822.8 J. Same answer.
  7. Constant pressure gives Q = ΔH = 5822.8 J. The gas does work W = PΔV = nRΔT = 1662.8 J, which is the difference between 5822.8 J and 4160 J.

Answer. At constant volume: ΔU = 4160 J, Q = 4160 J, W = 0 J. At constant pressure: ΔH = 5822.8 J, Q = 5822.8 J, W = 1662.8 J.

Exam traps

  • Using Cp for a constant-volume step. In a rigid vessel only Cv applies; at constant pressure only Cp applies.
  • Forgetting the moles. Cv in J/(mol·K) must be multiplied by n; if the value is in J/(kg·K), multiply by mass instead. The unit tells you which.
  • Treating h as heat. h is a state property in J/mol; Q is path-dependent in J. They are numerically equal only for a closed system at constant P with only P-V work.
  • Writing Cp − Cv = R after converting to specific heats. In J/(kg·K) the difference is R/M, with M the molar mass in kg/mol.
  • Applying Q = ΔU to an open steady-flow device. There the balance carries Δh plus kinetic and potential changes (see open systems and steady flow).

1.8 Flow processes: duct flow, nozzles and compressors

A nozzle, a duct, a throttling valve and a compressor all pass fluid steadily, so each obeys the steady-flow energy equation of 1.6. In each device one trade is left: enthalpy against kinetic energy in a nozzle, against shaft work in a compressor or turbine, and nothing gained in a throttling valve. This chapter writes those reduced forms, works out the work of compressing and expanding a gas, and shows why a fast gas in a duct behaves unlike water in a pipe.

You need: the steady-flow energy equation (1.6) and Cp, γ of an ideal gas (1.7).

One equation, five devices

Per kilogram passing a steady device, with c the speed, q the heat added and ws the shaft work done by the fluid:

h₁ + c₁²/2 + gz₁ + q = h₂ + c₂²/2 + gz₂ + ws

h in kJ/kg; c in m/s; z in m; g = 9.81 m/s². c²/2 is in J/kg: divide it by 1000 before adding it to h in kJ/kg.

Nozzle, diffuserq, ws, Δzh₁ + c₁²/2 = h₂ + c₂²/2enthalpy ↔ kinetic energy
Turbineq (small), Δc, Δzws = h₁ − h₂ (positive)enthalpy → shaft work

A throttling valve drops the pressure with no work and no heat, so h₁ = h₂. An ideal gas keeps its temperature; a real gas usually cools a little (the Joule–Thomson effect), which is how a refrigerator's expansion valve works. The lost pressure buys nothing: throttling is fully irreversible.

Nozzles and diffusers

A nozzle speeds the fluid up; a diffuser slows it down. With no shaft, no heat and no height change, the quantity h + c²/2, called the stagnation enthalpy h₀, stays constant. Whatever kinetic energy the fluid gains comes out of its own enthalpy, so in a nozzle the gas cools and its pressure falls; in a diffuser it warms and its pressure rises. A jet engine slows its intake air in a diffuser for this reason.

Compression and expansion of a gas

For a reversible steady-flow process with kinetic and potential terms neglected, the shaft work put into the fluid is win = ∫v dP from P₁ to P₂: the area to the left of the path on a P–v plot, not the area under it, which is the closed-system ∫P dv. For an ideal gas (R per kilogram, T in K) the three standard paths give:

isothermal:   win = RT₁ ln(P₂/P₁)
polytropic (Pvⁿ = constant):   win = [n/(n − 1)] RT₁ [(P₂/P₁)(n−1)/n − 1]
isentropic (n = γ):   win = cp T₁ [(P₂/P₁)(γ−1)/γ − 1] = cp (T₂s − T₁)

win is work into the gas per kg; R = 8.314/M kJ/(kg·K), 0.287 for air; T₂s is the isentropic exit temperature. For an expansion (P₂ < P₁) win comes out negative: work leaves.

Isothermal compression needs the least work and isentropic the most of the reversible paths with 1 < n < γ. A real uncooled compressor is adiabatic but irreversible, and its isentropic efficiency ηc = wisentropic/wactual (typically 0.75 to 0.90) says how much more work it takes. For a turbine the ratio is turned over: ηt = wactual/wisentropic. Several stages with intercooling between them bring a compressor close to the isothermal limit.

Duct flow of compressible fluids

Water keeps its density in a pipe; a gas does not, because its pressure and density fall as it speeds up. The Mach number Ma = c/a, with the speed of sound a = √(γRT), decides which effect wins; for air at 300 K, a = √(1.4 × 287 × 300) = 347 m/s. For steady isentropic flow in a duct of area A:

dA/A = (Ma² − 1) dc/c

dA and dc are small changes of area and speed along the duct; Ma is the local Mach number.

Below Ma = 1 the flow speeds up only where the duct narrows; above Ma = 1 it speeds up only where the duct widens; Ma = 1 can occur only at the narrowest section, the throat. So a converging nozzle can at most reach sonic speed at its exit, and a converging–diverging (de Laval) nozzle is needed for supersonic steam or gas jets. The throat pressure at Ma = 1 is the critical pressure: P*/P₀ = [2/(γ + 1)]γ/(γ−1), which is 0.528 for air (γ = 1.4) and about 0.546 for superheated steam (n ≈ 1.3). Once the back pressure falls below P*, the throat stays at Ma = 1 and the mass flow no longer rises: the nozzle is choked.

In a long insulated pipe of constant area, friction lowers the pressure while h + c²/2 stays constant; the density falls, so ṁ = ρAc forces the speed up toward Ma = 1, which the pipe cannot pass. A gas pipeline at Ma well below 0.3 flows close to isothermally.

Worked example

Question. Air enters an adiabatic nozzle at h₁ = 300 kJ/kg and c₁ = 50 m/s, and leaves at h₂ = 280 kJ/kg. Neglect height change. Find the exit speed c₂.

Takeaways

  1. A thermodynamic system is defined only after you draw the boundary; every balance is written for what crosses that boundary.
  2. Intensive properties locate the state; extensive properties scale with amount and become specific or molar properties after dividing by mass or moles.
  3. State functions depend only on where you start and end; work and heat depend on the road taken between them.
  4. Energy is stored as internal, kinetic or potential energy, or is in transit as heat or work; only transit terms cross the boundary.
  5. The zeroth law is what makes temperature measurable, and the ideal-gas scale lets any gas thermometer agree with the rest.
  6. ΔU = Q − W for a closed system is the whole first law; Joule's paddle-wheel experiment is what tells us U is a state function.
  7. Enthalpy is not a new kind of energy, only U + PV, and it enters open-system balances because the fluid carries flow work in and out.
  8. Cv and Cp convert temperature changes into internal-energy and enthalpy changes; for an ideal gas Cp − Cv = R.
  9. In a nozzle enthalpy turns into kinetic energy and in a throttling valve enthalpy stays constant; a compressor needs win = ∫v dP, least when isothermal.
  10. A gas speeds up in a narrowing duct only below Mach 1; supersonic flow needs a diverging section, and below the critical pressure ratio a nozzle is choked.

Take a control volume, apply conservation of mass and of energy over a time interval; Work terms: shaft work Ws plus work done by the fluid on the fluid ahead (flow work Pv); Ein − Eout = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE per unit mass plus flow work; Combine u + Pv = h; Q − Ws = ṁ[Δh + Δ(c²/2) + gΔz]; For a closed system the boundary moves with the mass so Pv terms cancel; Check limiting cases: no flow gives ΔU = Q − W; Q = Ws = 0 in a nozzle gives h1 + c1²/2 = h2 + c2²/2.

Model answer.

  1. Set up the control volume. A fixed region with one inlet (section 1) and one outlet (section 2), a shaft, and heat crossing the walls. Q is heat added, Ws shaft work done by the fluid. Consider a short time dt in which mass dm₁ enters and dm₂ leaves.
  2. Mass balance. Mass inside changes by dm₁ − dm₂. At steady flow nothing inside changes, so dm₁ = dm₂ = ṁ dt.
  3. Count the work at the openings. To push the element dm₁ (volume v₁dm₁) into the control volume, the fluid behind it pushes with force P₁A₁ through the distance v₁dm₁/A₁, doing work P₁v₁dm₁ on the system. At the outlet the system does P₂v₂dm₂ on the fluid ahead. This is flow work; it exists only because mass crosses the boundary.
    flow work in  = P₁v₁ dm₁      flow work out = P₂v₂ dm₂
  4. Energy balance over dt. Energy stored per kilogram of stream is e = u + c²/2 + gz.
    dEcv = δQ − δWs + (u₁ + c₁²/2 + gz₁ + P₁v₁) dm₁ − (u₂ + c₂²/2 + gz₂ + P₂v₂) dm₂
  5. Group u + Pv into h, then apply steady flow (dEcv = 0, dm₁ = dm₂ = ṁ dt) and divide by dt:
    h = u + Pv
Q − Ws = ṁ[(h₂ − h₁) + (c₂² − c₁²)/2 + g(z₂ − z₁)]
  6. Why the closed system uses U. In a closed system no mass crosses the boundary, so there are no flow-work terms; any moving boundary work is included in W. The Pv terms appear only with mass flow, and because they always ride with u in each stream, it is natural to combine them into one property h. That is the only reason enthalpy replaces internal energy.

    no flow, closed systemṁ, flow work; moving-boundary work in WΔU = Q − W
    nozzleQ, Ws, Δzh₁ + c₁²/2 = h₂ + c₂²/2

Evaluate A student argues: since Q and W depend on the path, the first law ΔU = Q − W means internal energy must also depend on the path. Examine this argument and explain what makes ΔU path-independent. Include a cyclic process in your answer. 14 marks

Points to cover. Identify the error: Q and W change together so their difference is path-independent; State exact-differential test for dU versus δQ and δW; Illustrate with two paths between the same states: different Q, different W, same ΔU; Cycle: ∮dU = 0 while ∮δQ = ∮δW ≠ 0; Joule's experiment as experimental evidence; Consequence: property tables and equations of state are possible; Strength/limitation: first law alone does not say which direction a process can go — that needs the second law.

Model answer.

  1. Find the error. The argument assumes that because Q and W each depend on the path, their difference must too. It does not follow: Q and W can change together in such a way that Q − W stays the same. Experiment (Joule) shows that they do.
  2. Use the exactness test. With T and V as variables for one mole of ideal gas, dU = Cv dT passes the test, while δW = (RT/V) dV fails it (∂(RT/V)/∂T = R/V ≠ 0), and so does δQ = Cv dT + (RT/V) dV. The sum of two inexact differentials can be exact:
    dU = δQ − δW = [Cv dT + (RT/V) dV] − (RT/V) dV = Cv dT   (exact)
  3. Show it with two paths (ideal gas from A at 3 kPa, 1 L to B at 1 kPa, 3 L; PV is 3 J at both ends, so T and U are the same at A and B).
    PathW (J)ΔU (J)Q = ΔU + W (J)
    A, via (2 L, 1.5 kPa)3.5003.50
    B, via (2 L, 2 kPa)4.0004.00
    difference0.5000.50
  4. Make a cycle. Go A → B along path A and return along path B reversed. ∮δW = 3.50 − 4.00 = −0.50 J and ∮δQ = −0.50 J, both non-zero; ∮dU = 0. A cycle returns every property to its starting value, so a quantity with a non-zero cyclic integral cannot be a property, while ∮dU = 0 for every cycle is what makes U one.
  5. Experimental evidence. Joule raised the temperature of insulated water by the same amount with different combinations of falling mass and height, and the same rise could be produced by heating. The end state, and so ΔU, fixed the energy needed, whatever mix of work and heat supplied it. The cyclic statement ∮δQ = ∮δW is the general form of this observation, and ΔU = Q − W follows from it.
  6. Consequences. Because U is a property, it can be tabulated (steam tables, property charts) and calculated along any convenient path, real or hypothetical, between the same end states; that is how ΔU of a sudden irreversible process is found.
  7. Limitation of the first law. It fixes the energy balance but not the direction: it allows heat to flow from cold to hot or a free expansion to reverse itself, as long as energy is conserved. Deciding which processes can actually happen needs the second law and entropy.
Analyze Compare a throttling valve, a nozzle and a compressor as steady-flow devices. For each, write the reduced energy equation, state the assumptions and explain where the energy goes. 13 marks

Points to cover. All three: steady flow, single inlet and outlet, neglect ΔPE; Throttling: Q = 0, W = 0, ΔKE ≈ 0 → h1 = h2, isenthalpic; pressure drops through friction, irreversible; Nozzle: Q = 0, W = 0 → enthalpy converts into kinetic energy, pressure and temperature fall; Compressor: ΔKE small, Q small → Ws ≈ Δh, work input raises pressure and enthalpy; Identify which terms may be dropped and why; mention typical assumptions of negligible heat loss; Compare reversibility: nozzle and diffuser designed near-isentropic, throttling fully irreversible.

Model answer.

  1. Common assumptions. Steady flow, one inlet and one outlet, height change negligible. Per kilogram, with ws work done by the fluid:
    h₁ + c₁²/2 + q = h₂ + c₂²/2 + ws
  2. Throttling valve. No shaft (ws = 0); the valve is small and the flow fast, so q ≈ 0; inlet and outlet pipes are sized so that c₁ ≈ c₂.
    h₁ = h₂    (isenthalpic)
  3. Where the energy goes in the valve: nowhere. The pressure drop is used up in friction and eddies, which turn ordered flow energy into internal energy of the same fluid. An ideal gas keeps its temperature (air throttled from 5 bar, 300 K to 1 bar stays at 300 K); a real gas usually cools a little (the Joule–Thomson effect, the basis of refrigeration expansion valves). The process is completely irreversible: the pressure is lost and nothing is gained for it.
  4. Nozzle. No shaft, nearly adiabatic, but the speed changes a lot:
    h₁ + c₁²/2 = h₂ + c₂²/2
example: h₁ = 300, h₂ = 280 kJ/kg, c₁ = 50 m/s → c₂ = 206.2 m/s
  5. Where the energy goes in the nozzle: enthalpy turns into kinetic energy. The fluid speeds up, and its enthalpy, temperature and pressure fall. A well-designed nozzle is close to isentropic (reversible and adiabatic), and a diffuser does the reverse.
  6. Compressor. Large pipes, so c₁ ≈ c₂; heat loss usually small:
    win = −ws = h₂ − h₁ − q
example: air 1 → 5 bar, 300 K, ηc = 0.80: win = 220.0 kJ/kg, T₂ = 518.9 K
  7. Where the energy goes in the compressor: shaft work turns into enthalpy (higher pressure and temperature). Some extra work is lost in friction, which is why the real machine needs 220.0 kJ/kg against 176.0 for the isentropic ideal.
  8. Summary.

Evaluate Choose a control volume for a steam turbine in a power plant and carry out a full energy accounting for it. Discuss which terms you may neglect, how you would estimate the heat loss, and what the first law alone cannot tell you about the turbine's performance. 15 marks

Points to cover. Draw the control volume around the turbine casing with inlet, outlet and shaft; Write Q − Ws = m[Δh + ΔKE + ΔPE]; Neglect ΔPE; check whether ΔKE is significant against Δh; Shaft work from the enthalpy drop; sign convention for work out; Estimate casing heat loss from insulation and surface temperature, show it is small but not zero; First law gives quantity of work only, not the best possible work — need the second law and isentropic efficiency; Practical points: steam quality at exit, condensate, unit consistency.

Model answer.

  1. Draw the control volume around the turbine casing, cutting the steam inlet pipe (1), the exhaust (2) and the shaft. Mark Q (heat into the steam, here a loss) and Ws (shaft power out, positive). Use the worked example data: ṁ = 2.0 kg/s, h₁ = 3200 kJ/kg, c₁ = 60 m/s, h₂ = 2600 kJ/kg, c₂ = 120 m/s.
  2. Write the full balance:
    Q − Ws = ṁ[(h₂ − h₁) + (c₂² − c₁²)/2 + g(z₂ − z₁)]
  3. Size each term per kilogram before dropping anything (assume the exhaust is 3 m below the inlet).
    TermValue (kJ/kg)Share of |Δh|
    Δh = h₂ − h₁−600100 %
    Δke = (120² − 60²)/2000+5.40.9 %
    Δpe = 9.81 × (−3)/1000−0.030.005 %
    q = Q/ṁto be estimated
  4. Decide what to neglect. Δpe is negligible. Δke is under 1 % here, so it may be dropped for a quick estimate, but it is kept below because it is easy; with a high exhaust velocity (large last-stage blades, 200 to 300 m/s) it would not be negligible.
  5. Estimate the heat loss. Q = hoA(Tsurface − Tair), with assumed values: insulated casing area A = 25 m², outer surface 70 °C, air 30 °C, combined convection-plus-radiation coefficient ho ≈ 12 W/(m²·K).
    Q ≈ 12 × 25 × (70 − 30) = 12 000 W = 12 kW lost   (Q = −12 kW)
worked example used Q = −50 kW, a casing with poorer insulation
  6. Shaft power with both heat-loss values.
    Ws = Q − ṁ(Δh + Δke) = Q − 2.0 × (−600 + 5.4) = Q + 1189.2
Q = −50 kW → Ws = 1139.2 kW;  Q = −12 kW → Ws = 1177.2 kW
  7. Sign check. Ws is positive, so work leaves the steam, as it must for a turbine; heat loss reduces the work available from the same enthalpy drop.
  8. What the first law cannot say. It gives the quantity of work for the measured inlet and exit states, not the best possible work. Suppose the isentropic exit enthalpy at the same exhaust pressure, read from a Mollier chart, is 2450 kJ/kg. Then the ideal drop is 750 kJ/kg and the isentropic efficiency is ηt = 600/750 = 0.80: 20 % of the available drop is lost to friction and leakage. Finding h₂s and judging that loss needs entropy and the second law.
  9. Practical points. At the exhaust of a condensing turbine the steam is usually wet: its quality must be checked because droplets erode the last blades. Keep units consistent (kJ/kg × kg/s = kW; c²/2 in J/kg ÷ 1000). Measure h from pressure and temperature only where the steam is superheated; in the wet region temperature alone does not fix the state.
Evaluate An insulated rigid tank is divided into two parts by a partition; one side holds a compressed gas and the other is evacuated. The partition is removed and the gas fills the tank. Analyse the process using the first law, state the assumptions, and comment on whether the gas temperature changes for an ideal gas. 12 marks

Points to cover. Closed system, rigid: W = 0; insulated: Q = 0 → ΔU = 0; Free expansion is highly irreversible, not a reversible adiabatic path; For an ideal gas U depends on T only, so T is unchanged and P falls as V increases; For a real gas, U depends on V too, so T changes (Joule coefficient); Contrast with reversible adiabatic expansion where T falls; Note that the energy balance gives the answer without knowing the intermediate states.

Model answer.

  1. System and assumptions. The whole tank (both halves) is the system: closed, since no mass crosses its outer wall. Rigid, so no boundary work; no shaft or electrical work: W = 0. Insulated: Q = 0. The tank does not move: ΔKE = ΔPE = 0. Take numbers: air (ideal gas, R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K), cv = 0.718 kJ/(kg·K)) at 400 kPa and 300 K fills one half of 0.5 m³; the other half of 0.5 m³ is empty.
  2. First law.
    ΔU = Q − W = 0 − 0 = 0
  3. Nature of the process. When the partition breaks, the gas rushes into the vacuum with large pressure and velocity differences; it passes through no equilibrium states, so it is highly irreversible and cannot be drawn as a line on a P–V diagram. The first law still applies, because it needs only the end states. Work is zero because nothing outside the gas is pushed.
  4. Ideal gas. U depends on T only, so ΔU = 0 gives ΔT = 0.
    m = P₁V₁/(RT₁) = 400 × 0.5/(0.287 × 300) = 2.323 kg
T₂ = T₁ = 300 K,  V₂ = 1.0 m³
P₂ = mRT₂/V₂ = P₁V₁/V₂ = 400 × 0.5/1.0 = 200 kPa
  5. Contrast with a reversible adiabatic expansion to the same volume (a piston pushed out slowly, PV1.4 constant):
    T₂ = 300 × (0.5/1.0)0.4 = 227.4 K
P₂ = 400 × (0.5)1.4 = 151.6 kPa
W = m cv (T₁ − T₂) = 2.323 × 0.718 × (300 − 227.4) = 121.2 kJ
  6. Compare. Both processes are adiabatic and end at 1.0 m³, but the reversible one does 121.2 kJ of work and cools the gas by 72.6 K, while the free expansion does no work and leaves the temperature unchanged. Q = 0 alone does not fix the final state; the work does.
  7. Real gas. In a real gas U depends slightly on volume too, because the molecules attract each other. When the gas spreads out, work is done against these attractions at the expense of molecular kinetic energy, so most real gases cool slightly in a free expansion (the Joule coefficient (∂T/∂V)U is negative). Joule could not measure this with air at low pressure, which is why the result is stated exactly only for an ideal gas.
  8. Conclusion. Q = 0, W = 0, ΔU = 0; for an ideal gas the temperature stays at 300 K and the pressure halves to 200 kPa. The energy balance gives this without knowing any intermediate state.

Glossary

Adiabatic
No heat crosses the boundary, Q = 0.
Boundary
The surface, real or imaginary, that separates the system from its surroundings and across which heat, work and mass are counted.
Choked flow
Flow in which the throat has reached sonic speed, so lowering the back pressure no longer raises the mass flow.
Closed system
A system of fixed mass in which no mass crosses the boundary.
Control volume
A fixed region in space, with mass able to flow in and out, used for open-system analysis.
Diathermal wall
A wall that allows heat to pass so that two bodies can reach thermal equilibrium.
Enthalpy
H = U + PV, a state function convenient for constant-pressure and flow processes.
Extensive property
A property proportional to the quantity of matter, such as mass, volume or total internal energy.
Flow work
The Pv work a flowing fluid does on the fluid ahead of it and receives from the fluid behind it.
Heat
Energy transferred across a boundary because of a temperature difference.
Heat capacity
The heat needed to raise the temperature of a unit amount by one kelvin, at constant volume or constant pressure.
Intensive property
A property independent of size, such as temperature, pressure or density.
Internal energy
The total kinetic and potential energy of the molecules of the system, a state function whose changes are measurable.
Irreversible process
A real process with friction, finite gradients or unrestrained expansion that cannot be undone without external work.
Isentropic efficiency
For a compressor, isentropic work over actual work; for a turbine, actual work over isentropic work.
Isolated system
A system that exchanges neither mass nor energy with the surroundings.
Kelvin
The SI unit of thermodynamic temperature; since 2019 it is fixed by the Boltzmann constant, and the triple point of water is 273.16 K to within a fraction of a millikelvin.
Mach number
Flow speed divided by the local speed of sound, Ma = c/a with a = √(γRT) for an ideal gas.
Molar property
An extensive property divided by the number of moles, in units per mole.
Nozzle
A duct shaped so that the fluid speeds up while its enthalpy and pressure fall: converging for subsonic flow, converging–diverging to pass sonic speed.
Open system
A system in which mass crosses the boundary, also called a control volume.
Path function
A quantity whose value between two states depends on the route, such as heat and work.
Reversible process
An idealised process through continuous equilibrium states that can be reversed exactly by an infinitesimal change in the surroundings.
Specific property
An extensive property divided by mass, in units per kilogram.
State function
A property fixed by the state of the system, so its change depends only on the end states.
Steady flow
A flow in which properties at any point do not change with time, so mass and energy within the control volume stay constant.
Throttling
A pressure drop through a valve or porous plug with no work and negligible heat, so h1 = h2.
Zeroth law
If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other, which justifies temperature as a property.
