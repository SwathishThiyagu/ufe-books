2.1 PVT behaviour of pure fluids and phase diagrams

Water in a kettle, LPG in a cylinder, steam entering a turbine: each is a pure fluid whose state is fixed by pressure P, molar volume V and temperature T. Plot every equilibrium state and you get a surface. Its three flat views, P–V, P–T and T–V, are the phase diagrams. They show which phase exists, where two phases coexist, and where boiling stops being possible at all. Every equation of state is drawn on them.

You need: system, state, intensive and extensive properties and the meaning of a pure substance, from the basic concepts of thermodynamics.

The P-V-T surface and its three views

A pure fluid has one chemical species. In equilibrium its state is fixed by any two of P (pressure, in Pa or bar), T (temperature, in K) and V (molar volume, in m³/mol). The third follows.

Plot all such states with P up, V to the right and T going back. The sheet you get is the P-V-T surface.

Cut the surface three ways. At constant T you get a P–V isotherm. At constant P you get a T–V isobar. At constant V you get a P–T isochore. Each cut is a flat drawing that loses one variable but keeps every phase boundary.

Each view answers a different question. P–V answers work questions, because work is ∫P dV. P–T answers stability questions: which phase exists. T–V answers density questions: how much the volume changes on heating.

For water at 1 bar the two ends of the two-phase region are vf = 0.001043 m³/kg (saturated liquid) and vg = 1.694 m³/kg (saturated vapour).

A liquid far from the critical point has nearly vertical P–V isotherms, so its volume hardly changes. Two coefficients say how little. The volume expansivity β = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)P measures growth on heating; the isothermal compressibility κ = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T measures shrinkage on squeezing. When both are nearly constant, ln(V2/V1) = β(T2 − T1) − κ(P2 − P1). For liquid water at 25 °C, β ≈ 2.6 × 10−4 K−1 and κ ≈ 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1: raising the pressure by 100 bar shrinks it by only about 0.45 %.

The saturation dome

Below the critical temperature a P–V isotherm has three parts. The left branch rises almost vertically: liquid hardly compresses. The right branch falls gently: vapour does compress. Between them the isotherm is horizontal at one pressure, the vapour pressure Psat(T).

The flat part is the two-phase region. Its left end is saturated liquid, the last liquid before a bubble of vapour appears. Its right end is saturated vapour, the last vapour before a drop of liquid appears. The horizontal length is vg − vf.

Repeat for every temperature below the critical temperature Tc. The left ends trace the saturated-liquid curve, the right ends the saturated-vapour curve. Together they close the saturation dome, also called the vapour dome. Inside the dome liquid and vapour coexist at the same P and T but at different V. A mixture of quality x has V = (1 − x)vf + x·vg, where x is the mass fraction of vapour and vf and vg are the saturated-liquid and saturated-vapour volumes.

The two curves meet at the critical point: for water, Tc = 374 °C and Pc = 221 bar. There saturated liquid and saturated vapour are identical, the latent heat is zero, and the meniscus between them disappears.

The P-T phase diagram and the phase rule

The P–T diagram is the most-used view. Three curves split the plane into solid, liquid and vapour regions. The sublimation curve separates solid from vapour. The fusion curve separates solid from liquid. The vaporisation curve separates liquid from vapour and ends at the critical point. All three meet at the triple point: for water, 0.01 °C and 0.00611 bar, where ice, liquid water and steam coexist.

For water the fusion curve leans left, so ice melts when the pressure rises. That is unusual; for most substances the fusion curve leans right.

Above the critical point the liquid and vapour regions merge. The fluid there is supercritical — neither liquid nor gas. No amount of pressure liquefies it while T stays above Tc. Supercritical carbon dioxide is used to strip caffeine from coffee beans, and dense supercritical water is studied as a reaction medium.

F = C − Pph + 2 = 3 − Pph

F is the number of intensive variables you may fix freely (degrees of freedom); C = 1 is the number of components; Pph is the number of phases present. P on its own always means pressure, in bar or Pa.

One phase gives F = 2: choose T and P independently. Two phases give F = 1: choosing T fixes P = Psat(T). Three phases give F = 0: the triple point is one single state.

Mathematical representation: the equation of state

An equation of state is an algebraic relation among P, V and T for a fluid: f(P, V, T) = 0, or P = f(V, T). Every curve in the diagrams above is a solution of it. Some syllabi call this whole topic volumetric properties of pure fluids.

The simplest relation is the ideal gas law, PV = RT, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant. It holds when molecules are far apart; the next chapter develops it.

Real fluids need corrections. The compressibility factor Z = PV/RT measures the departure from ideal behaviour: Z = 1 for an ideal gas, Z < 1 when attraction between molecules pulls them together, and Z > 1 when their own size pushes them apart (high pressure). Virial equations add terms in 1/V. Cubic equations such as van der Waals carry two constants, a for attraction and b for molecular size, and can reproduce the dome and the critical point — see virial and cubic equations and compressibility factor.

An equation of state can also be built from the forces between pairs of molecules, but that is statistical mechanics, not this course. Mixtures, fugacity and the Clapeyron equation come later, with solution thermodynamics and property relations.

Worked example

Question. Water is held at 1 bar (100 kPa). (a) Name the phase at 25 °C, at 99.6 °C and at 120 °C. (b) Treat the vapour at 120 °C and 1 bar as ideal and estimate its specific volume. Data: R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), M = 18.015 g/mol.

  1. At 1 bar the saturation temperature of water is 99.6 °C. The normal boiling point, 100.0 °C, is defined at 1 atm = 1.01325 bar, slightly above 1 bar.
  2. 25 °C is below the saturation temperature. The fluid is a compressed (subcooled) liquid. One phase, so F = 3 − 1 = 2.
  3. 99.6 °C is the saturation temperature. Liquid and vapour coexist; any quality 0 ≤ x ≤ 1 is possible. Two phases, F = 1, so P alone fixes T.
  4. 120 °C is above the saturation temperature. The fluid is superheated vapour. One phase, F = 2.
  5. On the P–T diagram (figure above) the 1 bar line crosses the vaporisation curve once, at 99.6 °C: below it is liquid, above it vapour.
  6. Specific gas constant: Rs = R/M = 8.314/0.018015 = 461.5 J/(kg·K).
  7. Absolute temperature: T = 120 + 273.15 = 393.15 K.
  8. Ideal gas law per unit mass: v = Rs·T/P = 461.5 × 393.15/100 000 = 1.8144 m³/kg.
  9. Compare with the molar form: V = RT/P = 8.314 × 393.15/100 000 = 0.03269 m³/mol, and 0.03269 m³/mol ÷ 0.018015 kg/mol = 1.8144 m³/kg. Both routes agree.

Answer. Liquid at 25 °C, liquid–vapour at 99.6 °C and vapour at 120 °C; the ideal-gas specific volume at 120 °C and 1 bar is 1.8144 m³/kg.

Exam traps

  • Reading the saturation curve as a single phase. On a P–T diagram every point of the vaporisation curve is a two-phase state: saturated liquid and saturated vapour together. On a P–V diagram the two branches of the dome are saturated liquid and saturated vapour, and only points strictly inside the dome are two-phase mixtures.
  • Calling the critical point a boiling point. At the critical point liquid and vapour are one phase and the latent heat is zero.
  • Thinking a gas above Tc can be liquefied by pressure alone. It cannot; you must first cool it below Tc.
  • Putting °C into PV = RT or any ideal-gas formula. Always convert to K.
  • Mixing molar or specific volume with total volume. Vtotal = n·V; phase diagrams use volume per mole or per kilogram.

2.2 Ideal gas and ideal-gas processes

The ideal-gas law, PV = nRT, is the simplest equation of state. It treats molecules as points with no attractions, so heat, work and internal-energy change follow from the path alone. Engineers use it for a first estimate of compressor duty, pipeline pressure drop or the air in a tyre, and it is the reference against which real gases are measured. This chapter fixes the sign convention and works the five common paths with numbers.

You need: P–V diagrams, and the first law, enthalpy and heat capacities from the basic concepts of thermodynamics.

The ideal-gas law and its energy

The ideal-gas law is PV = nRT, with P the absolute pressure, V the volume, n the moles and T the absolute temperature. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, or 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when V is in L and P in bar. Never put T in °C: 25 °C is 298.15 K.

In this model molecules have no volume and no forces except elastic collisions, so U depends on T only, and so does H = U + PV = U + nRT. An isothermal change therefore has ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0 even when P and V change.

PV = nRT; Cp − Cv = R; γ = Cp/Cv

P is absolute pressure; V is volume; n is moles; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; Cp and Cv are molar heat capacities at constant pressure and volume; γ is the heat-capacity ratio.

Here Cv = (∂U/∂T)V is the heat to raise 1 mol by 1 K at constant volume and Cp = (∂H/∂T)P the same at constant pressure. For a diatomic gas near room temperature, Cv ≈ (5/2)R and Cp ≈ (7/2)R, so γ ≈ 1.4; monatomic gases have γ ≈ 1.67.

Five paths and one sign convention

Use W as work done by the gas on the surroundings. Then the first law for a closed system is ΔU = Q − W. Q is heat added to the gas; W is positive for expansion and negative for compression. State this convention before any calculation.

ΔU = Q − W

ΔU is change in internal energy; Q is heat added to the gas; W is work done by the gas on the surroundings.

Each path fixes one quantity. Integrate W = ∫P dV along the path. The table gives closed-system results for an ideal gas with constant Cv and Cp; ΔT = T2 − T1. The work formulas are for reversible paths; for irreversible paths use the actual external pressure.

An isochoric path has no volume change, so W = 0; all heat goes into U. An isobaric path expands against constant pressure, so W = PΔV and Q = ΔH. An isothermal path keeps U and H fixed; heat and work cancel. An adiabatic path has Q = 0; work comes from U, so expansion cools the gas. Mixtures and the limit of the model

For a mixture of ideal gases each component obeys PiV = niRT, with Pi its partial pressure, and P = ΣPi (Dalton's law). The mixture's U and H depend on T only, and its Cv and Cp are mole-fraction averages.

The ideal-gas law is accurate at low pressure and high temperature, when molecules are far apart. At high pressure or near condensation use the compressibility factor or a cubic equation of state; the paths here still give a first estimate.

Worked example

Question. One mole of an ideal gas is at 300 K and 1.00 bar. Its volume is 24.94 L. It is taken to 12.47 L by three separate paths: (a) isothermal, (b) adiabatic with γ = 1.4, (c) isobaric. Find the final pressure for each path. Use R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Check the start: V1 = nRT1/P1 = 1 × 0.08314 × 300/1.00 = 24.94 L, as given.
  2. (a) Isothermal: T is constant, so P1V1 = P2V2. P2 = P1V1/V2 = (1.00 bar)(24.94 L)/(12.47 L) = 2.00 bar.
  3. (b) Adiabatic: PVγ = constant, so P2 = P1(V1/V2)γ. The volume ratio is 24.94/12.47 = 2.000. P2 = (1.00 bar)(2.000)1.4 = 2.64 bar. The temperature follows from TVγ−1 = constant: T2 = 300 × 2.0000.4 = 395.9 K.
  4. (c) Isobaric: P is fixed, P2 = P1 = 1.00 bar. Halving V at fixed P needs T to halve too: T2 = 300 × 12.47/24.94 = 150 K, so heat must be removed.
  5. Check: L cancels in each ratio and pressure stays in bar. The adiabatic pressure is the highest because compression work raises the gas temperature to 396 K with no heat let out; at the same small volume a higher T gives a higher P.

Answer. Final pressures: (a) 2.00 bar at 300 K, (b) 2.64 bar at 395.9 K, (c) 1.00 bar at 150 K.

Exam traps

  • PVγ = constant is for adiabatic, not isothermal. Isothermal is PV = constant; using γ there gives a wrong final pressure.
  • Put T in kelvin. 27 °C is 300 K; using 27 in PV = nRT is a large error.
  • State the W sign convention. Many engineering texts take W by gas positive (ΔU = Q − W); many chemistry texts take W on gas positive (ΔU = Q + W). Mixing them flips Q.
  • For a rigid tank use Cv, not Cp. Q = nCvΔT for isochoric; Q = nCpΔT for isobaric.
  • Adiabatic does not mean isothermal. Q = 0, but T changes and W = nCv(T1 − T2) is not zero.

2.3 Virial and cubic equations of state (van der Waals)

Real gases deviate from PV = nRT because molecules occupy space and attract one another. The virial equation writes the deviation as a series in 1/V or in P. Cubic equations such as van der Waals add two physical corrections: a finite molecular volume b and an attraction term a/V². Engineers use them to size gas lines and storage vessels when the ideal gas law is too rough.

You need: the ideal gas law and the critical constants Tc and Pc from the phase diagram.

Compressibility factor and the virial series

Z = PV/RT is 1 for an ideal gas at every pressure. For a real gas it departs from 1 because molecules attract and occupy volume. The virial equation is an exact infinite series for that departure:

Z = 1 + B/V + C/V² + ... = 1 + B'P + C'P² + ...

Z is PV/(RT); V is molar volume in L/mol; P is pressure in bar; B is the second virial coefficient in L/mol; C is the third virial coefficient in L²/mol²; B' and C' are pressure-series coefficients with units 1/bar and 1/bar².

B comes from pair interactions, C from three-body interactions. B depends strongly on temperature: negative at low temperature, where attraction dominates, and positive at high temperature. The two series are linked by B′ = B/RT and C′ = (C − B²)/(RT)². At moderate pressure the series can be cut after B: Z ≈ 1 + B/V or Z ≈ 1 + BP/RT. This truncated virial equation serves gas metering and moderate-pressure densities, not dense liquids. Some syllabi call this the mathematical representation of PVT behaviour.

van der Waals cubic equation

The van der Waals equation replaces the ideal-gas pressure RT/V with two terms:

P = RT/(V − b) − a/V²

P is pressure in bar; V is molar volume in L/mol; T is temperature in K; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when V is in m³/mol and P is in Pa, or R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when V is in L/mol and P is in bar; b is the excluded volume per mole in L/mol; a is the attraction parameter in L² bar mol⁻².

RT/(V − b) is the pressure of hard molecules of finite size; b is the excluded volume per mole, about four times the molecules' own volume. The term −a/V² lowers the pressure because attraction pulls molecules back from the wall. As V grows, both corrections fade and the equation tends to PV = RT.

At the critical point the T = Tc isotherm has a horizontal inflection. So at that one point, V = Vc and P = Pc, both (∂P/∂V)T = 0 and (∂²P/∂V²)T = 0. Solving these two conditions with the equation itself gives the constants:

a = 27R²Tc²/(64Pc), b = RTc/(8Pc), Zc = PcVc/(RTc) = 3/8 = 0.375

Tc is critical temperature in K; Pc is critical pressure in bar; Vc is critical molar volume in L/mol; Zc is critical compressibility factor.

Real fluids have Zc of 0.23 to 0.29, so van der Waals overpredicts Zc and is poor near the critical point and for dense liquids.

Reduced equation and corresponding states

Put P = PrPc, V = VrVc and T = TrTc into the van der Waals equation. The constants a and b cancel. The result is the reduced van der Waals equation:

(Pr + 3/Vr²)(3Vr − 1) = 8Tr

Pr = P/Pc, Vr = V/Vc and Tr = T/Tc are reduced pressure, volume and temperature; all are dimensionless.

This equation contains no gas-specific constant. Two fluids at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure then have the same reduced volume, and hence the same Z: the law of corresponding states. It is the first generalized equation of state. Real fluids follow it only approximately; generalized correlations correct it.

The van der Waals equation is the simplest cubic. Redlich–Kwong (RK) divides the attraction term by T0.5: a/(T0.5V(V + b)). Soave (SRK) and Peng–Robinson (PR) make a depend on T and ω, and are the simulator defaults for gases and vapour–liquid equilibrium; mixtures need mixing rules for a and b.

Worked example

Question. For CO2 at 300 K and V = 1.00 L/mol, use R = 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K), a = 3.640 L²·bar/mol² and b = 0.04267 L/mol. Find P from the ideal gas law and from the van der Waals equation.

  1. Ideal gas: P = RT/V = (0.08314)(300)/(1.00) = 24.942 bar.
  2. van der Waals: first compute V − b = 1.00 − 0.04267 = 0.95733 L/mol.
  3. Repulsion term: RT/(V − b) = 24.942/0.95733 = 26.054 bar.
  4. Attraction term: a/V² = 3.640/(1.00)² = 3.640 bar.
  5. Subtract: P = 26.054 − 3.640 = 22.414 bar, which rounds to 22.41 bar.

Answer. The ideal gas law gives 24.94 bar and the van der Waals equation gives 22.41 bar.

2.4 Compressibility factor and generalized correlations

Real gases do not obey PV = RT, especially near the critical point and at high pressure. The compressibility factor Z = PV/RT measures the departure. Instead of a separate table for every gas, generalized correlations use reduced temperature and pressure, and the acentric factor, to give Z from one chart or one equation. Chemical engineers use them for quick volume estimates, pipeline sizing and checking equations of state.

You need: equations of state for critical constants and cubic equations, and the ideal gas law.

What Z measures

For one mole, an ideal gas obeys PV = RT. Define the compressibility factor Z = PV/RT. Here P is pressure, V is molar volume, R is the gas constant, and T is absolute temperature. Z is dimensionless. An ideal gas has Z = 1 at every T and P. A real gas has Z below or above 1.

Z below 1 means attractive forces dominate, so the volume is smaller than ideal. Z above 1 means molecular size and repulsion dominate, so the volume is larger than ideal. At very low pressure every gas approaches Z = 1. Near the critical point Z can be far from 1. Z is a function of T and P for each gas, not a constant.

V = ZRT/P

V is molar volume, Z is the compressibility factor, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and P is pressure.

  • Reduced temperature: Tr = T/Tc, where Tc is the critical temperature.
  • Reduced pressure: Pr = P/Pc, where Pc is the critical pressure.
  • Reduced volume: Vr = V/Vc, where Vc is the critical molar volume.
  • Tr, Pr and Vr are dimensionless.

Corresponding states and the acentric factor

The principle of corresponding states says that fluids at the same Tr and Pr have approximately the same Z. This is why one generalized compressibility chart can serve many gases. It is a two-parameter correlation: Tc and Pc fix the reduced coordinates.

Simple spherical molecules (argon, krypton, methane) follow this rule well. Polar and long molecules need a third parameter, the Pitzer acentric factor ω = −1.0 − log10(Prsat) at Tr = 0.7. For simple fluids Prsat = 0.1 there, so ω = 0; for n-butane ω = 0.200. The three-parameter correlation adds a correction term proportional to ω.

Z = Z⁰ + ωZ¹

Z⁰ is the simple-fluid compressibility factor, Z¹ is its correction, and ω is the Pitzer acentric factor; both Z⁰ and Z¹ depend on Tr and Pr.

Some syllabi call this the generalized compressibility factor correlation. For nonpolar gases it is accurate enough for quick design checks. For strongly polar or associating gases, use an equation of state with better mixing rules.

Reading a chart and using generalized equations

  1. Look up Tc and Pc for the gas.
  2. Compute Tr = T/Tc and Pr = P/Pc.
  3. Find the curve for Tr and move to the value Pr on the horizontal axis.
  4. Read Z from the vertical axis.
  5. Compute V = ZRT/P, keeping R consistent with the pressure and volume units.

A generalized equation of state is written in Tr, Pr and ω, so it returns Z for any gas from three tabulated constants. The simplest is the Pitzer virial correlation, the truncated virial equation with B made generalized:

Z = 1 + (B⁰ + ωB¹)Pr/Tr,   B⁰ = 0.083 − 0.422/Tr1.6,   B¹ = 0.139 − 0.172/Tr4.2

B⁰ and B¹ are dimensionless functions of Tr only; the group (B⁰ + ωB¹) equals BPc/RTc.

Z is then linear in pressure, so the correlation holds only where the real isotherm is nearly straight: low to moderate Pr (roughly Vr ≥ 2, or Pr below about 1 at Tr = 1.2). At higher pressure use the chart or a generalized cubic. Any cubic from the cubic table can be put in reduced form with β = ΩPr/Tr and q = Ψα(Tr)/(ΩTr), and solved by repeated substitution from Z = 1:

Z = 1 + β − qβ(Z − β)/[(Z + εβ)(Z + σβ)]

β and q are dimensionless; ε and σ are the same pure numbers as in the generic cubic.

Tpc = Σ yiTc,i,  Ppc = Σ yiPc,i  (Kay's rule)

yi is the mole fraction and Tc,i, Pc,i are the component critical constants; Tpc and Ppc are pseudo-critical values used in place of Tc and Pc. Kay's rule is rough when the components differ much in size or polarity (natural gas with CO2 or H2S); use a cubic with mixing rules there.

Worked example

Question. A gas has Tc = 300 K, Pc = 50 bar and ω = 0.20. At T = 360 K and P = 100 bar its generalized chart (Z⁰ + ωZ¹) gives Z = 0.61. Find Tr, Pr and the molar volume, using R = 0.08314 L·bar/(mol·K).

2.5 Heat effects: sensible heat, latent heat, heat of reaction

A one-kilogram block of ice left in a warm room warms, then sits at 0 °C while it melts, warms again, then sits at 100 °C while it boils. The same heat does different jobs: raising temperature, pulling a phase apart and, when a reaction runs, rearranging chemical bonds. Boiler design, refrigeration and every reactor energy balance need these kept separate.

You need: enthalpy and the first law from the basic concepts of thermodynamics, and Cp from ideal-gas processes.

Sensible heat moves the thermometer

Sensible heat is heat that changes the temperature of a substance. No phase change, no reaction. A thermometer shows the change, so the heat is called sensible.

Q = ∫ n Cp dT, from T1 to T2

Q is heat added (kJ); n is amount of substance (mol); Cp is molar heat capacity at constant pressure (kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹); T1 and T2 are the initial and final absolute temperatures (K).

When Cp is nearly constant over the range, the integral collapses to Q = n Cp (T₂ − T₁). Working in kilograms gives Q = m cp ΔT, where m is mass (kg), cp is specific heat capacity (kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), and ΔT is the temperature change (K or °C — the size is the same).

Substancecp (kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
Water (liquid)4.18
Ice2.09
Steam2.01
Aluminium0.90

Cp of a gas climbs with temperature, so one number is only an average over the band. Use a mean Cp, or fit Cp = a + bT + cT² (data books list a, b and c for each gas) and integrate term by term: Q = n[a(T2 − T1) + (b/2)(T2² − T1²) + (c/3)(T2³ − T1³)].

Sign rule: Q is positive when heat enters the system. A 1 kg aluminium pot heated from 30 °C to 120 °C takes in 1 × 0.90 × 90 = 81 kJ before the food inside warms.

Latent heat moves the molecules apart

Latent heat is heat that changes the phase of a substance at constant temperature and constant pressure. Nothing warms; the attraction between molecules gives way.

Q = m Δh

m is mass (kg); Δh is the specific latent heat (kJ kg⁻¹): Δhfus for melting, Δhvap for boiling, Δhsub for solid to vapour.

At 1 atm, for water, Δhfus = 334 kJ/kg at 0 °C and Δhvap = 2260 kJ/kg at 100 °C. Each pair belongs to its own pressure; at another pressure both the value and the transition temperature change.

The 2260 kJ/kg does two jobs. About 170 kJ/kg pushes the atmosphere aside as the vapour expands: PΔv = 101.3 kPa × (1.673 − 0.001) m³/kg = 169 kJ/kg, the PΔV part of ΔH = ΔU + PΔV. The rest raises internal energy by breaking hydrogen bonds. That is also why a steam burn at 100 °C is far worse than a water burn at 100 °C: condensing steam gives up its 2260 kJ/kg on the skin first.

Heat of reaction: constant pressure and constant volume

A reaction swaps one set of bonds for another, and the enthalpy difference leaves as heat. Run it in an open beaker at constant pressure and the measured heat is Qp = ΔHrxn. Run it in a sealed bomb at constant volume and the measured heat is Qv = ΔUrxn.

ΔH = ΔU + Δng R T

Δng is moles of gaseous products minus moles of gaseous reactants; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is in K. Only gases are counted.

The standard heat of formation ΔH°f is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states, at 1 bar and a stated temperature, almost always 298.15 K. Elements in their standard state have ΔH°f = 0 by definition: O₂ gas, N₂ gas, and carbon as graphite, not diamond.

ΔH°rxn = Σ ν ΔH°f(products) − Σ ν ΔH°f(reactants)

ν are the stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation.

The heat of combustion follows the same pattern: 1 mol of fuel burns completely to CO₂ gas and liquid water. It is easy to measure, so tables are full of it.

Hess's law: enthalpy is a state function, so a reaction run in several steps releases the same total heat as one step. Add the ΔH of the steps, flipping the sign of any step you run backwards.

Kirchhoff's law: heats of reaction at other temperatures

Heats of reaction are tabulated at 298 K, but plants run hotter. Kirchhoff's law carries ΔH from one temperature to another.

ΔCp = Σ ν Cp(products) − Σ ν Cp(reactants)

ΔCp is the heat capacity change per mole of reaction (kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), using the same ν as the balanced equation.

ΔHT2 = ΔHT1 + ΔCp (T2 − T1)

valid when ΔCp is constant; otherwise use ΔHT2 = ΔHT1 + ∫ΔCp dT from T1 to T2.

The reason: products and reactants warm by different amounts, so the enthalpy gap between them drifts with temperature. Kirchhoff's law is sensible heat applied to that gap.

Real fluids need real data: enthalpies along the ice-to-steam path come from steam tables, not constant cp, and gases far from ideal need a residual-enthalpy term, which comes later with property relations.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of ice at −10 °C is heated at 1 atm until it becomes steam at 120 °C. Take cp,ice = 2.09 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Δhfus = 334 kJ/kg, cp,water = 4.18 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Δhvap = 2260 kJ/kg, cp,steam = 2.01 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find the total heat supplied and the percentage of it that is latent heat.

  1. warm the ice from −10 °C to 0 °C: Q1 = m cp ΔT = 1 × 2.09 × 10 = 20.9 kJ.
  2. melt the ice at 0 °C: Q2 = m Δhfus = 1 × 334 = 334 kJ. Temperature stays at 0 °C.
  3. warm the liquid water from 0 °C to 100 °C: Q3 = 1 × 4.18 × 100 = 418 kJ.
  4. boil the water at 100 °C: Q4 = 1 × 2260 = 2260 kJ. Temperature stays at 100 °C.
  5. superheat the steam from 100 °C to 120 °C: Q5 = 1 × 2.01 × 20 = 40.2 kJ.
  6. add the five parts: Qtotal = 20.9 + 334 + 418 + 2260 + 40.2 = 3073.1 kJ.
  7. latent part = Q2 + Q4 = 334 + 2260 = 2594 kJ. Percentage = 2594 ÷ 3073.1 × 100 = 84.4 %.

Answer. The total heat is 3073.1 kJ per kg, of which 2594 kJ (84.4 %) is latent heat.

Worked example 2

Question. For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) → 2NH₃(g), find ΔH°298 and ΔU°298, then ΔH at 700 K by Kirchhoff's law. Data: ΔH°f(NH₃, g) = −46.11 kJ/mol; Cp (J/(mol·K), taken constant): NH₃ 35.06, N₂ 29.12, H₂ 28.82.

  1. ΔH°298 = 2(−46.11) − [0 + 3(0)] = −92.22 kJ per mole of reaction (elements have ΔH°f = 0).
  2. Gas moles change by Δng = 2 − 4 = −2, so ΔU°298 = ΔH − ΔngRT = −92.22 − (−2)(8.314 × 10−3)(298.15) = −92.22 + 4.96 = −87.26 kJ.
  3. ΔCp = 2(35.06) − [29.12 + 3(28.82)] = 70.12 − 115.58 = −45.46 J/K.
  4. Kirchhoff: ΔH700 = ΔH298 + ΔCp(700 − 298.15) = −92.22 + (−45.46 × 10−3)(401.85) = −92.22 − 18.27 = −110.49 kJ.
  5. Sign check: ΔCp < 0, so the reaction becomes more exothermic on heating, as found. The Cp of NH₃ rises faster with T than those of N₂ and H₂, so |ΔCp| shrinks on heating and a polynomial Cp gives a smaller shift than this constant-Cp estimate.

Exam traps

  • Adding latent heat at the wrong temperature: fusion belongs at 0 °C and vaporization at 100 °C only at 1 atm. At 2 atm the boiling term sits near 120 °C with a smaller Δhvap.
  • Forgetting the sensible heat on one side of a phase change — the ice warming to 0 °C, or the steam superheating after 100 °C.
  • Sign of ΔH: exothermic is negative, endothermic is positive. Write the sign in every line, not just the last one.
  • Using Qp = ΔH when the vessel is sealed: at constant volume the measured heat is ΔU, and ΔH = ΔU + Δng RT with T in kelvin and only gases counted in Δng.

Takeaways

  1. A pure fluid in one phase is fixed by two intensive variables, on a phase boundary by one, and at the triple point by none; the phase diagram shows which case applies.
  2. The ideal gas law is a limiting law at low pressure and high temperature; it fails whenever molecules are close together.
  3. Each ideal-gas path (isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric, isobaric, polytropic) has its own work and heat relations; state the sign convention before using them.
  4. The virial equation is a systematic correction that gets better as more coefficients are added; the van der Waals equation is a simple closed-form model that predicts a critical point.
  5. The compressibility factor Z can be read from a generalized chart or computed from the Pitzer virial correlation or a generalized cubic, using only Tr, Pr and ω.
  6. Real-gas behaviour is well organized: attraction makes Z < 1 at moderate pressure, size makes Z > 1 at high pressure, and fluids with the same ω sit on a common curve.
  7. Heat effects come in three kinds — sensible, latent and chemical — and each has its own calculation route.
  8. Kirchhoff's equation is what lets you take a table value at 298 K and use it at your reactor temperature.

Sketch: three curves leaving Z = 1 at P = 0, the ideal flat, the virial a straight falling line, the van der Waals curve falling with it and then bending up.
  8. Which to trust. Ideal gas: only near P = 0. Truncated virial: to moderate density (Vr above about 2, a few tens of bar for CO₂ at 300 K); it already differs by 0.03 at 40 bar. van der Waals: qualitatively right at all densities, but below Tc it loops through the two-phase region and needs the Maxwell construction, and its numbers are poor near the critical point.
Evaluate A pipeline carries natural gas, taken as pure methane, at 150 bar and 300 K (Tc = 190.6 K, Pc = 45.99 bar, ω = 0.012). Compare the ideal gas, van der Waals and generalized (Pitzer and cubic) routes for the molar volume used to size the pipe, and recommend one for plant design, giving reasons. 14 marks

Points to cover. Tr = 1.574, Pr = 3.262: high reduced pressure; Ideal gas V = RT/P = 0.1663 L/mol; van der Waals Z = 0.785; SRK 0.851; Peng–Robinson 0.805; Pitzer virial is outside its range (Vr about 1.4); The ideal gas overstates the volume by 17 to 27 %; Recommend Peng–Robinson or SRK, checked against measured data; cost and safety of a wrong volume.

Model answer.

  1. Reduced state.
    Tr = 300/190.6 = 1.574,   Pr = 150/45.99 = 3.262

Evaluate The standard heat of a reaction is tabulated at 298.15 K. Explain how you would find the adiabatic reaction temperature of an industrial reactor operating at 700 K, and discuss which heat effects you must include. 13 marks

Points to cover. Use Kirchhoff's equation to shift ΔH°rxn to 700 K; Energy balance: heat released by reaction = sensible heat to raise products to Tad; Include any latent heats if a phase change occurs; Include excess reactants and inerts in the Cp sum; Iterate on Tad since Cp values depend on temperature; Discuss why actual reactor temperature is lower — heat losses, incomplete conversion, side reactions.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. The adiabatic reaction temperature is the temperature the products reach when the reaction runs with no heat loss and no work: all the heat released stays in the product stream. It is the highest temperature the reactor can reach and sets materials, catalyst life and whether cooling is needed.
  2. Use the state-function path. Enthalpy does not depend on the path, so split the real process (feed at Tin → products at Tad) into (1) the reaction at the feed temperature, then (2) heating all products, inerts and leftover reactants from Tin to Tad. The energy balance is ΔH1 + ΔH2 = 0.
  3. Shift ΔH°rxn from 298.15 K to the feed temperature with Kirchhoff's law. Ammonia synthesis, N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, feed at 700 K (Cp taken constant: NH₃ 35.06, N₂ 29.12, H₂ 28.82 J/(mol·K)):
    ΔH°298 = 2(−46.11) = −92.22 kJ
ΔCp = 2(35.06) − 29.12 − 3(28.82) = −45.46 J/K
ΔH700 = −92.22 + (−0.04546)(700 − 298.15) = −110.49 kJ per mole of reaction
  4. Energy balance. Basis: 1 mol N₂ + 3 mol H₂ fed at 700 K, 15 % of the N₂ converted (extent ξ = 0.15 mol).
    heat released  = ξ(−ΔH700) = 0.15 × 110.49 = 16.57 kJ
products       = 0.85 N₂ + 2.55 H₂ + 0.30 NH₃
ΣnCp          = 0.85(29.12) + 2.55(28.82) + 0.30(35.06) = 108.76 J/K
Tad − 700 = 16 573 / 108.76 = 152.4 K   →  Tad = 852 K
  5. Heat effects to include. (1) The heat of reaction at the right temperature (Kirchhoff). (2) Sensible heat of every species leaving: products, unconverted reactants and inerts, since inerts and excess reactants absorb heat and lower Tad. (3) Latent heat if any species condenses or evaporates on the way (water in a combustor leaves as vapour, so the lower heating value applies). (4) Side reactions with their own heats. (5) The extent actually reached: equilibrium limits conversion, and for an exothermic reaction the limit falls as the temperature rises.
  6. Iteration. Cp rises with temperature, so with polynomial Cp data the sensible-heat integral is nonlinear in Tad: guess Tad, evaluate the mean Cp between Tin and the guess, solve for a new Tad, and repeat until it stops changing. If conversion depends on temperature, the energy balance and the equilibrium relation are solved together.
  7. Why the real reactor runs cooler. Heat leaks through walls, conversion is incomplete or limited by equilibrium, inerts dilute the feed, and endothermic side reactions take heat. The adiabatic value is therefore an upper bound used for safety and design, while the actual outlet temperature comes from the full balance with losses.
Analyze Relate the shape of a van der Waals P-V isotherm below Tc to the phase behaviour of a real fluid. Explain the Maxwell equal-area construction and why the experimental path is flat. 13 marks

Points to cover. Below Tc the van der Waals isotherm has a minimum and a maximum (a loop); Between them (∂P/∂V)T > 0: unstable; between the spinodal and the saturation points: metastable; Maxwell: horizontal line at Psat with equal areas above and below; CO₂ at 280 K: Psat = 52.83 bar, Vl = 0.0809, Vg = 0.2655 L/mol; Real fluid: liquid and vapour coexist at constant P while V changes; Above Tc the loop vanishes; at Tc it shrinks to the inflection point.

Model answer.

  1. Shape below Tc. Below the critical temperature a van der Waals isotherm, followed from large V to small V, rises, reaches a local maximum, falls to a local minimum, then rises steeply on the liquid side: an S-shaped loop. Between the maximum and the minimum (∂P/∂V)T > 0, so squeezing the fluid would lower its pressure; such states are mechanically unstable and never observed.
  2. Real behaviour. A real fluid compressed at constant T condenses at a single pressure, the vapour pressure: the isotherm is flat between the saturated-vapour volume Vg and the saturated-liquid volume Vl, and the system is a mixture of the two. The loop parts between the saturation points and the extrema are metastable: supersaturated vapour and superheated liquid, which can exist briefly in very clean systems (cloud chambers, bumping in boiling).
  3. Maxwell equal-area construction. At equilibrium the two phases have equal Gibbs energy. Along an isotherm dG = V dP, so ∫V dP over the loop from Vl to Vg must be zero. That is the same as requiring the horizontal line at Psat to cut equal areas from the loop:
    ∫ P dV (from Vl to Vg, along the loop) = Psat(Vg − Vl)
van der Waals:  RT ln[(Vg − b)/(Vl − b)] + a(1/Vg − 1/Vl) = Psat(Vg − Vl)
with Psat = P(Vl) = P(Vg) from the equation itself
  4. Worked for CO₂ at 280 K (a = 3.640 L²·bar/mol², b = 0.04267 L/mol). Guess Psat between the loop's minimum and maximum, find the outer roots of the cubic, check the areas, and adjust until they match:
    loop: minimum 41.53 bar at 0.0951 L/mol, maximum 57.20 bar at 0.1852 L/mol
Psat = 52.83 bar:  Vl = 0.08087,  Vg = 0.26553 L/mol
left side  = 23.279 ln(0.22286/0.03820) + 3.640(3.7661 − 12.3659) = 9.7555 L·bar/mol
right side = 52.83 × (0.26553 − 0.08087) = 9.7555 L·bar/mol   ✓
  5. How good is it. van der Waals puts Psat of CO₂ at 280 K at 52.8 bar; the measured value is about 42 bar, so the equation overpredicts by roughly a quarter. Modern cubics (SRK, PR) with the ω-dependent α(Tr) are fitted to vapour pressures and do much better; the equal-area idea is the same and is how they compute VLE.
  6. Approach to the critical point. As T rises the maximum and minimum move together and the two-phase line shortens. At Tc (304.0 K for these constants) they merge into a single inflection with (∂P/∂V)T = 0 and (∂²P/∂V²)T = 0, at Vc = 3b = 0.128 L/mol and Pc = a/(27b²) = 74.0 bar: exactly the conditions used to find a and b. Above Tc the isotherm falls monotonically, there is one root at every pressure, and no phase split.

Glossary

Acentric factor (ω)
ω = −1.0 − log10(Prsat) at Tr = 0.7; zero for simple spherical molecules, 0.200 for n-butane.
Adiabatic reaction temperature
The final temperature reached when the heat of reaction is entirely absorbed by the products, with no heat loss.
Boyle temperature
The temperature at which the second virial coefficient is zero, so a gas stays nearly ideal over a range of low pressures; a/(Rb) for a van der Waals gas.
Critical pressure (Pc)
The vapour pressure at the critical temperature, the highest pressure at which liquid and vapour can coexist.
Critical temperature (Tc)
The temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone.
Cubic equation of state
An equation of state that is cubic in molar volume, such as the van der Waals, Redlich-Kwong or Peng-Robinson equation.
Degrees of freedom
The number of intensive variables that can be fixed independently; for a pure fluid F = 3 − π: two in one phase, one on a phase boundary, zero at the triple point.
Enthalpy (H)
H = U + PV, a state function convenient for constant-pressure processes.
Generalized compressibility chart
A graph of Z against Pr with lines of constant Tr, allowing Z to be read for any gas.
Heat capacity at constant pressure (Cp)
The heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin at constant pressure; Cp = (∂H/∂T)P.
Heat capacity at constant volume (Cv)
The heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin at constant volume; Cv = (∂U/∂T)V.
Intensive variable
A property that does not depend on the amount of material, such as P or T.
Internal energy (U)
The energy stored in the molecules of a system, excluding bulk kinetic and potential energy.
Isobaric process
A path at constant pressure; for an ideal gas Q = ΔH and the work done by the gas is W = PΔV.
Isochoric process
A path at constant volume; no P–V work is done, so W = 0 and Q = ΔU.
Isothermal compressibility (κ)
κ = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T, how much volume shrinks as pressure rises; about 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1 for liquid water at 25 °C.
Kay's rule
Pseudo-critical constants of a mixture as mole-fraction averages, Tpc = ΣyiTc,i and Ppc = ΣyiPc,i; rough when the components differ much.
Maxwell equal-area rule
The vapour pressure from a cubic equation below Tc is the horizontal line that cuts equal areas from the loop of the isotherm.
Pure fluid
A substance made of one chemical species, so its state needs only two independent intensive variables.
Residual property
The difference between a real-fluid property and its ideal-gas value at the same T and P; generalized correlations give it from Tr, Pr and ω.
Reversible path
A path through a series of equilibrium states; between the same two end states it gives the most work in an expansion and needs the least in a compression.
Saturation
The condition where two phases coexist at equilibrium at a given T and P.
Standard state
The pure substance at 1 bar and a stated temperature, usually 298.15 K, used as the reference for heats of formation.
Steam table
A tabulated set of properties of water and steam, organized by saturation pressure or temperature and by superheat.
Supercritical fluid
A fluid above both Tc and Pc, with properties between those of a liquid and a gas.
Virial coefficient
A coefficient in the virial series; the second virial coefficient B carries the correction for pairs of molecules interacting.
Volume expansivity (β)
β = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)P, how fast volume grows with temperature; about 2.6 × 10−4 K−1 for liquid water at 25 °C.
