2.1 PVT behaviour of pure fluids and phase diagrams

Water in a kettle, LPG in a cylinder, steam entering a turbine: each is a pure fluid whose state is fixed by pressure P, molar volume V and temperature T. Plot every equilibrium state and you get a surface. Its three flat views — P–V, P–T and T–V — are the phase diagrams. They tell you which phase exists, where two phases coexist, and where boiling stops being possible at all. Power plants, refineries and refrigerant selection all begin by reading these diagrams, and every equation of state is drawn on them.

You need: Unit 1 — system, state, intensive and extensive variables, and the meaning of a pure substance.

The P-V-T surface and its three views

A pure fluid has one chemical species. In equilibrium its state is fixed by any two of P (pressure, in Pa or bar), T (temperature, in K) and V (molar volume, in m³/mol). The third follows.

Plot all such states with P up, V to the right and T going back. The sheet you get is the P-V-T surface.

Cut the surface three ways. At constant T you get a P–V isotherm. At constant P you get a T–V isobar. At constant V you get a P–T isochore. Each cut is a flat drawing that loses one variable but keeps every phase boundary.

Each view answers a different question. P–V answers work questions, because work is ∫P dV. P–T answers stability questions: which phase exists. T–V answers density questions: how much the volume changes on heating.

For water at 1 bar the two ends of the two-phase region are v_f = 0.001043 m³/kg (saturated liquid) and v_g = 1.694 m³/kg (saturated vapour).

The saturation dome

Below the critical temperature a P–V isotherm has three parts. The left branch rises almost vertically: liquid hardly compresses. The right branch falls gently: vapour does compress. Between them the isotherm is horizontal at one pressure, the vapour pressure Psat(T).

The flat part is the two-phase region. Its left end is saturated liquid, the last liquid before a bubble of vapour appears. Its right end is saturated vapour, the last vapour before a drop of liquid appears. The horizontal length is v_g − v_f.

Repeat for every temperature below the critical temperature T_c. The left ends trace the saturated-liquid curve, the right ends the saturated-vapour curve. Together they close the saturation dome, also called the vapour dome. Inside the dome liquid and vapour coexist at the same P and T but at different V. A mixture of quality x has V = (1 − x)v_f + x·v_g, where x is the mass fraction of vapour and v_f and v_g are the saturated-liquid and saturated-vapour volumes.

The two curves meet at the critical point: for water, T_c = 374 °C and P_c = 221 bar. There saturated liquid and saturated vapour are identical, the latent heat is zero, and the meniscus between them disappears.

The P-T phase diagram and the phase rule

The P–T diagram is the most-used view. Three curves split the plane into solid, liquid and vapour regions. The sublimation curve separates solid from vapour. The fusion curve separates solid from liquid. The vaporisation curve separates liquid from vapour and ends at the critical point. All three meet at the triple point: for water, 0.01 °C and 0.00611 bar, where ice, liquid water and steam coexist.

For water the fusion curve leans left, so ice melts when the pressure rises. That is unusual; for most substances the fusion curve leans right.

Above the critical point the liquid and vapour regions merge. The fluid there is supercritical — neither liquid nor gas. No amount of pressure liquefies it while T stays above T_c. Supercritical carbon dioxide is used to strip caffeine from coffee beans, and dense supercritical water is studied as a reaction medium.

F = C − P_ph + 2 = 3 − P_ph

F is the number of intensive variables you may fix freely (degrees of freedom); C = 1 is the number of components; P_ph is the number of phases present. P on its own always means pressure, in bar or Pa.

One phase gives F = 2: choose T and P independently. Two phases give F = 1: choosing T fixes P = Psat(T). Three phases give F = 0: the triple point is one single state.

Mathematical representation: the equation of state

An equation of state is an algebraic relation among P, V and T for a fluid: f(P, V, T) = 0, or P = f(V, T). Every curve in the diagrams above is a solution of it. Some syllabi call this whole topic volumetric properties of pure fluids.

The simplest relation is the ideal gas law, PV = RT, where R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant. It holds when molecules are far apart; the next chapter develops it.

Real fluids need corrections. The compressibility factor Z = PV/RT measures the departure from ideal behaviour: Z = 1 for an ideal gas, Z < 1 when attraction between molecules pulls them together, and Z > 1 when their own size pushes them apart (high pressure). Virial equations add terms in 1/V. Cubic equations such as van der Waals carry two constants, a for attraction and b for molecular size, and can reproduce the dome and the critical point — see virial and cubic equations and compressibility factor.

Only a few universities go further and build the equation of state from the intermolecular potential energy u(r) between a pair of molecules. That step is statistical mechanics, not thermodynamics. Mixtures belong to Unit 6, fugacity to Unit 5, and property tables with the Clapeyron equation to Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. Water is held at 1 bar (100 kPa). (a) Name the phase at 25 °C, at 100 °C and at 120 °C. (b) Treat the vapour at 120 °C and 1 bar as ideal and estimate its specific volume. Data: R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), M = 18.015 g/mol.

  1. At 1 bar the saturation temperature of water is 99.6 °C, close enough to call 100 °C for this example. The normal boiling point, 100 °C, is at 1 atm = 1.01325 bar, slightly above 1 bar.
  2. 25 °C is below the saturation temperature. The fluid is a compressed (subcooled) liquid. One phase, F = 2.
  3. 100 °C equals the saturation temperature. Liquid and vapour coexist; any quality 0 ≤ x ≤ 1 is possible. Two phases, F = 1, so P alone fixes T.
  4. 120 °C is above the saturation temperature. The fluid is superheated vapour. One phase, F = 2.
  5. Specific gas constant: R_s = R/M = 8.314/0.018015 = 461.5 J/(kg·K).
  6. Absolute temperature: T = 120 + 273.15 = 393.15 K.
  7. Ideal gas law per unit mass: v = R_s·T/P = 461.5 × 393.15/100 000 = 1.8144 m³/kg.

Answer. Liquid at 25 °C, liquid–vapour at 100 °C and vapour at 120 °C; the ideal-gas specific volume at 120 °C and 1 bar is 1.8144 m³/kg.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the molar volume of water vapour at 1 bar and 120 °C, assuming ideal gas behaviour. Use R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).

  1. Convert to SI: P = 100 000 Pa, T = 120 + 273.15 = 393.15 K.
  2. Ideal gas law in molar form: V = RT/P.
  3. V = 8.314 × 393.15/100 000.
  4. 8.314 × 393.15 = 3268.65 J/mol.
  5. V = 3268.65/100 000 = 0.03269 m³/mol.

Answer. The molar volume is 0.03269 m³/mol, that is 32.69 L/mol.

Exam traps

  • Reading the saturation curve as a single phase. On a P–T diagram every point of the vaporisation curve is a two-phase state: saturated liquid and saturated vapour together. On a P–V diagram the two branches of the dome are saturated liquid and saturated vapour, and only points strictly inside the dome are two-phase mixtures.
  • Calling the critical point a boiling point. At the critical point liquid and vapour are one phase and the latent heat is zero.
  • Thinking a gas above T_c can be liquefied by pressure alone. It cannot; you must first cool it below T_c.
  • Putting °C into PV = RT or any ideal-gas formula. Always convert to K.
  • Mixing molar or specific volume v with total volume V_total = n·v. Phase diagrams use volume per mole or per kilogram.

2.2 Ideal gas and ideal-gas processes

The ideal-gas law, PV = nRT, is the simplest equation of state. It treats molecules as points with no attractions, so pressure, volume and temperature are tied by one equation. That makes every path calculable: heat, work and internal-energy change follow from the path and the gas. Engineers use it for a first estimate of compressor duty, pipeline pressure drop, or the air in a tyre. It is also the reference state against which real gases are measured. This chapter fixes the sign convention, lists the five common paths, and works one numerical example.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for P–V diagrams, and Unit 1 for the first law, enthalpy and heat capacities.

The ideal-gas law and its energy

The ideal-gas law is PV = nRT. P is absolute pressure, V is volume, n is amount in moles, R is the gas constant, and T is absolute temperature in kelvin. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. The second value matches litres and bar, so use it when V is in L and P in bar. Never put T in °C; 25 °C is 298.15 K, not 25.

For an ideal gas, molecules have no volume and no forces except elastic collisions. That model makes internal energy U a function of T only. Enthalpy H = U + PV is also a function of T only, because PV = nRT. So an isothermal change has ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0, even when P and V change. This is the key fact behind all paths below.

PV = nRT; Cp − Cv = R; γ = Cp/Cv

P is absolute pressure; V is volume; n is moles; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; Cp and Cv are molar heat capacities at constant pressure and volume; γ is the heat-capacity ratio.

Heat capacities follow from that. Cv = (∂U/∂T)V and Cp = (∂H/∂T)P. For an ideal gas Cp − Cv = R and γ = Cp/Cv. Cv is the heat needed to raise 1 mol by 1 K at constant volume; Cp is the same at constant pressure. For a diatomic gas near room temperature, Cv ≈ (5/2)R and Cp ≈ (7/2)R, so γ ≈ 1.4. Monatomic gases have γ ≈ 1.67.

Five paths and one sign convention

Use W as work done by the gas on the surroundings. Then the first law for a closed system is ΔU = Q − W. Q is heat added to the gas; W is positive for expansion and negative for compression. State this convention before any calculation.

ΔU = Q − W

ΔU is change in internal energy; Q is heat added to the gas; W is work done by the gas on the surroundings.

Each path fixes one quantity. Integrate W = ∫P dV along the path. The table gives closed-system results for an ideal gas with constant Cv and Cp; ΔT = T2 − T1. The work formulas are for reversible paths; for irreversible paths use the actual external pressure.

PathConstraintWork W by gasHeat QΔUΔH
IsochoricV fixed0nCvΔTnCvΔTnCpΔT
IsobaricP fixedPΔV = nRΔTnCpΔTnCvΔTnCpΔT
IsothermalT fixednRT ln(V2/V1)W00
AdiabaticQ = 0nCv(T1 − T2)0nCvΔTnCpΔT
PolytropicPVk fixed(P1V1 − P2V2)/(k − 1), k ≠ 1ΔU + WnCvΔTnCpΔT

An isochoric path has no volume change, so W = 0; all heat goes into U. An isobaric path expands against constant pressure, so W = PΔV and Q = ΔH. An isothermal path keeps U and H fixed; heat and work cancel. An adiabatic path has Q = 0; work comes from U, so expansion cools the gas. Also PVγ = constant, TVγ−1 = constant and TP(1−γ)/γ = constant. A polytropic path PVk = constant uses k, the polytropic exponent; it covers the range between isothermal (k = 1) and adiabatic (k = γ); for k = 1 use the isothermal formula.

Some syllabi call these non-flow processes. The flow version for nozzles and compressors is in Unit 1; do not mix the two.

Mixtures and the limit of the model

For a mixture of ideal gases, each component i obeys PiV = niRT, where Pi is its partial pressure and ni its moles. The total pressure is P = ΣPi = nRT/V, with n = Σni. This is Dalton's law in ideal-gas form. The mixture's U and H depend on T only, and its Cv and Cp are mole-fraction averages of the pure-component values. Treat this as a limiting case; real mixtures need fugacity.

The ideal-gas law is accurate at low pressure and high temperature, when molecules are far apart. At high pressure or near condensation, use the <a href="#chapter-2.4">compressibility factor or a <a href="#chapter-2.3">cubic equation of state. The simple paths in this chapter still give the limiting behaviour and a first estimate for compressor and pipeline calculations.

Worked example

Question. One mole of an ideal gas is at 300 K and 1.00 bar. Its volume is 24.94 L. It is taken to 12.47 L by three separate paths: (a) isothermal, (b) adiabatic with γ = 1.4, (c) isobaric. Find the final pressure for each path. Use R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Find the initial volume from PV = nRT: V1 = nRT1/P1 = (1 mol)(0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)(300 K)/(1.00 bar) = 24.94 L. This matches the given value.
  2. (a) Isothermal: T is constant, so P1V1 = P2V2. P2 = P1V1/V2 = (1.00 bar)(24.94 L)/(12.47 L) = 2.00 bar.
  3. (b) Adiabatic: PVγ = constant, so P2 = P1(V1/V2)γ. The volume ratio is 24.94/12.47 = 2.000. P2 = (1.00 bar)(2.000)1.4 = 2.64 bar.
  4. (c) Isobaric: P is fixed. P2 = P1 = 1.00 bar.
  5. Check the volume ratio and units: L cancels in the ratio; pressure stays in bar. The adiabatic final pressure is higher than the isothermal one because compression work raises the gas temperature above 300 K (T₂ ≈ 396 K here). At the same smaller volume, a higher T gives a higher P; in the isothermal path T stays at 300 K.

Answer. Final pressures: (a) 2.00 bar, (b) 2.64 bar, (c) 1.00 bar.

Exam traps

  • PVγ = constant is for adiabatic, not isothermal. Isothermal is PV = constant; using γ there gives a wrong final pressure.
  • Put T in kelvin. 27 °C is 300 K; using 27 in PV = nRT is a large error.
  • State the W sign convention. Many engineering texts take W by gas positive (ΔU = Q − W); many chemistry texts take W on gas positive (ΔU = Q + W). Mixing them flips Q.
  • For a rigid tank use Cv, not Cp. Q = nCvΔT for isochoric; Q = nCpΔT for isobaric.
  • Adiabatic does not mean isothermal. Q = 0, but T changes and W = nCv(T1 − T2) is not zero.

2.3 Virial and cubic equations of state (van der Waals)

Real gases deviate from PV = nRT because molecules occupy space and attract one another. The virial equation writes the deviation as a series in 1/V or in P. Cubic equations such as van der Waals add two physical corrections: a finite molecular volume b and an attraction term a/V². Engineers use these equations to estimate pressure, volume, density and compressor duty when the ideal gas law is too rough. In Indian plants, such estimates size gas lines, storage vessels and process equipment before detailed simulation.

You need: the ideal gas law from Chapter 2.2 and critical constants Tc and Pc from Chapter 2.1.

Compressibility factor and the virial series

Start with the compressibility factor Z = PV/(RT). Z is dimensionless. For an ideal gas Z = 1 at every pressure. For a real gas Z departs from 1 because molecules attract and occupy volume. The virial equation is an exact infinite series for that departure:

Z = 1 + B/V + C/V² + ... = 1 + B'P + C'P² + ...

Z is PV/(RT); V is molar volume in L/mol; P is pressure in bar; B is the second virial coefficient in L/mol; C is the third virial coefficient in L²/mol²; B' and C' are pressure-series coefficients with units 1/bar and 1/bar².

B comes from pair interactions, C from three-body interactions, and so on. B depends strongly on temperature. At low temperature attraction dominates and B is negative; at high temperature B is positive. At moderate pressure the series can be cut after B: Z ≈ 1 + B/V or Z ≈ 1 + B'P. This truncated virial equation is used for gas metering and moderate-pressure density estimates. It is not for dense liquids. Some syllabi call this the mathematical representation of PVT behaviour.

van der Waals cubic equation

The van der Waals equation replaces the ideal-gas pressure RT/V with two terms:

P = RT/(V − b) − a/V²

P is pressure in bar; V is molar volume in L/mol; T is temperature in K; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when V is in m³/mol and P is in Pa, or R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ when V is in L/mol and P is in bar; b is the excluded volume per mole in L/mol; a is the attraction parameter in L² bar mol⁻².

RT/(V − b) is the pressure of hard molecules of finite size. b is not the molecule's own volume; it is the excluded volume per mole, roughly four times the molecular volume. The term −a/V² lowers the pressure because attraction pulls molecules together and reduces the force on the wall. As V becomes large, b and a/V² become small, and the equation tends to PV = RT.

At the critical point the isotherm has a horizontal inflection. At T = Tc, V = Vc, P = Pc, the conditions are (∂P/∂V) = 0 and (∂²P/∂V²) = 0. Solving these two conditions gives the constants:

a = 27R²Tc²/(64Pc), b = RTc/(8Pc), Zc = PcVc/(RTc) = 3/8 = 0.375

Tc is critical temperature in K; Pc is critical pressure in bar; Vc is critical molar volume in L/mol; Zc is critical compressibility factor.

Real fluids have Zc near 0.23 to 0.29, so van der Waals overpredicts Zc. It is poor near the critical point and for dense liquids. Use it for simple gases at moderate conditions, not as a universal liquid equation.

Reduced equation and corresponding states

Put P = PrPc, V = VrVc and T = TrTc into the van der Waals equation. The constants a and b cancel. The result is the reduced van der Waals equation:

(Pr + 3/Vr²)(3Vr − 1) = 8Tr

Pr = P/Pc, Vr = V/Vc and Tr = T/Tc are reduced pressure, volume and temperature; all are dimensionless.

This equation contains no gas-specific constant. It says that two fluids at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure have the same reduced volume, and hence the same Z. That is the law of corresponding states. It is the first generalized equation of state. Real fluids follow it only approximately; Chapter 2.4 gives generalized charts that correct it.

The van der Waals equation is the simplest cubic. Better cubics keep the same cubic structure but change the attraction term. Redlich-Kwong uses a/(√T · V(V + b)): a divided by (√T times V times (V + b)). In RK, the constant a carries T0.5 in its units. Soave-Redlich-Kwong makes a depend on temperature. Peng-Robinson uses a(T)/[V² + 2bV − b²]. These are used in process simulators for vapour-liquid equilibrium and supercritical gases. Gas mixtures need mixing rules for a and b; see Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. For 1 mol of CO2 at 300 K and V = 1.00 L, use R = 0.08314 L bar mol−1 K−1, a = 3.640 L² bar mol−2 and b = 0.04267 L mol−1. Find P from the ideal gas law and from the van der Waals equation.

  1. Write the data: T = 300 K, V = 1.00 L/mol, R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, a = 3.640 L² bar mol⁻², b = 0.04267 L mol⁻¹.
  2. Ideal gas: P = RT/V = (0.08314)(300)/(1.00) = 24.942 bar.
  3. van der Waals: first compute V − b = 1.00 − 0.04267 = 0.95733 L/mol.
  4. Repulsion term: RT/(V − b) = 24.942/0.95733 = 26.054 bar.
  5. Attraction term: a/V² = 3.640/(1.00)² = 3.640 bar.
  6. Subtract: P = 26.054 − 3.640 = 22.414 bar, which rounds to 22.41 bar.

Answer. The ideal gas law gives 24.94 bar and the van der Waals equation gives 22.41 bar.

Exam traps

  • Putting V instead of (V − b) in the repulsion term; b must be subtracted from molar volume before dividing.
  • Mixing units: R = 0.08314 needs V in L/mol and P in bar, while R = 8.314 needs V in m³/mol and P in Pa. Convert before substituting.
  • Using a and b of one gas with P, V, T data of another gas. a and b are gas-specific.
  • Treating b as the molecule's own volume; it is the excluded volume per mole, about four times the molecular volume.
  • Forcing van der Waals near the critical point or for a dense liquid; it is poor there. Use a better cubic or generalized correlation.

Modern work adds association terms for water, alcohols and CO2-rich mixtures, and uses mixing rules for mixtures. 2.4 Compressibility factor and generalized correlations

Real gases do not obey PV = RT, especially near the critical point and at high pressure. The compressibility factor Z = PV/RT measures the departure: Z = 1 means ideal, and Z away from 1 means real. Instead of a separate table for every gas, generalized correlations use reduced temperature and pressure, and sometimes the acentric factor, to read Z from one chart. Chemical engineers use this for quick volume estimates, compressor work, pipeline sizing, and checking cubic equations of state. It is a first estimate, not a substitute for a good equation of state.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for equations of state and critical constants, and Chapter 2.2 for the ideal gas law.

What Z measures

For one mole, an ideal gas obeys PV = RT. Define the compressibility factor Z = PV/RT. Here P is pressure, V is molar volume, R is the gas constant, and T is absolute temperature. Z is dimensionless. An ideal gas has Z = 1 at every T and P. A real gas has Z below or above 1.

Z below 1 means attractive forces dominate, so the volume is smaller than ideal. Z above 1 means molecular size and repulsion dominate, so the volume is larger than ideal. At very low pressure every gas approaches Z = 1. Near the critical point Z can be far from 1. Z is a function of T and P for each gas, not a constant.

V = ZRT/P

V is molar volume, Z is the compressibility factor, R is the gas constant, T is absolute temperature, and P is pressure.

  • Reduced temperature: Tr = T/Tc, where Tc is the critical temperature.
  • Reduced pressure: Pr = P/Pc, where Pc is the critical pressure.
  • Reduced volume: Vr = V/Vc, where Vc is the critical molar volume.
  • Tr, Pr and Vr are dimensionless.

Corresponding states and the acentric factor

The principle of corresponding states says that fluids at the same Tr and Pr have approximately the same Z. This is why one generalized compressibility chart can serve many gases. It is a two-parameter correlation: Tc and Pc fix the reduced coordinates.

Simple spherical molecules follow this rule well. Polar molecules and long molecules need a third parameter. The Pitzer acentric factor ω measures the vapour-pressure curve shape and is tabulated with Tc and Pc. The three-parameter correlation is Z = Z⁰ + ωZ¹. Read Z⁰ and Z¹ from tables or charts at the given Tr and Pr.

Z = Z⁰ + ωZ¹

Z⁰ is the simple-fluid compressibility factor, Z¹ is its correction, and ω is the Pitzer acentric factor; both Z⁰ and Z¹ depend on Tr and Pr.

Some syllabi call this the generalized compressibility factor correlation. For nonpolar gases it is accurate enough for quick design checks. For strongly polar or associating gases, use an equation of state with better mixing rules.

Reading a chart and using generalized equations

  1. Look up Tc and Pc for the gas.
  2. Compute Tr = T/Tc and Pr = P/Pc.
  3. Find the curve for Tr and move to the value Pr on the horizontal axis.
  4. Read Z from the vertical axis.
  5. Compute V = ZRT/P, keeping R consistent with the pressure and volume units.

A generalized equation of state is an equation written in Tr, Pr and ω so that it returns Z directly. A common Pitzer-type form is Z = 1 + (B⁰ + ωB¹)Pr/Tr, where B⁰ and B¹ are tabulated functions of Tr. Charts are graphical versions of such correlations.

Tpc = Σ yiTc,i,  Ppc = Σ yiPc,i

For a gas mixture, yi is the mole fraction and Tc,i, Pc,i are component critical constants; use Tpc and Ppc as pseudo critical values on the same chart.

Worked example

Question. A gas has Tc = 300 K and Pc = 50 bar. At T = 360 K and P = 100 bar, a generalized chart gives Z = 0.65. Find Tr, Pr and the molar volume using R = 0.08314 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Tr = T/Tc = 360 K / 300 K = 1.20.
  2. Pr = P/Pc = 100 bar / 50 bar = 2.00.
  3. V = ZRT/P = 0.65 × 0.08314 × 360 / 100.
  4. 0.65 × 0.08314 = 0.054041 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  5. 0.054041 × 360 = 19.455 L bar mol⁻¹.
  6. 19.455 / 100 bar = 0.19455 L mol⁻¹.

Answer. Tr = 1.2, Pr = 2.0, and V = 0.195 L/mol.

Exam traps

  • Using T and P directly on the chart without first computing Tr and Pr.
  • Assuming Z = 1 at high pressure because the gas is called permanent; high pressure usually increases deviation from ideal behaviour.
  • Mixing units: T must be absolute, P and Pc must use the same unit, and R must match the chosen pressure and volume units.
  • Treating a chart reading as exact; generalized charts are approximate, so do not report more digits than the chart supports.
  • Using the two-parameter chart for a polar gas when the acentric factor or a better equation of state is needed.

2.5 Heat effects: sensible heat, latent heat, heat of reaction

One kilogram block of ice left in a warm room takes in heat. It warms, then sits at 0 °C while it melts, then warms again, then sits at 100 °C while it boils. The same heat does three different jobs: raising temperature, pulling a phase apart, and — when a reaction runs — rearranging chemical bonds. Boiler design, refrigeration and every energy balance around a reactor need these three quantities kept separate. Add them wrongly and a plant's heat demand comes out wrong by a factor of five.

You need enthalpy and the first law from Unit 1, and Cp from Chapter 2.2.

Sensible heat moves the thermometer

Sensible heat is heat that changes the temperature of a substance. No phase change, no reaction. A thermometer shows the change, so the heat is called sensible.

Q = ∫ n Cp dT

Q is heat added (kJ); n is amount of substance (mol); Cp is molar heat capacity at constant pressure (kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹); T is absolute temperature (K).

When Cp is nearly constant over the range, the integral collapses to Q = n Cp (T₂ − T₁). Working in kilograms gives Q = m cp ΔT, where m is mass (kg), cp is specific heat capacity (kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), and ΔT is the temperature change (K or °C — the size is the same).

Substancecp (kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
Water (liquid)4.18
Ice2.09
Steam2.01
Aluminium0.90

Cp of a real gas climbs with temperature, so one number is only an average over the band. Use a mean Cp for the range, or fit Cp = a + bT + cT² and integrate term by term; Unit 4 shows where those fits come from.

Sign rule: Q is positive when heat enters the system. A 1 kg aluminium pressure cooker heated from 30 °C to 120 °C takes in 1 × 0.90 × 90 = 81 kJ before the food inside even warms.

Latent heat moves the molecules apart

Latent heat is heat that changes the phase of a substance at constant temperature and constant pressure. Nothing warms; the attraction between molecules gives way.

Q = m Δh

m is mass (kg); Δh is the specific latent heat (kJ kg⁻¹): Δh_fus for melting, Δh_vap for boiling, Δh_sub for solid to vapour.

At 1 atm, for water, Δh_fus = 334 kJ/kg at 0 °C and Δh_vap = 2260 kJ/kg at 100 °C. Each pair belongs to its own pressure; at another pressure both the value and the transition temperature change.

The 2260 kJ/kg does two jobs. Roughly 170 kJ/kg pushes the atmosphere aside as the vapour expands — that is the PΔV part of ΔH = ΔU + PΔV. The rest raises internal energy by breaking hydrogen bonds. So a steam burn at 100 °C is far worse than a water burn at 100 °C.

Heat of reaction: constant pressure and constant volume

A reaction swaps one set of bonds for another, and the enthalpy difference leaves as heat. Run it in an open beaker at constant pressure and the measured heat is Qp = ΔH_rxn. Run it in a sealed bomb at constant volume and the measured heat is Qv = ΔU_rxn. Some syllabi call all of this heat effects accompanying chemical reactions.

ΔH = ΔU + Δn_g R T

Δn_g is moles of gaseous products minus moles of gaseous reactants; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is in K. Only gases are counted.

The standard heat of formation ΔH°f is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states at 1 bar. Elements in their standard state have ΔH°f = 0 by definition: O₂ gas, N₂ gas, and carbon as graphite, not diamond.

ΔH°rxn = Σ ν ΔH°f(products) − Σ ν ΔH°f(reactants)

ν are the stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation.

The heat of combustion follows the same pattern for burning: 1 mol of fuel burns completely in oxygen to CO₂ gas and liquid water. It is easier to measure than formation, so data tables are full of it.

Hess's law: enthalpy is a state function, so a reaction run in several steps releases the same total heat as one step. Add the ΔH of the steps, flipping the sign of any step you run backwards.

Kirchhoff's law: heats of reaction at other temperatures

Heats of reaction are tabulated at 298 K, but plants run hotter. Kirchhoff's law carries ΔH from one temperature to another.

ΔCp = Σ ν Cp(products) − Σ ν Cp(reactants)

ΔCp is the heat capacity change per mole of reaction (kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), using the same ν as the balanced equation.

ΔH_T2 = ΔH_T1 + ΔCp (T2 − T1)

valid when ΔCp is constant; otherwise use ΔH_T2 = ΔH_T1 + ∫ΔCp dT from T1 to T2.

The physical reason is simple. Products and reactants warm up by different amounts, so the enthalpy gap between them drifts with temperature. Kirchhoff's law is just sensible heat applied to that gap.

Real fluids need real data: enthalpies along the ice-to-steam path come from steam tables, not constant cp. See Unit 4 for property tables, and add a residual term for gases far from ideal behaviour.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of ice at −10 °C is heated at 1 atm until it becomes steam at 120 °C. Take cp,ice = 2.09 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Δh_fus = 334 kJ/kg, cp,water = 4.18 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, Δh_vap = 2260 kJ/kg, cp,steam = 2.01 kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Find the total heat supplied and the percentage of it that is latent heat.

  1. Step 1 — warm the ice from −10 °C to 0 °C: Q1 = m cp ΔT = 1 × 2.09 × 10 = 20.9 kJ.
  2. Step 2 — melt the ice at 0 °C: Q2 = m Δh_fus = 1 × 334 = 334 kJ. Temperature stays at 0 °C.
  3. Step 3 — warm the liquid water from 0 °C to 100 °C: Q3 = 1 × 4.18 × 100 = 418 kJ.
  4. Step 4 — boil the water at 100 °C: Q4 = 1 × 2260 = 2260 kJ. Temperature stays at 100 °C.
  5. Step 5 — superheat the steam from 100 °C to 120 °C: Q5 = 1 × 2.01 × 20 = 40.2 kJ.
  6. Step 6 — add the five parts: Q_total = 20.9 + 334 + 418 + 2260 + 40.2 = 3073.1 kJ.
  7. Step 7 — latent part = Q2 + Q4 = 334 + 2260 = 2594 kJ. Percentage = 2594 ÷ 3073.1 × 100 = 84.4 %.

Answer. The total heat is 3073.1 kJ per kg, of which 2594 kJ (84.4 %) is latent heat.

Exam traps

  • Adding latent heat at the wrong temperature: fusion belongs at 0 °C and vaporization at 100 °C only at 1 atm. At 2 atm the boiling term sits near 120 °C with a smaller Δh_vap.
  • Forgetting the sensible heat on one side of a phase change — the ice warming to 0 °C, or the steam superheating after 100 °C.
  • Mixing the molar and mass formulas: n Cp ΔT needs moles, m cp ΔT needs kilograms. Check units before you add the numbers.
  • Sign of ΔH: exothermic is negative, endothermic is positive. Write the sign in every line, not just the last one.
  • Using Qp = ΔH when the vessel is sealed: at constant volume the measured heat is ΔU, and ΔH = ΔU + Δn_g RT with T in kelvin and only gases counted in Δn_g.

Takeaways

  1. A pure fluid's state is fixed by two intensive variables once the phase is known, and the phase diagram shows which phase wins.
  2. The ideal gas law is a limiting law at low pressure and high temperature; it fails whenever molecules are close together.
  3. Each ideal-gas path (isothermal, adiabatic, isochoric, isobaric) has its own fixed work and heat relations that you should be able to write from memory.
  4. The virial equation is a systematic correction that gets better as more coefficients are added; the van der Waals equation is a simple closed-form model that predicts a critical point.
  5. The compressibility factor Z turns an unknown real gas into a number you can read from a generalized chart using Tr and Pr.
  6. Real-gas behaviour is well organized: attraction makes Z < 1, size makes Z > 1, and most fluids sit near a common curve.
  7. Heat effects come in three kinds — sensible, latent and chemical — and each has its own calculation route.
  8. Kirchhoff's equation is what lets you take a table value at 298 K and use it at your reactor temperature.

Glossary

Adiabatic reaction temperature
The final temperature reached when the heat of reaction is entirely absorbed by the products, with no heat loss.
Critical pressure (Pc)
The pressure needed to liquefy a fluid at its critical temperature.
Critical temperature (Tc)
The temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone.
Cubic equation of state
An equation of state that is cubic in molar volume, such as the van der Waals, Redlich-Kwong or Peng-Robinson equation.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent intensive variables that must be fixed to describe a system; for a pure fluid, one on a phase boundary and two in a single-phase region.
Enthalpy (H)
H = U + PV, a state function convenient for constant-pressure processes.
Generalized compressibility chart
A graph of Z against Pr with lines of constant Tr, allowing Z to be read for any gas.
Heat capacity at constant pressure (Cp)
The heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin at constant pressure; Cp = (∂H/∂T)P.
Heat capacity at constant volume (Cv)
The heat needed to raise one mole by one kelvin at constant volume; Cv = (∂U/∂T)V.
Intensive variable
A property that does not depend on the amount of material, such as P or T.
Internal energy (U)
The energy stored in the molecules of a system, excluding bulk kinetic and potential energy.
Isobaric process
A path at constant pressure; for an ideal gas q = ΔH and w = −P ΔV.
Isochoric process
A path at constant volume; no P-V work is done, so w = 0 and q = ΔU.
Isothermal compressibility
κ = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T, which measures how much volume shrinks as pressure rises.
Pure fluid
A substance made of one chemical species, so its state needs only two independent intensive variables.
Residual property
The difference between a real-fluid property and the ideal-gas value at the same T and P, used to apply generalized charts.
Reversible path
A path taken through a series of equilibrium states, giving the maximum work obtainable.
Saturation
The condition where two phases coexist at equilibrium at a given T and P.
Standard state
The pure substance at 1 bar and a stated temperature, usually 298.15 K, used as the reference for heats of formation.
Steam table
A tabulated set of properties of water and steam, organized by saturation pressure or temperature and by superheat.
Supercritical fluid
A fluid above both Tc and Pc, with properties between those of a liquid and a gas.
Thermal expansion coefficient
α = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)P, which measures how fast volume grows with temperature.
Virial coefficient
A coefficient in the virial series; the second virial coefficient B carries the correction for pairs of molecules interacting.
