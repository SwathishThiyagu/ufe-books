5.1 Chemical potential and phase equilibrium

Chemical potential μ is the partial molar Gibbs energy of a species. It measures how strongly that species tends to leave a phase. At equilibrium, every species has the same μ in every phase present. If μ differs, matter moves from the phase with higher μ to the phase with lower μ, lowering the total Gibbs energy G at fixed temperature T and pressure P. This one rule explains evaporation, absorption, distillation, and many separations in chemical engineering. It is the foundation for fugacity, activity, and phase-equilibrium calculations.

You need the fundamental property relation dG = −S dT + V dP (from thermodynamic property relations) and the difference between intensive and extensive variables (from basic concepts and the first law).

Definition for a mixture

For a mixture, Gibbs energy G depends on temperature T, pressure P, and the number of moles ni of every species i. Chemical potential μi is the rate at which G changes, per mole of i, when a very small amount of i is added with T, P and all other nj held constant.

μi = (∂G/∂ni) at fixed T, P, and nj

μi is the chemical potential of species i in J/mol or kJ/mol; G is total Gibbs energy in J or kJ; ni is moles of i; nj means moles of every species except i.

For a pure substance, μi = G/ni, the molar Gibbs energy. In a mixture, μi also depends on composition. Some syllabi simply call μi the partial molar Gibbs energy. Read it as a measure of how strongly species i tends to leave its phase, not of how much i is present. Chapter 5.2 develops partial molar properties in more detail.

The fundamental equation with changing moles

For a closed system of fixed amount and composition, the fundamental property relation is dG = −S dT + V dP. When moles can change, add one term for each species.

dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μi dni

dG is the small change in total Gibbs energy; S is total entropy; T is absolute temperature; V is total volume; P is pressure; μi is chemical potential of species i; dni is the small change in moles of i; Σ means sum over all species.

You can derive this by writing G as a function of T, P, n1, n2, ... and taking the total differential. The T derivative is −S. The P derivative is V. The ni derivative defines μi. At fixed T and P, dG = Σ μi dni. This is the working equation for mass transfer between phases. The μi are not independent at fixed T and P; the Gibbs-Duhem equation in Chapter 5.3 links their changes.

Phase equilibrium and spontaneous transfer

Take two phases α and β. For each species i, equilibrium requires μiα = μiβ. This holds for a pure component and for every component in a mixture. Temperature equality stops heat flow. Pressure equality stops volume change. Chemical potential equality stops net mass transfer.

If μiβ > μiα, move dn moles of i from β to α. Then dG = (μiα − μiβ) dn. The right side is negative, so G falls. Spontaneous transfer is from higher μ to lower μ. When the two values are equal, dG = 0 and there is no net transfer. The equality is dynamic: molecules still cross, but both directions balance. At fixed T and P the equilibrium state is the one with the lowest total G, so at equilibrium no small transfer of any species between the phases can lower G further.

Worked example

Question. At 300 K, liquid A has μA(liquid) = 100.0 kJ/mol and vapour A has μA(vapour) = 101.5 kJ/mol. 0.010 mol of A moves from vapour to liquid at constant T and P. Find ΔG for this transfer. State whether the transfer is spontaneous.

  1. Identify the donor and receiver. Vapour is the donor with μvap = 101.5 kJ/mol. (Only the difference between the two values matters; both are measured from the same reference state.) Liquid is the receiver with μliq = 100.0 kJ/mol.
  2. For a transfer of dn = 0.010 mol from vapour to liquid, the liquid gains Δnliq = +0.010 mol and the vapour loses Δnvap = −0.010 mol.
  3. At fixed T and P, ΔG = μliq Δnliq + μvap Δnvap. The 0.010 mol is small, so both μ values stay practically constant while it moves; a large transfer would change the compositions and need an integral.
  4. Substitute: ΔG = (100.0 kJ/mol)(0.010 mol) + (101.5 kJ/mol)(−0.010 mol) = 1.000 kJ − 1.015 kJ = −0.015 kJ.
  5. Convert to joules: ΔG = −0.015 kJ × 1000 J/kJ = −15 J.
  6. ΔG is negative, so the vapour-to-liquid transfer is spontaneous at these conditions.

Answer. ΔG = −0.015 kJ = −15 J, so 0.010 mol of A moves from vapour to liquid spontaneously.

Exam traps

  • Using μ only for pure substances. In a mixture, μ depends on composition. A higher μ means higher escaping tendency, not higher concentration.
  • Setting only T and P equal for phase equilibrium. Thermal and mechanical equilibrium are not enough; every species must also have equal μ in all phases.
  • Making a sign slip in ΔG. For transfer from a donor to a receiver, use ΔG = (μreceiver − μdonor) dn. Spontaneous transfer needs a negative result.
  • Confusing μ with molar Gibbs energy for a mixture. They are equal only for a pure substance; for a mixture, μ is the partial molar Gibbs energy.
  • Forgetting units. μ in kJ/mol times n in mol gives kJ. Convert to J only at the end if the question asks for joules.

5.2 Partial molar properties

One mole of ethanol added to a large tank of water does not change the volume by the same amount as one mole of pure ethanol. The surroundings change what each molecule can do. A partial molar property measures the real contribution of one component to a mixture property while temperature, pressure and all other amounts stay fixed. It converts measured mixture data into component properties for chemical potential, phase equilibrium and energy balances.

You need chemical potential and partial derivatives taken with T and P fixed.

Definition and the total property

Take a mixture with total moles n and ni moles of component i. Let M be any molar property: volume, enthalpy, entropy or Gibbs energy. The total property is nM, an extensive quantity. A partial molar property M̄i answers one question: how fast does nM change, per mole of i, when a very small amount of i is added with T, P and every other nj fixed? The definition is:

M̄i = (∂(nM)/∂ni)T,P,nj

M is any molar property of the mixture; n is total moles; ni is moles of component i; nj means all other mole numbers held fixed; T is temperature and P is pressure.

M̄i has the same units as M (for example cm³/mol for volume). For a pure component it equals the ordinary molar property. In a mixture it depends on composition. Adding the contributions of all components gives the mixture property:

M = Σ xi M̄i

M is the mixture molar property; xi is mole fraction of component i; M̄i is its partial molar property; the sum runs over all components.

The sum M = Σ xi M̄i uses partial molar values at the actual composition, not pure-component values. The Gibbs energy case is special: Ḡi = μi, the chemical potential, as in Chapter 5.1.

Tangent construction and binary formulas

For a binary mixture, plot M against x₁ at fixed T and P. Draw the tangent at the chosen composition. Extend it to x₁ = 1 and x₁ = 0. The intercept at x₁ = 1 is M̄₁; the intercept at x₁ = 0 is M̄₂. This tangent-intercept construction reads both partial molar properties from one curve.

The binary formulas are:

M̄₁ = M + x₂ dM/dx₁

M̄₁ is the partial molar property of component 1; M is the mixture molar property; x₂ is the mole fraction of component 2; dM/dx₁ is the slope of M against x₁ at fixed T and P.

M̄₂ = M − x₁ dM/dx₁

M̄₂ is the partial molar property of component 2; M is the mixture molar property; x₁ is the mole fraction of component 1; dM/dx₁ is the slope of M against x₁ at fixed T and P.

Here x₂ = 1 − x₁ and dM/dx₁ is the slope of the M curve at the chosen composition. The slope equals M̄₁ − M̄₂. The figure uses V = 18 + 28x₁ − 6x₁². At x₁ = 0.25, V = 24.625 cm³/mol and dV/dx₁ = 28 − 12(0.25) = 25.0 cm³/mol. The tangent is 18.375 + 25x₁; its intercepts are V̄₂ = 18.375 and V̄₁ = 43.375 cm³/mol.

Volume, enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy

Each property has its own partial molar version. V̄i is the partial molar volume, used in mixture density and volume change on mixing. H̄i is the partial molar enthalpy, used in heat effects. S̄i is the partial molar entropy, used in entropy balances. Ḡi is the partial molar Gibbs energy, equal to μi. All four change with composition.

Use the values at the composition of the mixture. A pure-component table gives Vi, Hi or Si only at xi = 1. At x₁ = 0.25 in the example, V̄₁ = 43.375 and V̄₂ = 18.375 cm³/mol. At another x₁, recompute both. The changes are linked by the Gibbs-Duhem equation in Chapter 5.3.

Partial molar properties appear in energy balances, phase-equilibrium calculations and property estimates. Later, activity coefficients are built from partial molar Gibbs energies, and VLE data reduction uses partial molar volumes.

Worked example

Question. For a binary liquid mixture at 298 K and 1 bar, the molar volume is V = 40x₁ + 18x₂ + 6x₁x₂ cm³/mol, where x₁ and x₂ are mole fractions. Find V̄₁ and V̄₂ at x₁ = 0.25.

  1. x₂ = 1 − x₁ = 0.75.
  2. Substitute: V = 40(0.25) + 18(0.75) + 6(0.25)(0.75) = 10 + 13.5 + 1.125 = 24.625 cm³/mol.
  3. Differentiate with x₂ = 1 − x₁: V = 18 + 22x₁ + 6x₁(1 − x₁), so dV/dx₁ = 22 + 6 − 12x₁ = 28 − 12x₁.
  4. At x₁ = 0.25: dV/dx₁ = 28 − 3 = 25.0 cm³/mol.
  5. V̄₁ = V + x₂ dV/dx₁ = 24.625 + 0.75(25.0) = 24.625 + 18.75 = 43.375 cm³/mol.
  6. V̄₂ = V − x₁ dV/dx₁ = 24.625 − 0.25(25.0) = 24.625 − 6.25 = 18.375 cm³/mol.

Answer. V̄₁ = 43.375 cm³/mol and V̄₂ = 18.375 cm³/mol at x₁ = 0.25.

Exam traps

  • Using pure-component V₁ and V₂ in V = x₁V₁ + x₂V₂. The sum needs V̄₁ and V̄₂ at the mixture composition; pure values are valid only at xi = 1.
  • Dropping the fixed variables in M̄i = (∂(nM)/∂ni). The derivative is at constant T, P and all other nj, not at constant total n alone.
  • Mixing the signs in the binary formulas. M̄₁ has +x₂ dM/dx₁; M̄₂ has −x₁ dM/dx₁. Reversing them swaps the two intercepts.
  • Differentiating M instead of nM. The definition uses the extensive property nM; for molar M the same result follows only after the ni dependence of xi is handled.
  • Treating μi as the pure Gibbs energy. μi equals Ḡi, the partial molar Gibbs energy at the mixture composition.

5.3 Gibbs-Duhem equation

At fixed temperature and pressure, the partial molar properties of a mixture cannot change independently. A change in one component's partial molar property forces a matching change in the others, and the weighted sum of the changes is zero: Σ xᵢ dM̄ᵢ = 0. This is the Gibbs–Duhem equation. It comes from the fact that the total property is homogeneous in the number of moles. It is used to check measured slopes, to test vapour–liquid equilibrium data, and to reduce the number of properties that must be measured or modelled.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for partial molar properties, and Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions from mathematics.

Why a mixture property is homogeneous

Fix temperature T and pressure P. Let nM be any total property of the mixture (total volume, total enthalpy or total Gibbs energy), where M is the molar property and n the total moles, as in partial molar properties. Let nᵢ be the moles of component i. nM depends on T, P and all nᵢ.

Scale every nᵢ by the same factor λ. The mixture keeps the same composition, so at fixed T and P its total property also scales by λ. Mathematics calls this a homogeneous function of degree one in the mole numbers.

Euler's theorem for such a function gives a value relation:

nM(T, P, n₁, n₂, …) = Σ nᵢ M̄ᵢ

nM is the total property of the mixture, nᵢ is the number of moles of component i, and M̄ᵢ is its partial molar property at the same T, P and other moles.

Divide by total moles n = Σ nᵢ. With xᵢ = nᵢ/n:

M = Σ xᵢ M̄ᵢ

M is the molar property of the mixture and xᵢ is the mole fraction of component i.

This says the molar property is the mole-fraction weighted average of the partial molar properties. It holds at every composition. It does not yet say how the partial molar properties move when composition changes. That needs differentiation.

Differentiating at fixed T and P

Start with nM = Σ nᵢ M̄ᵢ. At fixed T and P, differentiate both sides. The product rule gives

d(nM) = Σ M̄ᵢ dnᵢ + Σ nᵢ dM̄ᵢ

d(nM) is the change in total property at fixed T and P, dnᵢ is the change in moles of component i, and dM̄ᵢ is the change in its partial molar property.

From the definition of partial molar property at fixed T and P, a change in nM caused only by mole changes is

d(nM) = Σ M̄ᵢ dnᵢ

M̄ᵢ = [∂(nM)/∂nᵢ] at fixed T, P and other moles.

Subtract the two expressions. The Σ M̄ᵢ dnᵢ terms cancel:

Σ nᵢ dM̄ᵢ = 0 at fixed T and P

nᵢ is the moles of component i and dM̄ᵢ is the change in its partial molar property.

Divide by n = Σ nᵢ. Use xᵢ = nᵢ/n:

Σ xᵢ dM̄ᵢ = 0 at fixed T and P

xᵢ is the mole fraction of component i and dM̄ᵢ is the change in its partial molar property.

This is the Gibbs–Duhem equation. For Gibbs energy (M = G), the partial molar property is the chemical potential μᵢ, so

Σ xᵢ dμᵢ = 0 at fixed T and P

μᵢ is the chemical potential of component i and xᵢ is its mole fraction.

In a binary mixture x₁ + x₂ = 1, so

x₁ dM̄₁ + x₂ dM̄₂ = 0 → dM̄₂/dx₁ = −(x₁/x₂)(dM̄₁/dx₁)

subscripts 1 and 2 are the two components; the derivative is taken with T and P fixed and x₂ = 1 − x₁.

Notice the difference: Σ xᵢ M̄ᵢ is the molar property, not zero. Gibbs–Duhem sets the differential sum to zero.

Checking slopes and VLE data

Use the binary form to check any measured or calculated slope. At fixed T and P, the two partial molar slopes with respect to x₁ must have opposite signs when both mole fractions are positive. Their magnitudes are in the ratio x₂ : x₁ — that is, |dM̄₁/dx₁| / |dM̄₂/dx₁| = x₂/x₁. For example, at x₁ = 0.40, x₂ = 0.60, slopes of magnitude 30 and 20 satisfy 30/20 = 0.60/0.40. If a model gives dM̄₁/dx₁ = −30.0 kJ mol⁻¹ per unit x₁ at x₁ = 0.40, it must give dM̄₂/dx₁ = +20.0 kJ mol⁻¹ per unit x₁. A slip in one slope is easy to catch this way.

For vapour–liquid equilibrium, write the chemical potential through fugacity: μᵢ = μᵢ⁰ + RT ln fᵢ at fixed T. The Gibbs–Duhem form becomes

x₁ d ln f₁ + x₂ d ln f₂ = 0 at fixed T and P

fᵢ is the fugacity of component i in the mixture and xᵢ is its mole fraction.

For an activity-coefficient model, fᵢ = xᵢγᵢ fᵢpure at fixed T and P. The ideal mole-fraction part cancels because Σ xᵢ d ln xᵢ = Σ dxᵢ = 0. What remains is

x₁ d ln γ₁ + x₂ d ln γ₂ = 0 at fixed T and P

γᵢ is the activity coefficient of component i and xᵢ is its mole fraction.

Measured VLE data must obey this relation. If the activity coefficients from experiment violate it, the data are suspect or the model is wrong. Complete VLE data sets are tested the same way, point by point or with the area test ∫₀¹ ln(γ₁/γ₂) dx₁ = 0, when the data are reduced for phase-equilibrium work.

Worked example

Question. At fixed T and P, a binary liquid mixture has x₁ = 0.40 and x₂ = 0.60. The partial molar property M̄₁ changes with composition at the rate dM̄₁/dx₁ = −30.0 kJ mol⁻¹ per unit x₁. Find dM̄₂/dx₁.

  1. Write the binary Gibbs–Duhem equation at fixed T and P: x₁ dM̄₁ + x₂ dM̄₂ = 0.
  2. Divide by dx₁, with x₂ = 1 − x₁: x₁(dM̄₁/dx₁) + x₂(dM̄₂/dx₁) = 0.
  3. Solve for the unknown slope: dM̄₂/dx₁ = −(x₁/x₂)(dM̄₁/dx₁).
  4. Substitute x₁ = 0.40, x₂ = 0.60 and dM̄₁/dx₁ = −30.0 kJ mol⁻¹ per unit x₁: dM̄₂/dx₁ = −(0.40/0.60)(−30.0).
  5. The ratio 0.40/0.60 = 0.6667. Then −(0.6667)(−30.0) = +20.0. The positive sign means M̄₂ rises as x₁ rises.

Answer. dM̄₂/dx₁ = +20.0 kJ mol⁻¹ per unit x₁.

Worked example 2

Question. Two students fit activity coefficients for the same binary at fixed T and P. Student A writes ln γ₁ = 0.8x₂² and ln γ₂ = 0.8x₁². Student B writes ln γ₁ = 0.8x₂ and ln γ₂ = 0.8x₁. Test both pairs with the Gibbs–Duhem equation at x₁ = 0.30.

  1. The binary test is x₁(d ln γ₁/dx₁) + x₂(d ln γ₂/dx₁) = 0, with x₂ = 1 − x₁, so dx₂/dx₁ = −1.
  2. Pair A: d ln γ₁/dx₁ = 0.8 × 2x₂ × (−1) = −1.6x₂ = −1.6 × 0.70 = −1.12; d ln γ₂/dx₁ = 1.6x₁ = 1.6 × 0.30 = 0.48.
  3. Pair A sum: 0.30 × (−1.12) + 0.70 × 0.48 = −0.336 + 0.336 = 0. The pair passes, and it passes at every x₁ because x₁(−1.6x₂) + x₂(1.6x₁) = 0 identically.
  4. Pair B: d ln γ₁/dx₁ = −0.8; d ln γ₂/dx₁ = +0.8.
  5. Pair B sum: 0.30 × (−0.8) + 0.70 × 0.8 = −0.24 + 0.56 = 0.32, not zero. In general the sum is 0.8(x₂ − x₁), zero only at x₁ = 0.5.

Answer. Pair A satisfies Gibbs–Duhem at every composition (the sum is 0 at x₁ = 0.30). Pair B gives 0.32 at x₁ = 0.30, so it is thermodynamically inconsistent and cannot describe any real mixture.

Exam traps

  • Gibbs–Duhem is Σ xᵢ dM̄ᵢ = 0, not Σ xᵢ M̄ᵢ = 0. The weighted average of the properties is the molar property.
  • The equation holds at fixed T and P. Do not use it across a temperature or pressure change without adding the T and P terms.
  • In the binary slope form, dM̄₂/dx₁ = −(x₁/x₂)(dM̄₁/dx₁). A dropped minus sign flips the answer.
  • For Gibbs energy the equation is Σ xᵢ dμᵢ = 0. Do not replace μᵢ by xᵢμᵢ or by the pure-component chemical potential.
  • The activity-coefficient form needs the ideal mole-fraction part cancelled. Check that Σ xᵢ d ln xᵢ = 0 before using x₁ d ln γ₁ + x₂ d ln γ₂ = 0.

5.4 Fugacity and fugacity coefficients

Real gases do not obey μ = μ° + RT ln P, because their molecules attract and repel each other. Fugacity f is the corrected pressure that keeps the logarithmic form exactly true, and φ = f/P, the fugacity coefficient, measures how far the gas is from ideal. Every K-value and every VLE calculation in a simulator rests on fugacity.

You need chemical potential from chemical potential and phase equilibrium, the compressibility factor and the virial equation of state, and residual properties.

Why fugacity exists

For an ideal gas at fixed temperature, the chemical potential rises logarithmically with pressure: μ = μ°(T) + RT ln(P/P°). Doubling the pressure adds RT ln 2, about 1.7 kJ/mol at 300 K. The derivation assumes molecules do not interact and V = RT/P exactly.

A real gas breaks that assumption. Attraction pulls molecules together, so at moderate pressure the volume is smaller than RT/P, and the simple logarithm no longer gives the correct μ. G. N. Lewis (1901) kept the shape of the equation and replaced pressure by a corrected pressure, the fugacity.

μᵢ = μᵢ°(T) + RT ln(fᵢ / fᵢ°)

μᵢ is the chemical potential of species i in J/mol; μᵢ° is its value in the reference state at the same T; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); fᵢ is the fugacity in bar or Pa; fᵢ° is the fugacity of the reference state.

The definition is completed by a limit: fᵢ → Pᵢ (the partial pressure) as P → 0, where every gas becomes ideal. With the usual reference, the pure ideal gas at P° = 1 bar, f° = 1 bar, so fᵢ carries units of pressure.

φᵢ = fᵢ / Pᵢ

φᵢ is the fugacity coefficient, dimensionless; Pᵢ is the partial pressure, yᵢP for a gas mixture or P for a pure gas. Some books write the fugacity coefficient as f/P instead of φ.

φ → 1 as P → 0, and an ideal gas has φ = 1 at every pressure, so any value away from 1 measures non-ideality. Because a species has the same μ in every phase at equilibrium, it also has the same fugacity there: equality of fugacities, not of pressures, is the working condition for every VLE calculation.

Computing φ from volumetric data

At fixed temperature dG = V dP for any fluid and dGⁱᵍ = (RT/P) dP for an ideal gas. Integrating the difference from zero pressure gives the residual Gibbs energy, Gᴿ = RT ln φ for a pure fluid.

ln φ = (1/RT) ∫₀ᴾ (V − RT/P) dP = ∫₀ᴾ (Z − 1) dP/P

V is molar volume in m³/mol; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T in K; P in Pa; Z = PV/RT is the compressibility factor at the same T and P.

Read the sign. If Z < 1 the gas is more compressible than ideal, f is smaller than P and φ < 1. If Z > 1, φ > 1. As P → 0, Z − 1 shrinks in proportion to P (Z − 1 ≈ BP/RT), so (Z − 1)/P tends to the finite value B/RT. The integrand stays finite, which is why the lower limit can be zero even though P appears in the denominator.

Two routes give the integral quickly. For moderate pressures use the truncated virial equation Z = 1 + BP/RT, which gives ln φ = BP/RT with B in m³/mol. For high pressures, use a cubic equation of state and integrate its Z analytically.

Mixtures, residual Gibbs energy and generalized correlation

In a mixture each species has its own fugacity f̂ᵢ, defined by the same equation μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln(f̂ᵢ/fᵢ°), and its own coefficient φ̂ᵢ = f̂ᵢ/(yᵢP), with yᵢ the gas mole fraction. Using the partial molar volume V̄ᵢ, ln φ̂ᵢ = (1/RT) ∫₀ᴾ (V̄ᵢ − RT/P) dP.

Gᴿ/RT = Σ xᵢ ln φ̂ᵢ

Gᴿ is the residual Gibbs energy of the mixture in J/mol; xᵢ is the mole fraction; φ̂ᵢ is the fugacity coefficient of species i in the mixture. For a pure fluid this reduces to ln φ = Gᴿ/RT.

An ideal solution obeys the Lewis-Randall rule f̂ᵢ = xᵢ fᵢpure, so φ̂ᵢ equals the pure-fluid φᵢ at the same T and P (see Chapter 5.5). Real mixtures deviate, and that deviation is what activity coefficients in Chapter 5.7 correct for.

Generalized correlation. Put the Pitzer virial form into the integral: B⁰ = 0.083 − 0.422/Tᵣ1.6, B¹ = 0.139 − 0.172/Tᵣ4.2 and BPc/RTc = B⁰ + ωB¹, with Tᵣ = T/Tc, Pᵣ = P/Pc and ω the acentric factor. The result is ln φ = (Pᵣ/Tᵣ)(B⁰ + ωB¹). It needs only Tc, Pc and ω and holds where the two-term virial equation holds: non-polar or slightly polar gases at low reduced pressure (well below Pᵣ = 1); at higher Pᵣ use a cubic equation of state.

Liquids have a fugacity too. From the saturated liquid, where f = φsatPsat, compress the liquid at fixed T (d ln f = V dP/RT): fl = φsatPsat exp[Vl(P − Psat)/RT], where the exponential is the Poynting factor. For water at 100 °C (Psat = 1.013 bar, Vl = 18.8 cm³/mol, φsat taken as 0.99) at 50 bar the factor is exp(0.0297) = 1.030, so fl = 1.03 bar: close to the vapour pressure, not to the system pressure.

Worked example

Question. A pure gas at 10.0 bar has Z = 0.90, constant from 1.0 bar to 10.0 bar. Below 1.0 bar the gas is ideal. Taking the ideal-gas reference at 1.0 bar, estimate φ and f at 10.0 bar.

  1. Write the working formula: ln φ = ∫₀ᴾ (Z − 1) dP/P. Below 1 bar, Z = 1, so the integrand is zero and that part adds nothing.
  2. Substitute Z = 0.90 for the range 1.0 to 10.0 bar: ln φ = (0.90 − 1) ∫₁10 dP/P = (−0.10) ln(10.0/1.0).
  3. Evaluate: ln(10) = 2.3026, so ln φ = −0.10 × 2.3026 = −0.2303.
  4. Exponentiate: φ = e−0.2303 = 0.7943. The value is below 1, as expected for Z < 1.
  5. Convert to fugacity: f = φP = 0.7943 × 10.0 bar = 7.94 bar.

Answer. At 10.0 bar, φ = 0.794 and f = 7.94 bar, lower than the true pressure.

Worked example 2

Question. Estimate the fugacity coefficient and fugacity of n-butane at 500 K and 15 bar from the generalized virial (Pitzer) correlation. For n-butane Tc = 425.1 K, Pc = 37.96 bar and ω = 0.200.

Exam traps

  • Setting f = P for a real gas. That is true only in the ideal-gas limit; using it at 10 bar here would overstate the fugacity by 26 percent.
  • Mixing reference states: keep the same f° in every step, or the RT ln term picks up a false offset.
  • Using log₁₀ instead of ln in ln φ = ∫(Z − 1) dP/P. The formula needs natural logarithms throughout.
  • Taking a liquid's fugacity as the system pressure. It is close to Psat: f ≈ φsatPsat times the Poynting factor.
  • Treating φ̂ᵢ of a mixture as the pure φᵢ at the same T and P without checking the Lewis-Randall assumption.

5.5 Ideal and non-ideal solutions; Lewis-Randall rule

An ideal solution is a useful reference for liquid mixtures. It says each component's fugacity is proportional to its mole fraction at fixed temperature and pressure. The proportionality constant is the fugacity of the pure component. This simple rule, called the Lewis-Randall rule, gives Raoult's law at low pressure and lets you calculate vapour compositions without activity coefficients. Real mixtures deviate from it; the sign and size of the deviation tell you whether the components like each other more or less than they like themselves. Refineries, distilleries and solvent plants use this reference daily.

You need: chemical potential, fugacity, and ideal-gas mixtures (from ideal gas and ideal-gas processes).

Ideal solution and chemical potential

An ideal solution is a model for a liquid mixture in which the chemical potential of every component follows one simple equation. At fixed temperature T and pressure P:

μi = μipure + RT ln xi

μi is the chemical potential of component i in the solution (J/mol); μipure is its chemical potential as a pure liquid at the same T and P; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K; xi is its mole fraction.

The standard state μipure is the pure liquid at the same T and P, not at 1 bar and not the ideal gas. When xi = 1, ln xi = 0 and μi = μipure, so the equation returns the pure liquid. The model assumes molecules of similar size and similar intermolecular forces, mixed randomly. It does not assume zero forces. The forces still exist; the energy of unlike contacts is taken as the average of like contacts. Therefore an ideal solution has zero enthalpy of mixing, ΔHmix = 0, and zero volume change on mixing, ΔVmix = 0. Its entropy of mixing is the same as for ideal gases, ΔSmix = −R Σ xi ln xi. This definition is a reference, not a law of nature. See Chapter 5.1 for the meaning of μ.

Lewis-Randall rule and Raoult's law

Lewis and Randall extended the pure-component fugacity to mixtures. For an ideal solution, fi = xi fipure. Here fi (written f̂ᵢ in the fugacity chapter) is the fugacity of component i in the solution (kPa); fipure is the fugacity of pure liquid i at the same T and P; xi is the liquid mole fraction. This is the Lewis-Randall rule. At low pressure, fi ≈ pi and fipure ≈ Pisat, so the rule becomes pi = xi Pisat, which is Raoult's law. pi is the partial pressure (kPa), and Pisat is the vapour pressure of pure i at T (kPa). For two components, P = p1 + p2 = x1P1sat + (1−x1)P2sat, a straight line in x1. The bubble-pressure line plots this P against liquid x1; the dew curve plots P against vapour y1. The vapour mole fraction is yi = pi/P. Compare this with an ideal-gas mixture: there fi = yiP and the standard state is the ideal gas at 1 bar. An ideal gas has no intermolecular forces; an ideal solution has forces but no net change on mixing. Same word ideal, different physics.

Real solutions and deviations

Real liquid mixtures disobey the rule. If pi is greater than xiPisat, the mixture shows positive deviation from Raoult's law. The activity coefficient γi = ai/xi is greater than 1. The unlike molecules attract each other less than like molecules, so each component escapes more easily. Ethanol + water is a common example. If pi is less than xiPisat, the deviation is negative and γi < 1: the unlike molecules attract each other more strongly than like molecules, so each component escapes less easily (chloroform + acetone is a common example; such systems can form a maximum-boiling azeotrope). As xi → 0, Raoult's law fails for that component even when the solvent still follows it; Henry's law replaces it: pi = Hixi, where Hi is Henry's constant in kPa, found from data at the same T. Hi is not Pisat. The standard state for the solvent is the pure liquid, the Lewis-Randall choice. The standard state for a dilute solute is Henry's hypothetical pure liquid, obtained by extrapolating Henry's law to xi = 1. The general non-ideal form is fi = xiγifipure. Activity and γ are developed in Chapter 5.7.

Worked example

  6. y1 = p1/P = 30/58 = 0.5172. Also y2 = 28/58 = 0.4828, and y1 + y2 = 1.

  • Setting fipure equal to Pisat at high pressure. At low pressure they are close; at high pressure fipure must include the fugacity coefficient.

5.6 Excess properties and property changes of mixing

A real mixture usually differs from an ideal solution even at the same temperature, pressure and composition. The property change of mixing measures the whole change from pure components to mixture. The excess property measures only the part left after the ideal-solution change is removed. This split is useful because ideal mixing has a known entropy term, while real interactions add heat, volume change and activity coefficients. Distillation, solvent selection and heat-of-mixing calculations all need this split.

You need partial molar properties from partial molar properties, ideal solutions from ideal and non-ideal solutions, and G = H − TS.

Property change of mixing

For any molar property M of a mixture at fixed T and P, the property change of mixing is the mixture value minus the pure-component values weighted by mole fraction. Write it per mole of mixture.

ΔMmix = M − Σ xi Mipure

ΔMmix is the change per mole of mixture when pure components mix; M is the mixture molar property; xi is mole fraction of component i; Mipure is the molar property of pure i at the same T and P.

The same definition applies to U, H, S, G and V. For an ideal solution, ΔHmixid = 0 because unlike contacts have the average energy of like contacts, and ΔVmixid = 0 because the molecules are similar in size and pack the same way as in the pure liquids. Entropy and Gibbs energy still change because mixing increases randomness.

ΔSmixid = −R Σ xi ln xi

R is the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; xi are mole fractions; ln is natural logarithm. Each ln xi is negative, so Σ xi ln xi is negative and ΔSmixid is positive.

ΔGmixid = RT Σ xi ln xi

T is absolute temperature in K. Each ln xi is negative, so ΔGmixid is negative for spontaneous ideal mixing at fixed T and P.

A real mixture adds an excess part to each of these ideal changes. The excess part can be positive or negative.

Excess properties

An excess property is the real property change of mixing minus the ideal property change of mixing at the same T, P and composition.

ME = ΔMmix − ΔMmixid = M − Mid

ME is excess molar property; Mid is the molar property of an ideal solution at the same T, P and xi. The superscript E means excess.

For enthalpy and volume, the ideal mixing terms are zero, so HE = ΔHmix and VE = ΔVmix. A positive HE means mixing absorbs heat. A negative HE means mixing releases heat. A positive VE means the mixture expands on mixing; a negative VE means contraction.

For Gibbs energy the ideal term is not zero. The excess Gibbs energy is linked to activity coefficients.

GE = RT Σ xi ln γi

GE is excess molar Gibbs energy; γi is the activity coefficient of i from Chapter 5.7. For an ideal solution every γi = 1, so GE = 0.

Entropy follows from GE = HE − T SE. The excess entropy is SE = (HE − GE)/T. This relation lets you find one excess property if you know the other two at the same T, P and composition.

Activity coefficients come from the partial molar excess Gibbs energy, not from GE alone.

ln γi = (1/RT) [∂(nGE)/∂ni]T,P,nj

n is total moles; ni is moles of i; nj means other mole numbers held fixed. The derivative is partial molar. Chapter 5.8 builds models from this formula.

Reading excess values

Excess properties are per mole of mixture unless stated otherwise. To get a total property change, multiply by total moles. If a table gives VE in cm³/mol, a 2.00 mol mixture has total excess volume 2.00 × VE cm³.

The figure uses VE = 2x1x2 cm³/mol. At x1 = 0.50, VE = 2 × 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.500 cm³/mol. For an ideal solution VE = 0, so the curve would sit on the zero line. The positive hump is expansion on mixing.

Some pairs contract on mixing and release heat; others expand and absorb heat. Use measured or model values when a heat or volume balance matters. For mixing at constant pressure with no shaft work, the heat that must be supplied to keep the temperature constant is Q = nΔHmix = nHE: positive for endothermic mixing, negative (heat to be removed) for exothermic mixing. Do not assume ideal behaviour just because the components look similar.

Worked example

  1. x2 = 1 − x1 = 1 − 0.50 = 0.50.
  2. VE = 2 × 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.500 cm³/mol.
  3. For an ideal solution, ΔVmixid = 0.
  4. ΔVmix = VE + ΔVmixid = 0.500 + 0 = 0.500 cm³/mol.
  5. Total ΔVmix = n ΔVmix = 2.00 mol × 0.500 cm³/mol = 1.00 cm³.

Answer. ΔVmix = 0.500 cm³/mol, and the total change for 2.00 mol is 1.00 cm³.

Worked example 2

Question. At 300 K and 1 bar a binary liquid with x1 = 0.40 has γ1 = 1.60 and γ2 = 1.15 (pure-liquid standard states) and a measured heat of mixing HE = +800 J/mol. Find GE, ΔGmix, SE and ΔSmix, and the heat needed to keep 5.0 mol of this mixture at 300 K as it forms.

  1. RT = 8.314 × 300 = 2494.2 J/mol; x2 = 0.60.
  2. GE = RT(x1 ln γ1 + x2 ln γ2) = 2494.2 × (0.40 × 0.4700 + 0.60 × 0.1398) = 2494.2 × (0.1880 + 0.0839) = 2494.2 × 0.2719 = 678 J/mol.
  3. Ideal part: ΔGmixid = RT(x1 ln x1 + x2 ln x2) = 2494.2 × (0.40 × (−0.9163) + 0.60 × (−0.5108)) = 2494.2 × (−0.6730) = −1679 J/mol.
  4. ΔGmix = ΔGmixid + GE = −1679 + 678 = −1001 J/mol: still negative, so the liquids mix.
  5. SE = (HE − GE)/T = (800 − 678)/300 = 0.41 J/(mol·K).
  6. ΔSmixid = −R Σ xi ln xi = 8.314 × 0.6730 = 5.60 J/(mol·K); ΔSmix = 5.60 + 0.41 = 6.00 J/(mol·K).
  7. Heat: ΔHmix = HE = 800 J/mol, so Q = 5.0 × 800 = +4000 J must be supplied (mixing is endothermic).
  8. Check: ΔGmix = ΔHmix − TΔSmix = 800 − 300 × 6.00 = −1000 J/mol, matching −1001 J/mol within rounding.

Answer. GE = 678 J/mol, ΔGmix = −1001 J/mol, SE = 0.41 J/(mol·K), ΔSmix = 6.00 J/(mol·K); 4.0 kJ of heat must be supplied for 5.0 mol.

Exam traps

  • ΔMmix includes the ideal part; ME does not. Do not set ME = ΔMmix for S or G; that is true only for H, U and V.
  • Excess properties are per mole of mixture. Multiply by total moles for a vessel; do not add a per-mole value to a total mole value.
  • ME = ΔMmix − ΔMmixid. Reversing the subtraction gives the wrong sign; a negative VE means contraction.
  • For ideal solutions ΔHmix and ΔVmix are zero, but ΔSmix and ΔGmix are not zero. Examiners often ask which ideal mixing changes vanish.
  • Activity coefficients come from the partial molar excess Gibbs energy, (∂(nGE)/∂ni), not from GE by a simple division.

5.7 Activity and activity coefficients

Activity answers a practical question: how do we keep the chemical potential formula working when a liquid is not ideal? Replace mole fraction by activity aᵢ, a corrected composition. The activity coefficient γᵢ = aᵢ/xᵢ measures the correction. Engineers use these quantities to calculate vapour-liquid equilibrium, design distillation and absorption columns, and convert measured pressure-composition data into numbers a simulator can use. For a non-ideal liquid, xᵢ alone does not fix μᵢ; the activity aᵢ = γᵢxᵢ does.

You need: chemical potential and phase equilibrium from Chapter 5.1, fugacity from Chapter 5.4, ideal and non-ideal solutions from Chapter 5.5, excess properties from Chapter 5.6, ; the low-pressure VLE relation yᵢP = γᵢxᵢPᵢsat is derived below and used again in phase equilibria.

Activity replaces mole fraction

For a species i in a mixture at temperature T and pressure P, write μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ. μᵢ is chemical potential (J/mol), μᵢ° is the standard chemical potential at the same T in a chosen standard state, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is absolute temperature (K), and aᵢ is activity. Activity is dimensionless: it is the ratio of the species' fugacity in the mixture to its fugacity in the standard state, not an amount of substance.

Activity comes from fugacity: aᵢ = fᵢ/fᵢ°. fᵢ is the fugacity of i in the mixture (pressure units, for example bar), and fᵢ° is the standard-state fugacity at the same T. The activity coefficient is γᵢ = aᵢ/xᵢ, where xᵢ is liquid mole fraction. Then aᵢ = γᵢxᵢ, and γᵢ = 1 for an ideal solution.

μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln(γᵢ xᵢ)

μᵢ is chemical potential (J/mol); μᵢ° is standard chemical potential at the chosen standard state and T; aᵢ is activity (dimensionless); γᵢ is activity coefficient (dimensionless); xᵢ is mole fraction; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K.

Two standard states are common. For the pure-liquid standard, fᵢ° is the fugacity of pure liquid i at the same T and P. At low pressure fᵢ° ≈ Pᵢsat, the saturation vapour pressure of pure i at T. Then aᵢ = fᵢ/Pᵢsat, and γᵢ→1 as xᵢ→1. For the Henry standard for a dilute solute, fᵢ° = kH,i, the Henry constant at the same T and P. Then aᵢ = fᵢ/kH,i, and γᵢ→1 as xᵢ→0. Changing the standard state changes γᵢ and μᵢ°, not the actual μᵢ.

Finding γᵢ from vapour-liquid equilibrium

At low pressure, take the vapour as ideal and the pure liquid at its saturation pressure as the standard state. Equality of fugacity for each component gives yᵢP = γᵢ xᵢ Pᵢsat. yᵢ is vapour mole fraction, xᵢ is liquid mole fraction, P is total pressure (bar), Pᵢsat is saturation vapour pressure of pure i at T (bar), and γᵢ is liquid-phase activity coefficient. This is modified Raoult's law.

Rearranged, aᵢ = yᵢP/Pᵢsat and γᵢ = yᵢP/(xᵢPᵢsat). Measure T, P, xᵢ and yᵢ; get Pᵢsat from a table or Antoine equation; compute aᵢ and γᵢ for every component. A γᵢ above 1 means positive deviation and a γᵢ below 1 negative deviation. The activity itself does not decide this: a₁ can exceed x₁ in any non-ideal mixture with γ₁ > 1.

  1. Measure the equilibrium temperature T and total pressure P.
  2. Analyse the liquid and vapour compositions to get xᵢ and yᵢ.
  3. Find Pᵢsat for each pure component at T.
  4. Compute aᵢ = yᵢP/Pᵢsat, then γᵢ = aᵢ/xᵢ.

At higher pressure, replace yᵢP by φᵢVyᵢP and Pᵢsat by fᵢ°, including Poynting and vapour non-ideality corrections. The activity idea remains the same.

Activity coefficients and excess Gibbs energy

Activity coefficients are tied to the excess Gibbs energy GE, which measures departure from an ideal solution at the same T, P and composition. For a mixture, GE/RT = Σ xᵢ ln γᵢ. The sum runs over all components. This form is exact when every γᵢ uses the pure-liquid (Lewis-Randall) standard state; with a Henry standard for a solute the sum differs from the excess Gibbs energy by a term x₂ ln γ₂∞. Equivalently, the partial molar excess Gibbs energy of component i is RT ln γᵢ.

GE/RT = Σ xᵢ ln γᵢ

GE is excess Gibbs energy (J/mol); R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K; xᵢ is mole fraction; γᵢ is activity coefficient.

For a binary mixture, ln γ₁ and ln γ₂ cannot be chosen independently; they obey Gibbs-Duhem. If a model gives GE as a function of x₁, differentiate it to get ln γ₁ and ln γ₂. Margules, van Laar and Wilson models do exactly this; see Chapter 5.8.

Use one standard state consistently. With a pure-liquid standard, γᵢ→1 as xᵢ→1. With a Henry standard, γᵢ→1 as xᵢ→0. Both can describe the same mixture under different conventions.

Worked example

Question. At 1.00 bar total pressure, a binary vapour-liquid equilibrium has liquid mole fraction x₁ = 0.20 and vapour mole fraction y₁ = 0.50. The saturation vapour pressure of pure component 1 at the same temperature is P₁sat = 0.80 bar. Assume the vapour is ideal and take pure liquid at its saturation pressure as the standard state. The saturation pressure of component 2 is P₂sat = 0.60 bar. Calculate the activity a₁ and the activity coefficients γ₁ and γ₂ in the liquid.

  1. Write the low-pressure VLE relation: y₁P = γ₁x₁P₁sat.
  2. With an ideal vapour, the fugacity of 1 in the vapour is y₁P = 0.50 × 1.00 bar = 0.50 bar; at equilibrium the liquid has the same fugacity, f₁ = 0.50 bar.
  3. Pure-liquid standard-state fugacity at low pressure: f₁° = P₁sat = 0.80 bar.
  4. Activity: a₁ = f₁/f₁° = 0.50 bar / 0.80 bar = 0.625 (dimensionless).
  5. Activity coefficient: γ₁ = a₁/x₁ = 0.625/0.20 = 3.125 (dimensionless).
  6. Check: γ₁x₁P₁sat = 3.125 × 0.20 × 0.80 bar = 0.50 bar = y₁P.

Answer. a₁ = 0.625, γ₁ = 3.125 and γ₂ = 1.042; the liquid shows positive deviation, strong for the dilute component.

Exam traps

  • Do not set aᵢ = xᵢ unless γᵢ = 1. Activity equals mole fraction only for an ideal solution in the same standard state.
  • Do not mix standard states. A γᵢ calculated with the pure-liquid standard is not comparable with a γᵢ calculated using the Henry standard; μᵢ° and the limiting behaviour change.
  • In yᵢP = γᵢxᵢPᵢsat, xᵢ is liquid and yᵢ is vapour. Swapping them is a common numerical error.
  • Do not use Pᵢsat as fᵢ° at high pressure. The low-pressure form omits vapour fugacity coefficients and the Poynting correction.
  • Do not treat γᵢ as a constant across composition. It varies with xᵢ and is tied to other components by Gibbs-Duhem.

5.8 Activity coefficient models: Margules, van Laar, Wilson

Activity coefficients γ measure how far a liquid mixture is from ideal. Models for γ come from the excess Gibbs energy GE: differentiate GE and you get ln γ. The Margules, van Laar and Wilson equations are three such models with fitted constants. Engineers use them to predict non-ideal vapour-liquid equilibrium (VLE), distillation columns and azeotropes. Only a few universities teach this chapter in detail.

You need: Chapter 5.7 for γ and Chapter 5.6 for GE; logarithms and binary calculus.

Margules: excess Gibbs energy made simple

Activity coefficients are not independent. The excess Gibbs energy GE fixes them through ln γ₁ = [∂(nGE/RT)/∂n₁] at constant T, P and n₂. Here n is total moles, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is kelvin temperature, and n₁, n₂ are mole numbers. This relation is the reason models are written for GE first: one GE expression gives both γ₁ and γ₂ consistently.

The two-suffix Margules model assumes GE/RT = A x₁x₂. A is a dimensionless constant fitted to one binary pair. Differentiating gives the pair below. At x₁ = 0, γ₁ = eA and γ₂ = 1; at x₁ = 1 the roles swap. A measures the size of the non-ideality: A = 0 gives an ideal solution with both γ equal to 1. A positive A means positive deviation from Raoult's law, common when unlike molecules attract each other less strongly than like ones.

GE/RT = A x₁x₂   →   ln γ₁ = A x₂²,  ln γ₂ = A x₁²

GE is excess Gibbs energy (J mol⁻¹); R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature (K); A is a dimensionless fitted constant; x₁, x₂ are mole fractions.

Real data are often lopsided, so many syllabi use the two-parameter (three-suffix) Margules form GE/(RT x₁x₂) = A₂₁x₁ + A₁₂x₂. It gives ln γ₁ = x₂²[A₁₂ + 2(A₂₁ − A₁₂)x₁] and ln γ₂ = x₁²[A₂₁ + 2(A₁₂ − A₂₁)x₂], with ln γ₁∞ = A₁₂ and ln γ₂∞ = A₂₁ at infinite dilution. With A₁₂ = A₂₁ = A it falls back to the one-constant form.

van Laar and Wilson: two-parameter forms

van Laar uses two constants, A₁₂ and A₂₁, so it can fit asymmetric data: GE/RT = A₁₂A₂₁x₁x₂/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂). Then ln γ₁ = A₁₂[A₂₁x₂/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂)]² and ln γ₂ = A₂₁[A₁₂x₁/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂)]². A₁₂ controls the dilute end where x₁ → 0; A₂₁ controls x₂ → 0.

Wilson builds local compositions around each molecule: ln γ₁ = −ln(x₁ + Λ₁₂x₂) + x₂[Λ₁₂/(x₁ + Λ₁₂x₂) − Λ₂₁/(x₂ + Λ₂₁x₁)]. Λ₁₂ and Λ₂₁ are positive dimensionless parameters; ln γ₂ follows by swapping 1 and 2. Often Λ₁₂ = (V₂/V₁) exp(−a₁₂/RT), where V₁ and V₂ are liquid molar volumes and a₁₂ is an interaction energy in J/mol, so a₁₂/RT is dimensionless. The one-constant Margules form is symmetric about x₁ = 0.5; van Laar and Wilson can follow lopsided data, and both become symmetric when their two constants are equal. Wilson cannot predict two liquid phases: for any positive Λ values its Gibbs energy of mixing has no hump, so a single liquid is always the stable state.

Fitting constants and using them for VLE

Fit constants to measured γ data. For two-suffix Margules, A = ln γ₁/x₂². For van Laar, solve the two equations at one data point. For Wilson, regress several points with a computer. Then use modified Raoult's law: yi P = xi γi Pisat. Pisat is the pure-component vapour pressure at T; yi is the vapour mole fraction; P is total pressure.

Bubble pressure is P = x₁γ₁P₁sat + x₂γ₂P₂sat, and y₁ = x₁γ₁P₁sat/P. This is the standard hand calculation for non-ideal VLE, used again in phase equilibria. The constants belong to one binary pair at one temperature. Extrapolating far outside the fitted composition or temperature range gives wrong γ, wrong azeotropes and wrong column design. For a distillation column, small errors in γ near an azeotrope shift the predicted azeotropic composition.

Worked example

Question. For a binary mixture described by the two-suffix Margules equation with A = 0.80, find γ₁ and γ₂ at x₁ = 0.30.

  1. x₂ = 1 − x₁ = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70.
  2. ln γ₁ = A x₂² = 0.80 × 0.70² = 0.80 × 0.490 = 0.392.
  3. γ₁ = e0.392 = 1.48.
  4. ln γ₂ = A x₁² = 0.80 × 0.30² = 0.80 × 0.090 = 0.072.
  5. γ₂ = e0.072 = 1.075.

Answer. At x₁ = 0.30, γ₁ = 1.48 and γ₂ = 1.075.

Worked example 2

Question. A binary forms a minimum-boiling azeotrope at x₁ = y₁ = 0.40 and P = 101.3 kPa, where P₁sat = 70.0 kPa and P₂sat = 80.0 kPa. Find the van Laar constants, then predict γ₁ at x₁ = 0.10 at the same temperature.

  1. At the azeotrope yᵢ = xᵢ, so γᵢ = P/Pᵢsat: γ₁ = 101.3/70.0 = 1.4471, γ₂ = 101.3/80.0 = 1.2663; ln γ₁ = 0.3696, ln γ₂ = 0.2361.
  2. van Laar constants from one point: A₁₂ = ln γ₁ (1 + x₂ ln γ₂/(x₁ ln γ₁))² and A₂₁ = ln γ₂ (1 + x₁ ln γ₁/(x₂ ln γ₂))².
  3. x₂ ln γ₂/(x₁ ln γ₁) = (0.60 × 0.2361)/(0.40 × 0.3696) = 0.1416/0.1478 = 0.9581. A₁₂ = 0.3696 × (1.9581)² = 0.3696 × 3.834 = 1.417.
  4. x₁ ln γ₁/(x₂ ln γ₂) = 1/0.9581 = 1.0437. A₂₁ = 0.2361 × (2.0437)² = 0.2361 × 4.177 = 0.986.
  5. Check at x₁ = 0.40: A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂ = 0.5668 + 0.5916 = 1.1584; ln γ₁ = 1.417 × (0.5916/1.1584)² = 1.417 × 0.2608 = 0.3696. The fitted constants return the data.
  6. At x₁ = 0.10: A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂ = 0.1417 + 0.8874 = 1.0291; ln γ₁ = 1.417 × (0.8874/1.0291)² = 1.417 × 0.7436 = 1.054; γ₁ = e1.054 = 2.87.

Answer. A₁₂ = 1.417 and A₂₁ = 0.986; at x₁ = 0.10 the model predicts γ₁ = 2.87 (ln γ₁ = 1.054), approaching eA₁₂ = 4.12 at infinite dilution.

Exam traps

  • Fitting A at one temperature and using it at another; A is not universal.
  • Writing γ = A x₂² instead of ln γ = A x₂².
  • Mixing up the symmetric Margules A with van Laar A₁₂ and A₂₁; the latter are not mole fractions.
  • Forgetting Λ₁₂ and Λ₂₁ are positive and dimensionless; using Wilson for immiscible liquids.
  • Inverting the azeotrope relation. At an azeotrope yᵢ = xᵢ, so modified Raoult's law gives γᵢ = P/Pᵢsat, not Pᵢsat/P.

Takeaways

  1. Chemical potential is the partial molar Gibbs energy, and equality of μi across phases is the equilibrium condition.
  2. Partial molar properties describe how a mixture property changes when one component is added at fixed T, P and other amounts.
  3. The Gibbs-Duhem equation ties partial molar properties together, so they cannot all be varied independently.
  4. Fugacity preserves the simple chemical-potential formula for real gases, and φi measures gas nonideality.
  5. An ideal solution follows the Lewis-Randall rule, while real solutions deviate and need activity coefficients.
  6. Excess properties and property changes of mixing show how far a real mixture is from ideal-solution behavior.
  7. Activity replaces mole fraction in the chemical potential, and γi = ai/xi is the practical measure of liquid nonideality.
  8. Margules, van Laar and Wilson models convert excess Gibbs energy information into activity coefficients for VLE calculations.

Understand Define chemical potential and state the phase-equilibrium criterion for a species present in two phases. Explain why the criterion uses μ rather than concentration. 8 marks

Points to cover. Definition of μ as partial molar Gibbs energy; Equality of μi in all phases at equilibrium; Derivation from dG at fixed T and P; Concentration alone is insufficient because μ includes standard state and nonideality.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. For a mixture of total Gibbs energy nG, the chemical potential of species i is μi = [∂(nG)/∂ni]T,P,nj: the rate at which the total Gibbs energy rises per mole of i added, with T, P and every other nj fixed. It is the partial molar Gibbs energy, Ḡi, in J/mol. For a pure substance it is simply the molar Gibbs energy.
  2. Open-system relation. With moles free to change, d(nG) = −(nS) dT + (nV) dP + Σ μi dni. At fixed T and P this reduces to d(nG) = Σ μi dni.

Points to cover. Equilibrium requires equal chemical potential; Equal fugacity for each component; General relation φiV yiP = γi xi fipure; Low-pressure approximations; Final modified Raoult's law.

Model answer.

  1. Equilibrium condition. Two phases at the same T and P are in equilibrium when μiv = μil for every i (minimum total Gibbs energy).
  2. Fugacity. Writing μi = Γi(T) + RT ln f̂i in each phase, the same Γi(T) appears, so the equality becomes f̂iv = f̂il: the isofugacity condition. Fugacity, with pressure units, is easier to handle than μ, which has no absolute value.
  3. Activity. In the liquid, ai = f̂i/fi°; with the pure liquid at T and P as standard state, f̂il = xiγi fi, and γi measures the departure from the Lewis-Randall rule.
  4. General relation (gamma-phi). φ̂i yi P = xi γi fi, with fi = φisat Pisat exp[Vil(P − Pisat)/RT].
  5. Low-pressure approximations. φ̂i → 1 and φisat → 1 (ideal vapour); the Poynting factor → 1 because Vil(P − Pisat) is small next to RT. For water at 100 °C compressed to 50 bar the factor is only 1.030, and at 1 to 2 bar it differs from 1 by less than 0.1 %.
  6. Modified Raoult's law. yiP = xiγiPisat; with γi = 1, Raoult's law.
  7. Worked check. At 1.00 bar, x₁ = 0.20, y₁ = 0.50 and P₁sat = 0.80 bar: a₁ = y₁P/P₁sat = 0.625 and γ₁ = a₁/x₁ = 3.125; with P₂sat = 0.60 bar, γ₂ = 0.50/(0.80 × 0.60) = 1.042.
  8. Evaluation. The gamma-phi route needs Pisat, a γ model and, at higher P, an equation of state for φ̂i. It works well below a few bar. Near the critical region the liquid and vapour become alike and a single equation of state for both phases (phi-phi, φ̂il xi = φ̂iv yi) is used instead. Errors from dropping φ̂i grow with pressure and are largest for associating vapours.
Evaluate Compare Margules, van Laar and Wilson activity coefficient models. State their assumptions, parameters, strengths and limitations for VLE calculations. 15 marks

Points to cover. Excess Gibbs energy forms for each model; Number of parameters for a binary system; Wilson uses local composition; Margules and van Laar are empirical; Limitations for liquid-liquid equilibrium and multicomponent use.

Model answer.

  1. Common basis. All three models write GE/RT as a function of composition and get ln γi = ∂(nGE/RT)/∂ni, so the two activity coefficients satisfy Gibbs-Duhem automatically.
  2. Margules. One-constant: GE/RT = A x₁x₂, ln γ₁ = A x₂², ln γ₂ = A x₁²; symmetric, γ₁∞ = γ₂∞ = eA. Two-constant: GE/(RT x₁x₂) = A₂₁x₁ + A₁₂x₂, with ln γ₁∞ = A₁₂, ln γ₂∞ = A₂₁. Empirical power series; easy to fit and differentiate; can show liquid-liquid splitting when A is large (A > 2 for the one-constant form).
  3. van Laar. GE/RT = A₁₂A₂₁x₁x₂/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂), ln γ₁ = A₁₂[A₂₁x₂/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂)]², ln γ₂ = A₂₁[A₁₂x₁/(A₁₂x₁ + A₂₁x₂)]²; ln γ₁∞ = A₁₂, ln γ₂∞ = A₂₁. Derived originally from the van der Waals equation, now used as a two-constant empirical fit. Good for non-polar mixtures of different sizes; it cannot give a maximum or minimum in ln γ and fails when A₁₂ and A₂₁ have opposite signs.
  4. Fitting from one point (van Laar). Azeotrope at x₁ = 0.40, 101.3 kPa, P₁sat = 70.0 kPa, P₂sat = 80.0 kPa:
    γ₁ = 101.3/70.0 = 1.4471, ln γ₁ = 0.3696
γ₂ = 101.3/80.0 = 1.2663, ln γ₂ = 0.2361
A₁₂ = 0.3696 (1 + 0.6 × 0.2361/(0.4 × 0.3696))² = 1.417
A₂₁ = 0.2361 (1 + 0.4 × 0.3696/(0.6 × 0.2361))² = 0.986
at x₁ = 0.10: ln γ₁ = 1.054, γ₁ = 2.87
  5. Wilson. GE/RT = −x₁ ln(x₁ + Λ₁₂x₂) − x₂ ln(x₂ + Λ₂₁x₁), with Λ₁₂ = (V₂/V₁) exp(−a₁₂/RT). Based on local composition: the mixture around a molecule differs from the bulk. Two constants; the energy parameters a₁₂, a₂₁ carry a built-in temperature dependence, and the model extends to multicomponent mixtures using only binary constants. It represents strongly non-ideal miscible systems (alcohols with hydrocarbons) well.
  6. Limitations. Wilson cannot predict liquid-liquid splitting at all; van Laar and Margules can, but poorly. Margules and van Laar constants are fitted at one temperature and do not extend well to multicomponent mixtures. All three are fitted to one binary pair, and extrapolation outside the fitted range can place azeotropes wrongly. For partially miscible systems NRTL or UNIQUAC are used.

Analyze Explain excess properties and property changes of mixing, and how the signs of HE and VE indicate non-ideality and heat effects. Illustrate with a mixture at 300 K and x₁ = 0.40 that has γ₁ = 1.60, γ₂ = 1.15 and HE = +800 J/mol. 13 marks

Points to cover. Definition ME = M − Mid; Definition ΔMmix = M − Σ xi Mipure; HE positive means endothermic mixing; HE negative means exothermic mixing; VE positive means expansion, negative means contraction.

Model answer.

  1. Property change of mixing. ΔMmix = M − Σ xi Mi, the change per mole when pure liquids at T and P form the mixture at the same T and P.
  2. Excess property. ME = M − Mid = ΔMmix − ΔMmixid, the part of the mixing change that an ideal solution would not have.
  3. Ideal parts. ΔHmixid = 0, ΔVmixid = 0, ΔUmixid = 0, ΔSmixid = −R Σ xi ln xi, ΔGmixid = RT Σ xi ln xi. So HE = ΔHmix and VE = ΔVmix, but GE ≠ ΔGmix and SE ≠ ΔSmix. GE = RT Σ xi ln γi links excess properties to activity coefficients; SE = (HE − GE)/T.
  4. Worked numbers (T = 300 K, x₁ = 0.40, γ₁ = 1.60, γ₂ = 1.15, HE = +800 J/mol; RT = 2494.2 J/mol):
    QuantityIdeal partExcess partMixing change
    G (J/mol)2494.2 × (−0.6730) = −16792494.2 × 0.2719 = 678−1001
    H (J/mol)0800800
    S (J/(mol·K))5.60(800 − 678)/300 = 0.416.00
  5. Sign of HE. HE > 0: mixing is endothermic, unlike contacts are weaker than like ones; heat must be supplied to keep T constant, Q = nHE. Here 5.0 mol needs Q = 5.0 × 800 = +4.0 kJ. HE < 0: exothermic mixing (stronger unlike contacts, such as hydrogen bonding between components); heat must be removed, which matters in acid dilution and solvent blending.
  6. Sign of VE. VE > 0: the mixture expands (VE = 2x₁x₂ cm³/mol gives +0.50 cm³/mol at x₁ = 0.5, +1.00 cm³ for 2.00 mol). VE < 0: contraction, as when ethanol and water are mixed. Tank and pipeline volumes based on adding pure volumes are then wrong.
  7. Reading non-ideality. Any non-zero excess property shows a non-ideal mixture. GE > 0 (here 678 J/mol) means γ > 1 and positive deviation from Raoult's law; the mixture is still stable because ΔGmix = −1001 J/mol is negative.
  8. Check. ΔGmix = ΔHmix − TΔSmix = 800 − 300 × 6.00 = −1000 J/mol, matching −1001 J/mol within rounding.

Glossary

Activity
A dimensionless effective fugacity, ai = fi/fi⁰, used in the chemical-potential expression.
Activity coefficient
The ratio γi = ai/xi that measures departure from ideal-solution behavior.
Azeotrope
A mixture composition at which vapor and liquid have the same composition and cannot be separated by simple distillation.
Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs energy of a species, written μi, which determines its equilibrium behavior.
Consistency test
A check that experimental activity coefficients obey the Gibbs-Duhem relation.
Excess property
The difference between a real mixture property and its ideal-solution value at the same T, P and composition.
Fugacity
A corrected pressure that makes the chemical-potential formula valid for real gases and liquids.
Fugacity coefficient
The ratio of fugacity to pressure, or to yiP for a component of a gas mixture; it tends to 1 at low pressure.
Gibbs-Duhem equation
A relation showing that changes in partial molar properties in a mixture at fixed T and P are not independent.
Henry's law
For a dilute solute, pi = xi kH, where kH is the Henry constant.
Ideal solution
A solution that obeys the Lewis-Randall rule and has zero excess volume and zero heat of mixing.
Infinite-dilution activity coefficient
The limiting activity coefficient of a component as its mole fraction goes to zero.
Lewis-Randall rule
The statement fi = xi fipure for an ideal solution.
Margules equation
An excess Gibbs energy model that expresses activity coefficients as a polynomial in mole fractions.
Modified Raoult's law
The low-pressure VLE relation yiP = γi xi Pisat for nonideal liquids.
Partial molar property
The contribution of one mole of a component to a mixture property at fixed T, P and other amounts.
Property change of mixing
The difference between a solution property and the sum of pure-component properties in the same proportions.
Raoult's law
For an ideal liquid mixture at low pressure, pi = xi Pisat.
Residual property
The difference between a real-fluid property and its ideal-gas value at the same T and P.
Standard state
The reference state chosen for chemical potential and activity at a given T and P.
van Laar equation
A two-parameter excess Gibbs energy model for binary activity coefficients.
Vapor-liquid equilibrium
The condition in which vapor and liquid phases have equal chemical potential for every component.
Wilson equation
A local-composition activity coefficient model with binary parameters Λij.
