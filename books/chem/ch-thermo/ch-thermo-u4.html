4.1 Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies

A system held at a fixed temperature cannot turn all of its energy into work: the part TS is tied up with entropy. Subtracting it gives two free energies, the Helmholtz energy A = U − TS and the Gibbs energy G = H − TS. At fixed T and V, −ΔA is the largest total work a change can deliver; at fixed T and P, −ΔG is the largest work other than expansion work, such as electrical work from a cell. Industry uses G for reactors, separators and phase changes at constant pressure, and A for constant-volume and molecular calculations. This chapter defines A and G, derives their differentials and equilibrium tests, and works an ideal-gas compression and the boiling of water.

You need: internal energy and enthalpy from the first-law chapters, and entropy from the second-law chapters. The full set of four differentials is in fundamental property relations.

What A and G measure

A system at temperature T holds internal energy U. Part of U is tied to the random motion counted by entropy S: the product TS. Subtract that part and the remainder is Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS. The same idea at constant pressure starts from enthalpy H = U + PV, so Gibbs free energy G = H − TS = U + PV − TS. Here U is internal energy (J), H is enthalpy (J), T is absolute temperature (K), S is entropy (J/K), P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³). A and G carry units of J; per mole they are J/mol.

The subtraction TS removes energy that cannot become work in a reversible isothermal step. A is used when volume is fixed, as in a rigid bomb or a statistical-mechanics lattice. G is used when pressure is fixed, which is the usual laboratory, reactor and pipe condition. Many books write F for the Helmholtz energy; F and A mean the same property. A and G are state functions, because U, H, T and S are, and they are extensive: they double when the amount of material doubles, and for a system of several phases they add up phase by phase.

Differentials and equilibrium tests

For a closed system with only pressure-volume work, dU = T dS − P dV. Differentiate A = U − TS: dA = dU − T dS − S dT. Put dU in: dA = −S dT − P dV. Differentiate G = H − TS with dH = T dS + V dP: dG = −S dT + V dP. These are exact differentials for A(T,V) and G(T,P). The variables in brackets are the natural variables: T and V for A, T and P for G.

dA = −S dT − P dV

A is Helmholtz free energy (J); S entropy (J/K); T absolute temperature (K); P pressure (Pa); V volume (m³).

dG = −S dT + V dP

G is Gibbs free energy (J); the other symbols are defined above.

At constant T and V, the second law gives dA ≤ 0 for a spontaneous change in a closed system with only PV work; dA = 0 at equilibrium. At constant T and P, dG ≤ 0, and dG = 0 at equilibrium. The inequality is the practical test: a process at fixed T and P proceeds only if G falls. Work interpretation: at constant T and V, the maximum work obtainable is −ΔA; at constant T and P, the maximum non-PV useful work obtainable is −ΔG.

[∂(G/T)/∂T]P = −H/T²

the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation; G is Gibbs energy (J), H enthalpy (J), T absolute temperature (K).

Derivation: by the quotient rule, [∂(G/T)/∂T]P = (1/T)(∂G/∂T)P − G/T². From dG, (∂G/∂T)P = −S, so the right side is −S/T − G/T² = −(TS + G)/T² = −H/T², because G + TS = H. The equation lets you move a ΔG known at one temperature to another using ΔH alone. For example, a reaction with ΔG = −20.0 kJ and ΔH = −50.0 kJ at 298.15 K, with ΔH taken as constant, has ΔG/T at 350 K = −20 000/298.15 + (−50 000)(1/350 − 1/298.15) = −67.08 + 24.84 = −42.24 J/K, so ΔG(350 K) = −14.8 kJ: still spontaneous, but less so.

Signs and the isothermal ideal-gas result

For an isothermal process on an ideal gas, ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0. Then ΔA = −TΔS and ΔG = −TΔS, so ΔA = ΔG. The entropy change for n moles going from P1 to P2 at constant T is ΔS = nR ln(P1/P2). Therefore ΔA = ΔG = nRT ln(P2/P1). Here n is moles (mol), R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is in K, and P1 and P2 are initial and final pressures in the same unit.

Compression has P2 > P1, so ln(P2/P1) > 0 and both ΔA and ΔG are positive. Positive means the surroundings must supply work; the gas does not release it. Expansion gives a negative value. For a real gas or a phase change ΔA and ΔG differ, because ΔU and ΔH are not zero. In general ΔA = ΔU − Δ(TS); only when T is constant does it become ΔU − TΔS, and likewise ΔG = ΔH − TΔS at constant T. At constant T and P a negative ΔG marks a spontaneous phase change or reaction; chemical potential, which extends this to mixtures, belongs to solution thermodynamics.

Worked example

Question. One mole of ideal gas at 300 K is compressed isothermally and reversibly from 1 bar to 5 bar. Calculate ΔA and ΔG. Treat the gas as ideal.

  1. For an ideal gas at constant T, ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0.
  2. The entropy change is ΔS = nR ln(P1/P2) = 1 × 8.314 × ln(1/5) = 8.314 × (−1.6094) = −13.38 J/K.
  3. Then TΔS = 300 × (−13.38) = −4014 J.
  4. ΔA = ΔU − TΔS = 0 − (−4014) = +4014 J.
  5. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = 0 − (−4014) = +4014 J.
  6. As a check, ΔG = nRT ln(P2/P1) = 1 × 8.314 × 300 × ln(5/1) = +4014 J.

Answer. ΔA = ΔG = +4.01 kJ, so work must be supplied to compress the gas.

Worked example 2

Question. One mole of water boils at 1 atm (101.325 kPa) and its normal boiling point, 373.12 K. Steam tables give ΔHvap = 40.65 kJ/mol and ΔV = Vg − Vl = 0.03013 m³/mol. Find ΔS, ΔG, ΔU and ΔA, and say what −ΔA means here.

  1. At the normal boiling point liquid and vapour are in equilibrium at fixed T and P, so the change is reversible: ΔS = ΔH/T = 40 650/373.12 = 108.9 J/K.
  2. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = 40 650 − 40 650 = 0, since TΔS = ΔH for this reversible change. Equilibrium at fixed T and P means ΔG = 0.
  3. Expansion work: PΔV = 101 325 × 0.03013 = 3053 J = 3.05 kJ.
  4. ΔU = ΔH − PΔV = 40.65 − 3.05 = 37.60 kJ.
  5. ΔA = ΔU − TΔS = 37.60 − 40.65 = −3.05 kJ, which equals −PΔV.
  6. Meaning: at constant T the largest total work is −ΔA = 3.05 kJ, and all of it is the expansion work of the steam pushing back the atmosphere. There is no non-expansion work to be had, which is what ΔG = 0 says.

Answer. ΔS = 108.9 J/K, ΔG = 0, ΔU = 37.60 kJ, ΔA = −3.05 kJ; the 3.05 kJ is the expansion work of the vapour.

Exam traps

  • Writing G = U − TS instead of G = H − TS. Check that G = U + PV − TS; the PV term is missing in the wrong form.
  • Using Celsius in T. Every free-energy formula uses absolute temperature in kelvin.
  • Losing the sign for compression. If P2 > P1, ln(P2/P1) is positive, so ΔA and ΔG are positive; expansion gives negative values.
  • Swapping the equilibrium tests. dA ≤ 0 belongs to constant T,V; dG ≤ 0 belongs to constant T,P.
  • Forgetting that ΔA = ΔG is special to an ideal gas at constant T. For a real gas or a phase change, at constant T, ΔA = ΔU − TΔS and ΔG = ΔH − TΔS are different numbers; for boiling water at 1 atm, ΔG = 0 but ΔA = −3.05 kJ/mol.
  • Using ΔA = ΔU − TΔS when the temperature changes. The general form is ΔA = ΔU − Δ(TS) = ΔU − (T₂S₂ − T₁S₁).

4.2 Fundamental property relations

The first law gives energy changes; the second law gives entropy changes. The four fundamental property relations combine both laws into exact differentials for internal energy U, enthalpy H, Helmholtz free energy A and Gibbs free energy G. Each differential names the natural variables of one potential and lets you read derivatives directly from its coefficients. Chemical engineers use these relations to compute property changes from pressure–volume–temperature data, design compressors and heat exchangers, and build the property packages inside process simulators.

You need the first law for closed systems, equations of state from the PVT-behaviour chapters, and entropy from the second-law chapters; the free energies are defined in Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies.

From two laws to four exact differentials

Take a closed, simple compressible system: no chemical reaction, no shaft work, negligible kinetic and potential energy. For a reversible path the system pressure equals the external pressure P and the system temperature equals the bath temperature T. The first law is dU = δQ − δW. The second law for a reversible path gives δQ = T dS. The only work is P dV, so δW = P dV. Therefore dU = T dS − P dV.

Define the other potentials by Legendre transforms: H = U + PV, A = U − TS, and G = H − TS = U + PV − TS. Differentiate each definition and substitute dU. The P dV and T dS terms cancel in pairs.

dU = T dS − P dV; dH = T dS + V dP; dA = −S dT − P dV; dG = −S dT + V dP

U is internal energy (J), H enthalpy (J), A Helmholtz free energy (J), G Gibbs free energy (J). T is temperature (K), S entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³).

Each left side is an exact differential; each right side contains only the natural variables of that potential. They are also called the thermodynamic property relations.

Natural variables and coefficient identities

An exact differential dz = M dx + N dy has M = (∂z/∂x)y and N = (∂z/∂y)x. Compare this with each relation. The comparison identifies the natural variables and the coefficient identities.

PotentialExact differentialNatural variablesRead-off identities
UdU = T dS − P dVS, V(∂U/∂S)V = T; (∂U/∂V)S = −P
HdH = T dS + V dPS, P(∂H/∂S)P = T; (∂H/∂P)S = V
AdA = −S dT − P dVT, V(∂A/∂T)V = −S; (∂A/∂V)T = −P
GdG = −S dT + V dPT, P(∂G/∂T)P = −S; (∂G/∂P)T = V

The subscript on each derivative says what is held constant. For example, (∂U/∂S)V = T means entropy changes at fixed volume; (∂U/∂V)S = −P means volume changes at fixed entropy. A partial derivative is not a simple fraction: (∂U/∂S)V is not dU divided by dS in the ordinary sense.

Equality of mixed second derivatives of these potentials gives the Maxwell relations (see Maxwell relations). That step replaces entropy derivatives by measurable pressure, volume and temperature derivatives.

Integrating the relations for property changes

Because U, H, A and G are state functions, integrate along any convenient reversible path between the same two states. Choose a path that matches the natural variables. At constant T, dA = −P dV and dG = V dP; at constant S, dU = −P dV and dH = V dP.

For an ideal gas, U and H depend only on T. Per mole, dU = CV dT and dH = CP dT, where CV and CP are the molar heat capacities at constant volume and pressure (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹). Molar entropy then follows from dS = CV dT/T + R dV/V, or dS = CP dT/T − R dP/P, with V the molar volume and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; every term is in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. For n moles multiply by n. For a real gas use the measurable slope (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V from the equation of state, as in worked example 2.

At constant temperature, a useful identity for real gases is (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P. It follows by dividing dU = T dS − P dV by dV at fixed T and using the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V from Maxwell relations. It turns a hard entropy derivative into a measurable PVT derivative.

Worked example

Question. A van der Waals gas obeys P = RT/(V − b) − a/V², with a = 0.364 Pa m⁶ mol⁻². At T = 300 K and V = 0.200 m³ mol⁻¹, calculate (∂U/∂V)T.

  1. Start from the coefficient identity (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P.
  2. Differentiate P with respect to T at fixed V. The term RT/(V − b) gives R/(V − b); the term −a/V² has no T, so its derivative is zero.
  3. Then T(∂P/∂T)V = RT/(V − b).
  4. Subtract P: T(∂P/∂T)V − P = RT/(V − b) − [RT/(V − b) − a/V²] = a/V².
  5. Insert a = 0.364 Pa m⁶ mol⁻² and V = 0.200 m³ mol⁻¹. V² = 0.0400 m⁶ mol⁻².
  6. a/V² = 0.364/0.0400 = 9.1 Pa. The constants b and T cancel, so the result does not depend on their values here.
  7. The derivative is positive: at fixed T, U rises as V rises for this van der Waals gas.

Answer. (∂U/∂V)T = 9.1 Pa.

Worked example 2

Question. One mole of CO2, treated as a van der Waals gas (a = 0.364 Pa m⁶ mol⁻², b = 4.267 × 10⁻⁵ m³ mol⁻¹), expands isothermally at 300 K from V₁ = 1.00 × 10⁻³ to V₂ = 1.00 × 10⁻² m³ mol⁻¹. Find ΔU, ΔS and ΔA, and compare with an ideal gas.

  1. Internal energy: (∂U/∂V)T = a/V² (worked example 1), so ΔU = ∫ a/V² dV = a(1/V₁ − 1/V₂) = 0.364 × (1000 − 100) = 327.6 J/mol.
  2. Entropy: (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V = R/(V − b), so ΔS = R ln[(V₂ − b)/(V₁ − b)].
  3. V₂ − b = 0.0100 − 0.0000427 = 0.0099573; V₁ − b = 0.0010 − 0.0000427 = 0.0009573; ratio 10.401.
  4. ΔS = 8.314 × ln 10.401 = 8.314 × 2.3419 = 19.47 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  5. ΔA = ΔU − TΔS = 327.6 − 300 × 19.47 = 327.6 − 5841 = −5514 J/mol.
  6. Ideal gas between the same volumes: ΔU = 0, ΔS = R ln 10 = 19.14 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔA = −5743 J/mol. The real gas gains 327.6 J/mol of internal energy because the molecules move apart against their attraction.
  7. Check of the size of the effect: the pressure falls from 22.4 bar to 2.47 bar, so the gas is only mildly non-ideal and the corrections are a few per cent.

Answer. ΔU = +327.6 J/mol, ΔS = 19.47 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔA = −5514 J/mol (ideal gas: 0, 19.14 and −5743).

Exam traps

  • Mixing up natural variables: U is natural in (S,V), not (T,V). H is natural in (S,P), not (T,P).
  • Using the wrong sign for P dV: dU = T dS − P dV and dA = −S dT − P dV both carry a minus sign on P dV.
  • Treating a partial derivative as a simple fraction: (∂U/∂V)S cannot be cancelled with dV.
  • Using dG = −S dT + V dP for a mixture whose composition changes. The full relation then needs chemical-potential terms, which belong to solution thermodynamics.
  • Confusing (∂U/∂V)T with (∂H/∂P)T; they are different derivatives and different identities.

4.3 Maxwell relations and their applications

A Maxwell relation is an equality between two partial derivatives of a thermodynamic potential. It exists because U, H, A and G are state functions, so their mixed second derivatives are equal. The equalities replace an entropy derivative, which is hard to measure, with a pressure, volume or temperature derivative, which comes from PVT data. Engineers use them to find entropy changes, enthalpy changes and the Joule–Thomson coefficient.

You need the exact differentials from fundamental property relations, ideal-gas entropy from the second-law chapters, and the free energies from Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies.

Where the four relations come from

For a state function F of two variables x and y, dF = M dx + N dy is exact. Exactness means (∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y. Apply this test to each fundamental differential from fundamental property relations.

Symbols: U internal energy (J), H enthalpy (J), A Helmholtz energy (J), G Gibbs energy (J), T absolute temperature (K), S entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³). The definitions are H = U + PV, A = U − TS, G = H − TS.

  • dU = T dS − P dV gives (∂T/∂V)S = −(∂P/∂S)V.
  • dH = T dS + V dP gives (∂T/∂P)S = (∂V/∂S)P.
  • dA = −S dT − P dV gives (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V.
  • dG = −S dT + V dP gives (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P.

Each line is one Maxwell relation. In the dA and dG relations entropy appears in the numerator of the left side, so those two convert an entropy slope into a P–V–T slope. In the dU and dH relations entropy stays in the derivative, so they are less useful for that job.

What the relations buy you

The dG relation is the workhorse for entropy changes with pressure at fixed temperature. For an ideal gas, V = nRT/P, so (∂V/∂T)P = nR/P. Here n is amount (mol) and R is the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

(∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P

S is entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³), T temperature (K); the subscript T means temperature is held constant.

At constant T, integrate from P1 to P2. For an ideal gas, ΔS = −nR ln(P2/P1). Compression (P2 > P1) lowers entropy because the gas has less volume to occupy.

(∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P

H is enthalpy (J); the other symbols are as above. Substitute the dG Maxwell relation into dH = T dS + V dP at constant T.

The Joule–Thomson coefficient μJT = (∂T/∂P)H follows from the same enthalpy slope. Since dH = Cp dT + (∂H/∂P)T dP at constant H, μJT = [T(∂V/∂T)P − V]/Cp. Cp is heat capacity at constant pressure (J/K). An ideal gas has V = nRT/P, so T(∂V/∂T)P = V and μJT = 0.

Reading the derivatives correctly

The variable held constant is part of the derivative. (∂V/∂T)P fixes pressure, not entropy or volume. Dropping the subscript changes the value of the derivative, so always state the variable held constant.

The dA relation can be more convenient when volume data are available: at constant T, ΔS = ∫(∂P/∂T)V dV. For an ideal gas, P = nRT/V, so (∂P/∂T)V = nR/V and ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1). This is the same entropy change written with volume ratio.

For a real gas, do not replace (∂V/∂T)P by nR/P. Use measured or equation-of-state values of V and its temperature slope. For a liquid, write (∂V/∂T)P = Vα with α the thermal expansion coefficient, as in worked example 2. Residual-property formulas built from these derivatives are in residual properties.

Worked example

Question. Use a Maxwell relation to calculate ΔS when 2 mol of ideal gas at 300 K is compressed from 1 bar to 10 bar at constant temperature.

  1. Start from dG = −S dT + V dP. Exactness gives (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P.
  2. For an ideal gas PV = nRT, so V = nRT/P. At constant P, (∂V/∂T)P = nR/P.
  3. Substitute: (∂S/∂P)T = −nR/P.
  4. Integrate at constant T from P1 = 1 bar to P2 = 10 bar: ΔS = −nR ln(P2/P1).
  5. Put n = 2 mol and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. ln(10) = 2.3026. nR = 16.628 J K⁻¹. Product = 16.628 × 2.3026 = 38.29 J/K.
  6. Apply the negative sign: ΔS = −38.29 J/K, which rounds to −38.3 J/K. The 300 K value does not enter the ideal-gas pressure formula.

Answer. ΔS = −38.3 J/K for the compression.

Worked example 2

Question. Liquid water at 25 °C (298.15 K) is pumped from 1 bar to 100 bar. Its molar volume is V = 18.07 × 10⁻⁶ m³ mol⁻¹ and its thermal expansion coefficient α = 2.573 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹; take both as constant. Use Maxwell relations to find ΔS and ΔH, and compare with the steam-table values (−0.0469 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ and 164.5 J/mol).

  1. Maxwell relation from dG: (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P = −Vα.
  2. ΔS = −VαΔP = −18.07 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.573 × 10⁻⁴ × 99 × 10⁵ = −0.0460 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  3. Enthalpy slope: (∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P = V(1 − Tα).
  4. 1 − Tα = 1 − 298.15 × 2.573 × 10⁻⁴ = 1 − 0.0767 = 0.9233.
  5. ΔH = V(1 − Tα)ΔP = 18.07 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.9233 × 99 × 10⁵ = 165.2 J/mol.

4.4 Residual properties

A residual property is the gap between a real-fluid property and its ideal-gas value at the same temperature and pressure. Ideal-gas formulas alone give wrong enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy for a real gas; the residual isolates the deviation. Found from an equation of state or a generalized correlation and added to the ideal-gas value, it gives compressor power, throttle effects, heat-exchanger duties and fugacity coefficients. This chapter shows the defining integrals and works them for carbon dioxide with the virial equation.

You need the exact differentials of fundamental property relations, the Maxwell relation of Maxwell relations, and the compressibility factor Z and the virial equation from the PVT-behaviour chapters.

Definition at fixed T and P

At temperature T and pressure P, a real fluid has molar property M. The ideal gas at the same T and P has molar property Mig. The residual property is MR = M − Mig. M can be V, U, H, S, G or A; the units follow M. The ideal gas at the same T and P is a reference, not a second real state.

MR = M − Mig

M is any molar property at T and P; Mig is the ideal-gas value at the same T and P; MR is the residual property.

For an ideal gas, Uig and Hig depend only on T. Sig and Gig also depend on P, through Sig(T,P) = Sig(T,P°) − R ln(P/P°) and Gig = Hig − T Sig. The pressure terms cancel in some residuals and remain in others. Every residual property of an ideal gas is zero. As P → 0, HR, SR, GR, AR and UR tend to zero. VR is different: VR = (Z − 1)RT/P tends to B, the second virial coefficient, a finite value, because V and Vig both diverge while their difference stays finite.

VR = (Z − 1)RT/P

VR is residual molar volume (m³/mol); Z = PV/RT is compressibility factor; R = 8.314 J/(mol K); T is temperature (K); P is pressure (Pa).

Negative VR means the real gas occupies less volume than the ideal gas at the same T and P. Attractive forces usually cause this at moderate pressure.

Residual Gibbs energy and fugacity

For a pure fluid, the fugacity coefficient is φ = f/P, where f is fugacity and P is pressure. Solution thermodynamics develops φ fully. The residual Gibbs energy is tied to it by GR = RT ln φ. The integrated form uses the compressibility factor Z = PV/RT.

GR/RT = ln φ = ∫₀P (Z − 1) dP/P

GR is residual molar Gibbs energy (J/mol); R = 8.314 J/(mol K); T is absolute temperature (K); φ is fugacity coefficient; Z = PV/RT; P is pressure (Pa).

At P → 0, (Z − 1)/P tends to a finite limit, the pressure virial coefficient B′, so the integral remains finite. If φ < 1, then ln φ < 0 and GR is negative: the real gas has lower Gibbs energy than the ideal gas at that T and P. If φ > 1, GR is positive; φ = 1 gives GR = 0. This sign check catches many calculation errors. The same integral also gives the fugacity coefficient used in phase equilibrium, in solution thermodynamics and phase equilibria.

Enthalpy, entropy and volume residuals

At constant T, the fundamental relation for enthalpy gives dH = [V − T(∂V/∂T)P] dP. Integrate from zero pressure, where the gas is ideal, to pressure P.

HR = ∫₀P [V − T(∂V/∂T)P] dP

HR is residual molar enthalpy (J/mol); V is molar volume (m³/mol); T is temperature (K); (∂V/∂T)P is the temperature derivative of V at constant pressure; P is pressure (Pa).

For an ideal gas, V = RT/P and T(∂V/∂T)P = RT/P. The bracket is zero, so HR = 0, as required. For a cubic equation of state, substitute V(T,P) and integrate. The result is a closed-form departure function.

Entropy follows from dS = −(∂V/∂T)P dP at constant T. With MR = M − Mig, the residual is:

SR = ∫₀P [R/P − (∂V/∂T)P] dP

SR is residual molar entropy (J/(mol K)); R = 8.314 J/(mol K); P is pressure (Pa); V is molar volume (m³/mol); (∂V/∂T)P is at constant pressure.

The ideal-gas part is R/P, so the integrand vanishes for an ideal gas. The three residuals are consistent through GR = HR − T SR. Cubic equations of state such as van der Waals, Redlich-Kwong and Peng-Robinson supply V(T,P) and its temperature derivative. Generalized correlations use reduced temperature Tr = T/Tc, reduced pressure Pr = P/Pc and acentric factor ω. They tabulate HR/RTc and SR/R. The values feed compressor power, valve throttling and heat-exchanger duties.

With the two-term virial equation Z = 1 + BP/RT, where B(T) is the second virial coefficient, the integrals close at once: GR = BP, HR = P(B − T dB/dT) and SR = −P dB/dT. For a gas without B data, the Pitzer correlation of Abbott gives BPc/RTc = B⁰ + ωB¹ with B⁰ = 0.083 − 0.422/Tr1.6 and B¹ = 0.139 − 0.172/Tr4.2, valid at low to moderate reduced pressure.

4.5 Property tables and diagrams; two-phase systems

In a two-phase region steam is part liquid and part vapour. Its properties are not ideal-gas values. Steam tables list saturated liquid and saturated vapour; a single number, quality, fixes the mixture. The same idea powers the steam plant and the refrigerator: you read two properties, locate the state, and calculate the rest.

You need enthalpy from the first-law chapters, phase diagrams and latent heat from the PVT-behaviour chapters, and the steam cycles of the second-law chapters.

Saturated states and quality

At a given pressure, water boils at one temperature, the saturation temperature Tsat. The liquid just before boiling is saturated liquid. Its properties carry subscript f: vf [m³/kg], hf [kJ/kg], sf [kJ/(kg·K)]. The vapour just after boiling is saturated vapour, subscript g. Between them is a wet mixture.

Quality x is the mass fraction of vapour: x = mg/(mf + mg). mf is mass of saturated liquid [kg]; mg is mass of saturated vapour [kg]. x = 0 is saturated liquid, x = 1 is saturated vapour, 0 < x < 1 is wet mixture.

M = (1 − x)Mf + xMg = Mf + x(Mg − Mf)

M stands for v, u, h or s per kg; Mf is the saturated-liquid value; Mg is the saturated-vapour value; x is quality.

h = hf + x hfg,  v = vf + x(vg − vf)

hfg = hg − hf is latent heat of vaporisation [kJ/kg]; vg − vf is the volume change on vaporisation [m³/kg]. Use hfg, never hg, in the second term.

Above the saturation temperature at the same pressure the vapour is superheated. Read its properties from the superheated table, not from the saturated columns. The degree of superheat is T − Tsat [K or °C].

Reading a steam table and interpolating

  1. Compare the given T with Tsat at the given P, or the given P with Psat at the given T. This tells you liquid, wet mixture or superheated vapour.
  2. For a wet mixture, use one known property to find x. For example x = (h − hf)/hfg or x = (v − vf)/(vg − vf).
  3. Use x in M = Mf + x(Mg − Mf) for every other property. Do not use x for temperature or pressure; both stay at saturation.
  4. For superheated vapour, enter the superheated table at the given P and T. If the table has no exact T or P, interpolate.

y = y₁ + (z − z₁)(y₂ − y₁)/(z₂ − z₁)

z is the known value (T, P or h); y is the wanted property; (z₁, y₁) and (z₂, y₂) are the two table points that bracket z. Keep x for quality only, never for the interpolation variable.

Interpolate only between two rows of the same table region. Interpolating between saturated liquid and saturated vapour rows with T or P gives nonsense, because both rows share the same T and P.

Diagrams: P-v, T-s, h-s and P-h

A property diagram plots two properties so a cycle becomes a path. The saturation dome is the two-phase region. Under the dome, an isobar is also an isotherm: at fixed P, T stays at Tsat until the last liquid evaporates. That horizontal line is the easiest feature to spot.

On a T-s diagram the area under a reversible path is heat. On an h-s diagram, the Mollier chart, a vertical drop is an isentropic turbine or nozzle, so it is used for steam turbines. On a P-h diagram, an isenthalpic throttle is a vertical line, so it is used for refrigeration and heat pumps.

Quality lines run across the dome. The critical point closes the dome; above it there is no liquid-vapour split. They are also called thermodynamic property charts. Use them for quick reads and for checking table values, not as a replacement for the table when an exact number is needed.

4.6 Clausius-Clapeyron equation

The Clausius–Clapeyron equation gives the slope of a saturation curve from latent heat and volume change. It answers a practical question: water boils at 373 K at 1 atm, so what is its vapour pressure at 350 K? One measured (P, T) pair plus ΔH gives another. A plot of ln P against 1/T gives ΔH. Chemical engineers use it for steam, refrigerants, distillation and vacuum drying.

You need two-phase properties from property tables and diagrams, the equality of Gibbs energy at equilibrium from Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies, and phase diagrams and latent heat from the PVT-behaviour chapters.

The Clapeyron equation

At saturation, liquid and vapour coexist. The saturation curve Psat(T) has a slope. Equality of Gibbs free energy of the two phases gives the Clapeyron equation:

dPsat/dT = ΔH/(T ΔV)

Psat is saturation pressure (Pa), T is absolute temperature (K), ΔH is latent heat (J/mol), and ΔV = Vg − Vl is molar volume change (m³/mol).

Derivation: along the coexistence curve Gl = Gg, so a small step keeps dGl = dGg: −Sl dT + Vl dP = −Sg dT + Vg dP. Rearranging, dP/dT = (Sg − Sl)/(Vg − Vl) = ΔS/ΔV, and at equilibrium ΔS = ΔH/T.

A large ΔH makes the curve steep. A large ΔV makes it flatter. For vaporisation ΔV > 0, so dPsat/dT > 0. For ice melting, ΔV = Vliquid − Vsolid < 0, so dPsat/dT < 0: the melting point falls as pressure rises, but only slightly, by about 0.0074 K per bar (worked example 2). The same equation also follows from the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V of Maxwell relations applied across the two-phase region, where ∂S/∂V = ΔS/ΔV = ΔH/(TΔV). The equation is exact for a pure substance at coexistence; no ideal-gas assumption is used yet. ΔH is the enthalpy change from phase 1 to phase 2; its sign follows the direction. For vaporisation, ΔH is positive and the pressure rises with temperature. It is the starting point for the ideal-vapour form below.

Ideal-vapour form and the straight line

For liquid–vapour saturation away from the critical point, Vg ≫ Vl, so ΔV ≈ Vg. Treat the vapour as ideal: Vg = RT/Psat, with R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Substitution gives:

dPsat/Psat = (ΔH/R) dT/T²

Psat is saturation pressure, T is absolute temperature, ΔH is molar latent heat, and R is the gas constant.

Integrate from (P1, T1) to (P2, T2). For constant ΔH:

ln(P2/P1) = −(ΔH/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1)

P1 and P2 are saturation pressures in the same unit, T1 and T2 are in K, ΔH is in J/mol, and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Equivalently, ln Psat = A − ΔH/(R T). A plot of ln Psat against 1/T is a straight line with slope −ΔH/R. Two measured points give ΔH = −R ln(P2/P1)/(1/T2 − 1/T1). To estimate P2, use P2 = P1 exp[−(ΔH/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1)]. Convert T to kelvin and keep pressures in one unit. The figure shows water with ΔHvap = 40.65 kJ/mol. Accuracy: the true vapour pressure of water at 350 K is 41.7 kPa, so the estimate of worked example 1 (42.8 kPa) is 2.7 % high, mainly because ΔHvap grows as the temperature falls.

4.7 Relations for Cp, Cv and the heat capacity ratio

CP − CV is the extra heat per kelvin a substance needs at constant pressure, because it also expands against its surroundings. The difference follows from the equation of state through two PVT derivatives: the thermal expansion coefficient α and the isothermal compressibility κT. Second derivatives of the equation of state then give how CP and CV change with pressure and volume, and the ratio γ = CP/CV follows. These relations turn table data into heat capacities for liquids and real gases.

You need the Maxwell relations of Maxwell relations, the coefficient identities of fundamental property relations, heat capacities from the first-law chapters and equations of state from the PVT-behaviour chapters.

Cp − Cv from PVT data

At constant volume, heat raises internal energy only. At constant pressure, heat also pays for expansion work and for pulling the molecules apart. So CP ≥ CV, with equality only when α = 0, as for liquid water at about 4 °C, where its density is greatest. The gap depends on how fast volume grows with temperature and how hard the fluid is to compress.

Let v be molar volume in m³ mol⁻¹, T temperature in K, α thermal expansion coefficient in K⁻¹, and κT isothermal compressibility in Pa⁻¹.

Cp − Cv = T v α² / κT = −T (∂P/∂T)V² / (∂P/∂V)T

Cp and Cv are molar heat capacities in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; α = (1/v)(∂v/∂T)P; κT = −(1/v)(∂v/∂P)T.

For an ideal gas α = 1/T and κT = 1/P. Substitution gives Cp − Cv = R, where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Read T and v from the problem.
  2. Look up α and κT in tables.
  3. Compute Tvα²/κT in consistent SI units.

For liquids α is small and κT is very small, so CP − CV is only about 1 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water, not R. For a dense gas the difference rises above R, as worked example 2 shows for CO2.

Ratio and pressure or volume dependence

Define γ = Cp/Cv. For ideal monatomic gas γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.67; for ideal diatomic gas γ = 7/5 = 1.40 at ordinary temperature.

Heat capacities are not truly constant. Their slopes come from second derivatives of the equation of state:

(∂Cp/∂P)T = −T (∂²v/∂T²)P

v is molar volume; the derivative is at fixed T.

(∂Cv/∂v)T = T (∂²P/∂T²)v

Cv is molar heat capacity at constant volume.

For an ideal gas both second derivatives are zero, so CP and CV depend only on T. For a real gas or liquid they drift with pressure or volume.

A common use is checking whether tabulated Cp at 1 bar can be used at 50 bar; integrate the pressure derivative.

Worked example

Question. Liquid water at 300 K and 1 bar has v = 18.08 × 10⁻⁶ m³ mol⁻¹, α = 2.75 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ and κT = 4.51 × 10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹ (IAPWS values). Calculate CP − CV, and γ given CP = 75.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

4.8 Partial derivatives and the Jacobian method

Every thermodynamic quantity you can measure sits inside a partial derivative, and most of those derivatives are not measurable directly. The Jacobian method turns any such derivative into a fixed recipe: write it as a ratio of two Jacobians, expand both in one chosen pair of variables such as (T, P), and read off pressure, volume, temperature and heat-capacity terms. It removes the guesswork from property relations.

You need the four relations of fundamental property relations, the Maxwell relations of Maxwell relations, the heat capacities of CP and CV relations and the ideal-gas law.

The Jacobian and its one key identity

The Jacobian of two functions u(x, y) and v(x, y) is the determinant of the 2×2 matrix of their first partial derivatives.

J(u, v) = ∂(u, v)/∂(x, y) = (∂u/∂x)y(∂v/∂y)x − (∂u/∂y)x(∂v/∂x)y

u and v are any two state functions; x and y are the two independent variables chosen to describe the state; the subscript is the variable held constant.

Swapping the two functions flips the sign, J(u, v) = −J(v, u), and a repeated function gives zero, J(u, u) = 0. Everything else is bookkeeping.

(∂u/∂x)y = J(u, y)/J(x, y)

the held-constant variable y appears in both Jacobians. A partial derivative has become a genuine ratio of two determinants.

That identity is the whole method. Partials can now be multiplied and cancelled like fractions, but only in this Jacobian form, never as du/dx.

Cyclic relation and Maxwell relations as ratios

Any target derivative is handled in four steps.

  1. Rewrite the target as J(u, y)/J(x, y), with y the variable held constant.
  2. Pick one pair of variables, (T, P) or (T, V), and expand every Jacobian in that same pair.
  3. Cancel the common pair; only P, V, T and heat capacities remain.
  4. Replace any leftover entropy derivative with a Maxwell relation.

The cyclic relation drops out for free. For any three variables x, y, z:

(∂x/∂y)z (∂y/∂z)x (∂z/∂x)y = −1

each factor is a ratio of two Jacobians in the same pair, and the three ratios cancel to (−1)³ = −1. The minus sign carries the whole content.

Maxwell relations are Jacobian ratios too. (∂S/∂V)T = J(S, T)/J(V, T); expanding both in (T, P) gives (∂S/∂P)T/(∂V/∂P)T, and the dG Maxwell relation turns this into −(∂V/∂T)P/(∂V/∂P)T = (∂P/∂T)V, as in worked example 1. Maxwell relations gives the same result from mixed second derivatives.

Pressure dependence of enthalpy

Take the pressure dependence of enthalpy. From dH = T dS + V dP (fundamental property relations), at constant T: (∂H/∂P)T = T(∂S/∂P)T + V.

The entropy term is a ratio: (∂S/∂P)T = J(S, T)/J(P, T). Expand both Jacobians in (T, P): J(S, T) = −(∂S/∂P)T and J(P, T) = −1, which only returns the target. Expand instead in (T, V): J(S, T) = −(∂S/∂V)T and J(P, T) = −(∂P/∂V)T, so (∂S/∂P)T = (∂S/∂V)T/(∂P/∂V)T. The Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V leaves (∂P/∂T)V/(∂P/∂V)T, which by the cyclic relation equals −(∂V/∂T)P.

(∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P

V is molar volume in m³/mol, T absolute temperature in K, P pressure in Pa; the derivative is taken at constant T.

For an ideal gas V = RT/P and (∂V/∂T)P = R/P, so the right side is RT/P − RT/P = 0. Enthalpy of an ideal gas is independent of pressure, and its Joule–Thomson coefficient is zero. The same expansion gives the internal pressure, (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P.

Worked example

Question. Using the Jacobian method, show that (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V for any fluid, then evaluate it for an ideal gas at 300 K with molar volume v = 0.0240 m³/mol. Take R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Write the target as a ratio: (∂S/∂V)T = J(S, T)/J(V, T).
  2. Expand the numerator in (T, P): J(S, T) = (∂S/∂T)P(0) − (∂S/∂P)T(1) = −(∂S/∂P)T.
  3. Expand the denominator in the same pair: J(V, T) = (∂V/∂T)P(0) − (∂V/∂P)T(1) = −(∂V/∂P)T.
  4. Divide: the two minus signs cancel, leaving (∂S/∂P)T/(∂V/∂P)T.
  5. Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P, so the ratio is −(∂V/∂T)P/(∂V/∂P)T = (∂P/∂T)V.
  6. Ideal gas: P = RT/v, so (∂P/∂T)v = R/v = 8.314/0.0240 = 346.4 Pa/K.

Answer. (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V = R/v = 346.4 Pa/K, that is 346.4 J m⁻³ K⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • ∂ is not a fraction: (∂u/∂x)y cannot be split into du and dx. Only J(u, y)/J(x, y) may be cancelled.
  • Sign of a swap: J(u, v) = −J(v, u). If you write the two functions in the other order, change the sign as well; reordering without the minus sign gives an answer of the wrong sign.
  • Mixed variable pairs: both Jacobians must be expanded in the same pair, or nothing cancels.
  • The cyclic relation equals −1, not +1; writing +1 reverses every derived slope.
  • Unit slip: v in cm³/mol instead of m³/mol changes R/v by a factor of 10⁶.

Takeaways

  1. Free energies exist because at fixed T and V, or fixed T and P, part of the internal energy is unavailable as work.
  2. All thermodynamic relations for a pure fluid follow from just two statements: energy conservation and the entropy inequality.
  3. Maxwell relations turn entropy derivatives, which cannot be measured, into P–V–T derivatives, which can.
  4. A residual property carries an equation of state into H, S and G: the real value is the ideal-gas value plus the residual at the same T and P.
  5. Inside the saturation dome every specific property is a straight-line mixture of saturated liquid and saturated vapour values, weighted by quality.
  6. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation predicts how boiling point shifts with pressure, which is why a pressure cooker cooks faster.
  7. Cp − Cv equals R for an ideal gas, about 1 J/(mol·K) for liquid water and more than R for a dense gas; it is never negative.
  8. The Jacobian method removes guesswork: any partial derivative can be reduced to P, V, T, Cp and Cv by a fixed recipe.

Understand State the four fundamental property relations for U, H, A and G. Explain what the natural variables of each potential are and why that pair is natural. 12 marks

Points to cover. dU = T dS − P dV from the two laws; dH, dA, dG from the definitions; Natural variables and the read-off identities; Why the pair is natural: complete information and the equilibrium test.

Model answer.

  1. Take a closed, simple compressible system on a reversible path. The first law gives dU = δQ − δW; the second law gives δQrev = T dS; the only work is P dV. So dU = T dS − P dV. Because U, S and V are properties, this holds between any two neighbouring equilibrium states, reversible path or not.
  2. Definitions: H = U + PV, A = U − TS, G = H − TS. Units: U, H, A, G in J (J/mol per mole), T in K, S in J/K, P in Pa, V in m³.
  3. Differentiate each definition and substitute dU:
    dH = dU + P dV + V dP = T dS + V dP
dA = dU − T dS − S dT = −S dT − P dV
dG = dH − T dS − S dT = −S dT + V dP
  4. Natural variables and read-off identities (compare dz = M dx + N dy, where M = (∂z/∂x)y):
    PotentialNatural variablesFirst identitySecond identity
    US, V(∂U/∂S)V = T(∂U/∂V)S = −P
    HS, P(∂H/∂S)P = T(∂H/∂P)S = V
    AT, V(∂A/∂T)V = −S(∂A/∂V)T = −P
    GT, P(∂G/∂T)P = −S(∂G/∂P)T = V
  5. Why these pairs are natural. (1) Complete information: if G is known as a function of T and P, every other property follows by differentiation: S = −(∂G/∂T)P, V = (∂G/∂P)T, H = G + TS, U = H − PV. If G were known as a function of, say, T and V, that would not be true. (2) Each pair is produced by a Legendre transform: subtracting TS swaps S for T, adding PV swaps V for P.
  6. (3) Equilibrium test: at fixed natural variables the potential is a minimum at equilibrium. U is least at fixed S and V, H at fixed S and P, A at fixed T and V, and G at fixed T and P. Since temperature and pressure are what a plant controls, G(T, P) is the potential of chemical engineering.
  7. Numerical check of (∂G/∂P)T = V. One mole of ideal gas at 300 K, 1 → 5 bar: ΔG = ∫V dP = RT ln 5 = 8.314 × 300 × 1.6094 = 4014 J. Understand Explain why the Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies are called Legendre transforms, and show that −ΔA is the maximum work at constant T and V while −ΔG is the maximum non-expansion work at constant T and P. 10 marks

Points to cover. Legendre transform: swap a variable for its conjugate; Second law for an isothermal process: Q ≤ TΔS; W ≤ −ΔA; non-PV work ≤ −ΔG; A numerical example such as a fuel cell.

Model answer.

  1. A Legendre transform changes the independent variable of a function from x to its slope. U(S, V) has slopes T = (∂U/∂S)V and −P = (∂U/∂V)S. The new function A = U − TS has dA = −S dT − P dV, so its variables are T and V; G = U − TS + PV has variables T and P.
  2. This matters because S cannot be set on a dial, while T and P can. The transform keeps all the information of U(S, V) but expresses it in controllable variables.
  3. Maximum work at constant T. A closed system exchanges heat only with surroundings at T. The second law gives ΔS ≥ Q/T, so Q ≤ TΔS. The first law gives W = Q − ΔU, with W the total work done by the system.
  4. Then W ≤ TΔS − ΔU = −(ΔU − TΔS) = −ΔA, because T is constant. The equality holds for a reversible change. So −ΔA is the largest total work, including any expansion work.
  5. At constant T and P, split the work into expansion work PΔV and other work W′ (electrical, shaft or surface work): W = PΔV + W′. Then W′ ≤ −ΔA − PΔV = −(ΔU + PΔV − TΔS) = −ΔG. So −ΔG is the largest non-expansion work.

The other 285.8 − 237.1 = 48.7 kJ must leave as heat even in a reversible cell, because the reaction lowers the entropy of the system by 48 700/298.15 = 163.3 J/K.
  8. Second example, boiling water at 1 atm: ΔG = 0 because the phases are in equilibrium, so no non-expansion work is available; but ΔA = −PΔV = −3.05 kJ/mol, which is the expansion work of the steam.
  9. Conclusion: A and G are U with the "unavailable" TS removed, written in the variables that are held fixed. Their decrease sets an upper bound on work, and a negative ΔG at fixed T and P is the test for a spontaneous change.

Analyze Show from the Gibbs energy that (∂Cp/∂P)T = −T(∂²V/∂T²)P, and use it with the virial equation to judge whether the heat capacity of CO₂ at 350 K can be treated as a function of temperature alone at 1 bar and at 20 bar. 14 marks

Points to cover. Maxwell relation from dG, then differentiate with respect to T; Cp = T(∂S/∂T)P and the order of differentiation; Virial: Cp − Cpig = −TP d²B/dT²; Judge the size at 1 bar and 20 bar.

Model answer.

  1. From dG = −S dT + V dP, exactness gives the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P.
  2. CP = T(∂S/∂T)P, so (∂CP/∂P)T = T ∂/∂P[(∂S/∂T)P]T = T ∂/∂T[(∂S/∂P)T]P, because the order of mixed second derivatives of the state function S(T, P) does not matter.
  3. Substituting the Maxwell relation: (∂CP/∂P)T = T ∂/∂T[−(∂V/∂T)P]P = −T(∂²V/∂T²)P.
  4. Ideal gas: V = RT/P, (∂V/∂T)P = R/P, (∂²V/∂T²)P = 0. So CPig depends on T only.
  5. Virial gas: V = RT/P + B(T), so (∂²V/∂T²)P = d²B/dT² and (∂CP/∂P)T = −T d²B/dT². Integrating from P = 0: CP − CPig = −TP d²B/dT².
  6. With the Abbott correlation, d²B/dT² = (R/(Pc Tc))(d²B⁰/dTr² + ω d²B¹/dTr²), where d²B⁰/dTr² = −1.755/Tr3.6 and d²B¹/dTr² = −3.754/Tr6.2. For CO₂ at 350 K (Tr = 1.1506, ω = 0.224): d²B/dT² = −5.226 × 10⁻⁹ m³ mol⁻¹ K⁻².
  7. Size of the correction (CPig of CO₂ at 350 K = 39.39 J/(mol·K)):
    PCP − CPig = −TP d²B/dT²Fraction of CPig
    1 bar350 × 10⁵ × 5.226 × 10⁻⁹ = 0.18 J/(mol·K)0.5 %
    20 bar350 × 20 × 10⁵ × 5.226 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.66 J/(mol·K)9.3 %
  8. Check: an accurate equation of state gives CP − CPig = 3.96 J/(mol·K) at 20 bar, so the virial estimate is within 8 % of the correction itself.
  9. Judgement: at 1 bar the pressure effect (0.5 %) is below the uncertainty of most CP data, so CP = CPig(T) is fine. At 20 bar it is 9 %, too large to ignore in an energy balance; and near the critical point (Tr → 1, Pr → 1) d²V/dT² grows sharply and CP rises steeply.
  10. Consistency with residual enthalpy: since CP = (∂H/∂T)P, CP − CPig = (∂HR/∂T)P. With HR = P(B − T dB/dT), (∂HR/∂T)P = P(dB/dT − dB/dT − T d²B/dT²) = −PT d²B/dT², exactly the same expression, so the two routes agree.

Model answer.

  2. Since G + TS = H, this is the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation: [∂(G/T)/∂T]P = −H/T². It holds for each species, and so for a reaction: d(ΔG°/T)/dT = −ΔH°/T².

Evaluate Using the Jacobian method and a Maxwell relation, derive an expression for the Joule–Thomson coefficient in terms of P–V–T data and Cp. Evaluate it for nitrogen and hydrogen at 300 K with the virial equation, and judge why hydrogen must be pre-cooled before throttling in a liquefaction plant. 14 marks

Points to cover. μJT = J(T, H)/J(P, H) expanded in (T, P); Maxwell relation gives μ = [T(∂V/∂T)P − V]/Cp; Virial form μ = (T dB/dT − B)/Cp with numbers; Inversion temperature and the pre-cooling decision.

Model answer.

  1. Target: μJT = (∂T/∂P)H = J(T, H)/J(P, H).
  2. Expand in (T, P). Numerator: J(T, H) = (∂T/∂T)P(∂H/∂P)T − (∂T/∂P)T(∂H/∂T)P = (∂H/∂P)T. Denominator: J(P, H) = (∂P/∂T)P(∂H/∂P)T − (∂P/∂P)T(∂H/∂T)P = −CP.
  3. So μJT = −(∂H/∂P)T/CP. From dH = T dS + V dP and the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P: (∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P. Hence μJT = [T(∂V/∂T)P − V]/CP.
  4. Ideal gas: T(∂V/∂T)P = RT/P = V, so μJT = 0 and an ideal gas neither cools nor warms on throttling.
  5. Virial gas, V = RT/P + B(T): T(∂V/∂T)P − V = T dB/dT − B, so μJT = (T dB/dT − B)/CP at low pressure. Cooling needs T dB/dT > B.

  8. Inversion temperature: μJT = 0 where T dB/dT = B. Solving with the Abbott correlation for hydrogen gives about 220 K at low pressure; accurate data put it near 200 K. Nitrogen's is above 600 K, so nitrogen cools when throttled from room temperature.
  9. Why: at 300 K hydrogen molecules are so light and weakly attracting that repulsion dominates (B > 0); throttling then raises the temperature. Above the inversion temperature a Linde valve would heat the stream and no liquid could form.
  10. At higher pressure the virial form is not enough: μJT then depends on P as well, and the full inversion curve on a T–P diagram is a dome. Inside the dome throttling cools; outside it warms. For hydrogen the whole dome lies below about 200 K.

Helium (inversion about 40 K) needs pre-cooling by liquid hydrogen or an expansion engine for the same reason. Pre-cooling costs energy but is unavoidable: without it the throttle works against liquefaction.

Glossary

Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs free energy, equal to (∂G/∂ni) at constant T, P and other mole numbers.
Compressibility factor
Z = PV/(RT); equal to 1 for an ideal gas and different from 1 for a real gas.
Corresponding states
The principle that fluids at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure have similar properties.
Critical point
The highest temperature and pressure at which liquid and vapour can be distinguished; above it there is one fluid phase.
Cyclic relation
The identity (∂x/∂y)z (∂y/∂z)x (∂z/∂x)y = −1 for three variables linked by a state relation.
Equation of state
A relation among P, V and T for a fluid, such as the ideal-gas, van der Waals or Peng–Robinson equation.
Exact differential
A differential expression whose integral between two states is path independent.
Extensive property
A property that scales with the amount of material, such as total volume, internal energy or entropy.
Fugacity
An effective pressure that replaces the true pressure in the ideal-gas chemical-potential formula, so that real-fluid behaviour is retained.
Fugacity coefficient
The ratio φ = f/P, tending to 1 as pressure tends to zero.
Intensive property
A property independent of the size of the system, such as temperature, pressure or molar volume.
Inversion temperature
The temperature below which a gas cools on isenthalpic throttling and above which it warms.
Isothermal compressibility
κ = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)T, a positive quantity measuring volume change under pressure at fixed temperature.
Latent heat of vaporisation
Enthalpy change when unit mass changes from saturated liquid to saturated vapour at constant temperature and pressure.
Reduced property
A property divided by its critical value, such as Tr = T/Tc, used in generalized correlations.
Saturated liquid
Liquid at its boiling point for the given pressure, with quality zero.
Saturated vapour
Vapour at its condensation point for the given pressure, with quality one.
State function
A property whose change depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken.
Subcooled liquid
Liquid at a temperature below its saturation temperature for the given pressure.
Superheated vapour
Vapour at a temperature above its saturation temperature for the given pressure.
Thermal expansion coefficient
α = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)P, the fractional volume change per kelvin at constant pressure.
Thermodynamic chart
A plot such as a T–s, P–h or Mollier diagram from which properties can be read graphically.
Triple point
The single temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium.
