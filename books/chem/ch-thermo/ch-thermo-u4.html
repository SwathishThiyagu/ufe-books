4.1 Helmholtz and Gibbs free energies

A system at fixed temperature has two useful energy buckets: Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS for fixed volume, and Gibbs free energy G = H − TS for fixed pressure. They subtract the unavailable energy TS from internal energy or enthalpy, leaving the part that can become work. Industry uses G for reactors, separators and phase changes at constant pressure; A appears in statistical mechanics and constant-volume calculations. This chapter defines A and G, gives their differentials, equilibrium tests and one worked compression example.

You need: internal energy and enthalpy from Unit 1, and entropy from Unit 3; the full set of differentials is in 4.2.

What A and G measure

A system at temperature T holds internal energy U. Part of U is tied to the random motion counted by entropy S: the product TS. Subtract that part and the remainder is Helmholtz free energy A = U − TS. The same idea at constant pressure starts from enthalpy H = U + PV, so Gibbs free energy G = H − TS = U + PV − TS. Here U is internal energy (J), H is enthalpy (J), T is absolute temperature (K), S is entropy (J/K), P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³). A and G carry units of J; per mole they are J/mol.

The subtraction TS removes energy that cannot become work in a reversible isothermal step. A is used when volume is fixed, as in a rigid bomb or a statistical-mechanics lattice. G is used when pressure is fixed, which is the usual laboratory, reactor and pipe condition. Some syllabi call A the Helmholtz free energy F; F and A mean the same property.

Differentials and equilibrium tests

For a closed system with only pressure-volume work, dU = T dS − P dV. Differentiate A = U − TS: dA = dU − T dS − S dT. Put dU in: dA = −S dT − P dV. Differentiate G = H − TS with dH = T dS + V dP: dG = −S dT + V dP. These are exact differentials for A(T,V) and G(T,P). The variables in brackets are the natural variables: T and V for A, T and P for G.

dA = −S dT − P dV

A is Helmholtz free energy (J); S entropy (J/K); T absolute temperature (K); P pressure (Pa); V volume (m³).

dG = −S dT + V dP

G is Gibbs free energy (J); the other symbols are defined above.

At constant T and V, the second law gives dA ≤ 0 for a spontaneous change in a closed system with only PV work; dA = 0 at equilibrium. At constant T and P, dG ≤ 0, and dG = 0 at equilibrium. The inequality is the practical test: a process at fixed T and P proceeds only if G falls. Work interpretation: at constant T and V, the maximum work obtainable is −ΔA; at constant T and P, the maximum non-PV useful work obtainable is −ΔG.

(∂(G/T)/∂T) at constant P = −H/T²

This is the Gibbs-Helmholtz equation; G is Gibbs free energy (J), T is temperature (K), H is enthalpy (J).

Signs and the isothermal ideal-gas result

For an isothermal process on an ideal gas, ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0. Then ΔA = −TΔS and ΔG = −TΔS, so ΔA = ΔG. The entropy change for n moles going from P1 to P2 at constant T is ΔS = nR ln(P1/P2). Therefore ΔA = ΔG = nRT ln(P2/P1). Here n is moles (mol), R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, T is in K, and P1 and P2 are initial and final pressures in the same unit.

Compression has P2 > P1, so ln(P2/P1) > 0 and both ΔA and ΔG are positive. Positive means the surroundings must supply work; the gas does not release it. Expansion gives a negative value. For a real gas or a phase change, ΔA and ΔG differ because ΔU and ΔH are not zero. At constant T,P, a negative ΔG marks a spontaneous phase change or reaction; see Unit 5 for chemical potential.

Worked example

Question. One mole of ideal gas at 300 K is compressed isothermally and reversibly from 1 bar to 5 bar. Calculate ΔA and ΔG. Treat the gas as ideal.

  1. For an ideal gas at constant T, ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0.
  2. The entropy change is ΔS = nR ln(P1/P2) = 1 × 8.314 × ln(1/5) = 8.314 × (−1.6094) = −13.38 J/K.
  3. Then TΔS = 300 × (−13.38) = −4014 J.
  4. ΔA = ΔU − TΔS = 0 − (−4014) = +4014 J.
  5. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = 0 − (−4014) = +4014 J.
  6. As a check, ΔG = nRT ln(P2/P1) = 1 × 8.314 × 300 × ln(5/1) = +4014 J.

Answer. ΔA = ΔG = +4.01 kJ, so work must be supplied to compress the gas.

Exam traps

  • Writing G = U − TS instead of G = H − TS. Check that G = U + PV − TS; the PV term is missing in the wrong form.
  • Using Celsius in T. Every free-energy formula uses absolute temperature in kelvin.
  • Losing the sign for compression. If P2 > P1, ln(P2/P1) is positive, so ΔA and ΔG are positive; expansion gives negative values.
  • Swapping the equilibrium tests. dA ≤ 0 belongs to constant T,V; dG ≤ 0 belongs to constant T,P.
  • Forgetting that ΔA = ΔG is a special result for an ideal gas at constant T. For a real gas or phase change, ΔA = ΔU − TΔS and ΔG = ΔH − TΔS are different.

4.2 Fundamental property relations

The first law gives energy changes; the second law gives entropy changes. The four fundamental property relations combine both laws into exact differentials for internal energy U, enthalpy H, Helmholtz free energy A and Gibbs free energy G. Each differential names the natural variables of one potential and lets you read derivatives directly from its coefficients. Chemical engineers use these relations to compute property changes from pressure–volume–temperature data, design compressors and heat exchangers, and build the property packages inside process simulators.

You need Unit 1 (first law for closed systems), Unit 2 (equations of state) and Unit 3 (entropy); free energies are defined in 4.1.

From two laws to four exact differentials

Take a closed, simple compressible system: no chemical reaction, no shaft work, negligible kinetic and potential energy. For a reversible path the system pressure equals the external pressure P and the system temperature equals the bath temperature T. The first law is dU = δQ − δW. The second law for a reversible path gives δQ = T dS. The only work is P dV, so δW = P dV. Therefore dU = T dS − P dV.

Define the other potentials by Legendre transforms: H = U + PV, A = U − TS, and G = H − TS = U + PV − TS. Differentiate each definition and substitute dU. The P dV and T dS terms cancel in pairs.

dU = T dS − P dV; dH = T dS + V dP; dA = −S dT − P dV; dG = −S dT + V dP

U is internal energy (J), H enthalpy (J), A Helmholtz free energy (J), G Gibbs free energy (J). T is temperature (K), S entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³).

Each left side is an exact differential; each right side contains only the natural variables of that potential. Some syllabi call these the thermodynamic property relations.

Natural variables and coefficient identities

An exact differential dz = M dx + N dy has M = (∂z/∂x)y and N = (∂z/∂y)x. Compare this with each relation. The comparison identifies the natural variables and the coefficient identities.

PotentialExact differentialNatural variablesRead-off identities
UdU = T dS − P dVS, V(∂U/∂S)V = T; (∂U/∂V)S = −P
HdH = T dS + V dPS, P(∂H/∂S)P = T; (∂H/∂P)S = V
AdA = −S dT − P dVT, V(∂A/∂T)V = −S; (∂A/∂V)T = −P
GdG = −S dT + V dPT, P(∂G/∂T)P = −S; (∂G/∂P)T = V

The subscript on each derivative says what is held constant. For example, (∂U/∂S)V = T means entropy changes at fixed volume; (∂U/∂V)S = −P means volume changes at fixed entropy. A partial derivative is not a simple fraction: (∂U/∂S)V is not dU divided by dS in the ordinary sense.

Equality of mixed second derivatives of these potentials gives the Maxwell relations (see 4.3). That step replaces entropy derivatives by measurable pressure, volume and temperature derivatives.

Integrating the relations for property changes

Because U, H, A and G are state functions, integrate along any convenient reversible path between the same two states. Choose a path that matches the natural variables. At constant T, dA = −P dV and dG = V dP; at constant S, dU = −P dV and dH = V dP.

For an ideal gas, U and H depend only on T. Then dU = CV dT and dH = CP dT, where CV is heat capacity at constant volume (J/K) and CP is heat capacity at constant pressure (J/K). Entropy follows from dS = CV dT/T + R dV/V, or dS = CP dT/T − R dP/P, with R the gas constant (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹). For a real gas, insert its equation of state.

At constant temperature, a useful identity for real gases is (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P. It follows by dividing dU = T dS − P dV by dV at fixed T and using the Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V from 4.3. It turns a hard entropy derivative into a measurable PVT derivative.

Worked example

Question. A van der Waals gas obeys P = RT/(V − b) − a/V², with a = 0.364 Pa m⁶ mol⁻². At T = 300 K and V = 0.200 m³ mol⁻¹, calculate (∂U/∂V)T.

  1. Start from the coefficient identity (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P.
  2. Differentiate P with respect to T at fixed V. The term RT/(V − b) gives R/(V − b); the term −a/V² has no T, so its derivative is zero.
  3. Then T(∂P/∂T)V = RT/(V − b).
  4. Subtract P: T(∂P/∂T)V − P = RT/(V − b) − [RT/(V − b) − a/V²] = a/V².
  5. Insert a = 0.364 Pa m⁶ mol⁻² and V = 0.200 m³ mol⁻¹. V² = 0.0400 m⁶ mol⁻².
  6. a/V² = 0.364/0.0400 = 9.1 Pa. The constants b and T cancel, so the result does not depend on their values here.
  7. The derivative is positive: at fixed T, U rises as V rises for this van der Waals gas.

Answer. (∂U/∂V)T = 9.1 Pa.

Exam traps

  • Mixing up natural variables: U is natural in (S,V), not (T,V). H is natural in (S,P), not (T,P).
  • Using the wrong sign for P dV: dU = T dS − P dV and dA = −S dT − P dV both carry a minus sign on P dV.
  • Treating a partial derivative as a simple fraction: (∂U/∂V)S cannot be cancelled with dV.
  • Using dG = −S dT + V dP for a mixture with changing composition. The full relation then needs chemical potential terms (Unit 5).
  • Confusing (∂U/∂V)T with (∂H/∂P)T; they are different derivatives and different identities.

4.3 Maxwell relations and their applications

A Maxwell relation is an equality between two partial derivatives of a thermodynamic potential. It exists because U, H, A and G are state functions, so their mixed second derivatives are equal. The equalities replace an entropy derivative, which is hard to measure, with a pressure, volume or temperature derivative, which comes from PVT data. Engineers use them to find entropy changes, enthalpy changes and the Joule–Thomson coefficient. Some syllabi call these Maxwell's thermodynamic relations.

You need exact differentials from Chapter 4.2, ideal-gas entropy from Unit 3, and free energies from Chapter 4.1.

Where the four relations come from

For a state function F of two variables x and y, dF = M dx + N dy is exact. Exactness means (∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y. Apply this test to each fundamental differential from Chapter 4.2.

Symbols: U internal energy (J), H enthalpy (J), A Helmholtz energy (J), G Gibbs energy (J), T absolute temperature (K), S entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³). The definitions are H = U + PV, A = U − TS, G = H − TS.

  • dU = T dS − P dV gives (∂T/∂V)S = −(∂P/∂S)V.
  • dH = T dS + V dP gives (∂T/∂P)S = (∂V/∂S)P.
  • dA = −S dT − P dV gives (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V.
  • dG = −S dT + V dP gives (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P.

Each line is one Maxwell relation. In the dA and dG relations entropy appears in the numerator of the left side, so those two convert an entropy slope into a P–V–T slope. In the dU and dH relations entropy stays in the derivative, so they are less useful for that job.

What the relations buy you

The dG relation is the workhorse for entropy changes with pressure at fixed temperature. For an ideal gas, V = nRT/P, so (∂V/∂T)P = nR/P. Here n is amount (mol) and R is the gas constant, 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

(∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P

S is entropy (J/K), P pressure (Pa), V volume (m³), T temperature (K); the subscript T means temperature is held constant.

At constant T, integrate from P1 to P2. For an ideal gas, ΔS = −nR ln(P2/P1). Compression (P2 > P1) lowers entropy because the gas has less volume to occupy.

(∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P

H is enthalpy (J); the other symbols are as above. Substitute the dG Maxwell relation into dH = T dS + V dP at constant T.

The Joule–Thomson coefficient μJT = (∂T/∂P)H follows from the same enthalpy slope. Since dH = Cp dT + (∂H/∂P)T dP at constant H, μJT = [T(∂V/∂T)P − V]/Cp. Cp is heat capacity at constant pressure (J/K). An ideal gas has V = nRT/P, so T(∂V/∂T)P = V and μJT = 0.

Reading the derivatives correctly

The variable held constant is part of the derivative. (∂V/∂T)P fixes pressure, not entropy or volume. Dropping the subscript changes the value of the derivative, so always state the variable held constant.

The dA relation can be more convenient when volume data are available: at constant T, ΔS = ∫(∂P/∂T)V dV. For an ideal gas, P = nRT/V, so (∂P/∂T)V = nR/V and ΔS = nR ln(V2/V1). This is the same entropy change written with volume ratio.

For a real gas, do not replace (∂V/∂T)P by nR/P. Use measured or equation-of-state values of V and its temperature slope. Residual-property formulas built from these derivatives are in Chapter 4.4.

Worked example

Question. Use a Maxwell relation to calculate ΔS when 2 mol of ideal gas at 300 K is compressed from 1 bar to 10 bar at constant temperature.

  1. Start from dG = −S dT + V dP. Exactness gives (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P.
  2. For an ideal gas PV = nRT, so V = nRT/P. At constant P, (∂V/∂T)P = nR/P.
  3. Substitute: (∂S/∂P)T = −nR/P.
  4. Integrate at constant T from P1 = 1 bar to P2 = 10 bar: ΔS = −nR ln(P2/P1).
  5. Put n = 2 mol and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. ln(10) = 2.3026. nR = 16.628 J K⁻¹. Product = 16.628 × 2.3026 = 38.29 J/K.
  6. Apply the negative sign: ΔS = −38.29 J/K, which rounds to −38.3 J/K. The 300 K value does not enter the ideal-gas pressure formula.

Answer. ΔS = −38.3 J/K for the compression.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: (∂S/∂P)T is negative for an ideal gas; compression at constant T lowers entropy.
  • Wrong constant variable: (∂V/∂T)P is not the same as (∂V/∂T)S or (∂V/∂P)T.
  • Celsius instead of kelvin: T in (∂H/∂P)T and μJT must be absolute temperature.
  • Using nR/P for a real gas: that slope is only the ideal-gas value.
  • Mixing dA and dG signs: dA has −S dT − P dV, while dG has −S dT + V dP.

4.4 Residual properties

A residual property is the gap between a real-fluid property and its ideal-gas value at the same temperature and pressure. Real gases deviate from ideal behaviour, so ideal-gas formulas alone give wrong enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy. The residual isolates that deviation. It is used with cubic equations of state and generalized correlations to get compressor power, throttle effects, heat-exchanger duties and fugacity coefficients. Once the residual is known, add it to the ideal-gas value at the same T and P. This chapter shows the defining integrals and one worked calculation.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for exact differentials, the compressibility factor Z from Unit 2, and the fugacity coefficient from Unit 5.

Definition at fixed T and P

At temperature T and pressure P, a real fluid has molar property M. The ideal gas at the same T and P has molar property M^ig. The residual property is M^R = M − M^ig. M can be V, U, H, S, G or A; the units follow M. The definition uses the same T and P because that fixes one comparison state for both fluids. The ideal gas is a reference, not a second real state.

M^R = M − M^ig

M is any molar property at T and P; M^ig is the ideal-gas value at the same T and P; M^R is the residual property.

For an ideal gas, U^ig and H^ig depend only on T. S^ig and G^ig also depend on P, through S^ig(T,P) = S^ig(T,P°) − R ln(P/P°) and G^ig = H^ig − T S^ig. The pressure terms cancel in some residuals and remain in others. Every residual property of an ideal gas is zero. As P → 0, H^R, S^R, G^R, A^R and U^R tend to zero. V^R is different: V^R = (Z − 1)RT/P tends to B, the second virial coefficient, a finite value, because V and V^ig both diverge while their difference stays finite.

V^R = (Z − 1)RT/P

V^R is residual molar volume (m³/mol); Z = PV/RT is compressibility factor; R = 8.314 J/(mol K); T is temperature (K); P is pressure (Pa).

Negative V^R means the real gas occupies less volume than the ideal gas at the same T and P. Attractive forces usually cause this at moderate pressure.

Residual Gibbs energy and fugacity

For a pure fluid, the fugacity coefficient is φ = f/P, where f is fugacity and P is pressure. Unit 5 develops φ fully. The residual Gibbs energy is tied to it by G^R = RT ln φ. The integrated form uses the compressibility factor Z = PV/RT.

G^R/RT = ln φ = ∫₀^P (Z − 1) dP/P

G^R is residual molar Gibbs energy (J/mol); R = 8.314 J/(mol K); T is absolute temperature (K); φ is fugacity coefficient; Z = PV/RT; P is pressure (Pa).

At P → 0, (Z − 1)/P tends to a finite limit, the pressure virial coefficient B′, so the integral remains finite. If φ < 1, then ln φ < 0 and G^R is negative: the real gas has lower Gibbs energy than the ideal gas at that T and P. If φ > 1, G^R is positive; φ = 1 gives G^R = 0. This sign check catches many calculation errors. The same integral also gives the fugacity coefficient used in phase equilibrium; see Unit 5 and Unit 6.

Enthalpy, entropy and volume residuals

At constant T, the fundamental relation for enthalpy gives dH = [V − T(∂V/∂T)_P] dP. Integrate from zero pressure, where the gas is ideal, to pressure P.

H^R = ∫₀^P [V − T(∂V/∂T)_P] dP

H^R is residual molar enthalpy (J/mol); V is molar volume (m³/mol); T is temperature (K); (∂V/∂T)_P is the temperature derivative of V at constant pressure; P is pressure (Pa).

For an ideal gas, V = RT/P and T(∂V/∂T)_P = RT/P. The bracket is zero, so H^R = 0, as required. For a cubic equation of state, substitute V(T,P) and integrate. The result is a closed-form departure function.

Entropy follows from dS = −(∂V/∂T)_P dP at constant T. With M^R = M − M^ig, the residual is:

S^R = ∫₀^P [R/P − (∂V/∂T)_P] dP

S^R is residual molar entropy (J/(mol K)); R = 8.314 J/(mol K); P is pressure (Pa); V is molar volume (m³/mol); (∂V/∂T)_P is at constant pressure.

The ideal-gas part is R/P, so the integrand vanishes for an ideal gas. The three residuals are consistent through G^R = H^R − T S^R. Cubic equations of state such as van der Waals, Redlich-Kwong and Peng-Robinson supply V(T,P) and its temperature derivative. Generalized correlations use reduced temperature Tr = T/Tc, reduced pressure Pr = P/Pc and acentric factor ω. They tabulate H^R/RTc and S^R/R. The values feed compressor power, valve throttling and heat-exchanger duties.

Worked example

Question. At 300 K and 50 bar, a pure gas has fugacity coefficient φ = 0.90. Calculate its residual molar Gibbs energy G^R. Use R = 8.314 J/(mol K).

4.5 Property tables and diagrams; two-phase systems

In a two-phase region steam is part liquid and part vapour. Its properties are not ideal-gas values. Steam tables list saturated liquid and saturated vapour; a single number, quality, fixes the mixture. The same idea powers the steam plant and the refrigerator: you read two properties, locate the state, and calculate the rest.

You need Unit 1 enthalpy, Unit 2 phase diagrams and latent heat, and Unit 3 steam cycles.

Saturated states and quality

At a given pressure, water boils at one temperature, the saturation temperature Tsat. The liquid just before boiling is saturated liquid. Its properties carry subscript f: vf [m³/kg], hf [kJ/kg], sf [kJ/(kg·K)]. The vapour just after boiling is saturated vapour, subscript g. Between them is a wet mixture.

Quality x is the mass fraction of vapour: x = mg/(mf + mg). mf is mass of saturated liquid [kg]; mg is mass of saturated vapour [kg]. x = 0 is saturated liquid, x = 1 is saturated vapour, 0 < x < 1 is wet mixture.

M = (1 − x)Mf + xMg = Mf + x(Mg − Mf)

M stands for v, u, h or s per kg; Mf is the saturated-liquid value; Mg is the saturated-vapour value; x is quality.

h = hf + x hfg,  v = vf + x(vg − vf)

hfg = hg − hf is latent heat of vaporisation [kJ/kg]; vg − vf is the volume change on vaporisation [m³/kg]. Use hfg, never hg, in the second term.

Above the saturation temperature at the same pressure the vapour is superheated. Read its properties from the superheated table, not from the saturated columns. The degree of superheat is T − Tsat [K or °C].

StateTvh

Reading a steam table and interpolating

  1. Compare the given T with Tsat at the given P, or the given P with Psat at the given T. This tells you liquid, wet mixture or superheated vapour.
  2. For a wet mixture, use one known property to find x. For example x = (h − hf)/hfg or x = (v − vf)/(vg − vf).
  3. Use x in M = Mf + x(Mg − Mf) for every other property. Do not use x for temperature or pressure; both stay at saturation.
  4. For superheated vapour, enter the superheated table at the given P and T. If the table has no exact T or P, interpolate.

y = y₁ + (z − z₁)(y₂ − y₁)/(z₂ − z₁)

z is the known value (T, P or h); y is the wanted property; (z₁, y₁) and (z₂, y₂) are the two table points that bracket z. Keep x for quality only, never for the interpolation variable.

Interpolate only between two rows of the same table region. Interpolating between saturated liquid and saturated vapour rows with T or P gives nonsense, because both rows share the same T and P.

Diagrams: P-v, T-s, h-s and P-h

A property diagram plots two properties so a cycle becomes a path. The saturation dome is the two-phase region. Under the dome, an isobar is also an isotherm: at fixed P, T stays at Tsat until the last liquid evaporates. That horizontal line is the easiest feature to spot.

On a T-s diagram the area under a reversible path is heat. On an h-s diagram, the Mollier chart, a vertical drop is an isentropic turbine or nozzle, so it is used for steam turbines. On a P-h diagram, an isenthalpic throttle is a vertical line, so it is used for refrigeration and heat pumps.

Quality lines run across the dome. The critical point closes the dome; above it there is no liquid-vapour split. Some syllabi call these thermodynamic property charts. Use them for quick reads and for checking table values, not as a replacement for the table when an exact number is needed.

Worked example

Question. Steam at 10 bar has quality 0.90. Given hf = 762.8 kJ/kg, hfg = 2015.3 kJ/kg, vf = 0.001127 m³/kg and vg = 0.1944 m³/kg, calculate the specific enthalpy h and specific volume v of the mixture.

  1. The state is a wet mixture because 0 < x < 1. Use the saturated-liquid and saturated-vapour values at 10 bar.
  2. Write the enthalpy formula: h = hf + x hfg.
  3. Substitute: h = 762.8 + 0.90 × 2015.3.
  4. Compute the second term: 0.90 × 2015.3 = 1813.77 kJ/kg.
  5. Add: h = 762.8 + 1813.77 = 2576.57 kJ/kg ≈ 2576.6 kJ/kg.
  6. Write the volume formula: v = vf + x(vg − vf).
  7. Compute the volume change: vg − vf = 0.1944 − 0.001127 = 0.193273 m³/kg.
  8. Multiply by quality: 0.90 × 0.193273 = 0.1739457 m³/kg.
  9. Add vf: v = 0.001127 + 0.1739457 = 0.1750727 m³/kg ≈ 0.1751 m³/kg.

  • Taking h = hg for a wet mixture: at 10 bar and x = 0.90 this gives 2778.1 kJ/kg, about 201 kJ/kg too high.
  • Substituting hg in place of hfg: h = hf + x hg = 762.8 + 0.90 × 2778.1 = 3263.1 kJ/kg, about 686 kJ/kg too high, because x hf is then counted an extra time.

  • Interpolating in the wrong region: saturated tables for superheated steam, or superheated tables inside the dome.
  • Forgetting that T and P are not averaged with quality in the wet region: both stay at Tsat and Psat.

4.6 Clausius-Clapeyron equation

The Clausius–Clapeyron equation gives the slope of a saturation curve from latent heat and volume change. It answers a practical question: water boils at 373 K at 1 atm, so what is its vapour pressure at 350 K? One measured (P, T) pair plus ΔH gives another. A plot of ln P against 1/T gives ΔH. Chemical engineers use it for steam, refrigerants, distillation and vacuum drying. Only a few universities teach this inside this unit; some syllabi write Clausius–Clapeyron.

You need: Chapter 4.5 for two-phase properties, Unit 2 for phase diagrams, and Unit 3 for latent heat.

The Clapeyron equation

At saturation, liquid and vapour coexist. The saturation curve P_sat(T) has a slope. Equality of Gibbs free energy of the two phases gives the Clapeyron equation:

dP_sat/dT = ΔH/(T ΔV)

P_sat is saturation pressure (Pa), T is absolute temperature (K), ΔH is latent heat (J/mol), and ΔV = V_g − V_l is molar volume change (m³/mol).

A large ΔH makes the curve steep. A large ΔV makes it flatter. For vaporisation ΔV > 0, so dP_sat/dT > 0. For ice melting, ΔV = V_liquid − V_solid < 0, so dP_sat/dT < 0: the melting point falls as pressure rises. This is why ice skates on a thin water film under pressure (see Chapter 4.3). The derivative also follows from a Maxwell relation. The equation is exact for a pure substance at coexistence; no ideal-gas assumption is used yet. ΔH is the enthalpy change from phase 1 to phase 2; its sign follows the direction. For vaporisation, ΔH is positive and the pressure rises with temperature. Use it at one temperature when ΔH and ΔV are known. It is the starting point for the ideal-vapour form below.

Ideal-vapour form and the straight line

For liquid–vapour saturation away from the critical point, V_g ≫ V_l, so ΔV ≈ V_g. Treat the vapour as ideal: V_g = RT/P_sat, with R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Substitution gives:

dP_sat/P_sat = (ΔH/R) dT/T²

P_sat is saturation pressure, T is absolute temperature, ΔH is molar latent heat, and R is the gas constant.

Integrate from (P1, T1) to (P2, T2). For constant ΔH:

ln(P2/P1) = −(ΔH/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1)

P1 and P2 are saturation pressures in the same unit, T1 and T2 are in K, ΔH is in J/mol, and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Equivalently, ln P_sat = A − ΔH/(R T). A plot of ln P_sat against 1/T is a straight line with slope −ΔH/R. Two measured points give ΔH = −R ln(P2/P1)/(1/T2 − 1/T1). To estimate P2, use P2 = P1 exp[−(ΔH/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1)]. Convert T to kelvin and keep pressures in one unit. The figure shows water with ΔH_vap = 40.65 kJ/mol.

Worked example

Question. Water has ΔH_vap = 40.65 kJ/mol and a normal boiling point of 373 K at 1 atm (101.325 kPa). Estimate its saturation pressure at 350 K using the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

  1. T1 = 373 K, P1 = 101.325 kPa, T2 = 350 K, ΔH = 40 650 J/mol, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  2. 1/T2 − 1/T1 = 1/350 − 1/373 = 0.00017618 K⁻¹.
  3. ΔH/R = 40 650/8.314 = 4889.3 K.
  4. ln(P2/P1) = −4889.3 × 0.00017618 = −0.8614.
  5. P2/P1 = e^(−0.8614) = 0.4226.
  6. P2 = 101.325 × 0.4226 = 42.8 kPa = 0.428 bar.

Answer. The estimated saturation pressure of water at 350 K is 42.8 kPa, about 0.428 bar.

  • Dropping the minus sign in ln(P2/P1) = −(ΔH/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1).
  • Using V_l instead of ΔV = V_g − V_l; for vaporisation the gas volume dominates.
  • Mixing pressure units in P2/P1; the ratio is dimensionless, so P1 and P2 must match.
  • Taking the slope from P against T; −ΔH/R is the slope of ln P against 1/T.

4.7 Relations for Cp, Cv and the heat capacity ratio

Cp − Cv is the extra heat a substance needs at constant pressure because it also expands. The difference follows from the equation of state through two PVT derivatives: thermal expansion coefficient α and isothermal compressibility κ_T. The same derivatives give how Cp and Cv change with pressure and volume, and the ratio γ = Cp/Cv. These relations turn table data into heat capacities for liquids and real gases. Only a few universities teach this inside Unit 4.

You need: Chapter 4.3 for Maxwell relations, plus heat capacities from Unit 1 and PVT behaviour from Unit 2.

Cp − Cv from PVT data

At constant volume, heat raises internal energy only. At constant pressure, heat also pays for expansion work. So Cp is always larger than Cv. The gap depends on how fast volume grows with temperature and how hard the fluid is to compress.

Let v be molar volume in m³ mol⁻¹, T temperature in K, α thermal expansion coefficient in K⁻¹, and κ_T isothermal compressibility in Pa⁻¹.

Cp − Cv = T v α² / κ_T = −T (∂P/∂T)_V² / (∂P/∂V)_T

Cp and Cv are molar heat capacities in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; α = (1/v)(∂v/∂T)_P; κ_T = −(1/v)(∂v/∂P)_T.

For an ideal gas α = 1/T and κ_T = 1/P. Substitution gives Cp − Cv = R, where R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Read T and v from the problem.
  2. Look up α and κ_T in tables.
  3. Compute Tvα²/κ_T in consistent SI units.

For liquids α is small and κ_T is very small, so Cp − Cv is only a few J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, not R.

Ratio and pressure or volume dependence

Define γ = Cp/Cv. For ideal monatomic gas γ = 5/3 ≈ 1.67; for ideal diatomic gas γ = 7/5 = 1.40 at ordinary temperature.

Heat capacities are not truly constant. Their slopes come from second derivatives of the equation of state:

(∂Cp/∂P)_T = −T (∂²v/∂T²)_P

v is molar volume; the derivative is at fixed T.

(∂Cv/∂v)_T = T (∂²P/∂T²)_v

Cv is molar heat capacity at constant volume.

For an ideal gas both second derivatives are zero, so Cp and Cv depend only on T. For a real gas or liquid they drift with pressure or volume. The Gibbs-Helmholtz relation is a cousin: (∂(G/T)/∂T)_P = −H/T², with G Gibbs energy and H enthalpy.

A common use is checking whether tabulated Cp at 1 bar can be used at 50 bar; integrate the pressure derivative.

Worked example

Question. Liquid water at 300 K has v = 18.0 × 10⁻⁶ m³ mol⁻¹, α = 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, and κ_T = 4.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹. Calculate Cp − Cv in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Write the relation: Cp − Cv = T v α² / κ_T.
  2. Square α: (2.57 × 10⁻⁴)² = 6.6049 × 10⁻⁸ K⁻².
  3. Multiply T v α²: 300 × 18.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.6049 × 10⁻⁸ = 3.5666 × 10⁻¹⁰ m³ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  4. Divide by κ_T: 3.5666 × 10⁻¹⁰ / 4.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 0.7926.
  5. Round to three significant figures: 0.793 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Answer. Cp − Cv = 0.793 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for liquid water at 300 K.

Exam traps

  • Using Cp − Cv = R for a liquid. R applies only to an ideal gas.
  • Mixing mass volume with molar volume; v must be m³ mol⁻¹.
  • Getting the sign of α or κ_T wrong; κ_T is defined with a minus sign.
  • Forgetting to square α, or using T in °C instead of kelvin.
  • Using v in cm³ mol⁻¹ without converting to m³ mol⁻¹.

4.8 Partial derivatives and the Jacobian method

Every thermodynamic quantity you can measure sits inside a partial derivative, and most of those derivatives are not measurable directly. The Jacobian method turns any such derivative into a fixed recipe: write it as a ratio of two Jacobians, expand both in one chosen pair of variables such as (T, P), and read off pressure, volume, temperature and heat-capacity terms. Only a few universities teach it inside this unit, but it removes the guesswork from property relations.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for the four fundamental relations, Chapter 4.3 for Maxwell relations, and the ideal gas law from Unit 2.

The Jacobian and its one key identity

The Jacobian of two functions u(x, y) and v(x, y) is the determinant of the 2×2 matrix of their first partial derivatives.

J(u, v) = ∂(u, v)/∂(x, y) = (∂u/∂x)y(∂v/∂y)x − (∂u/∂y)x(∂v/∂x)y

u and v are any two state functions; x and y are the two independent variables chosen to describe the state; the subscript is the variable held constant.

Swapping the two functions flips the sign, J(u, v) = −J(v, u), and a repeated function gives zero, J(u, u) = 0. Everything else is bookkeeping.

(∂u/∂x)y = J(u, y)/J(x, y)

the held-constant variable y appears in both Jacobians. A partial derivative has become a genuine ratio of two determinants.

That identity is the whole method. Partials can now be multiplied and cancelled like fractions, but only in this Jacobian form, never as du/dx.

Cyclic relation and Maxwell relations as ratios

Any target derivative is handled in four steps.

  1. Rewrite the target as J(u, y)/J(x, y), with y the variable held constant.
  2. Pick one pair of variables, (T, P) or (T, V), and expand every Jacobian in that same pair.
  3. Cancel the common pair; only P, V, T and heat capacities remain.
  4. Replace any leftover entropy derivative with a Maxwell relation.

The cyclic relation drops out for free. For any three variables x, y, z:

(∂x/∂y)z (∂y/∂z)x (∂z/∂x)y = −1

each factor is a ratio of two Jacobians in the same pair, and the three ratios cancel to (−1)³ = −1. The minus sign carries the whole content.

Maxwell relations are Jacobian ratios too. (∂S/∂V)T = J(S, T)/J(V, T); expanding both in (T, P) gives the ratio −(∂S/∂P)T over −(∂V/∂P)T, which the Maxwell relation turns into (∂P/∂T)V. See Chapter 4.3 for the same result from mixed second derivatives.

Pressure dependence of enthalpy

Take the pressure dependence of enthalpy. From dH = T dS + V dP (Chapter 4.2), at constant T: (∂H/∂P)T = T(∂S/∂P)T + V.

The entropy term is a ratio: (∂S/∂P)T = J(S, T)/J(P, T). Divide numerator and denominator by J(T, V) and expand; the result is −(∂S/∂V)T over −(∂P/∂V)T. The Maxwell relation (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V leaves (∂P/∂T)V divided by (∂P/∂V)T, which equals −(∂V/∂T)P.

(∂H/∂P)T = V − T(∂V/∂T)P

V is molar volume in m³/mol, T absolute temperature in K, P pressure in Pa; the derivative is taken at constant T.

For an ideal gas V = RT/P and (∂V/∂T)P = R/P, so the right side is RT/P − RT/P = 0. Enthalpy of an ideal gas is independent of pressure, and its Joule–Thomson coefficient is zero. The same expansion gives the internal pressure, (∂U/∂V)T = T(∂P/∂T)V − P.

Worked example

Question. Using the Jacobian method, show that (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V for any fluid, then evaluate it for an ideal gas at 300 K with molar volume v = 0.0240 m³/mol. Take R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. Write the target as a ratio: (∂S/∂V)T = J(S, T)/J(V, T).
  2. Expand the numerator in (T, P): J(S, T) = (∂S/∂T)P(0) − (∂S/∂P)T(1) = −(∂S/∂P)T.
  3. Expand the denominator in the same pair: J(V, T) = (∂V/∂T)P(0) − (∂V/∂P)T(1) = −(∂V/∂P)T.
  4. Divide: the two minus signs cancel, leaving (∂S/∂P)T/(∂V/∂P)T.
  5. Maxwell relation (∂S/∂P)T = −(∂V/∂T)P, so the ratio is −(∂V/∂T)P/(∂V/∂P)T = (∂P/∂T)V.
  6. Ideal gas: P = RT/v, so (∂P/∂T)v = R/v = 8.314/0.0240 = 346.4 Pa/K.

Answer. (∂S/∂V)T = (∂P/∂T)V = R/v = 346.4 Pa/K, that is 346.4 J m⁻³ K⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • ∂ is not a fraction: (∂u/∂x)y cannot be split into du and dx. Only J(u, y)/J(x, y) may be cancelled.
  • Sign of a swap: J(u, v) = −J(v, u). If you swap the order of the two functions you must also change the sign; swapping the order and forgetting the minus sign gives an answer with the wrong sign.
  • Mixed variable pairs: both Jacobians must be expanded in the same pair, or nothing cancels.
  • The cyclic relation equals −1, not +1; writing +1 reverses every derived slope.
  • Unit slip: v in cm³/mol instead of m³/mol changes R/v by a factor of 10⁶.

Remember Write the determinant definition of J(u, v) in the variables x and y. 2 marks

J(u, v) = (∂u/∂x)y(∂v/∂y)x − (∂u/∂y)x(∂v/∂x)y.

Remember State the cyclic relation for three variables x, y, z. 2 marks

(∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂z)x(∂z/∂x)y = −1.

Understand Why is (∂u/∂x)y equal to J(u, y)/J(x, y) and not J(u, x)/J(y, x)? 3 marks

The held-constant variable y must appear in both Jacobians. The second form holds x constant and gives a different derivative.

Apply A gas obeys V = RT/P + b, with b a constant. Find (∂H/∂P)T. 4 marks

(∂V/∂T)P = R/P, so V − T(R/P) = (RT/P + b) − RT/P = b. The derivative equals b at every T and P.

Apply For an ideal gas P = RT/V, evaluate J(P, T) using (T, V) as the pair. 4 marks

J(P, T) = (∂P/∂T)V(0) − (∂P/∂V)T(1) = −(∂P/∂V)T = RT/V².

Analyze Show that the cyclic relation follows from J(u, v) = −J(v, u). 5 marks

Each factor is a Jacobian ratio: J(x, z)/J(y, z), J(y, x)/J(z, x), J(z, y)/J(x, y). Each pair differs by one swap, so the product is (−1)³ = −1.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. The Jacobian technique is the engine behind printed tables of thermodynamic derivatives, and symbolic-algebra software reproduces it in a single line. Research codes that build equations of state use Jacobian ratios to convert measured quantities such as sound speed and thermal expansivity into model parameters. If you go on to process modelling or molecular simulation, search for Bridgman tables, thermodynamic derivative identities, and Jacobian thermodynamic variables.

Takeaways

  1. Free energies exist because at fixed T and V, or fixed T and P, part of the internal energy is unavailable as work.
  2. All thermodynamic relations for a pure fluid follow from just two statements: energy conservation and the entropy inequality.
  3. Maxwell relations turn entropy derivatives, which cannot be measured, into P–V–T derivatives, which can.
  4. Residual properties are the bridge between an equation of state and the steam tables, because both are measured from the same ideal-gas reference.
  5. Inside the saturation dome every specific property is a straight-line mixture of saturated liquid and saturated vapour values, weighted by quality.
  6. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation predicts how boiling point shifts with pressure, which is why a pressure cooker cooks faster.
  7. Cp − Cv is zero for an ideal gas and positive for every real fluid, and its size comes purely from PVT data.
  8. The Jacobian method removes guesswork: any partial derivative can be reduced to P, V, T, Cp and Cv by a fixed recipe.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Show from the Gibbs free energy that (∂Cp/∂P)_T = −T(∂²V/∂T²)_P, and use it to judge whether the heat capacity of a real gas can be treated as a function of temperature alone. 14 marks

Glossary

Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs free energy, equal to (∂G/∂n_i) at constant T, P and other mole numbers.
Compressibility factor
Z = PV/(RT); equal to 1 for an ideal gas and different from 1 for a real gas.
Corresponding states
The principle that fluids at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure have similar properties.
Critical point
The highest temperature and pressure at which liquid and vapour can be distinguished; above it there is one fluid phase.
Cyclic relation
The identity (∂x/∂y)_z (∂y/∂z)_x (∂z/∂x)_y = −1 for three variables linked by a state relation.
Equation of state
A relation among P, V and T for a fluid, such as the ideal-gas, van der Waals or Peng–Robinson equation.
Exact differential
A differential expression whose integral between two states is path independent.
Extensive property
A property that scales with the amount of material, such as total volume, internal energy or entropy.
Fugacity
An effective pressure that replaces the true pressure in the ideal-gas chemical-potential formula, so that real-fluid behaviour is retained.
Fugacity coefficient
The ratio φ = f/P, tending to 1 as pressure tends to zero.
Intensive property
A property independent of the size of the system, such as temperature, pressure or molar volume.
Inversion temperature
The temperature below which a gas cools on isenthalpic throttling and above which it warms.
Isothermal compressibility
κ = −(1/V)(∂V/∂P)_T, a positive quantity measuring volume change under pressure at fixed temperature.
Latent heat of vaporisation
Enthalpy change when unit mass changes from saturated liquid to saturated vapour at constant temperature and pressure.
Reduced property
A property divided by its critical value, such as T_r = T/T_c, used in generalized correlations.
Saturated liquid
Liquid at its boiling point for the given pressure, with quality zero.
Saturated vapour
Vapour at its condensation point for the given pressure, with quality one.
State function
A property whose change depends only on the initial and final states, not on the path taken.
Subcooled liquid
Liquid at a temperature below its saturation temperature for the given pressure.
Superheated vapour
Vapour at a temperature above its saturation temperature for the given pressure.
Thermal expansion coefficient
α = (1/V)(∂V/∂T)_P, the fractional volume change per kelvin at constant pressure.
Thermodynamic chart
A plot such as a T–s, P–h or Mollier diagram from which properties can be read graphically.
Triple point
The single temperature and pressure at which solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium.
