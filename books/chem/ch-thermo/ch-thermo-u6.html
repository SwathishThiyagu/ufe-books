6.1 Criteria for phase equilibrium and stability

At equilibrium two phases of the same mixture share the same temperature, the same pressure, and the same chemical potential for every component. Chemical potential μ_i is the partial molar Gibbs energy in J/mol. It measures how G changes when a tiny amount of component i is added. Fugacity f_i in Pa is the working variable because μ has no absolute zero. A phase is stable only if its Gibbs energy is a minimum, not merely flat. Distillation, extraction, crystallisation and refinery separations all use these tests.

You need: Unit 4 (Gibbs energy and fundamental relations) and Unit 5 (chemical potential, fugacity and activity).

The three equalities

Start from the fundamental relation for a closed system of C components: dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μ_i dn_i. Here G is Gibbs energy in J, S entropy in J/K, T absolute temperature in K, V volume in m³, P pressure in Pa, n_i moles of component i, and μ_i chemical potential in J/mol. μ_i is also the partial molar Gibbs energy: the change in G when one mole of i is added at fixed T, P and other moles. At fixed T and P with no reaction, dG = Σ μ_i dn_i. Transfer dn_i moles of i from phase β to phase α changes G by (μ_i^α − μ_i^β) dn_i. If this is negative, transfer lowers G. Equilibrium needs it zero for every component, so μ_i^α = μ_i^β for every i and every pair of phases. Thermal equilibrium needs T^α = T^β. Mechanical equilibrium needs P^α = P^β. A curved interface can hold a small pressure jump because surface tension supports it.

μ_i = μ_i° + RT ln(f_i/f_i°)

μ_i is chemical potential in J/mol; μ_i° is the standard chemical potential in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K; f_i is fugacity in Pa; f_i° is the standard fugacity in Pa. For the same component, the standard term cancels between phases, so μ_i^α = μ_i^β becomes f_i^α = f_i^β.

The fugacity form is the working criterion. It removes the arbitrary reference value inside μ. See 6.2 for counting the variables you may set, and 6.4 for using the equality in vapour-liquid equilibrium.

Stability: a minimum, not a saddle

At fixed T and P, a stable phase has the lowest G. For a binary mixture write g^M/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + A x₁x₂. Here g^M is molar Gibbs energy of mixing in J/mol, R is the gas constant, T is in K, x₁ and x₂ are mole fractions, and A is a dimensionless interaction parameter. The first derivative with respect to x₁ is ln(x₁/x₂) + A(1 − 2x₁). Setting it to zero gives a stationary composition. The second derivative is 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A. Stability requires (∂²G/∂x₁²)_{T,P} > 0, equivalently this expression > 0. The spinodal is the locus where the second derivative is zero. The binodal is the common-tangent line between two coexisting phases; it gives the actual phase compositions. Between binodal and spinodal the mixture is metastable. Inside the spinodal it is unstable: any small composition wave lowers G, so the mixture splits without a nucleation barrier. Some syllabi call the first equality the criterion of equilibrium and this curvature test the criterion of stability.

Using the criteria without mixing up variables

Use the criteria in order. First confirm T and P are equal; for a flat interface this is enough for thermal and mechanical equilibrium. Then write f_i^α = f_i^β for each component; this is one equation per component. Finally check curvature. The equimolar point with A = 2.5 has negative curvature, so it cannot be a single phase even though its first derivative is zero. A = 1.5 gives positive curvature at x₁ = 0.5 and is locally stable. The same algebra appears in liquid-liquid extraction and polymer blends; see 6.7. For pure components the fugacity equality reduces to the vapour-pressure curve; see 6.3.

Worked example

Question. At 300 K and 1 bar, the molar Gibbs energy of mixing of a binary liquid is g^M/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + A x₁x₂ with A = 2.5. Test the equimolar mixture (x₁ = 0.5) for stability.

  1. For x₁ = 0.5, x₂ = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5.
  2. First derivative: ln(x₁/x₂) + A(1 − 2x₁) = ln(1) + 2.5(1 − 1) = 0. The point is stationary.
  3. Second derivative: 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A = 2 + 2 − 5 = −1.0.
  4. The value is negative, so G curves downward at x₁ = 0.5. A small split into two compositions lowers G; the mixture is unstable.
  5. Value of g^M/RT at x₁ = 0.5: 0.5 ln 0.5 + 0.5 ln 0.5 + 2.5(0.5)(0.5) = −0.6931 + 0.625 = −0.0681.

Answer. The equimolar mixture is unstable because d²(g^M/RT)/dx₁² = −1.0 < 0; it splits, and g^M/RT at x₁ = 0.5 is −0.0681.

Exam traps

  • Equating partial pressures p_i instead of fugacities f_i. At high pressure the fugacity coefficient φ_i changes the answer: f_i = φ_i y_i P in a gas.
  • Calling a mixture stable because the first derivative of G is zero. A stationary point can be a maximum or a saddle; the second derivative must be positive.
  • Confusing fugacity f_i (Pa) with fugacity coefficient φ_i (dimensionless). They differ by pressure and composition.
  • Calling the spinodal the phase boundary. The actual coexisting compositions come from the binodal, the common tangent where μ_i is equal in both phases.
  • Using a pure-component standard state when the problem needs a Henry standard state for a dilute solute; the activity definition and the numerical fugacity change.

Understand Why is fugacity used instead of chemical potential in phase-equilibrium calculations? 3 marks

Chemical potential contains an arbitrary reference value. Fugacity is defined through μ_i = μ_i° + RT ln(f_i/f_i°). For the same component the reference cancels, so equality of μ becomes equality of f. Fugacity also has pressure units and connects directly to measurable P, x and y.

Apply For a binary mixture, g^M/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + A x₁x₂ with A = 1.5. Compute the second derivative at x₁ = 0.5 and classify the mixture. 4 marks

x₂ = 0.5. d²(g^M/RT)/dx₁² = 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A = 2 + 2 − 3 = 1.0. The value is positive, so the equimolar mixture is locally stable.

Analyze At A = 2.5 and x₁ = 0.5 the first derivative is zero but the mixture is unstable. Explain what the second derivative shows. 4 marks

The first derivative being zero only makes x₁ = 0.5 stationary. The second derivative is −1.0, so G curves downward there. Any small composition change lowers G, and the system lowers G further by splitting into two phases whose compositions lie on the common tangent.

Remember Define spinodal and binodal in a binary mixture. 2 marks

The spinodal is the locus where (∂²G/∂x₁²)_{T,P} = 0. The binodal gives the compositions of two coexisting phases, found from the common tangent or equal chemical potentials.

Understand Why can a curved interface have P^α ≠ P^β while a flat interface cannot? 3 marks

Surface tension at a curved interface supports a pressure jump, often written ΔP = 2γ/r for a spherical droplet, with γ in N/m and r in m. For a flat interface r → ∞, so the pressure jump goes to zero and P^α = P^β.

Apply A binary mixture has (∂²G/∂x₁²)_{T,P} = +2.0 kJ/mol at a stationary composition. Is it stable? What if the value is −2.0 kJ/mol? 5 marks

Positive curvature means a local minimum of G, so the mixture is locally stable. Negative curvature means a maximum or saddle in the composition direction, so it is unstable and tends to split. The units are kJ/mol because G is per mole and x₁ is dimensionless.

Analyze A student finds μ_i^α = μ_i^β for every component but T^α ≠ T^β. Is the system at phase equilibrium? 4 marks

No. Phase equilibrium needs thermal, mechanical and chemical equality together. If temperatures differ, heat can flow and G can fall; the chemical equality alone does not make the whole system equilibrium.

6.2 Phase rule and Duhem's theorem

The phase rule answers one practical question: how many properties of a system can you choose before the rest are decided for you? It counts phases, components and equilibrium equations and returns a single number F, the degrees of freedom. Duhem's theorem extends the same counting to a closed vessel of known overall composition: pick any two independent intensive variables and everything else follows. Flash-drum specification, crystalliser control and distillation column design all rest on this count, so a wrong F sends a whole flowsheet in the wrong direction.

You need: 6.1 for the equal-chemical-potential equations that the count subtracts, and Unit 1 for intensive versus extensive properties.

Count the unknowns, count the equations

A phase is a part of the system with uniform properties; P counts such parts. A component is a chemically independent constituent; C is the smallest number of them needed to describe the composition of every phase. A degree of freedom is an intensive variable you may change on your own without a phase appearing or vanishing; F counts them.

Inside one phase the intensive variables are temperature T, pressure and the mole fractions. The C mole fractions sum to 1, so only C − 1 of them are free. Temperature and pressure are common to all phases at equilibrium, so across P phases the unknowns number 2 + P(C − 1).

The equations are the equilibrium conditions from 6.1. Each component has one chemical potential, and it must match in every phase. Matching C components across P phases gives C(P − 1) equalities. Nothing else is needed, because the mole-fraction sums were already used up in the unknown count.

F = 2 + P(C − 1) − C(P − 1) = C − P + 2

F is the number of degrees of freedom, C the number of components, P the number of phases; the 2 stands for temperature and pressure.

Pure water with liquid and vapour present: C = 1, P = 2, so F = 1. You may pick the temperature, and the pressure then sits on the vapour-pressure curve. Try to set both and one phase disappears.

Reactions and the reduced rule

A reaction ties species together, so species are not components. Count species S, independent reactions R, and any extra constraint a, then C = S − R − a. Independent means no reaction in the set is a sum of multiples of the others.

  • Example: N₂, H₂ and NH₃ with NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂. S = 3, R = 1, so C = 2. Two components means one independent mole fraction per phase; the extent follows from reaction equilibrium (see Unit 7, Chemical Reaction Equilibria).
  • The extra constraint a covers things like electroneutrality in a salt solution, or a known fixed ratio of two species.

Most laboratory and plant equipment runs at a fixed pressure — a fume hood at 1 atm, an open beaker at 1 atm, or a reactor vented through a pressure-control valve set at 2 bar. Freezing the pressure removes one variable and gives the reduced rule.

F = C − P + 1

F is degrees of freedom, C components, P phases; the rule applies when either temperature or pressure is held fixed.

Water boiling in an open pan at 1 atm: C = 1, P = 2, so F = 1 − 2 + 1 = 0. At that pressure the boiling temperature is fixed at 100 °C; you have no freedom left. Raise the pressure in a cooker and the fixed temperature rises with it.

Duhem's theorem

In a closed system of known overall composition, fixing any two independent intensive variables fixes every remaining intensive variable and the amounts of all phases. That statement is Duhem's theorem, and it is stronger than the phase rule because it also pins down how much of each phase is present.

Why, in one line: the phase amounts add P − 1 unknowns (they sum to the total) and the component balances add C − 1 independent equations (the overall fractions sum to 1). Unknowns go up by P − 1 and equations by C − 1, so the count becomes (C − P + 2) + (P − 1) − (C − 1) = 2.

Take binary vapour-liquid equilibrium with overall mole fraction z₁ fixed. Unknowns are temperature, pressure, x₁, y₁ and the liquid fraction, five in all. Equations are the two fugacity equalities plus one material balance, three in all. Fix temperature and x₁ and nothing is free: pressure and y₁ are fixed by the two equilibrium equations, and the material balance then fixes the liquid fraction. With T fixed the phase rule leaves F = 1 for the two-phase binary, so pressure, x₁ and y₁ lie on a pair of curves, not a surface (see 6.3).

Worked example

Question. Find the number of degrees of freedom for each case. (a) Pure water with liquid and vapour present. (b) Ethanol–water liquid and vapour at a fixed total pressure of 1 atm. (c) A gas of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ at 1 atm in which NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂ can occur. State which variables are free in each case.

  1. (a) One species, one reaction-free system: C = 1. Two phases: P = 2. F = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1. Only one variable is free; choosing T fixes P on the vapour-pressure curve.
  2. (b) Ethanol and water do not react: C = 2, two phases P = 2, so C − P + 2 = 2. The pressure is already fixed at 1 atm, which uses one degree of freedom, so F = 2 − 1 = 1. That one variable is temperature; fixing it fixes x₁ and y₁.
  3. (c) Species present S = 3 (N₂, H₂, NH₃). Independent reactions R = 1, since NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂ relates all three. No other constraint, so C = 3 − 1 = 2.
  4. One gas phase, P = 1. F = 2 − 1 + 2 = 3: temperature, pressure and one composition variable; once temperature, pressure and that composition variable are set, reaction equilibrium plus the sum of the mole fractions fixes all three species mole fractions.
  5. At the stated 1 atm, pressure is used up: F = 3 − 1 = 2, namely temperature and one composition variable.

Answer. F = 1 for (a), F = 1 for (b) at 1 atm, and F = 3 for (c) generally, falling to F = 2 at 1 atm.

Exam traps

  • Counting species as components when a reaction exists. Three species with one independent reaction give C = 2, not 3.
  • Adding the full +2 when the pressure is already fixed. Once the pressure is set, use F = C − P + 1 (P = number of phases), otherwise every answer is one too high.
  • Remembering that F counts intensive variables only. Phase amounts are extensive and are not degrees of freedom; Duhem's theorem is what brings them in.
  • Missing extra constraints such as electroneutrality in an electrolyte solution, which reduce C by one more.
  • Thinking any two intensive variables will do for Duhem's theorem. They must be independent; fixing T and the boiling pressure of a pure liquid is a contradiction, not a specification.

6.3 Phase equilibria of pure components and mixtures

Phase equilibrium is the state where every component has the same fugacity in every phase present. For one pure substance that equality fixes the vapour pressure at each temperature. For a mixture it fixes two curves: the bubble line and the dew line. Those curves are the working maps for distillation, flash drums, condensers and LPG storage. This chapter reads the maps from fugacity equality, without adding activity coefficients yet.

You need the fugacity and chemical-potential criterion from <a href='#criteria-for-phase-equilibrium-and-stability'>6.1, fugacity definitions from Unit 5, and the Clausius-Clapeyron equation from Unit 4.

Pure component: one curve from fugacity equality

At saturation a pure liquid and its vapour coexist. Equilibrium requires equal temperature, equal pressure and equal fugacity. For a fixed T only one pressure satisfies fL=fV. That pressure is Psat(T), the vapour pressure. Its plot against T is the vapour-pressure curve. On a P-T diagram it ends at the triple point and the critical point; fusion and sublimation curves meet it at the triple point.

fL(T, Psat) = fV(T, Psat)

fL and fV are the liquid and vapour fugacities in the same pressure unit; Psat is the saturation vapour pressure; T is absolute temperature.

The Antoine equation fits the curve over a modest range. The constants A, B and C belong to one unit set. A set with P in mmHg and T in °C is not interchangeable with one using kPa and K. Check the units before substituting.

ln Psat = A − B/(T + C)

Psat is vapour pressure in the unit of the table; T is temperature in the unit of the table; A, B and C are fitted constants.

The slope gives latent heat. From Unit 4, two vapour pressures at T1 and T2 give Δhvap = R ln(P2/P1)/(1/T1 − 1/T2).

d ln Psat/d(1/T) = −Δhvap/R

Δhvap is enthalpy of vaporization in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1; T is absolute temperature in K.

Pressure changes liquid fugacity at fixed T through the Poynting correction. It is small unless P is far from Psat.

fL(P) = fL(Psat) exp[VL(P − Psat)/(RT)]

VL is liquid molar volume in m³/mol; P is pressure; Psat is saturation pressure; R is the gas constant; T is absolute temperature.

Mixtures: bubble and dew lines

For a mixture the equality is per component: fiV=fiL for every component i. At fixed T, a liquid of composition x has a bubble pressure where the first vapour bubble forms. A vapour of composition y has a dew pressure where the first liquid drop forms. The P-x-y diagram draws both at fixed T; the T-x-y diagram draws both at fixed P.

For the ideal liquid mixture used here, fiV=yiP and fiL=xiPisat. Then yiP=xiPisat. A non-ideal liquid adds γi, treated in <a href='#vle-raoult-s-law-and-modified-raoult-s-law'>6.4.

yiP = xiPisat

xi is liquid mole fraction; yi is vapour mole fraction; P is total pressure; Pisat is pure i vapour pressure.

For a binary with x2=1−x1 and y2=1−y1, the bubble pressure is Pbub = x1P1sat + x2P2sat and the dew pressure is 1/Pdew = y1/P1sat + y2/P2sat. At x1=0 and x1=1 the two curves meet the pure vapour pressures.

Pbub = x1P1sat + (1−x1)P2sat;   1/Pdew = y1/P1sat + (1−y1)/P2sat

Pbub is bubble pressure; Pdew is dew pressure; x1 and y1 are mole fractions of component 1; P1sat and P2sat are pure-component vapour pressures.

The figure uses benzene(1)–toluene(2) at 373.15 K: P1sat=180.1 kPa and P2sat=74.2 kPa. The bubble line is P=74.2+105.9x1; the dew line is P=1/(y1/180.1+(1−y1)/74.2).

Tie lines, lever rule and azeotropes

Between the curves a mixture splits into liquid x and vapour y at the same T and P. A horizontal tie line joins x and y. For overall composition z1 between x1 and y1, the lever rule gives nL/nV=(y1−z1)/(z1−x1). The liquid fraction is (y1−z1)/(y1−x1); the vapour fraction is (z1−x1)/(y1−x1).

On a T-x-y diagram at fixed P, heating a liquid reaches the bubble line where the first vapour appears. Further heating reaches the dew line where the last drop disappears. Between the lines both phases exist. The tie line again joins x and y.

At an azeotrope the two branches touch and x1=y1. Vapour and liquid have the same composition, so simple distillation cannot change composition. Common azeotropes can be minimum-boiling or maximum-boiling; changing pressure can move or break them. See 6.4 for the activity-coefficient cause.

Worked example

Question. Benzene(1)–toluene(2) at 373.15 K has P₁sat = 180.1 kPa and P₂sat = 74.2 kPa. (a) For a liquid with x₁ = 0.30, find the bubble pressure and vapour composition y₁. (b) For a vapour with y₁ = 0.30, find the dew pressure and liquid composition x₁.

  1. Write the data: P₁sat=180.1 kPa, P₂sat=74.2 kPa, x₁=0.30, y₁=0.30.
  2. Bubble pressure: Pbub = x₁P₁sat +(1−x₁)P₂sat = 0.30×180.1 +0.70×74.2 =54.03+51.94=105.97 kPa.
  3. Vapour composition: y₁ = x₁P₁sat/Pbub =54.03/105.97=0.5099.
  4. Dew pressure: 1/Pdew = y₁/P₁sat +(1−y₁)/P₂sat =0.30/180.1+0.70/74.2 =0.0016657+0.0094340=0.0110997 kPa−1, so Pdew=90.09 kPa.
  5. Liquid composition: x₁ = y₁Pdew/P₁sat =0.30×90.09/180.1=27.03/180.1=0.1501.

Answer. The bubble point is 105.97 kPa with y₁ = 0.5099, and the dew point is 90.09 kPa with x₁ = 0.1501.

Exam traps

  • Swapping x and y on a P-x-y diagram. The bubble line is read with liquid x; the dew line is read with vapour y.
  • Using Antoine constants without checking the unit set. P may be mmHg, kPa or bar, and T may be °C or K; mixing sets gives a wrong Psat.
  • Calling the bubble point and dew point the same thing. Bubble point is the first vapour from liquid; dew point is the first liquid from vapour.
  • Using a pure-component Psat for a mixture without the composition term. For an ideal mixture the term xiPisat is essential.
  • Treating an azeotrope as a pure component. At an azeotrope x=y, but the mixture still has two components and the branches touch only at that point.

6.4 VLE: Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law

An ideal solution has no special attraction or repulsion between unlike molecules. Then each component's partial pressure in the vapour is just its liquid mole fraction times its own vapour pressure: y_i P = x_i P_i^sat. That is Raoult's law. Most real pairs — ethanol and water, acetone and chloroform — disobey it, so one correction per component is added, the activity coefficient γ_i. The modified form y_i P = x_i γ_i P_i^sat is the working equation behind distillation, absorption and flash-drum design in every chemical plant.

You need: 6.1 for the equal-fugacity criterion, 6.3 for the P-x-y diagram, and Unit 5 for the activity coefficient and the Margules, van Laar and Wilson models.

Where Raoult's law comes from

At equilibrium every component has the same fugacity in the vapour and in the liquid, f_i^V = f_i^L (see 6.1). Writing each side in terms of composition gives the general starting equation.

y_i φ_i^V P = x_i γ_i f_i^L,pure(T, P)

y_i is the vapour mole fraction, x_i the liquid mole fraction, P the total pressure in kPa, φ_i^V the vapour-phase fugacity coefficient, γ_i the liquid-phase activity coefficient, and f_i^L,pure the fugacity of pure liquid i at the mixture temperature and pressure.

Three assumptions reduce this to Raoult's law.

  • Ideal-gas vapour: φ_i^V = 1. Acceptable up to a few bar.
  • Standard state is pure liquid i at the same T and P, with the Poynting correction neglected, so f_i^L,pure ≈ P_i^sat, the vapour pressure of pure i at T.
  • Ideal solution: γ_i = 1, meaning unlike molecules attract each other exactly as strongly as like molecules.

y_i P = x_i P_i^sat

y_i and x_i are mole fractions in vapour and liquid, P the total pressure, and P_i^sat the saturation (vapour) pressure of pure component i at the system temperature T, in kPa. Both Σy_i and Σx_i equal 1.

For a pure component, x_i = y_i = 1, and the same equation collapses to P = P_i^sat, the vapour-pressure curve. Get P_i^sat from an Antoine correlation, ln P_i^sat = A − B/(T + C). Use each correlation exactly as tabulated: the constants come with a stated temperature unit (K or °C) and pressure unit (kPa or mmHg). Plug T in that stated temperature unit, get P_i^sat in the stated pressure unit, then convert the result to kPa (1 mmHg = 0.13332 kPa) before adding terms (see Unit 4, Clausius-Clapeyron equation). Benzene–toluene near 1 atm is the classic pair that obeys the law closely, because the two molecules are similar in size and shape.

Modified Raoult's law and the bubble-pressure recipe

Drop only the third assumption. Keep the vapour ideal and keep the pure-liquid standard state. All liquid non-ideality now sits inside γ_i, and γ_i depends on the whole composition, not on x_i alone (Unit 5).

y_i P = x_i γ_i P_i^sat

γ_i is the activity coefficient of component i in the liquid at the system T and composition x, dimensionless, and γ_i → 1 as x_i → 1. The other symbols are as before.

  1. Fix the temperature T and the liquid composition x_i.
  2. Get every P_i^sat from Antoine at that T.
  3. Choose an activity model — Margules, van Laar or Wilson — and evaluate every γ_i at the given x.
  4. Add the partial pressures: P = Σ x_i γ_i P_i^sat.
  5. Divide each term by P: y_i = x_i γ_i P_i^sat / P. Check that Σ y_i = 1.

Bubble temperature: P and x are known, T is not, and both P_i^sat and γ_i move with T. Guess T, compute the sum, and repeat until it equals the given P. Dew point: y and P are known; T is not, and x is not. Guess T, get P_i^sat, start with γ_i = 1. Compute x_i = y_i P/(γ_i P_i^sat), normalise the x_i so they sum to 1, recompute γ_i at that x, then evaluate Σ y_i/(γ_i P_i^sat). Adjust T (or P, if it is the dew pressure you want) and repeat until the sum equals 1.

K_i = y_i/x_i = γ_i P_i^sat / P

K_i is the K-value (vapour-liquid equilibrium ratio) of component i, dimensionless. It is the number a stage-by-stage column calculation uses.

α₁₂ = K₁/K₂ = γ₁P₁^sat / (γ₂P₂^sat)

α₁₂ is the relative volatility of component 1 with respect to component 2. α₁₂ above 1 means the vapour is richer in component 1; distillation gets easier as α moves away from 1.

Working backwards: γ from data and from an azeotrope

Measured P, x and y give activity coefficients directly, with no model: γ_i = y_i P/(x_i P_i^sat). Do this at every data point and you have the experimental γ(x) curve; fit a model through it.

An azeotrope is faster still. There x_i = y_i, the composition cancels, and each component gives P = γ_i P_i^sat, so γ₁P₁^sat = γ₂P₂^sat = P. One azeotrope point therefore fixes two activity coefficients. A one-parameter model such as Margules, ln γ₁ = A x₂² and ln γ₂ = A x₁², is then solved for A on the spot; a two-parameter model needs the Gibbs-Duhem equation (Unit 5) as the second relation.

A minimum-boiling azeotrope has P above the ideal value, so γ_i > 1: positive deviation, unlike molecules repel. A maximum-boiling azeotrope has P below the ideal value, so γ_i < 1: negative deviation, unlike molecules attract.

The azeotrope also marks the limit of ordinary distillation. At x_i = y_i the driving force vanishes and a plain column cannot cross that composition; pressure-swing or azeotropic distillation with an entrainer is needed.

Worked example

Question. At 343 K a binary liquid has x₁ = 0.40. The saturation pressures at 343 K are P₁^sat = 60.0 kPa and P₂^sat = 20.0 kPa. The liquid follows the one-parameter Margules model, ln γ₁ = A x₂² and ln γ₂ = A x₁², with A = 1.20. (a) Find the bubble pressure P and the vapour mole fraction y₁. (b) Compare with ideal Raoult's law.

  1. x₂ = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60.
  2. ln γ₁ = 1.20 × (0.60)² = 1.20 × 0.36 = 0.4320, so γ₁ = e^0.4320 = 1.5403.
  3. ln γ₂ = 1.20 × (0.40)² = 1.20 × 0.16 = 0.1920, so γ₂ = e^0.1920 = 1.2116.
  4. Partial pressures: x₁γ₁P₁^sat = 0.40 × 1.5403 × 60.0 = 36.97 kPa; x₂γ₂P₂^sat = 0.60 × 1.2116 × 20.0 = 14.54 kPa.
  5. Bubble pressure: P = 36.97 + 14.54 = 51.51 kPa.
  6. Vapour composition: y₁ = 36.97/51.51 = 0.7177 and y₂ = 14.54/51.51 = 0.2823; the sum is 1.0000, as it must be.
  7. Ideal case: P = 0.40 × 60.0 + 0.60 × 20.0 = 24.0 + 12.0 = 36.0 kPa, and y₁ = 24.0/36.0 = 0.6667.
  8. The real pressure is 15.5 kPa above the ideal value. Both γ values exceed 1, so this is a positive-deviation system: component 1 escapes more easily than an ideal solution would allow.

Answer. P = 51.51 kPa with y₁ = 0.7177, against 36.0 kPa and y₁ = 0.6667 from ideal Raoult's law.

Exam traps

  • Applying plain Raoult's law to a pair with a known azeotrope. Ideal Raoult's law cannot produce an azeotrope at all unless the two P_i^sat are equal, so it will badly misfit ethanol–water.
  • Evaluating γ_i at the wrong composition. In the one-parameter Margules model γ₁ uses x₂² and γ₂ uses x₁²; swapping the two squares is the commonest slip in the exam hall.
  • Check which T unit and P unit the Antoine constants were fitted for. If they are in °C and mmHg, put T in °C, get P_i^sat in mmHg, then convert to kPa. Converting T to K first, or pressure only, gives nonsense.
  • Reusing γ_i across temperatures. Both P_i^sat and γ_i change with T, so a bubble-temperature answer cannot borrow activity coefficients from a bubble-pressure answer at another temperature.
  • Reading a dew point as a bubble point. At a dew point the given composition is y; substituting it as x gives a liquid far richer in the heavy component and the wrong pressure.

6.5 Henry's law and dilute solutions

A dilute solute does not see a pure-liquid environment. It sees solvent, so its vapour pressure is not x_i P_i^sat. Henry's law replaces P_i^sat with a measured slope H_i: partial pressure equals mole fraction times H_i. This chapter shows why H_i is the limiting slope at x_i → 0, how it links to the infinite-dilution activity coefficient, how temperature and pressure shift it, and how a dilute binary uses Henry for the solute and Raoult for the solvent. Dissolved oxygen in boiler feed water and CO₂ in soft drinks are the working examples.

You need: 6.4 for Raoult's law and 6.3 for the standard states.

Why the solute needs its own slope

Raoult's law describes the solvent of a dilute solution well and the solute badly. A gas molecule in water is surrounded almost entirely by water molecules. It has no pure-liquid environment, so its pure vapour pressure Pisat is the wrong reference. Henry's law fixes this by replacing the pure-liquid vapour pressure with a measured slope.

pi = xi Hi    or    fi = xi ki

pi is the partial pressure of solute i in the gas (Pa); xi is its mole fraction in the liquid; Hi is Henry's constant on the mole-fraction scale (Pa or bar). fi is fugacity and ki is Henry's constant on the fugacity basis, in the same units as fi.

Hi is the limiting slope of the pi against xi curve as xi → 0. It is a property of the solute–solvent pair and of temperature. For CO₂ in water at 298 K, H is about 1.6 × 10³ bar. For O₂ in water it is far larger, so oxygen is much less soluble.

The standard state behind Hi is a hypothetical pure liquid: the solute at xi = 1 behaving as it does at infinite dilution. The reference is hypothetical because no real pure liquid solute has that environment. Because of this choice, the solute's activity coefficient approaches 1 as xi → 0. The two constants are linked:

γi∞ = Hi / Pisat

γi∞ is the activity coefficient of solute i at infinite dilution; Pisat is the vapour pressure of pure liquid i (Pa).

This ratio can be large. For a gas above its critical temperature, Pisat does not exist at all. Henry's constant is then the only usable reference.

One curve, two laws

In a binary solution the solvent is nearly pure and the solute is nearly absent. The same measured curve therefore obeys Raoult's law at the solvent end and Henry's law at the solute end. A one-parameter Margules model reproduces both ends with a single constant A:

p₁ = x₁ exp[A(1 − x₁)²] P₁sat,    A = ln(H₁ / P₁sat)

p₁ is the partial pressure of component 1 (Pa); x₁ is its liquid mole fraction; P₁sat is its pure vapour pressure (Pa); H₁ is its Henry constant (Pa); A is a dimensionless Margules constant.

Check the two limits. As x₁ → 1, the exponential becomes 1 and p₁ → P₁sat. As x₁ → 0, p₁ ≈ x₁ exp(A) P₁sat = x₁ H₁. The Henry line is the tangent at x₁ = 0. The Raoult line p₁ = x₁ P₁sat is the tangent at x₁ = 1. The figure uses P₁sat = 60 kPa and H₁ = 90 kPa, so A = ln(90/60) = 0.405.

The Henry line is accurate only while the solute is dilute. A common working range is xi below 0.02 to 0.05. Inside that range the solvent still follows Raoult's law: psolvent = xsolvent Psolventsat. This is why a dilute binary uses two standard states at once — Raoult-based for the solvent, Henry-based for the solute. Both are the same fugacity equality from 6.1, just written with different references.

Temperature and pressure move H

Henry's constant is not a constant. It changes with temperature and, weakly, with pressure. The temperature dependence is:

d ln Hi / dT = (h̄iV − h̄i∞) / (R T²)

Hi is Henry's constant (Pa); T is absolute temperature (K); R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; h̄iV is the partial molar enthalpy of i in the vapour (J mol⁻¹); h̄i∞ is the partial molar enthalpy of i at infinite dilution in the liquid (J mol⁻¹).

For most gases in water the right side is positive: dissolving releases heat, so H rises with T and solubility falls. That is why a warm soft drink goes flat. Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water. Boiler feed water is deaerated because even the small amount of oxygen that remains at high temperature corrodes steel.

d ln Hi / dP = V̄i∞ / (R T)

V̄i∞ is the partial molar volume of i at infinite dilution (m³ mol⁻¹); P is pressure (Pa); R and T as above.

V̄i∞ is small for gases, so pressure changes H only slightly. The bigger effect of pressure is on the partial pressure itself. A soda bottle at 3 bar of CO₂ holds more dissolved CO₂ than one at 1 bar because xCO₂ = pCO₂/HCO₂ rises with pCO₂.

Worked example

Question. Carbon dioxide in water at 298 K has a Henry constant of about 1.6 × 10³ bar on the mole-fraction scale. A soft drink is carbonated at a CO₂ partial pressure of 3.0 bar. Find the mole fraction of dissolved CO₂, and its molality in water.

  1. Henry's law on the mole-fraction scale: pCO₂ = xCO₂ HCO₂. Rearrange: xCO₂ = pCO₂ / HCO₂.
  2. Substitute: xCO₂ = 3.0 bar / (1.6 × 10³ bar) = 1.875 × 10⁻³.
  3. Take 1 mol of solution. Then nCO₂ = 1.875 × 10⁻³ mol and nwater = 1 − 1.875 × 10⁻³ = 0.998125 mol.
  4. Mass of water = 0.998125 mol × 18.015 g mol⁻¹ = 17.981 g = 0.017981 kg.
  5. Molality m = nCO₂ / mass of water = 1.875 × 10⁻³ mol / 0.017981 kg = 0.1043 mol kg⁻¹.

Answer. xCO₂ = 1.875 × 10⁻³, and the molality of dissolved CO₂ is 0.1043 mol kg⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Reaching for Pisat for a gas held above its critical temperature, where no vapour pressure exists. Henry's constant is the correct reference there.
  • Treating H as dimensionless. On the mole-fraction scale it carries pressure units — Pa or bar — and must match the units of pi.
  • Using Hi and Pisat as if they were the same number. They coincide only when γi∞ = 1, which is rare for gases in water.
  • Applying pi = xi Hi far outside the dilute range. Above xi ≈ 0.05 the real curve bends away from the Henry line.
  • Getting the temperature sign wrong. For most gases in water H rises with T, so solubility falls on warming — the reason warm soda is flat.

6.6 High-pressure vapour-liquid equilibrium

At 1 bar the vapour phase is nearly ideal, so modified Raoult's law works. At 40 bar or 150 bar a gas is dense, its molecules interact, and the vapour fugacity coefficient φiV is far from 1. The liquid standard state and Poynting correction also lose accuracy. High-pressure vapour-liquid equilibrium therefore writes the same fugacity equality, yiφiV = xiφiL, and takes both coefficients from one equation of state. It is how natural gas is dried and separated, how high-pressure absorbers are designed, and how supercritical CO₂ extracts spice oleoresins.

You need: 6.1, 6.4, and Unit 2 for cubic equations of state and mixing rules.

Where modified Raoult's law breaks

Modified Raoult's law is yiP = xiγiPisat. Here yi is vapour mole fraction, xi liquid mole fraction, γi activity coefficient, Pisat vapour pressure, and P total pressure. It works near 1 bar because three corrections are hidden and small.

  • The vapour is treated as ideal, so φiV = 1.
  • The liquid standard fugacity is taken as Pisat, with Poynting factor exp[ViL(P − Pisat)/(RT)] near 1.
  • γi comes from low-pressure data.

At 40 bar a vapour is dense; φiV can be 0.8 or lower. A component above its critical temperature has no Pisat. The Poynting factor grows, and a γ model fitted at low pressure cannot describe a liquid whose density and composition approach the vapour's.

yiφiV = xiφiL

yi, xi are mole fractions in vapour and liquid; φiV, φiL are fugacity coefficients at T, P and phase composition.

The safe route uses one equation of state for both phases. This is the phi-phi method; the gamma-phi method of 6.4 is its low-pressure limit.

K-values from one equation of state

For each component the equilibrium ratio is Ki = yi/xi. Divide the fugacity equality by xiP:

Ki = φiL / φiV

Ki is the distribution ratio (dimensionless); values above 1 mean the component prefers vapour.

Use a cubic EOS from Unit 2. Its mixture parameters are a = ΣiΣjxixjaij and b = Σixibi. The cross term is aij = (1 − kij)√(aiaj), with kij a fitted binary interaction parameter; for the vapour phase use yi in place of xi.

Solve the EOS for compressibility factor Z. Take the smallest positive root for liquid and largest for vapour. Put each root and composition into the EOS fugacity expression to get φiL and φiV. Both coefficients must come from the same EOS, same kij and same root rule; mixing two models quietly destroys Ki = φiL/φiV.

The flash loop, bubble point and dew point

  1. Fix T, P and overall composition zi.
  2. Estimate Ki by Wilson: Ki = (Pc,i/P) exp[5.373(1+ωi)(1 − Tc,i/T)], with critical pressure Pc,i, critical temperature Tc,i and acentric factor ωi.
  3. Solve Rachford-Rice Σzi(Ki − 1)/[1 + β(Ki − 1)] = 0 for vapour fraction β between 0 and 1. Then xi = zi/[1 + β(Ki − 1)] and yi = Kixi.
  4. Get φiL and φiV from the EOS at those compositions. Set Ki,new = φiL/φiV.
  5. Return to Rachford-Rice until every Ki changes by less than the tolerance. Report xi, yi, β.

Bubble point fixes x and T and solves for P with ΣxiKi = 1. Dew point fixes y and T and solves for P with Σyi/Ki = 1. Full convergence proofs are taught by only a few universities; the loop above is the working method.

At high pressure the dew point can be crossed twice. On isothermal compression a gas may condense, then re-vaporise; this is retrograde condensation. Very high pressure can also give gas-gas equilibrium, two dense phases neither of which is a normal liquid. Uses include natural gas and natural gas liquid recovery, high-pressure absorption, and supercritical CO₂ extraction of spices.

Worked example

Question. A high-pressure flash at T = 400 K and P = 40 bar is being checked. The trial liquid composition is x₁ = 0.25 and x₂ = 0.75. The equation of state gives φ₁L = 0.52, φ₁V = 0.88, φ₂L = 0.95 and φ₂V = 0.97. Find K₁ and K₂. Find S = x₁K₁ + x₂K₂. State what S = 0.8823 means for the bubble point at this temperature.

  1. K₁ = φ₁L/φ₁V = 0.52/0.88 = 0.5909.
  2. K₂ = φ₂L/φ₂V = 0.95/0.97 = 0.9794.
  3. x₂ = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75. S = 0.25 × 0.5909 + 0.75 × 0.9794 = 0.1477 + 0.7345 = 0.8823.
  4. At a saturated liquid Σyi = ΣxiKi = 1. Here S < 1, so this trial liquid is not at its bubble point at 40 bar. At fixed T the bubble-point pressure is below 40 bar; at fixed P the bubble temperature is above 400 K.

Answer. K₁ = 0.5909, K₂ = 0.9794 and S = 0.8823; S < 1 means the trial liquid is above its bubble-point pressure at 400 K, and the bubble point lies below 40 bar.

Exam traps

  • Setting φiV = 1 at 50 bar. A dense vapour can have φ near 0.7; the error goes straight into K.
  • Using one EOS for vapour and another for liquid. The equality yiφiV = xiφiL is then solved with inconsistent φ, so the reported split is not equilibrium.
  • Choosing the largest Z root for the liquid. The liquid root is the smallest positive root; swapping roots gives K values that may look plausible but are wrong.
  • Using Pisat for a supercritical component. Above Tc there is no vapour pressure; use the EOS fugacity coefficient instead.
  • Reading S = ΣxiKi = 1 as a check on any composition. It holds only at the bubble point; at other x it is a saturation test, not a purity or yield.

Bubble point fixes liquid x and T, then solves ΣxiKi = 1 for P. Dew point fixes vapour y and T, then solves Σyi/Ki = 1 for P. Both use EOS φ values inside the loop.

On isothermal compression a high-pressure gas reaches a dew point, liquid appears, and further compression can move back into the single-phase region; the dew point is crossed twice. The retrograde liquid is a dense phase, not the ordinary liquid seen at low pressure.

6.7 Liquid-liquid and solid-liquid equilibria

Two liquid phases in a beaker, or a crystal sitting in its own solution, are both phase-equilibrium problems. The rule is the same as for vapour-liquid: every component has equal fugacity in every phase present. Liquid-liquid equality gives the tie lines of solvent extraction. Solid-liquid equality gives the solubility curve and the eutectic. Industry uses these equations for extracting essential oils, crystallising sugar and salt, and predicting wax deposition in pipelines.

You need: 6.1, 6.4, and Unit 5 for activity coefficients and excess Gibbs energy.

Solid-liquid equilibrium: the solubility curve

Put a pure solid 1 in a liquid and stir. The solid dissolves until the liquid solution is saturated. At equilibrium the fugacity of the solid equals the fugacity of component 1 in the liquid. For a pure solid, the fugacity ratio to its pure liquid at the same T and P follows from the fusion process. With ΔCp ≈ 0, the result is:

ln(x₁γ₁) = −(Δhfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm)

x₁ is the mole fraction of the solid in the saturated liquid; γ₁ is its activity coefficient in that liquid; Δhfus is the enthalpy of fusion of the pure solid in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is the temperature in K; Tm is the normal melting point of the pure solid in K.

Set γ₁ = 1 to get the ideal solubility. For a non-ideal solution with γ₁ > 1, this ideal value is too high; the real solubility is lower. A larger Δhfus gives a steeper curve and lower solubility at the same T, as the figure shows. This equation is the working tool for crystallisation design: choose T to reach a required x₁.

Liquid-liquid equilibrium: tie lines and extraction

Two liquid phases α and β form when two components dislike each other enough. At equilibrium, every component i has equal fugacity in both phases. Using the same pure-liquid standard state in both phases gives:

xiαγiα = xiβγiβ

xiα and xiβ are mole fractions of component i in phases α and β; γiα and γiβ are the corresponding activity coefficients.

A tie line joins two liquid compositions that coexist. The binodal curve is the boundary of the two-phase region. In a ternary diagram the tie lines shrink to a point called the plait point. Only the ratio of activity coefficients comes out of one tie line: γiα/γiβ = xiβ/xiα. To get individual γ values, use an activity-coefficient model plus the Gibbs-Duhem equation from Unit 5.

Solvent extraction uses this. The distribution coefficient is KD = xiβ/xiα. A large KD means the solute prefers the solvent phase β. Choose a solvent with high KD and low mutual solubility with the feed phase. The same equations describe extraction of essential oils and recovery of acetic acid from water.

Three phases, eutectics and industrial uses

When two liquid phases and a vapour are present, every component has equal fugacity in all three phases: fiL1 = fiL2 = fiV. For a binary at three phases, F = C − P + 2 = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1. Fix T and the three-phase pressure is a single number. This is the heteroazeotrope used to dry solvents by distillation.

Two pure solids in a liquid give a eutectic. At the eutectic temperature the liquid has a fixed composition and both solids crystallise together. Cool a solution below its saturation temperature and one solid appears; cool below the eutectic and both appear.

Crystallisation uses cooling or evaporation to make x₁ exceed the solubility. Sugar and salt are made this way. In pipelines, wax dissolves in warm crude oil and crystallises when the oil cools; the SLE equation predicts the wax appearance temperature, and inhibitors stop the deposit. In all these calculations, keep γ and the correct standard state.

Worked example

Question. A solid melts at 330 K with Δhfus = 20.0 kJ/mol. (a) Find its ideal solubility as a mole fraction in a liquid at 300 K. (b) Find the real solubility if the activity coefficient of the solid in the liquid is γ₁ = 1.8.

  1. Convert the heat of fusion: Δhfus = 20.0 kJ/mol = 20000 J/mol. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  2. Compute the temperature term: 1/T − 1/Tm = 1/300 − 1/330 = 0.0033333 − 0.0030303 = 3.0303 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
  3. Compute the right side: −(Δhfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm) = −(20000/8.314)(3.0303 × 10⁻⁴) = −0.72896.
  4. For the ideal solubility set γ₁ = 1: ln x₁ = −0.72896, so x₁ = exp(−0.72896) = 0.4824.
  5. For the real solubility use ln(x₁γ₁) = −0.72896. Then x₁γ₁ = 0.4824, so x₁ = 0.4824/1.8 = 0.268.

Answer. (a) The ideal solubility is x₁ = 0.4824. (b) With γ₁ = 1.8 the real solubility is x₁ = 0.268.

Exam traps

  • Dropping γ₁ in a solid-liquid calculation when γ₁ > 1 and then believing the high ideal solubility obtained.
  • Using different standard states for the two liquid phases in an LLE calculation; then the activity coefficients cannot be compared.
  • Putting Δhfus in kJ/mol while R is in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, or putting T in °C instead of K.
  • For a binary at three phases, treating F as 2. At fixed T the three-phase pressure is one value, not a range.
  • Confusing the binodal curve (phase boundary) with a tie line (two coexisting compositions).

Takeaways

  1. At phase equilibrium every phase has the same T, the same P, and the same chemical potential (hence the same fugacity) for every component.
  2. A phase exists only where the Gibbs energy is a minimum; a concave region of the Gibbs energy curve forces the mixture to split into two phases.
  3. The phase rule F = C − P + 2 counts the intensive variables you may set freely; Duhem's theorem says a closed system needs only two.
  4. For a pure component the equality of chemical potentials reduces to equality of pure-phase fugacities, which defines the vapour-pressure curve.
  5. Raoult's law yᵢP = xᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ is the starting point for every VLE calculation; real systems need only γᵢ added.
  6. In a very dilute solution the solute follows Henry's law fᵢ = Hᵢxᵢ, not Raoult's law, because its environment is solvent, not itself.
  7. At high pressure the liquid is also compressible and non-ideal, so both phases are described by fugacity coefficients from an equation of state.
  8. The same fugacity equality applied to two liquid phases gives liquid-liquid extraction; applied to a solid and a solution it gives the solubility curve.

Glossary

Activity
The dimensionless ratio of a component's fugacity to its value in a chosen standard state.
Activity coefficient
The factor by which a component's behaviour in a mixture differs from ideal, equal to activity divided by mole fraction.
Azeotrope
A mixture whose liquid and vapour compositions are identical at equilibrium, so simple distillation cannot change its composition.
Binodal curve
The boundary curve on a phase diagram, given by the common tangent construction, separating one-phase from two-phase behaviour.
Bubble point
The temperature or pressure at which the first bubble of vapour forms in a liquid of given composition.
Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs energy of a component, equal to the change in Gibbs energy when one mole of that component is added at constant T and P.
Component
An independent chemical species needed to define the composition of all phases in the system.
Degree of freedom
One intensive variable that can be changed independently without changing the number of phases in equilibrium.
Dew point
The temperature or pressure at which the first drop of liquid forms in a vapour of given composition.
Distribution coefficient
The equilibrium ratio of the mole fractions of a component in two coexisting phases.
Duhem's theorem
The statement that in a closed system of known overall composition, any two independent intensive variables fix the entire equilibrium state.
Fugacity
A pseudo-pressure that makes the ideal-gas chemical potential formula work for real fluids.
Fugacity coefficient
The ratio of a component's fugacity to its partial pressure, equal to 1 for an ideal gas.
Henry's constant
The slope of solute fugacity against solute mole fraction at infinite dilution, having units of pressure.
Henry's law
The relation that the solute fugacity in a very dilute solution is proportional to the solute mole fraction, with Henry's constant as the proportionality.
Ideal solution
A solution in which every component obeys Raoult's law over the whole composition range and the heat of mixing is zero.
Lever rule
A mass-balance rule giving the relative amounts of two coexisting phases from the distances of the overall composition from the two phase compositions on a tie line.
Phase equilibrium
A state in which every phase has the same temperature and pressure and every component has the same chemical potential in every phase, so no net transfer occurs.
Phase rule
The relation F = C − P + 2 giving the number of intensive variables that can be set independently.
Poynting correction
A small exponential factor that corrects liquid fugacity for pressure difference from the saturation pressure, usually near 1 at low pressure.
Raoult's law
The relation yᵢP = xᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ that describes vapour-liquid equilibrium for ideal solutions.
Relative volatility
The ratio K₁/K₂ of the K-values of two components, which measures how easily they can be separated.
Spinodal curve
The locus where the second derivative of Gibbs energy with composition is zero, marking the limit of spontaneous phase splitting.
Standard state
A defined reference state at the same temperature and pressure as the system, used to measure fugacity and activity; pure liquid is usual for liquids and ideal gas at 1 bar for vapours.
Tie line
A line joining the compositions of two phases that are in equilibrium with each other.
