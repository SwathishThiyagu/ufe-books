6.1 Criteria for phase equilibrium and stability

At equilibrium two phases of the same mixture share the same temperature, the same pressure (when the interface between them is flat), and the same chemical potential for every component. Chemical potential μi is the partial molar Gibbs energy in J/mol. It is the rate at which G changes, per mole of i, when a very small amount of component i is added. Fugacity fi in Pa is the working variable because μ contains an arbitrary reference term, and that term cancels only when the equality is written in fugacities. A phase is stable only if its Gibbs energy is a minimum, not merely flat. Distillation, extraction, crystallisation and refinery separations all use these tests.

You need the fundamental property relation dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μi dni (from thermodynamic property relations) and chemical potential, fugacity and activity (from solution thermodynamics).

The three equalities

Start from the fundamental relation for a closed system of C components: dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μi dni. Here G is Gibbs energy in J, S entropy in J/K, T absolute temperature in K, V volume in m³, P pressure in Pa, ni moles of component i, and μi chemical potential in J/mol. μi is also the partial molar Gibbs energy: the rate of change of G per mole of i added, at fixed T, P and other moles. At fixed T and P with no reaction, dG = Σ μi dni. Transfer dni moles of i from phase β to phase α changes G by (μiα − μiβ) dni. If this is negative, transfer lowers G. Equilibrium needs it zero for every component, so μiα = μiβ for every i and every pair of phases. Thermal equilibrium needs Tα = Tβ. Mechanical equilibrium needs Pα = Pβ. A curved interface can hold a small pressure jump because surface tension supports it.

μi = μi° + RT ln(fi/fi°)

μi is chemical potential in J/mol; μi° is the standard chemical potential in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K; fi is fugacity in Pa; fi° is the standard fugacity in Pa. For the same component, the standard term cancels between phases, so μiα = μiβ becomes fiα = fiβ.

The fugacity form is the working criterion. It removes the arbitrary reference value inside μ. See 6.2 for counting the variables you may set, and 6.4 for using the equality in vapour-liquid equilibrium.

Stability: a minimum, not a saddle

At fixed T and P, a stable phase has the lowest G. For a binary mixture write gM/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + A x₁x₂. Here gM is molar Gibbs energy of mixing in J/mol, R is the gas constant, T is in K, x₁ and x₂ = 1 − x₁ are mole fractions, and A is a dimensionless interaction parameter. The first derivative with respect to x₁ is ln(x₁/x₂) + A(1 − 2x₁). Setting it to zero gives a stationary composition. The second derivative is 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A. Stability requires (∂²gM/∂x₁²)T,P > 0. Because ∂²gM/∂x₁² = RT × d²(gM/RT)/dx₁² and RT is positive, the test is simply the sign of 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A. The spinodal is the locus where the second derivative is zero. The binodal is the locus of the two compositions joined by a common tangent to the gM curve; at those two points each μi is the same in both phases, so they are the actual coexisting compositions. Between binodal and spinodal the mixture is metastable. Inside the spinodal it is unstable: any small composition wave lowers G, so the mixture splits without a nucleation barrier. Equality of chemical potentials is the criterion of equilibrium; this curvature test is the criterion of stability.

Using the criteria without mixing up variables

Use the criteria in order. First confirm T and P are equal; for a flat interface this is enough for thermal and mechanical equilibrium. Then write fiα = fiβ for each component; this is one equation per component. Finally check curvature. The equimolar point with A = 2.5 has negative curvature, so it cannot be a single phase even though its first derivative is zero. A = 1.5 gives positive curvature at x₁ = 0.5 and is locally stable. The same algebra appears in liquid-liquid extraction and polymer blends; see 6.7. For pure components the fugacity equality reduces to the vapour-pressure curve; see 6.3.

Worked example

Question. At 300 K and 1 bar, the molar Gibbs energy of mixing of a binary liquid is gM/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + A x₁x₂ with A = 2.5. Test the equimolar mixture (x₁ = 0.5) for stability.

  1. For x₁ = 0.5, x₂ = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5.
  2. First derivative: ln(x₁/x₂) + A(1 − 2x₁) = ln(1) + 2.5(1 − 1) = 0. The point is stationary.
  3. Second derivative: 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A = 2 + 2 − 5 = −1.0.
  4. The value is negative, so G curves downward at x₁ = 0.5. A small split into two compositions lowers G; the mixture is unstable.
  5. Value of gM/RT at x₁ = 0.5: 0.5 ln 0.5 + 0.5 ln 0.5 + 2.5(0.5)(0.5) = −0.6931 + 0.625 = −0.0681.

Answer. The equimolar mixture is unstable because d²(gM/RT)/dx₁² = −1.0 < 0; it splits, and gM/RT at x₁ = 0.5 is −0.0681.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same mixture (gM/RT = x₁ ln x₁ + x₂ ln x₂ + 2.5x₁x₂ at 300 K), find the two spinodal compositions and the compositions of the two liquid layers that coexist (the binodal).

  1. Spinodal: set the second derivative to zero, 1/x₁ + 1/x₂ − 2A = 0. With x₂ = 1 − x₁ this is 1/[x₁(1 − x₁)] = 5, so x₁(1 − x₁) = 0.2, or x₁² − x₁ + 0.2 = 0.
  2. Roots: x₁ = [1 ± √(1 − 0.8)]/2 = (1 ± 0.4472)/2, so x₁ = 0.2764 and x₁ = 0.7236.
  3. Binodal: the model is symmetric in x₁ and x₂, so the two layers are mirror images, x₁α = x and x₁β = 1 − x. The common tangent is then horizontal, and the condition is that the first derivative is zero at x: ln[x/(1 − x)] + 2.5(1 − 2x) = 0.
  4. Solve by trial. x = 0.14: −1.8153 + 1.8000 = −0.0153. x = 0.15: −1.7346 + 1.7500 = +0.0154. Interpolating gives x = 0.145, where the sum is +0.0006, close enough to zero.
  5. Check with activities, a₁ = x₁γ₁ and γ₁ = exp(2.5x₂²). Layer α (x₁ = 0.145): γ₁ = exp(2.5 × 0.855²) = exp(1.8276) = 6.22, a₁ = 0.902. Layer β (x₁ = 0.855): γ₁ = exp(2.5 × 0.145²) = exp(0.0526) = 1.054, a₁ = 0.901. The activities match, so μ₁ is equal in both layers.

Answer. The spinodal lies at x₁ = 0.276 and 0.724, and the coexisting layers are x₁ = 0.145 and 0.855. A mixture between 0.145 and 0.276 (or 0.724 and 0.855) is metastable; one between 0.276 and 0.724 is unstable and splits at once.

Exam traps

  • Equating partial pressures pi instead of fugacities fi. At high pressure the fugacity coefficient φi changes the answer: fi = φi yi P in a gas.
  • Calling a mixture stable because the first derivative of G is zero. A stationary point can be a maximum or a saddle; the second derivative must be positive.
  • Calling the spinodal the phase boundary. The actual coexisting compositions come from the binodal, the common tangent where μi is equal in both phases.
  • Using a pure-component standard state when the problem needs a Henry standard state for a dilute solute; the activity definition and the numerical fugacity change.

6.2 Phase rule and Duhem's theorem

The phase rule answers one practical question: how many properties of a system can you choose before the rest are decided for you? It counts phases, components and equilibrium equations and returns a single number F, the degrees of freedom. Duhem's theorem extends the same counting to a closed vessel of known overall composition: pick any two independent intensive variables and everything else follows. Flash-drum specification, crystalliser control and distillation column design all rest on this count, so a wrong F sends a whole flowsheet in the wrong direction.

You need the equal-chemical-potential equations from criteria for phase equilibrium and stability, which the count subtracts, and the difference between intensive and extensive properties.

Count the unknowns, count the equations

A phase is a part of the system with uniform properties; π counts such parts. A component is a chemically independent constituent; C is the smallest number of them needed to describe the composition of every phase. A degree of freedom is an intensive variable you may change on your own without a phase appearing or vanishing; F counts them.

Inside one phase the intensive variables are temperature T, pressure and the mole fractions. The C mole fractions sum to 1, so only C − 1 of them are free. Temperature and pressure are common to all phases at equilibrium, so across π phases the unknowns number 2 + π(C − 1).

The equations are the equilibrium conditions from 6.1. Each component has one chemical potential, and it must match in every phase. Matching C components across π phases gives C(π − 1) equalities. Nothing else is needed, because the mole-fraction sums were already used up in the unknown count.

F = 2 + π(C − 1) − C(π − 1) = C − π + 2

F is the number of degrees of freedom, C the number of components, π the number of phases; the 2 stands for temperature and pressure.

Pure water with liquid and vapour present: C = 1, π = 2, so F = 1. You may pick the temperature, and the pressure then sits on the vapour-pressure curve. Try to set both and one phase disappears.

Reactions and the reduced rule

A reaction ties species together, so species are not components. Count species S, independent reactions R, and any extra constraint a, then C = S − R − a. Independent means no reaction in the set is a sum of multiples of the others.

  • Example: N₂, H₂ and NH₃ with NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂. S = 3, R = 1, so C = 2. Two components means one independent mole fraction per phase; the extent follows from reaction equilibrium.
  • The extra constraint a covers things like electroneutrality in a salt solution, or a known fixed ratio of two species.

Most laboratory and plant equipment runs at a fixed pressure — a fume hood at 1 atm, an open beaker at 1 atm, or a reactor vented through a pressure-control valve set at 2 bar. Freezing the pressure removes one variable and gives the reduced rule.

F = C − π + 1

F is degrees of freedom, C components, π phases; the rule applies when either temperature or pressure is held fixed.

Water boiling in an open pan at 1 atm: C = 1, π = 2, so F = 1 − 2 + 1 = 0. At that pressure the boiling temperature is fixed at 100 °C; you have no freedom left. Raise the pressure in a cooker and the fixed temperature rises with it.

Duhem's theorem

In a closed system of known overall composition, fixing any two independent variables fixes every remaining intensive variable and the amounts of all phases. When F = 2 the two may both be intensive; when F = 1 at least one must be extensive (a phase amount or the total volume, say). That statement is Duhem's theorem, and it is stronger than the phase rule because it also pins down how much of each phase is present.

Why, in one line: the phase amounts add π − 1 unknowns (they sum to the total) and the component balances add C − 1 independent equations (the overall fractions sum to 1). Unknowns go up by π − 1 and equations by C − 1, so the count becomes (C − π + 2) + (π − 1) − (C − 1) = 2.

Take binary vapour-liquid equilibrium with overall mole fraction z₁ fixed. Unknowns are temperature, pressure, x₁, y₁ and the liquid fraction, five in all. Equations are the two fugacity equalities plus one material balance, three in all. Fix temperature and x₁ and nothing is free: pressure and y₁ are fixed by the two equilibrium equations, and the material balance then fixes the liquid fraction. With T fixed the phase rule leaves F = 1 for the two-phase binary, so pressure, x₁ and y₁ lie on a pair of curves, not a surface (see 6.3).

Worked example

Question. Find the number of degrees of freedom for each case. (a) Pure water with liquid and vapour present. (b) Ethanol–water liquid and vapour at a fixed total pressure of 1 atm. (c) A gas of N₂, H₂ and NH₃ at 1 atm in which NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂ can occur. State which variables are free in each case.

  1. (a) One species, one reaction-free system: C = 1. Two phases: π = 2. F = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1. Only one variable is free; choosing T fixes the pressure on the vapour-pressure curve.
  2. (b) Ethanol and water do not react: C = 2, two phases π = 2, so C − π + 2 = 2. The pressure is already fixed at 1 atm, which uses one degree of freedom, so F = 2 − 1 = 1. That one variable is temperature; fixing it fixes x₁ and y₁.
  3. (c) Species present S = 3 (N₂, H₂, NH₃). Independent reactions R = 1, since NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 3/2H₂ relates all three. No other constraint, so C = 3 − 1 = 2.
  4. One gas phase, π = 1. F = 2 − 1 + 2 = 3: temperature, pressure and one composition variable; once temperature, pressure and that composition variable are set, reaction equilibrium plus the sum of the mole fractions fixes all three species mole fractions.
  5. At the stated 1 atm, pressure is used up: F = 3 − 1 = 2, namely temperature and one composition variable.

Answer. F = 1 for (a), F = 1 for (b) at 1 atm, and F = 3 for (c) generally, falling to F = 2 at 1 atm.

Exam traps

  • Counting species as components when a reaction exists. Three species with one independent reaction give C = 2, not 3.
  • Adding the full +2 when the pressure is already fixed. Once the pressure is set, use F = C − π + 1, otherwise every answer is one too high.
  • Remembering that F counts intensive variables only. Phase amounts are extensive and are not degrees of freedom; Duhem's theorem is what brings them in.
  • Missing extra constraints such as electroneutrality in an electrolyte solution, which reduce C by one more.
  • Thinking any two intensive variables will do for Duhem's theorem. They must be independent; fixing T and the boiling pressure of a pure liquid is a contradiction, not a specification.

6.3 Phase equilibria of pure components and mixtures

Phase equilibrium is the state where every component has the same fugacity in every phase present. For one pure substance that equality fixes the vapour pressure at each temperature. For a mixture it fixes two curves: the bubble line and the dew line. Those curves are the working maps for distillation, flash drums, condensers and LPG storage. This chapter reads the maps from fugacity equality, without adding activity coefficients yet.

You need the equal-fugacity criterion from criteria for phase equilibrium and stability, the definitions of fugacity and activity, and the Clausius-Clapeyron equation.

Pure component: one curve from fugacity equality

At saturation a pure liquid and its vapour coexist. Equilibrium requires equal temperature, equal pressure and equal fugacity. For a fixed T only one pressure satisfies fL=fV. That pressure is Psat(T), the vapour pressure. Its plot against T is the vapour-pressure curve. On a P-T diagram it ends at the triple point and the critical point; fusion and sublimation curves meet it at the triple point.

fL(T, Psat) = fV(T, Psat)

fL and fV are the liquid and vapour fugacities in the same pressure unit; Psat is the saturation vapour pressure; T is absolute temperature.

The Antoine equation fits the curve over a modest range. The constants A, B and C belong to one unit set. A set with P in mmHg and T in °C is not interchangeable with one using kPa and K, and some tables fit log₁₀ P instead of ln P. Check the units and the log base before substituting.

ln Psat = A − B/(T + C)

Psat is vapour pressure in the unit of the table; T is temperature in the unit of the table; A, B and C are fitted constants.

The slope gives latent heat. Integrated between two temperatures, two vapour pressures at T1 and T2 give Δhvap = R ln(P2/P1)/(1/T1 − 1/T2).

d ln Psat/d(1/T) = −Δhvap/R

Δhvap is enthalpy of vaporization in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol−1 K−1; T is absolute temperature in K.

Pressure changes liquid fugacity at fixed T through the Poynting correction. It is small unless P is far from Psat.

fL(P) = fL(Psat) exp[VL(P − Psat)/(RT)]

VL is liquid molar volume in m³/mol; P is pressure; Psat is saturation pressure; R is the gas constant; T is absolute temperature.

Mixtures: bubble and dew lines

For a mixture the equality is per component: fiV=fiL for every component i. At fixed T, a liquid of composition x has a bubble pressure where the first vapour bubble forms. A vapour of composition y has a dew pressure where the first liquid drop forms. The P-x-y diagram draws both at fixed T; the T-x-y diagram draws both at fixed P.

Two separate assumptions are used here. The vapour is an ideal gas at low pressure, so fiV = yiP. The liquid is an ideal solution whose pure-liquid fugacity is taken as Pisat (saturated vapour ideal, Poynting factor neglected), so fiL = xiPisat. Then yiP=xiPisat. A non-ideal liquid adds γi, treated in <a href='#vle-raoult-s-law-and-modified-raoult-s-law'>6.4.

yiP = xiPisat

xi is liquid mole fraction; yi is vapour mole fraction; P is total pressure; Pisat is pure i vapour pressure.

For a binary with x2=1−x1 and y2=1−y1, the bubble pressure is Pbub = x1P1sat + x2P2sat and the dew pressure is 1/Pdew = y1/P1sat + y2/P2sat. At x1=0 and x1=1 the two curves meet the pure vapour pressures.

Pbub = x1P1sat + (1−x1)P2sat;   1/Pdew = y1/P1sat + (1−y1)/P2sat

Pbub is bubble pressure; Pdew is dew pressure; x1 and y1 are mole fractions of component 1; P1sat and P2sat are pure-component vapour pressures.

The figure uses benzene(1)–toluene(2) at 373.15 K: P1sat=180.1 kPa and P2sat=74.2 kPa. The bubble line is P=74.2+105.9x1; the dew line is P=1/(y1/180.1+(1−y1)/74.2).

Tie lines, lever rule and azeotropes

Between the curves a mixture splits into liquid x and vapour y at the same T and P. A horizontal tie line joins x and y. For overall composition z1 between x1 and y1, with nL and nV the moles of liquid and vapour, the lever rule gives nL/nV=(y1−z1)/(z1−x1). The liquid fraction is (y1−z1)/(y1−x1); the vapour fraction is (z1−x1)/(y1−x1).

On a T-x-y diagram at fixed P, heating a liquid reaches the bubble line where the first vapour appears. Further heating reaches the dew line where the last drop disappears. Between the lines both phases exist. The tie line again joins x and y.

At an azeotrope the two branches touch and x1=y1. Vapour and liquid have the same composition, so simple distillation cannot change composition. Common azeotropes can be minimum-boiling or maximum-boiling; changing pressure can move or break them. The cause is a non-ideal liquid, described by activity coefficients in Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law.

Worked example

Question. Benzene(1)–toluene(2) at 373.15 K has P₁sat = 180.1 kPa and P₂sat = 74.2 kPa. (a) For a liquid with x₁ = 0.30, find the bubble pressure and vapour composition y₁. (b) For a vapour with y₁ = 0.30, find the dew pressure and liquid composition x₁.

  1. Bubble pressure: Pbub = x₁P₁sat +(1−x₁)P₂sat = 0.30×180.1 +0.70×74.2 =54.03+51.94=105.97 kPa.
  2. Vapour composition: y₁ = x₁P₁sat/Pbub =54.03/105.97=0.5099.
  3. Dew pressure: 1/Pdew = y₁/P₁sat +(1−y₁)/P₂sat =0.30/180.1+0.70/74.2 =0.0016657+0.0094340=0.0110997 kPa−1, so Pdew=90.09 kPa.
  4. Liquid composition: x₁ = y₁Pdew/P₁sat =0.30×90.09/180.1=27.03/180.1=0.1501.

Answer. The bubble point is 105.97 kPa with y₁ = 0.5099, and the dew point is 90.09 kPa with x₁ = 0.1501.

Worked example 2

Question. The same benzene(1)–toluene(2) system at 373.15 K (P₁sat = 180.1 kPa, P₂sat = 74.2 kPa) has overall composition z₁ = 0.40 and is held at 100 kPa. Find x₁, y₁ and the fraction of the mixture that is liquid.

  1. Check that 100 kPa is between the dew and bubble pressures of z₁ = 0.40. Bubble: 74.2 + 105.9 × 0.40 = 116.56 kPa. Dew: 1/(0.40/180.1 + 0.60/74.2) = 1/0.0103073 = 97.02 kPa. Since 97.02 < 100 < 116.56, two phases exist.
  2. Liquid on the bubble line at 100 kPa: x₁ = (P − P₂sat)/(P₁sat − P₂sat) = (100 − 74.2)/105.9 = 0.2436.
  3. Vapour on the same tie line: y₁ = x₁P₁sat/P = 0.2436 × 180.1/100 = 0.4388.
  4. Lever rule: liquid fraction nL/(nL + nV) = (y₁ − z₁)/(y₁ − x₁) = (0.4388 − 0.40)/(0.4388 − 0.2436) = 0.0388/0.1952 = 0.199; vapour fraction = 0.801.
  5. Check the benzene balance: 0.199 × 0.2436 + 0.801 × 0.4388 = 0.0485 + 0.3515 = 0.4000 = z₁.

Answer. x₁ = 0.2436 and y₁ = 0.4388; about 19.9 % of the mixture is liquid and 80.1 % vapour.

Exam traps

  • Swapping x and y on a P-x-y diagram. The bubble line is read with liquid x; the dew line is read with vapour y.
  • Using Antoine constants without checking the unit set. P may be mmHg, kPa or bar, and T may be °C or K; mixing sets gives a wrong Psat.
  • Calling the bubble point and dew point the same thing. Bubble point is the first vapour from liquid; dew point is the first liquid from vapour.
  • Treating an azeotrope as a pure component. At an azeotrope x=y, but the mixture still has two components and the branches touch only at that point.

6.4 VLE: Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law

In an ideal solution each component's partial pressure is its liquid mole fraction times its own vapour pressure: yiP = xiPisat. That is Raoult's law. Most real pairs, such as ethanol–water, disobey it, so one correction per component is added, the activity coefficient γi. The modified form yiP = xiγiPisat is the working equation behind distillation and flash-drum design.

You need the equal-fugacity criterion from criteria for phase equilibrium and stability, the P-x-y diagram from phase equilibria of pure components and mixtures, and the activity coefficient with the Margules, van Laar and Wilson models.

Where Raoult's law comes from

At equilibrium every component has the same fugacity in the vapour and in the liquid, fiV = fiL (see 6.1). Writing each side in terms of composition gives the general starting equation.

yi φiV P = xi γi fiL,pure(T, P)

φiV is the vapour fugacity coefficient, γi the liquid activity coefficient, and fiL,pure the fugacity of pure liquid i at the mixture T and P; P is in kPa.

Three assumptions reduce this to Raoult's law.

  • Ideal-gas vapour: φiV = 1. Acceptable up to a few bar.
  • Standard state is pure liquid i at the same T and P, with the Poynting correction neglected, so fiL,pure ≈ Pisat, the vapour pressure of pure i at T.
  • Ideal solution: γi = 1, meaning unlike molecules attract each other exactly as strongly as like molecules.
yi P = xi Pisat

Pisat is the vapour pressure of pure i at the system T, in kPa; Σyi = Σxi = 1.

For a pure component x = y = 1, and the equation collapses to P = Pisat, the vapour-pressure curve. Get Pisat from an Antoine correlation, ln P = A − B/(T + C), used exactly as tabulated: put T in the table's unit (K or °C), read P in its unit (kPa or mmHg), check ln against log₁₀, and convert to kPa (1 mmHg = 0.13332 kPa) before adding terms. Benzene–toluene obeys the law closely because the two molecules are similar in size and shape.

Modified Raoult's law and the bubble-pressure recipe

Drop only the third assumption. Keep the vapour ideal and keep the pure-liquid standard state. All liquid non-ideality now sits inside γi, and γi depends on the whole composition, not on xi alone.

yi P = xi γi Pisat

γi is the liquid activity coefficient at the system T and x, with γi → 1 as xi → 1.

  1. Fix T and the liquid composition x.
  2. Get every Pisat from Antoine at that T.
  3. Evaluate every γi at x from the chosen model (Margules, van Laar or Wilson).
  4. Add the partial pressures: P = ΣxiγiPisat.
  5. Divide: yi = xiγiPisat/P, and check Σyi = 1.

Bubble temperature: P and x are known, but every Pisat moves with T, so guess T, compute ΣxiγiPisat, and correct T until the sum equals P (second example). Dew point: y and P (or T) are known, x is not. Start with γi = 1, compute xi = yiP/(γiPisat), normalise, recompute γi, and repeat until Σyi/(γiPisat) = 1/P.

Ki = yi/xi = γi Pisat / P

Ki is the vapour-liquid equilibrium ratio used stage by stage in column design.

α₁₂ = K₁/K₂ = γ₁P₁sat / (γ₂P₂sat)

α₁₂ is the relative volatility; the further it is from 1, the easier the distillation.

Working backwards: γ from data and from an azeotrope

Measured P, x and y give activity coefficients directly, with no model: γi = yi P/(xi Pisat). Do this at every data point and you have the experimental γ(x) curve; fit a model through it.

An azeotrope is faster still. There xi = yi, the composition cancels, and each component gives P = γi Pisat, so γ₁P₁sat = γ₂P₂sat = P. One azeotrope point therefore fixes two activity coefficients. Two γ values are enough to fix a two-parameter model: van Laar or two-parameter Margules is solved for both constants from γ₁ and γ₂ at the azeotropic composition. A one-parameter model such as Margules, ln γ₁ = A x₂² and ln γ₂ = A x₁², is over-determined: each γ gives its own A, and the two agree only if the model suits the system.

A minimum-boiling azeotrope has P above the ideal value, so γi > 1 (positive deviation: unlike molecules attract each other less than like ones). A maximum-boiling azeotrope has P below it, so γi < 1 (negative deviation: unlike molecules attract more).

The worked system below has one. At an azeotrope γ₁P₁sat = γ₂P₂sat, so ln(γ₁/γ₂) = A(x₂² − x₁²) = A(1 − 2x₁) = ln(P₂sat/P₁sat) = ln(20/60) = −1.0986. With A = 1.20, 1 − 2x₁ = −0.9155 and x₁ = 0.958, where P = 60.13 kPa, just above P₁sat = 60.0 kPa: a weak minimum-boiling azeotrope close to pure component 1.

6.5 Henry's law and dilute solutions

A dilute solute does not see a pure-liquid environment. It sees solvent, so its vapour pressure is not xi Pisat. Henry's law replaces Pisat with a measured slope Hi: partial pressure equals mole fraction times Hi. This chapter shows why Hi is the limiting slope at xi → 0, how it links to the infinite-dilution activity coefficient, how temperature and pressure shift it, and how a dilute binary uses Henry for the solute and Raoult for the solvent. Dissolved oxygen in boiler feed water and CO₂ in soft drinks are the working examples.

You need Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law and the standard states from phase equilibria of pure components and mixtures.

Why the solute needs its own slope

Raoult's law describes the solvent of a dilute solution well and the solute badly. A gas molecule in water is surrounded almost entirely by water molecules. It has no pure-liquid environment, so its pure vapour pressure Pisat is the wrong reference. Henry's law fixes this by replacing the pure-liquid vapour pressure with a measured slope.

pi = xi Hi    or    fi = xi ki

pi is the partial pressure of solute i in the gas (Pa); xi is its mole fraction in the liquid; Hi is Henry's constant on the mole-fraction scale (Pa or bar). fi is fugacity and ki is Henry's constant on the fugacity basis, in the same units as fi.

Hi is the limiting slope of the pi against xi curve as xi → 0. It is a property of the solute–solvent pair and of temperature. For CO₂ in water at 298 K, H is about 1670 bar. For O₂ in water it is about 4.4 × 10⁴ bar, some 26 times larger, so oxygen is much less soluble.

The standard state behind Hi is a hypothetical pure liquid: the solute at xi = 1 behaving as it does at infinite dilution. The reference is hypothetical because no real pure liquid solute has that environment. Because of this choice, the solute's activity coefficient approaches 1 as xi → 0. The two constants are linked:

γi∞ = Hi / Pisat

γi∞ is the activity coefficient of solute i at infinite dilution; Pisat is the vapour pressure of pure liquid i (Pa).

This ratio can be large. For a gas above its critical temperature, Pisat does not exist at all. Henry's constant is then the only usable reference.

One curve, two laws

In a binary solution the solvent is nearly pure and the solute is nearly absent. The same measured curve therefore obeys Raoult's law at the solvent end and Henry's law at the solute end. A one-parameter Margules model reproduces both ends with a single constant A:

p₁ = x₁ exp[A(1 − x₁)²] P₁sat,    A = ln(H₁ / P₁sat)

p₁ is the partial pressure of component 1 (Pa); x₁ is its liquid mole fraction; P₁sat is its pure vapour pressure (Pa); H₁ is its Henry constant (Pa); A is a dimensionless Margules constant.

Check the two limits. As x₁ → 1, the exponential becomes 1 and p₁ → P₁sat. As x₁ → 0, p₁ ≈ x₁ exp(A) P₁sat = x₁ H₁. The Henry line is the tangent at x₁ = 0. The Raoult line p₁ = x₁ P₁sat is the tangent at x₁ = 1. The figure uses P₁sat = 60 kPa and H₁ = 90 kPa, so A = ln(90/60) = 0.405.

The Henry line is accurate only while the solute is dilute. A common working range is xi below 0.02 to 0.05. Inside that range the solvent still follows Raoult's law: psolvent = xsolvent Psolventsat. This is why a dilute binary uses two standard states at once — Raoult-based for the solvent, Henry-based for the solute. Both are the same fugacity equality from 6.1, just written with different references.

Temperature and pressure move H

Henry's constant is not a constant. It changes with temperature and, weakly, with pressure. The temperature dependence is:

d ln Hi / dT = (h̄iV − h̄i∞) / (R T²)

Hi is Henry's constant (Pa); T is absolute temperature (K); R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; h̄iV is the partial molar enthalpy of i in the vapour (J mol⁻¹); h̄i∞ is the partial molar enthalpy of i at infinite dilution in the liquid (J mol⁻¹).

For most gases in water the right side is positive: dissolving releases heat, so H rises with T and solubility falls. That is why a warm soft drink goes flat. Cold water holds more dissolved oxygen than warm water. Boiler feed water is deaerated because even the small amount of oxygen that remains at high temperature corrodes steel.

d ln Hi / dP = V̄i∞ / (R T)

V̄i∞ is the partial molar volume of i at infinite dilution (m³ mol⁻¹); P is pressure (Pa); R and T as above.

V̄i∞ is small for gases, so pressure changes H only slightly. The bigger effect of pressure is on the partial pressure itself. A soda bottle at 3 bar of CO₂ holds more dissolved CO₂ than one at 1 bar because xCO₂ = pCO₂/HCO₂ rises with pCO₂.

6.6 High-pressure vapour-liquid equilibrium

At 1 bar the vapour phase is nearly ideal, so modified Raoult's law works. At 40 bar or 150 bar a gas is dense, its molecules interact, and the vapour fugacity coefficient φiV is far from 1. The liquid standard state and Poynting correction also lose accuracy. High-pressure vapour-liquid equilibrium therefore writes the same fugacity equality, yiφiV = xiφiL, and takes both coefficients from one equation of state. It is how natural gas is processed and separated, how high-pressure absorbers are designed, and how supercritical CO₂ extracts spice oleoresins.

You need the fugacity criterion from criteria for phase equilibrium and stability, modified Raoult's law from Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law, and cubic equations of state with their mixing rules.

Where modified Raoult's law breaks

Modified Raoult's law, yiP = xiγiPisat, works near 1 bar because three corrections are hidden and small.

  • The vapour is treated as ideal, so φiV = 1.
  • The liquid standard fugacity is taken as Pisat, with Poynting factor exp[ViL(P − Pisat)/(RT)] near 1.
  • γi comes from low-pressure data.

At 40 bar a vapour is dense; φiV can be 0.8 or lower. A component above its critical temperature has no Pisat. The Poynting factor grows, and a γ model fitted at low pressure cannot describe a liquid whose density and composition approach the vapour's.

yiφiV = xiφiL

yi, xi are mole fractions in vapour and liquid; φiV, φiL are fugacity coefficients at T, P and phase composition.

The safe route uses one equation of state for both phases. This is the phi-phi method; the gamma-phi method of 6.4 is its low-pressure limit.

K-values from one equation of state

For each component the equilibrium ratio is Ki = yi/xi. Divide the fugacity equality by xiP:

Ki = φiL / φiV

Ki is the distribution ratio (dimensionless); values above 1 mean the component prefers vapour.

Use a cubic EOS such as Soave–Redlich–Kwong or Peng–Robinson. Its mixture parameters are a = ΣiΣjxixjaij and b = Σixibi. The cross term is aij = (1 − kij)√(aiaj), with kij a fitted binary interaction parameter; for the vapour phase use yi in place of xi.

Solve the EOS for compressibility factor Z. Take the smallest positive root for liquid and largest for vapour. Put each root and composition into the EOS fugacity expression to get φiL and φiV. Both coefficients must come from the same EOS, same kij and same root rule; mixing two models quietly destroys Ki = φiL/φiV.

The flash loop, bubble point and dew point

  1. Fix T, P and overall composition zi.
  2. Estimate Ki by Wilson: Ki = (Pc,i/P) exp[5.373(1+ωi)(1 − Tc,i/T)], with critical pressure Pc,i, critical temperature Tc,i and acentric factor ωi.
  3. Solve Rachford-Rice Σzi(Ki − 1)/[1 + β(Ki − 1)] = 0 for vapour fraction β between 0 and 1. Then xi = zi/[1 + β(Ki − 1)] and yi = Kixi.
  4. Get φiL and φiV from the EOS at those compositions. Set Ki,new = φiL/φiV.
  5. Return to Rachford-Rice until every Ki changes by less than the tolerance. Report xi, yi, β.

Test the feed before solving Rachford-Rice. With the current Ki, if ΣziKi < 1 the feed is a subcooled liquid, and if Σzi/Ki < 1 it is a superheated vapour; only when both sums exceed 1 does a root β lie between 0 and 1. Near the critical region the EOS loop can also slide to the trivial solution, xi = yi with every Ki = 1, which satisfies the fugacity equality without being two phases. Reject it and restart from Wilson K-values or a stability test.

Bubble point fixes x and T and solves for P with ΣxiKi = 1. Dew point fixes y and T and solves for P with Σyi/Ki = 1. Inside the bubble-point loop the trial vapour is yi = Kixi, normalised to sum to 1 before φiV is evaluated; inside the dew-point loop the trial liquid is xi = yi/Ki, normalised the same way before φiL is evaluated.

At high pressure the dew point can be crossed twice. On isothermal compression a gas may condense, then re-vaporise; this is retrograde condensation. Very high pressure can also give gas-gas equilibrium, two dense phases neither of which is a normal liquid. Uses include natural gas and natural gas liquid recovery, high-pressure absorption, and supercritical CO₂ extraction of spices.

6.7 Liquid-liquid and solid-liquid equilibria

Two liquid phases in a beaker, or a crystal sitting in its own solution, are both phase-equilibrium problems. The rule is the same as for vapour-liquid: every component has equal fugacity in every phase present. Liquid-liquid equality gives the tie lines of solvent extraction. Solid-liquid equality gives the solubility curve and the eutectic. Industry uses these equations for extracting essential oils, crystallising sugar and salt, and predicting wax deposition in pipelines.

You need the fugacity criterion from criteria for phase equilibrium and stability, modified Raoult's law from Raoult's law and modified Raoult's law, and activity coefficients with the excess Gibbs energy.

Solid-liquid equilibrium: the solubility curve

Put a pure solid 1 in a liquid and stir. The solid dissolves until the liquid solution is saturated. At equilibrium the fugacity of the solid equals the fugacity of component 1 in the liquid. For a pure solid, the fugacity ratio to its pure liquid at the same T and P follows from the fusion process. With ΔCp ≈ 0, the result is:

ln(x₁γ₁) = −(Δhfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm)

x₁ is the mole fraction of the solid in the saturated liquid; γ₁ is its activity coefficient in that liquid; Δhfus is the enthalpy of fusion of the pure solid in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is the temperature in K; Tm is the normal melting point of the pure solid in K.

Set γ₁ = 1 to get the ideal solubility. For a non-ideal solution with γ₁ > 1, this ideal value is too high; the real solubility is lower. A larger Δhfus gives a steeper curve and lower solubility at the same T, as the figure shows. This equation is the working tool for crystallisation design: choose T to reach a required x₁. It holds only below the melting point (T < Tm), where the solid is the stable phase; the liquid reference for component 1 is then its pure subcooled liquid at T, and the ΔCp ≈ 0 step makes the result least accurate far below Tm.

Liquid-liquid equilibrium: tie lines and extraction

Two liquid phases α and β form when unlike molecules attract each other much less than like molecules do, so GE is large and positive. At equilibrium, every component i has equal fugacity in both phases. Using the same pure-liquid standard state in both phases gives:

xiαγiα = xiβγiβ

xiα and xiβ are mole fractions of component i in phases α and β; γiα and γiβ are the corresponding activity coefficients.

A tie line joins two liquid compositions that coexist. The binodal curve is the boundary of the two-phase region. In a ternary diagram the tie lines get shorter as the two layers become more alike, and they shrink to zero length at the plait point, where the two liquids become identical. Only the ratio of activity coefficients comes out of one tie line: γiα/γiβ = xiβ/xiα. To get individual γ values, fit an activity-coefficient model whose constants reproduce the measured tie lines.

Solvent extraction uses this. The distribution coefficient is KD = xiβ/xiα on a mole-fraction basis; handbooks often quote it as a ratio of mass fractions or concentrations, which gives a different number for the same system. A large KD means the solute prefers the solvent phase β. Choose a solvent with high KD and low mutual solubility with the feed phase. The same equations describe extraction of essential oils and recovery of acetic acid from water.

Three phases, eutectics and industrial uses

When two liquid phases and a vapour are present, every component has equal fugacity in all three phases: fiL1 = fiL2 = fiV. For a binary at three phases, F = C − π + 2 = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1. Fix T and the three-phase pressure is a single number. This is the heteroazeotrope used to dry solvents by distillation.

Two pure solids in a liquid give a eutectic. At the eutectic temperature the liquid has a fixed composition and both solids crystallise together. Cool a solution below its saturation temperature and one solid appears; cool below the eutectic and both appear.

Crystallisation uses cooling or evaporation to make x₁ exceed the solubility. Sugar and salt are made this way. In pipelines, wax dissolves in warm crude oil and crystallises when the oil cools; the SLE equation predicts the wax appearance temperature, and inhibitors stop the deposit. In all these calculations, keep γ and the correct standard state.

Worked example

Question. A solid melts at 330 K with Δhfus = 20.0 kJ/mol. (a) Find its ideal solubility as a mole fraction in a liquid at 300 K. (b) Find the real solubility if the activity coefficient of the solid in the liquid is γ₁ = 1.8.

  1. Convert the heat of fusion: Δhfus = 20.0 kJ/mol = 20000 J/mol. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  2. Compute the temperature term: 1/T − 1/Tm = 1/300 − 1/330 = 0.0033333 − 0.0030303 = 3.0303 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
  3. Compute the right side: −(Δhfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm) = −(20000/8.314)(3.0303 × 10⁻⁴) = −0.72896.
  4. For the ideal solubility set γ₁ = 1: ln x₁ = −0.72896, so x₁ = exp(−0.72896) = 0.4824.
  5. For the real solubility use ln(x₁γ₁) = −0.72896. Then x₁γ₁ = 0.4824, so x₁ = 0.4824/1.8 = 0.268.

Answer. (a) The ideal solubility is x₁ = 0.4824. (b) With γ₁ = 1.8 the real solubility is x₁ = 0.268.

Takeaways

  1. At phase equilibrium every phase has the same T, the same P, and the same chemical potential (hence the same fugacity) for every component.
  2. A phase exists only where the Gibbs energy is a minimum; a concave region of the Gibbs energy curve forces the mixture to split into two phases.
  3. The phase rule F = C − π + 2 counts the intensive variables you may set freely; Duhem's theorem says a closed system of known make-up is fixed by two variables.
  4. For a pure component the equality of chemical potentials reduces to equality of pure-phase fugacities, which defines the vapour-pressure curve.
  5. Raoult's law yᵢP = xᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ is the starting point for every VLE calculation; real systems need only γᵢ added.
  6. In a very dilute solution the solute follows Henry's law fᵢ = Hᵢxᵢ, not Raoult's law, because its environment is solvent, not itself.
  7. At high pressure the liquid is also compressible and non-ideal, so both phases are described by fugacity coefficients from an equation of state.
  8. The same fugacity equality applied to two liquid phases gives liquid-liquid extraction; applied to a solid and a solution it gives the solubility curve.

Understand State the conditions required for phase equilibrium between two phases of a multi-component system. Explain why equality of temperature and pressure is necessary, and why equality of chemical potential is the complete condition. 10 marks

Points to cover. Thermal equilibrium: no heat flow, so Tᵢ = T across phases; Mechanical equilibrium: no work of volume change, so Pᵢ = P across phases; For each component, dG = 0 at constant T and P gives μᵢα = μᵢβ for every species i; In fugacity form: fᵢα = fᵢβ, which is what is actually used in calculations; Mass transfer stops only when chemical potential is equal; T and P equality alone is not sufficient.

Model answer.

  1. The system. Two phases α and β of a C-component mixture sit inside one closed vessel that, as a whole, is isolated. Energy, volume and moles of every species can pass between the phases; the totals U, V and ni are fixed. At equilibrium the total entropy S = Sα + Sβ is at its maximum, so dS = 0 for every small exchange.
  2. Thermal equilibrium. Pass energy dU from β to α at fixed volumes and moles: dS = (1/Tα − 1/Tβ) dUα. For dS = 0 with any dUα, Tα = Tβ. If Tβ were higher, heat would flow into α and S would rise, so the state was not equilibrium.
  3. Mechanical equilibrium. Let the boundary move so α gains volume dVα: dS = (Pα/Tα − Pβ/Tβ) dVα. With equal temperatures this gives Pα = Pβ for a flat interface. (A curved interface carries a jump ΔP = 2σ/r, with σ the surface tension.)
  4. Chemical equilibrium. With T and P equal, the convenient potential is G, which is a minimum at fixed T and P. Moving dni of species i from β to α changes it by dG = (μiα − μiβ) dniα. dG = 0 for any dniα needs μiα = μiβ for every i. If μiβ − μiα = 2.0 kJ/mol, moving 0.050 mol from β to α gives ΔG = −2.0 × 0.050 = −0.10 kJ = −100 J, so the transfer happens by itself: species move from high μ to low μ.
  5. Working form. With μi = Γi(T) + RT ln f̂i, the term Γi depends on T only and cancels between phases at the same T, so the criterion becomes f̂iα = f̂iβ. For water over liquid water at 25 °C, the fugacity is about 3.17 kPa in both phases although the mole fraction is about 0.03 in the moist air above it and 1.0 in the liquid.
  6. Why T and P are not enough. A beaker of pure water in a sealed flask of dry air at 25 °C and 1 atm has equal T and P everywhere, yet water evaporates until its fugacity in the gas reaches 3.17 kPa. A sugar crystal in an unsaturated syrup at the same T and P keeps dissolving. In both cases μ differs between the phases, so mass transfer continues.
  7. Why μ equality completes the set. The three groups of conditions together make dS = 0 for every possible exchange: energy, volume and each species. For π phases they give (π − 1) temperature, (π − 1) pressure and C(π − 1) chemical-potential equalities, the equations that the phase rule subtracts from the unknowns.
  8. Conclusion. Tα = Tβ, Pα = Pβ (flat interface) and μiα = μiβ for every component, written in practice as f̂iα = f̂iβ.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Phase rule. For a non-reacting system of C components in π phases at equilibrium, F = C − π + 2.
  2. Terms. C is the smallest number of independent species that describe every phase (C = S − R − a for S species, R independent reactions and a extra constraints). π is the number of phases, each uniform and separable. The 2 is temperature and pressure. F is the number of intensive variables that can be changed independently without a phase appearing or vanishing.
  3. Derivation. Unknowns: T, P and C − 1 mole fractions in each phase, 2 + π(C − 1). Equations: equal μi in every phase, C(π − 1). F = 2 + π(C − 1) − C(π − 1) = C − π + 2.

If the bulge is big enough relative to the gap between the vapour pressures, the bubble curve passes through a maximum in P at fixed T, which is a minimum in T at fixed P. At that extremum dP/dx₁ = 0, and by Gibbs-Duhem the extremum is exactly where x₁ = y₁.

    condition:  ln(γ₁/γ₂) = 1.2(x₂² − x₁²) = 1.2(1 − 2x₁) = ln(20/60) = −1.0986

  4. Reading the result. 60.13 kPa is above P₁sat = 60.0 kPa, so it is the highest pressure on the isotherm: a maximum-pressure, minimum-boiling azeotrope at x₁ = 0.958. Because γ₂∞ = e1.2 = 3.32 is large but P₁sat is three times P₂sat, the azeotrope sits close to pure 1 and is weak. At x₁ = 0.40 the same system gives P = 51.51 kPa against 36.0 kPa for ideal Raoult's law.
  5. Phase rule at the azeotrope. Binary VLE has C = 2, π = 2, so F = 2. The azeotrope adds the condition x₁ = y₁, leaving one degree of freedom: at fixed T the azeotropic P and composition are fixed. Changing the pressure moves the azeotrope; ethanol–water at 1 atm boils at about 78 °C with about 89 mol % ethanol, and the composition shifts at lower pressure.
  6. Why ordinary distillation stops there. Distillation needs y₁ ≠ x₁ on each stage. At the azeotrope α₁₂ = 1; the tie line has zero length and the lever rule is 0/0. A column fed on one side reaches the azeotrope at the top (minimum boiling) and cannot cross it.
  7. Ways around it. Pressure-swing distillation (two columns at different pressures, where the azeotrope composition differs), azeotropic distillation with an entrainer that forms a heterogeneous azeotrope, extractive distillation with a heavy solvent that changes the γ ratio, or membranes.
  8. Data check. The γ values behind such a conclusion should pass a Gibbs-Duhem consistency test before they are trusted.
Evaluate Compare the γ-φ and φ-φ approaches to vapour-liquid equilibrium. For each approach, state the assumptions, the data required, the typical pressure range, and the kind of system where it fails. 15 marks

Points to cover. γ-φ: yᵢφᵢV P = xᵢγᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ, with φᵢV from a virial-type correlation and γᵢ from a liquid model; γ-φ suits low to moderate pressure (roughly up to 10 bar) where the vapour is nearly ideal and the liquid standard state is well defined; φ-φ: yᵢφᵢV = xᵢφᵢL, both coefficients from the same cubic equation of state with mixing rules; φ-φ suits high pressure and supercritical components, where no pure liquid vapour pressure exists; Failure of γ-φ: near the critical point, at very high pressure, and for supercritical gases; Failure of φ-φ: polar and associating liquids near atmospheric pressure, where simple cubic equations give poor liquid densities; Both reduce to the same fugacity-equality criterion of phase equilibrium.

Model answer.

  1. Common starting point. f̂iV = f̂iL for every component. The two methods differ only in how the liquid fugacity is written.
  2. γ-φ (modified Raoult) method. yiφ̂iP = xiγifi, with fi = φisatPisat × Poynting. At low pressure this reduces to yiP = xiγiPisat. φ̂i comes from a virial correlation; γi from a liquid model such as Margules, van Laar, Wilson, NRTL or UNIQUAC.
  3. φ-φ method. yiφ̂iV = xiφ̂iL, both coefficients from one cubic EOS (Soave-Redlich-Kwong or Peng-Robinson) with mixing rules and fitted kij; Ki = φ̂iL/φ̂iV.

  5. Example where γ-φ fails. Methane–n-butane at 300 K and 20 bar: methane (Tc = 190.6 K) is far above its critical temperature, so it has no Psat and no pure-liquid standard state. The φ-φ route, starting from Wilson K-values (Kmethane = 16.7), handles it directly.
  6. Example where γ-φ is better. Ethanol–water at 1 atm: the strong hydrogen bonding gives activity coefficients well above 1 and a minimum-boiling azeotrope; Wilson or NRTL fits these data well, while a simple cubic EOS with a single kij gives poor liquid densities and γ values.
  7. Practical choice. Use γ-φ for low-pressure chemical systems with polar liquids, and φ-φ for gas processing, refinery light ends and high-pressure separations. Modern practice joins them by putting a GE model inside the EOS mixing rule, but both still rest on the same fugacity equality.
Evaluate Duhem's theorem is stated for a closed system. Discuss how the intensive state of a vapour-liquid-liquid three-phase system is determined, and how the phase rule count and Duhem's theorem are reconciled. 14 marks

Points to cover. Write the phase rule for C = 2, π = 3: F = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1; Fixing T (say) fixes P and all three phase compositions, but not how much of each phase is present; Count: 5 intensive unknowns (T, P and one mole fraction per phase) against C(π − 1) = 4 equalities of chemical potential; Duhem adds π − 1 = 2 phase fractions and C − 1 = 1 material balance, leaving 2 variables; since only one may be intensive, the second must be extensive (total volume, heat added or a phase amount); At fixed P the three-phase state exists at a single temperature; heat added there only changes the phase amounts until one phase disappears, as in boiling a pure liquid.

Model answer.

  1. Phase rule count. A binary (C = 2) with two liquids and a vapour (π = 3): F = C − π + 2 = 1. Intensive unknowns are T, P and one mole fraction in each phase (x₁L1, x₁L2, y₁), five in all; the chemical-potential equalities number C(π − 1) = 4, so one is free.
  2. What one free variable means. Fix T and the three-phase pressure and all three compositions are fixed; fix P instead and the three-phase temperature is fixed. At fixed P the three-phase state is a single temperature, a horizontal line on the T-x-y diagram.
  3. Duhem's count. In a closed vessel charged with known amounts, add the π − 1 = 2 independent phase fractions as unknowns and the C − 1 = 1 independent material balance as an equation: 5 + 2 − 4 − 1 = 2 variables must be fixed.
  4. Reconciling the two. Duhem says two independent variables fix the state; the phase rule says only one of them can be intensive, because T and P are tied together here. The second must be extensive: the total volume, the heat added or the amount of one phase. Fixing T and P together is not two independent specifications in a three-phase binary.
  5. Physical picture. Heat a vessel holding the three phases at fixed P: T stays at the three-phase value while one liquid layer is used up, exactly as a pure liquid boils at one temperature; the added heat changes only the phase amounts. When a phase disappears, π = 2, F = 2 and T can rise again.
  6. Immiscible-liquid case. If the two liquids are practically insoluble in each other, each liquid layer is nearly pure, so f̂i = Pisat in its own layer and P = P₁sat + P₂sat. For example, if at some T the pure vapour pressures are 62 kPa and 40 kPa, the three-phase pressure is 102 kPa, so the mixture boils below either pure liquid at about 1 atm. This is the basis of steam distillation and of heteroazeotropic drying of solvents.
  7. Conclusion. The phase rule fixes how many intensive variables are free (one); Duhem's theorem adds that a closed system still needs two specifications in total, one of them extensive.
Evaluate Solid A dissolves in liquid B. Derive the expression for the solubility of A in B from the fugacity-equality condition, and discuss why solubility rises with temperature when the heat of fusion is positive. Illustrate with Tm = 330 K and Δhfus = 20.0 kJ/mol. 13 marks

Points to cover. Equilibrium: fAsolid = fAsolution; Solid fugacity from the pure solid; solution fugacity written as γA xA fA° with fA° the pure subcooled liquid; Integrate the Gibbs-Helmholtz relation from the melting point Tm: ln(γA xA) ≈ −(ΔHfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm); For an ideal solution (γA = 1) this gives ln xA = −(ΔHfus/R)(1/T − 1/Tm); With ΔHfus > 0 the right side becomes less negative as T rises, so xA increases with temperature; Activity coefficient corrections matter for polar solutes and non-ideal solvents.

Model answer.

  1. Equilibrium condition. Pure solid A is in contact with a liquid solution, so fAS = f̂AL = xAγAfAL, where fAL is the fugacity of pure liquid A at the same T and P. Below the melting point that pure liquid is subcooled, a hypothetical but well-defined reference. Hence xAγA = fAS/fAL.
  2. The fugacity ratio. RT ln(fAS/fAL) = gAS − gAL = −Δgfus(T), the Gibbs energy of melting at T.
  3. Path for Δgfus. Heat the solid from T to Tm, melt it at Tm (where Δgfus = 0), cool the liquid back to T. With ΔCp ≈ 0, Δhfus is constant and Δsfus = Δhfus/Tm, so Δgfus(T) = Δhfus − TΔhfus/Tm = Δhfus(1 − T/Tm).

Glossary

Activity
The dimensionless ratio of a component's fugacity to its value in a chosen standard state.
Activity coefficient
The factor by which a component's behaviour in a mixture differs from ideal, equal to activity divided by mole fraction.
Azeotrope
A mixture whose liquid and vapour compositions are identical at equilibrium, so simple distillation cannot change its composition.
Binodal curve
The boundary curve on a phase diagram, given by the common tangent construction, separating one-phase from two-phase behaviour.
Bubble point
The temperature or pressure at which the first bubble of vapour forms in a liquid of given composition.
Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs energy of a component, equal to the rate of change of the total Gibbs energy per mole of that component added, at constant T, P and amounts of the others.
Component
An independent chemical species needed to define the composition of all phases in the system.
Degree of freedom
One intensive variable that can be changed independently without changing the number of phases in equilibrium.
Dew point
The temperature or pressure at which the first drop of liquid forms in a vapour of given composition.
Distribution coefficient
The equilibrium ratio of the mole fractions of a component in two coexisting phases.
Duhem's theorem
The statement that in a closed system made from known amounts of each species, any two independent variables fix the entire equilibrium state.
Fugacity
A pseudo-pressure that makes the ideal-gas chemical potential formula work for real fluids.
Fugacity coefficient
The ratio of a component's fugacity to its partial pressure, equal to 1 for an ideal gas.
Henry's constant
The slope of solute fugacity against solute mole fraction at infinite dilution, having units of pressure.
Henry's law
The relation that the solute fugacity in a very dilute solution is proportional to the solute mole fraction, with Henry's constant as the proportionality.
Ideal solution
A solution in which every component obeys Raoult's law over the whole composition range and the heat of mixing is zero.
Lever rule
A mass-balance rule giving the relative amounts of two coexisting phases from the distances of the overall composition from the two phase compositions on a tie line.
Phase equilibrium
A state in which every phase has the same temperature and pressure and every component has the same chemical potential in every phase, so no net transfer occurs.
Phase rule
The relation F = C − π + 2 giving the number of intensive variables that can be set independently.
Poynting correction
A small exponential factor that corrects liquid fugacity for pressure difference from the saturation pressure, usually near 1 at low pressure.
Raoult's law
The relation yᵢP = xᵢPᵢˢᵃᵗ that describes vapour-liquid equilibrium for ideal solutions.
Relative volatility
The ratio K₁/K₂ of the K-values of two components, which measures how easily they can be separated.
Spinodal curve
The locus where the second derivative of Gibbs energy with composition is zero, marking the limit of spontaneous phase splitting.
Standard state
A defined reference state at the same temperature and pressure as the system, used to measure fugacity and activity; pure liquid is usual for liquids and ideal gas at 1 bar for vapours.
Tie line
A line joining the compositions of two phases that are in equilibrium with each other.
