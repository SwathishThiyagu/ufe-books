7.1 Reaction stoichiometry and extent of reaction

A reacting mixture changes composition as reactants turn into products. Instead of tracking every species separately, write the reaction as ΣνᵢAᵢ = 0, with negative coefficients for reactants and positive for products. Then one variable, the extent ξ, fixes every mole number through nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ. This bookkeeping is the base for equilibrium calculations in 7.2, reactor design, and material balances in process plants.

You need mole balances on a reacting system and a balanced chemical equation.

Stoichiometric coefficients carry a sign

A reaction such as N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3 gives the ratio in which molecules combine. To use it in a balance, move every species to one side: 0 = −N2 − 3H2 + 2NH3.

The number in front of each species is its stoichiometric coefficient νi. It is dimensionless. By convention νi is negative for a reactant, positive for a product, and zero for an inert.

Σᵢ νᵢ Aᵢ = 0

Aᵢ is the formula of species i and νᵢ its stoichiometric coefficient; the sum runs over every species in the mixture.

The sign convention turns the reaction into one linear equation. If species i is consumed, νᵢ < 0, so its moles fall as ξ grows; if it is formed, νᵢ > 0, so its moles rise.

Scaling the reaction multiplies every νᵢ. Writing 2N2 + 6H2 → 4NH3 doubles all coefficients. The physical reaction is the same, but the numerical value of ξ for a given change halves. Always state the reaction as written.

Extent of reaction ξ

One number tracks how far the reaction has gone. The extent of reaction ξ (unit: mol) is the moles of reaction as written that have occurred. It is also called the reaction coordinate.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢ ξ

nᵢ is the moles of species i at extent ξ (mol), nᵢ₀ is its initial moles (mol), νᵢ is its stoichiometric coefficient, and ξ is the extent (mol).

At the start ξ = 0. ξ is positive when the reaction proceeds as written and negative when it runs in reverse. The only firm limits are that no nᵢ may become negative. In the forward direction, a reactant needs ξ ≤ nᵢ₀/(−νᵢ), and a product that starts at zero needs ξ ≥ 0.

Adding the species balances gives the total moles:

nT = nT₀ + ξ Σᵢ νᵢ

nT is the total moles at extent ξ (mol), nT₀ is the initial total moles (mol), and Σᵢνᵢ is the sum of all stoichiometric coefficients.

For ammonia, Σν = −1 − 3 + 2 = −2, so total moles fall by 2 mol for each mole of reaction. The figure is the mole table for this case.

Several reactions, limiting reactant and conversion

A real feed may undergo more than one independent reaction, for example steam reforming followed by water-gas shift. Number them j = 1, ..., r. Each reaction has its own extent ξⱼ and its own coefficient νᵢⱼ for species i.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ

νᵢⱼ is the coefficient of species i in reaction j, ξⱼ is the extent of reaction j (mol), and the sum runs over r independent reactions.

Independent means no reaction in the set is a linear combination of the others. The count r equals the rank of the stoichiometric matrix. Using one ξ for two independent reactions is wrong; the mole balances cannot be satisfied.

The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. For one reaction, its maximum extent is ξmax = min over reactants of nᵢ₀/(−νᵢ).

For a single reaction, conversion of species i is the fraction of its initial moles that has reacted:

Xᵢ = (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀ = −νᵢ ξ/nᵢ₀

Xᵢ is the fractional conversion of species i (dimensionless), nᵢ₀ its initial moles (mol), nᵢ its moles at extent ξ, and νᵢ its coefficient.

With several reactions, Xᵢ = (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀ = −(Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ)/nᵢ₀.

An inert has νᵢ = 0, so nᵢ = nᵢ₀ at every extent. It still contributes to nT. Equilibrium conversion, where ξ stops changing, belongs to 7.6.

Worked example

Question. A feed of 10 mol N₂ and 30 mol H₂ enters an ammonia reactor. The reaction is N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. At extent ξ = 4 mol, find nN₂, nH₂, nNH₃ and the total moles nT.

  1. Write the coefficients with the sign convention: ν(N₂) = −1, ν(H₂) = −3, ν(NH₃) = +2.
  2. Apply nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ to N₂: nN₂ = 10 mol + (−1)(4 mol) = 10 − 4 = 6 mol.
  3. For H₂: nH₂ = 30 mol + (−3)(4 mol) = 30 − 12 = 18 mol.
  4. For NH₃: nNH₃ = 0 mol + (+2)(4 mol) = 0 + 8 = 8 mol.
  5. Add the species: nT = 6 + 18 + 8 = 32 mol.
  6. Check with total moles: Σν = −1 − 3 + 2 = −2; nT = nT₀ + ξΣν = 40 mol + (4 mol)(−2) = 40 − 8 = 32 mol.

Answer. nN₂ = 6 mol, nH₂ = 18 mol, nNH₃ = 8 mol, and nT = 32 mol.

Worked example 2

Question. A steam reformer is fed 1.00 mol CH₄ and 3.00 mol H₂O. Two independent reactions run: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ (extent ξ₁) and CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ (extent ξ₂). At the exit ξ₁ = 0.60 mol and ξ₂ = 0.20 mol. Find every mole number, the total, the conversions of CH₄ and H₂O, and the exit mole fractions.

  1. Use nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼνᵢⱼξⱼ. Coefficients: CH₄ (−1, 0), H₂O (−1, −1), CO (+1, −1), H₂ (+3, +1), CO₂ (0, +1).
  2. CH₄: 1.00 − 0.60 = 0.40 mol. H₂O: 3.00 − 0.60 − 0.20 = 2.20 mol. CO: 0.60 − 0.20 = 0.40 mol. H₂: 3(0.60) + 0.20 = 2.00 mol. CO₂: 0.20 mol.
  3. Total: 0.40 + 2.20 + 0.40 + 2.00 + 0.20 = 5.20 mol. Check: Σν is +2 for the first reaction and 0 for the second, so nT = 4.00 + 2(0.60) + 0(0.20) = 5.20 mol.
  4. Conversions: XCH₄ = (1.00 − 0.40)/1.00 = 0.60; XH₂O = (3.00 − 2.20)/3.00 = 0.267.
  5. Mole fractions: CH₄ 0.40/5.20 = 0.077, H₂O 0.423, CO 0.077, H₂ 0.385, CO₂ 0.038.

Answer. Exit: 0.40 mol CH₄, 2.20 mol H₂O, 0.40 mol CO, 2.00 mol H₂ and 0.20 mol CO₂ (5.20 mol in all); CH₄ conversion 60 %, H₂O conversion 26.7 %; yH₂ = 0.385.

Exam traps

  • Using one extent ξ for two independent reactions. Each independent reaction needs its own ξⱼ; otherwise the mole balances conflict.
  • Taking conversion as ξ divided by total moles. Conversion is (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀, that is ξ scaled by |νᵢ| and divided by the initial moles of that species.
  • Dropping the sign of νᵢ. A reactant has νᵢ < 0; a positive νᵢ with positive ξ increases that species.
  • Forgetting inerts. They have νᵢ = 0, stay constant, but appear in nT and in nT₀.
  • Doubling every coefficient without changing ξ. The reaction is scaled, so ξ must halve for the same physical change.

7.2 Equilibrium criteria for chemical reactions

At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed reacting mixture moves to the composition with the lowest total Gibbs energy. This is the chemical equilibrium criterion: no further change lowers G. The same idea decides whether a reaction goes forward, backward, or stands still, and it works for one reaction or several. Process engineers use it to check feasibility before building a reactor, to set operating temperature and pressure, and to predict equilibrium conversion. The key quantity is the reaction Gibbs energy, built from the chemical potentials of every species.

You need the fundamental property relation dG = −S dT + V dP + Σμᵢ dnᵢ, chemical potential, and reaction stoichiometry and extent for ξ and νᵢ.

Total Gibbs energy of a reacting mixture

For a closed mixture at fixed temperature T and pressure P, the total Gibbs energy is G = Σ nᵢ μᵢ. Here nᵢ is the number of moles of species i (mol) and μᵢ is its chemical potential (kJ/mol). The chemical potential μᵢ is the partial molar Gibbs energy of species i: μᵢ = (∂G/∂nᵢ) at fixed T, P and all other amounts. It equals the molar Gibbs energy of pure i only when i is pure.

At fixed T and P, the change in G when the amounts change is dG = Σ μᵢ dnᵢ. This comes from dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μᵢ dnᵢ with dT = 0 and dP = 0.

Describe composition by the extent ξ (mol). From Chapter 7.1, dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ. The stoichiometric coefficient νᵢ is negative for a reactant and positive for a product. Substituting gives dG = (Σ νᵢ μᵢ) dξ.

Define the reaction Gibbs energy ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ. Then dG/dξ = ΔGr, and the affinity is A = −ΔGr. The slope of G against ξ is ΔGr. A negative slope means G falls as ξ increases, so the forward reaction is favoured. A positive slope means the reverse reaction is favoured.

G = Σ nᵢ μᵢ,  dG = Σ μᵢ dnᵢ = (Σ νᵢ μᵢ) dξ,  ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ

G is total Gibbs energy (kJ); nᵢ is moles of species i (mol); μᵢ is chemical potential (kJ/mol); ξ is extent (mol); νᵢ is stoichiometric coefficient (negative for reactants, positive for products); ΔGr is reaction Gibbs energy (kJ/mol).

Minimum and the equilibrium condition

At fixed T and P, equilibrium is the state of minimum total Gibbs energy. At a minimum the first derivative is zero, so dG/dξ = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0. This equation is the equilibrium criterion for a chemical reaction.

For a stable minimum the second derivative is positive: d²G/dξ² > 0. A zero first derivative alone does not prove equilibrium: the point could be a maximum or an inflection. d²G/dξ² > 0 identifies the stable minimum.

The sign of ΔGr gives direction. If ΔGr < 0, then dG/dξ < 0 and G decreases as ξ increases: the forward reaction proceeds. If ΔGr > 0, then dG/dξ > 0 and G decreases as ξ decreases: the reverse reaction proceeds. If ΔGr = 0, the system is at equilibrium. The affinity A = −ΔGr is positive for a forward tendency.

Do not set ΔG° = 0 at equilibrium. ΔG° is the standard Gibbs energy change, a fixed value at a chosen standard state. ΔGr uses the actual chemical potentials and changes with composition. Equilibrium requires ΔGr = 0, not ΔG° = 0. The link from ΔG° to the equilibrium constant is in Chapter 7.3.

Heterogeneous reactions and degrees of freedom

For a heterogeneous reaction, the same species may appear in more than one phase. At equilibrium its chemical potential must be equal in every phase where it appears: μᵢα = μᵢβ for phases α and β. This is the phase-equilibrium condition.

Write one reaction equilibrium equation Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0 for each independent reaction. Use the chemical potential of each species in any phase where it is present; equality of μ makes the choice irrelevant. Do not write a separate reaction equation for each phase for the same reaction.

Let N be the number of species, π the number of phases, and r the number of independent reactions. The reacting phase rule is F = N − π + 2 − r. F is the number of intensive degrees of freedom. Each independent reaction adds one equilibrium relation and removes one freedom.

Duhem's theorem applies with reactions. For a closed system made from specified initial amounts, fixing any two independent variables fixes the whole equilibrium state, including the amount of each phase. In a single-phase reactor T and P are independent, so fixing them fixes the equilibrium composition; when the reacting phase rule leaves F = 1, one of the two must be extensive.

μᵢα = μᵢβ,  Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0,  F = N − π + 2 − r

μᵢα is chemical potential of species i in phase α (kJ/mol); N is number of species; π is number of phases; r is number of independent reactions; F is degrees of freedom.

Worked example

Question. At 500 K and 1 bar, for A(g) → B(g), a trial mixture has μA = −12.0 kJ/mol and μB = −20.0 kJ/mol. Find ΔGr and state the direction in which the reaction proceeds.

  1. Write coefficients for A → B: νA = −1 and νB = +1.
  2. Use ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = νA μA + νB μB.
  3. Substitute: ΔGr = (−1)(−12.0) + (+1)(−20.0) = +12.0 − 20.0 = −8.0 kJ/mol.
  4. Negative ΔGr means dG/dξ < 0 at fixed T and P. G falls as ξ increases, so the forward reaction A → B proceeds.

Answer. ΔGr = −8.0 kJ/mol; the reaction proceeds forward from A to B until ΔGr = 0 at equilibrium.

Worked example 2

Question. For the ideal-gas isomerisation A(g) ⇌ B(g) at 500 K and 1 bar, ΔG° = μB° − μA° = −4.00 kJ/mol. Starting from 1 mol A, write G(ξ), find the slope dG/dξ at ξ = 0.25 and 0.90 mol, and find the extent at which G is a minimum.

  1. Moles: nA = 1 − ξ, nB = ξ, nT = 1, so yA = 1 − ξ and yB = ξ. At P = P° each μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln yᵢ, with RT = 8.314 × 500 = 4157 J/mol.
  2. G = Σnᵢμᵢ. Measured from pure A (G = μA°): G − μA° = ξΔG° + RT[(1 − ξ) ln(1 − ξ) + ξ ln ξ].
  3. Slope: dG/dξ = μB − μA = ΔG° + RT ln[ξ/(1 − ξ)]. At ξ = 0.25: −4000 + 4157 ln(1/3) = −4000 − 4567 = −8567 J/mol, so the forward reaction lowers G. At ξ = 0.90: −4000 + 4157 ln 9 = −4000 + 9134 = +5134 J/mol, so the reverse reaction lowers G.
  4. Minimum: dG/dξ = 0 gives ln[ξ/(1 − ξ)] = 4000/4157 = 0.9622, so ξ/(1 − ξ) = 2.617 and ξ = 0.724 mol. There G − μA° = 0.724(−4000) + 4157(0.276 ln 0.276 + 0.724 ln 0.724) = −2894 − 2451 = −5345 J.
  5. Second derivative: d²G/dξ² = RT/[ξ(1 − ξ)] = 4157/(0.724 × 0.276) = 20 800 J/mol² > 0, so the point is a minimum.

Answer. G falls with a slope of −8.57 kJ/mol at ξ = 0.25 and rises with +5.13 kJ/mol at ξ = 0.90; the minimum, which is the equilibrium state, is at ξ = 0.724 mol, 5.35 kJ below pure A.

Exam traps

  • Setting ΔG° = 0 at equilibrium. ΔG° is fixed by standard states; the equilibrium condition is ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0.
  • Using ΔGr with standard chemical potentials only. Equilibrium uses actual μ at the mixture composition.
  • Reversing the sign convention for νᵢ. Reactants are negative and products positive; a sign slip flips ΔGr and the predicted direction.
  • For a heterogeneous reaction, writing one equilibrium equation per phase for the same reaction. Use one equation per independent reaction, plus μ equality for each species across phases.
  • Treating a zero first derivative as sufficient. A maximum or an inflection also has zero slope; stable equilibrium needs d²G/dξ² > 0.

7.3 Standard Gibbs energy change and the equilibrium constant

At fixed temperature and pressure, a reacting mixture moves toward Σνᵢμᵢ = 0. Splitting each μᵢ into a standard part and a composition part gives the standard Gibbs energy change ΔG°, which depends only on temperature, and from it a dimensionless number, the equilibrium constant K = exp(−ΔG°/RT). Engineers use K to screen reactions before building a reactor: ammonia synthesis, methanol from syngas, SO₂ oxidation. This chapter connects standard formation data to K, and then to the activities that describe real gases and liquids.

You need reaction stoichiometry and extent for νᵢ, the equilibrium criterion Σνᵢμᵢ = 0, and fugacity, activity and standard states.

Standard states and ΔG°

At equilibrium, 7.2 gives Σνᵢμᵢ = 0. μᵢ is the chemical potential of species i in the actual mixture. To get a number that depends only on temperature, split μᵢ into a standard part and a composition part. The standard chemical potential μᵢ° is the chemical potential of pure i in its standard state at reaction temperature T. For a gas the standard state is ideal gas at P° = 1 bar. For a liquid it is the pure liquid at 1 bar. Tables of ΔGf° use these conventions, so always record the standard state.

ΔG° = Σ νᵢ μᵢ° = Σ νᵢ ΔGf,i°

ΔG° is standard Gibbs energy change of reaction, J mol⁻¹; νᵢ is stoichiometric number, positive for products and negative for reactants; μᵢ° is standard chemical potential, J mol⁻¹; ΔGf,i° is standard Gibbs energy of formation, J mol⁻¹.

Formation data are the practical route. ΔGf° of an element in its stable standard state is zero. For A ⇌ 2B, ΔG° = 2μB° − μA° = 2ΔGf,B° − ΔGf,A°. Stoichiometric numbers carry the signs: positive for products, negative for reactants. No separate product-minus-reactant rule is needed.

Most tables are at 298.15 K. At another T, use ΔG° for that T; 7.4 gives the temperature correction.

Activity and the equilibrium constant

Activity is the composition variable that makes the chemical potential linear in a logarithm. Define aᵢ by μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ. Then aᵢ = exp[(μᵢ − μᵢ°)/(RT)].

K = Π aᵢνᵢ = exp(−ΔG°/(RT))

K is the equilibrium constant, dimensionless; aᵢ is activity, dimensionless; νᵢ is stoichiometric number; ΔG° is standard Gibbs energy change, J mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature, K.

For gases, aᵢ = fᵢ/fᵢ° = φᵢ yᵢ P/P°. fᵢ is fugacity; fᵢ° is the standard fugacity, equal to P° = 1 bar for every gas because the standard state is the ideal gas at 1 bar (a real gas enters through φᵢ); φᵢ is fugacity coefficient, yᵢ is mole fraction, P is total pressure. For an ideal gas, φᵢ = 1 and aᵢ = yᵢP/P°. For a liquid with pure-liquid standard state, aᵢ = γᵢ xᵢ; γᵢ is activity coefficient and xᵢ is mole fraction. An ideal solution has γᵢ = 1.

K is dimensionless because every activity is a ratio to a standard state. The pair (standard state, K) must be consistent. Change the standard state and μᵢ° changes; ΔG° and K change with it. The physical equilibrium composition does not change when the matching activities are used.

K in mole fractions and concentrations

For an ideal-gas mixture, substitute aᵢ = yᵢP/P° into K. The pressure factors collect into one power.

K = Ky (P/P°)Σν, Ky = Π yᵢνᵢ

Ky is the mole-fraction product, dimensionless; yᵢ is mole fraction; P is total pressure, Pa or bar; P° is standard pressure, same unit; Σν = Σνᵢ is the sum of stoichiometric numbers.

Example: A ⇌ 2B has Σν = 2 − 1 = +1, so K = Ky(P/P°). Increasing P raises P/P° and lowers Ky at fixed K. That is the pressure effect on composition treated in 7.7.

Concentration form: for an ideal gas pᵢ = cᵢRT and aᵢ = cᵢRT/P°. Substitution gives K = (RT/P°)Σν Kc, where Kc = Π cᵢνᵢ, cᵢ is concentration in mol m⁻³, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, and P° is in Pa. Kc has units unless Σν = 0; K remains dimensionless.

  • Reversing a reaction replaces K by 1/K.
  • Doubling every stoichiometric coefficient replaces K by K².
  • Adding two reactions multiplies their K values.

Worked example

Question. For the gas reaction A(g) ⇌ 2B(g) at 400 K, ΔG° = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate K. State whether reactants or products are favoured when every activity equals 1.

  1. Convert ΔG° to J mol⁻¹: ΔG° = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ = 5000 J mol⁻¹.
  2. Compute RT = 8.314 × 400 = 3325.6 J mol⁻¹.
  3. Apply ln K = −ΔG°/(RT) = −5000/3325.6 = −1.503.
  4. Exponentiate: K = e−1.503 = 0.222.
  5. When every activity equals 1, Q = 1. ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ > 0, where Q = Π aᵢνᵢ is the reaction quotient at the actual composition, so the reverse direction is spontaneous and reactants are favoured.

Answer. K = 0.222 (dimensionless); at unit activities reactants are favoured.

Worked example 2

Question. Find K at 298.15 K for ammonia synthesis, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), from the standard Gibbs energy of formation of NH₃(g), −16.45 kJ/mol. What is K for ½N₂ + 3/2H₂ ⇌ NH₃?

  1. Elements in their standard states have ΔGf° = 0, so ΔG° = 2(−16.45) − 1(0) − 3(0) = −32.90 kJ/mol = −32 900 J/mol.
  2. RT = 8.314 × 298.15 = 2478.8 J/mol.
  3. ln K = −ΔG°/RT = 32 900/2478.8 = 13.272, so K = e13.272 = 5.81 × 10⁵.
  4. Per mole of NH₃ every coefficient is halved, so K′ = K1/2 = √(5.81 × 10⁵) = 762.

Answer. K = 5.81 × 10⁵ for 2 mol NH₃ (ΔG° = −32.90 kJ/mol), and K′ = 762 for the reaction written per mole of NH₃: products are strongly favoured at 298 K.

Exam traps

  • Writing K as a product of partial pressures in bar and calling it dimensionless. Divide each pᵢ by P° first; a bar-based Kp is not K.

7.4 Effect of temperature on equilibrium constant (van't Hoff)

The equilibrium constant is not frozen when temperature changes. For a reaction, the standard enthalpy of reaction tells the direction and size of the change. The van't Hoff equation converts ΔH° into the slope of ln K against 1/T. This slope lets you estimate K at a new temperature from one known value, or read ΔH° from experimental K data. Reactor designers use it to choose operating temperature: some reactions need heat to raise K, others need cooling to avoid losing conversion.

You need K from the standard Gibbs energy change and the standard heat of reaction from thermochemistry.

The van't Hoff slope

At equilibrium, K is fixed by the standard Gibbs energy change, as Chapter 7.3 shows. Write ln K = −ΔG°/(RT). The Gibbs-Helmholtz relation gives d(ΔG°/RT)/dT = −ΔH°/(RT²), because ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. Hence d ln K/dT = −d(ΔG°/RT)/dT = ΔH°/(RT²), the van't Hoff equation.

d ln K/dT = ΔH°/(R T²)

K is the thermodynamic equilibrium constant (dimensionless); T is absolute temperature in K; ΔH° is the standard enthalpy of reaction in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

The derivative is local: it gives the slope of ln K against T at one temperature. If ΔH° is nearly constant, integrate it to compare two temperatures. A positive ΔH° makes the right side positive, so ln K rises with T. A negative ΔH° makes it negative, so ln K falls with T. The next section states the same rule through Le Chatelier.

Sign rule and the plot

Le Chatelier's principle gives the sign without calculus. Heat can be treated as a product of an exothermic reaction and as a reactant of an endothermic reaction.

  • Endothermic, ΔH° > 0: raising T adds a reactant, shifts the reaction right, and K rises.
  • Exothermic, ΔH° < 0: raising T adds a product, shifts the reaction left, and K falls.

On a plot of ln K against 1/T, the slope is −ΔH°/R. The figure uses the worked example: K = 10.0 at 300 K and K = 0.571 at 350 K. The point at 1/350 = 0.002857 K⁻¹ has ln K = −0.560; the point at 1/300 = 0.003333 K⁻¹ has ln K = 2.303. Moving to the right means lower temperature and higher K for this exothermic reaction. At fixed T, pressure and inerts do not change K; they change composition only, as Chapter 7.7 explains.

Constant ΔH and changing ΔCp

With ΔH° constant, integrate between T₁ and T₂. The formula estimates a new K without ΔS° data. Use it for small temperature intervals, or when ΔCp° is close to zero.

ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

K₁ and K₂ are equilibrium constants at T₁ and T₂; ΔH° is the constant standard enthalpy of reaction in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T₁ and T₂ are absolute temperatures in K.

For wide ranges, ΔH° and ΔS° change. Compute the standard heat capacity change ΔCp° = ΣνᵢCpᵢ°; the signs of νᵢ make it products minus reactants. Then update enthalpy and entropy from a reference temperature T₀, and get ΔG°, then K.

ΔH°(T) = ΔH°(T₀) + ∫(T₀→T) ΔCp° dT;  ΔS°(T) = ΔS°(T₀) + ∫(T₀→T) (ΔCp°/T) dT;  ΔG°(T) = ΔH°(T) − TΔS°(T);  ln K(T) = −ΔG°(T)/(R T)

ΔH°(T) and ΔS°(T) are standard enthalpy and entropy at T; T₀ is a reference temperature; ΔCp° is products minus reactants in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; ∫(T₀→T) means integrate from T₀ to T; ΔG°(T) is standard Gibbs energy at T; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Alternatively, integrate d ln K/dT numerically in small steps. At each step update ΔH°(T), then add ΔH°/(R T²) ΔT to ln K. A spreadsheet or short script handles this. Evaluation from formation data belongs to Chapter 7.5.

Worked example

Question. The standard enthalpy of reaction is ΔH° = −50.0 kJ/mol and is constant. At 300 K the equilibrium constant is K₁ = 10.0. Calculate K₂ at 350 K. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. T₁ = 300 K and T₂ = 350 K. ΔH° = −50.0 kJ/mol = −50 000 J/mol.
  2. ΔH°/R = −50 000 / 8.314 = −6014 K.
  3. 1/T₂ − 1/T₁ = 1/350 − 1/300 = 0.002857 − 0.003333 = −0.0004762 K⁻¹.
  4. ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) = −(−6014)(−0.0004762) = −2.864.
  5. K₂ = K₁ e−2.864 = 10.0 × 0.0571 = 0.571.

Answer. K₂ = 0.571 at 350 K; the exothermic reaction loses equilibrium constant as temperature rises.

7.5 Evaluation of equilibrium constants; relation to composition

The equilibrium constant K is a pure number fixed by temperature and standard-state Gibbs energies. To use it, convert it into a statement about what is actually in the reactor: mole fractions, total moles and extent of reaction. This chapter evaluates K from tabulated formation data or from ΔH° and ΔS°, corrects it to the operating temperature, then substitutes activities and mole balances to get one equation in ξ. That equation gives the composition of an equilibrium mixture, so it is used whenever a reactor design needs the maximum possible conversion.

You need: 7.1 mole balances, 7.3 K expression, and 7.4 van't Hoff.

Evaluating K from standard data

Evaluate K from standard-state data before you look at the reactor. At 298.15 K, add standard Gibbs energies of formation with stoichiometric signs. Products are positive, reactants negative. Tabulated values use pure ideal gas at 1 bar, pure liquid at 1 bar, and similar standard states. A large positive ΔG° gives a small K. If formation data are missing, use ΔG°rxn = ΔH°rxn − TΔS°rxn, where ΔH°rxn and ΔS°rxn are standard enthalpy and entropy changes. For another temperature, correct with van't Hoff: ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°rxn/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁). Use K₁ at 298.15 K. The equation assumes constant ΔH°rxn; if heat capacities matter, integrate ΔCp° as in the van't Hoff equation. K at the reactor temperature is still one number.

ΔG°rxn = Σi νi ΔG°f,i,  K = exp(−ΔG°rxn / (R T))

νi is the stoichiometric coefficient of species i (positive for products, negative for reactants); ΔG°f,i is the standard Gibbs energy of formation at 298.15 K in J/mol; Σi means sum over all species; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; K is dimensionless.

From K to gas-phase composition

Write mole balances before substituting K. For one reaction, ni = ni0 + νi ξ and nT = nT0 + ξΣνi. Mole fractions are yi = ni/nT. Total moles change with ξ when Σνi ≠ 0: for A ⇌ 2B fed with 1 mol pure A, nT = 1 + ξ, not 1. For an ideal gas, ai = yi P/P°. The standard state is pure ideal gas at P° = 1 bar. Then K = (P/P°)Σν Π (ni/nT)νi. This is one algebraic equation in ξ. For A ⇌ 2B with 1 mol pure A, nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ, nT = 1 + ξ, and P = P° = 1 bar, so K = yB²/yA = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). Keep (P/P°)Σν when P ≠ P°; it cancels when Σν = 0. Solve, then back-substitute to check that the reaction quotient Q = Π aiνi, evaluated at the computed composition, equals K.

ni = ni0 + νi ξ,  nT = nT0 + ξΣνi,  yi = ni / nT

ni0 is initial moles of species i; νi is its stoichiometric coefficient; ξ is extent of reaction in mol; nT is total moles; nT0 is initial total moles; yi is mole fraction; Σνi is the sum of stoichiometric coefficients. Π means product over all species.

Liquid solutions and the back-substitution check

For a liquid, ai = γi xi. The standard state is pure liquid at the system temperature and pressure, so pressure corrections are small. Then K = Π (γi xi)νi. For an ideal solution, γi = 1. Mole balances still use ξ; calculate nT = nT0 + ξΣνi even if the liquid volume is nearly constant. Nonideal systems need iteration. K stays fixed, but the γi depend on xi, so the equation Π(γixi)νi = K becomes more strongly nonlinear in ξ. Start with γi = 1, solve for ξ, compute xi, update γi from a model (Margules, van Laar or Wilson), and repeat. Check by back-substitution: compute Q from final xi and γi; Q must equal K. For several independent reactions, each has its own ξ and K, but all share ni and nT; solve simultaneously (see simultaneous reactions). The back-substitution check applies to every reaction.

ai = γi xi,  K = Π (γi xi)νi

ai is activity; γi is activity coefficient; xi is liquid mole fraction; νi is stoichiometric coefficient. For an ideal solution γi = 1.

Worked example

Question. At 400 K, A ⇌ 2B has K = 0.222. The feed is 1 mol pure A at P = 1 bar and P° = 1 bar. Assume ideal gas. Find the equilibrium extent ξ and mole fractions yA and yB.

  1. Stoichiometric table: nA0 = 1 mol, nB0 = 0; nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ; nT = 1 − ξ + 2ξ = 1 + ξ.
  2. Activities: aA = yA P/P°, aB = yB P/P°. Since P/P° = 1, K = aB²/aA = yB²/yA.
  3. Substitute yA = (1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ), yB = 2ξ/(1 + ξ). K = [2ξ/(1+ξ)]² / [(1−ξ)/(1+ξ)] = 4ξ²/(1−ξ²).
  4. Solve 0.222 = 4ξ²/(1−ξ²). Multiply: 0.222 − 0.222ξ² = 4ξ², so 0.222 = 4.222ξ². ξ² = 0.222/4.222 = 0.05258. ξ = 0.2293 mol. Take the positive root; nA ≥ 0 requires ξ ≤ 1.

  6. Check: Q = 4(0.2293)²/(1 − 0.2293²) = 0.222, equal to K.

7.6 Feasibility of reactions; equilibrium conversion and yield

A reaction can be thermodynamically possible and still stop far from complete conversion. At the current temperature, pressure and composition, compare the reaction quotient Q with the equilibrium constant K. The sign of ΔG = RT ln(Q/K) gives the spontaneous direction. Equilibrium conversion and yield then measure how far a given feed can go when Q reaches K. Engineers use this check to screen routes, size reactors and read plant data for ammonia, methanol and steam reforming.

You need: extent of reaction from 7.1, the equilibrium criterion from 7.2, K from 7.3 and composition from 7.5.

Reaction quotient decides direction

A reacting mixture has a composition at every instant, and that composition fixes Q. Q is not the equilibrium constant. Q changes as the reaction proceeds; K changes only with temperature.

Q = Π aᵢνᵢ

aᵢ is the activity of species i relative to its standard state; νᵢ is its stoichiometric number, positive for products and negative for reactants; Π means product over all species. Activities are dimensionless, so Q is dimensionless.

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K)

ΔG is the Gibbs energy change at the current composition, J per mole of reaction as written; ΔG° is the standard Gibbs energy change, J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; Q and K are dimensionless.

  • Q < K gives ΔG < 0: forward reaction is spontaneous.
  • Q = K gives ΔG = 0: the mixture is at equilibrium.
  • Q > K gives ΔG > 0: backward reaction is spontaneous.

Feasibility here means direction from the present state. K > 1 only says standard-state products are favoured. It does not fix conversion: feed composition, stoichiometry, pressure and inerts enter through Q. Pressure and inert effects are in 7.7.

Equilibrium conversion from mole balances

For one reaction, write every mole number in terms of one extent ξ (mol). Set Q(ξ) = K and solve for ξ. Then convert extent to conversion.

X = (ni0 − ni) / ni0

X is the conversion of limiting reactant i, dimensionless and often reported as %; ni0 is its initial moles; ni is its moles at the chosen state. At equilibrium, Xeq uses ni at equilibrium.

For ideal gases, aᵢ = yᵢ P/P°, where yᵢ is mole fraction, P is total pressure and P° is standard pressure, 1 bar.

  1. Choose a basis, usually 1 mol of limiting reactant fed.
  2. Write nᵢ(ξ) = ni0 + νᵢξ from the stoichiometric table.
  3. Write total moles nT(ξ) and yᵢ = nᵢ/nT.
  4. Substitute activities into Q = Π aᵢνᵢ.
  5. Set Q(ξ) = K and solve for ξ, then Xeq.

For A ⇌ 2B with 1 mol A fed, no B and P = 1 bar: nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ, nT = 1 + ξ, so Q = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). The worked example solves this with K = 0.222.

Yield and selectivity

Conversion tells how much limiting reactant disappeared. Yield tells how much desired product appeared. Selectivity compares desired product with undesired product.

YD = nD,formed / nL0

YD is yield of desired product D, dimensionless or mol D per mol limiting reactant fed; nD,formed is moles of D formed; nL0 is moles of limiting reactant fed. Some texts divide by νD nL0, where νD is the stoichiometric number of D in the reaction as written, and call the result the fractional yield (fraction of the theoretical maximum amount of D).

SD/U = nD,formed / nU,formed

SD/U is selectivity of desired product D over undesired product U, mol/mol; nU,formed is moles of U formed. Name the undesired product when several exist.

High selectivity does not mean high yield. A reactor may make almost pure D but convert very little feed. For several simultaneous reactions, see 7.8.

Worked example

Question. For A ⇌ 2B, K = 0.222 at 400 K. A feed of 1 mol A at 1 bar contains no B. Take ideal gas behaviour and standard pressure 1 bar. (a) Derive Q as a function of extent ξ. (b) Compute Q at ξ = 0.10 mol and ξ = 0.30 mol; state the spontaneous direction. (c) Find equilibrium extent, conversion of A and yield of B.

  1. Stoichiometric table: nA = 1 − ξ mol, nB = 2ξ mol, total nT = 1 + ξ mol.
  2. Mole fractions: yA = (1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ), yB = 2ξ/(1 + ξ).
  3. At P = 1 bar and P° = 1 bar, ideal-gas activities are aA = yA and aB = yB.
  4. Q = aB²/aA = [(2ξ/(1 + ξ))²]/[(1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ)] = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²).
  5. At ξ = 0.10 mol: Q = 4(0.10)²/(1 − 0.10²) = 0.0404. This is below K = 0.222, so ΔG < 0 and the reaction goes forward.
  6. At ξ = 0.30 mol: Q = 4(0.30)²/(1 − 0.30²) = 0.3956 ≈ 0.396. This is above K, so ΔG > 0 and the reaction goes backward.
  7. At equilibrium: 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) = 0.222, so 4ξ² = 0.222 − 0.222ξ², giving 4.222ξ² = 0.222 and ξ² = 0.05258.

Exam traps

  • Reporting K > 1 as high conversion. K fixes only the equilibrium activity ratio; stoichiometry, feed, pressure and inerts also matter.
  • Using ΔG° sign for feasibility at the plant composition. Feasibility uses ΔG = RT ln(Q/K), not ΔG° alone.
  • Treating Q as if it were K. Q can be evaluated at any composition and it changes as the reaction proceeds; only at equilibrium does Q equal K. K is the fixed value of Q at equilibrium for the given temperature.
  • Confusing yield with selectivity. Yield is desired product per limiting reactant fed; selectivity is desired product per undesired product.
  • Forgetting pressure in gas activities. aᵢ = yᵢP/P° for an ideal gas; yᵢ alone is correct only when P = P° = 1 bar.

7.7 Effect of pressure and inerts on conversion

At fixed temperature the equilibrium constant of a gas reaction is fixed. Pressure and inert gas change the mole fractions inside that constant without changing the constant itself. For an ideal gas K = Ky (P/P°)Σν, so the sign of Σν tells which way the mixture moves. Industry uses the rule directly: ammonia synthesis runs at high pressure because it makes fewer moles, while cracking and dehydrogenation run at low pressure or with steam dilution because they make more moles.

You need: equilibrium constants and composition from 7.5, and equilibrium conversion from 7.6.

Pressure changes the split, not K

At fixed temperature, K for a gas reaction does not change when pressure changes. What changes is the way K is split between mole fractions and pressure. For an ideal gas, the activity of species i is ai = yi P/P°, where yi is mole fraction, P is total pressure, and P° is standard pressure, 1 bar.

K = ∏ aiνi = [∏ yiνi] (P/P°)Σν = Ky (P/P°)Σν

ai is activity, yi is mole fraction, P is total pressure, P° is standard pressure (1 bar), νi is stoichiometric coefficient (positive for products, negative for reactants), Σν = Σνi, and Ky = ∏ yiνi.

At fixed T, K is fixed. Rearranging gives Ky = K (P/P°)−Σν. If Σν > 0, raising P lowers the Ky that K requires at the new pressure, so the mixture moves toward reactants until it reaches that new, lower Ky. If Σν < 0, raising P favours products. If Σν = 0, pressure cancels and there is no ideal-gas shift.

Sign of ΣνRaising P at fixed TExample
Σν > 0favours reactants (fewer moles)A ⇌ 2B
Σν < 0favours products (fewer moles)N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3
Σν = 0no ideal-gas shiftH2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI

Inert gas dilutes the reacting mixture

An inert gas does not appear in the reaction stoichiometry. It still occupies volume and adds to total moles. For an ideal gas at total pressure P, the partial pressure of i is pi = yi P = ni P / ntotal. Adding inert raises ntotal. At fixed T and P, pi of every reactant and product falls.

This is dilution. It acts like lowering total pressure while keeping the same mole numbers of reactants and products. The equilibrium shifts toward the side with more moles. So inerts raise conversion when Σν > 0 and lower conversion when Σν < 0. When Σν = 0, inerts have no ideal-gas effect on composition. At fixed T and V the case is different: adding inert does not change pi = ni RT/V for the reacting species, so for an ideal gas the composition does not shift at all; only the total pressure rises.

Volume and condensed phases

At fixed T, changing volume changes the pressure of a gas by P = nRT/V. Halving V roughly doubles P. The composition shift follows the same Σν rule: compression acts like higher pressure. For a reaction that makes more moles, compression lowers conversion; for one that makes fewer moles, compression raises conversion.

For liquid-phase reactions, pressure has little effect on K and activity coefficients over ordinary pressures. The pressure effect becomes noticeable near the critical point or at very high pressures, where fugacity coefficients differ from unity. Then replace ideal-gas activities by ai = yi φi P/P°, with φi the fugacity coefficient. This is the link to nonideal gas treatment in 7.8.

Worked example

Question. For A ⇌ 2B, K = 0.222 at 400 K. A feed of 1 mol A is at 400 K. (a) Find XA at P = 1 bar, 0.5 bar and 2 bar. (b) Find XA at 1 bar if 1 mol inert is also fed.

  1. Let ξ be the extent in mol. With 1 mol A initially and no inert: nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ, ntotal = 1 + ξ. Conversion of A is XA = ξ/1 = ξ.
  2. Mole fractions are yA = (1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ) and yB = 2ξ/(1 + ξ). For A ⇌ 2B, Σν = 2 − 1 = 1. With P° = 1 bar, K = (P/P°) × 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²).
  3. At P = 1 bar: 0.222 = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). This gives 0.222 = 4.222ξ², ξ² = 0.05258, ξ = 0.2293. So XA = 0.229.
  4. At P = 0.5 bar: 0.222 = 0.5 × 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) = 2ξ²/(1 − ξ²). This gives 0.222 = 2.222ξ², ξ² = 0.09991, ξ = 0.3161. So XA = 0.316.

7.8 Gas-phase, heterogeneous and simultaneous reactions

A real reactor rarely holds one ideal gas and one reaction. High pressure makes gases non-ideal, solids sit beside gases, and several reactions run together. The criterion is still Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 for each reaction; what changes is how activity is written and how many equations are solved at once. Fugacity coefficients handle high-pressure gases, pure solids and liquids have activity 1, and each independent reaction brings its own extent and K. Ammonia, methanol and steam-reforming reactors are designed this way.

You need K from the standard Gibbs energy change, K and composition, fugacity and activity coefficients, and phase equilibrium.

Gas phase: fugacity coefficients inside K

At a few bar a gas is ideal and the fugacity of species i is its partial pressure, fᵢ = yᵢP. At 50–300 bar — ammonia synthesis at 100–300 bar, low-pressure methanol synthesis at 50–100 bar — molecules attract and repel, so fᵢ differs from yᵢP by a factor φᵢ. Equilibrium then uses fugacity, not partial pressure.

K = Πᵢ (φᵢ yᵢ P / P°)νᵢ

K is the equilibrium constant at temperature T (dimensionless); φᵢ is the fugacity coefficient of species i in the mixture; yᵢ is its gas mole fraction; P is total pressure in bar; P° = 1 bar is standard pressure; νᵢ is the stoichiometric coefficient, positive for products and negative for reactants.

For an ideal gas every φᵢ = 1, giving K = Π(yᵢP/P°)νᵢ. The general form matters because φᵢ depends on T, P and mixture composition, not on species alone.

  1. Choose an equation of state: virial at moderate pressure, cubic at high pressure.
  2. At the trial composition compute every φᵢ.
  3. Evaluate Q = Π(φᵢ yᵢ P/P°)νᵢ and compare with K.
  4. Move composition along the extent, recompute φᵢ, repeat until Q = K.

The loop is unavoidable: φᵢ depends on the yᵢ you seek. At synthesis pressures, ignoring φᵢ can shift the predicted conversion noticeably, so design calculations keep it.

Heterogeneous systems: pure solids and liquids drop out

The standard state of a pure solid or pure liquid is the pure condensed phase at system T and P. Its chemical potential equals the standard value, so its activity is exactly 1, whatever the amount. It enters K as 1ν = 1. Only gases and dissolved species appear.

Limestone calcination, CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): both solids have activity 1, so K = aCO₂ = yCO₂P/P°. At fixed T, equilibrium CO₂ pressure is fixed, whether the kiln holds a gram or a tonne of lime. In a cement kiln, flue gas sweeps CO₂ away, keeps yCO₂ below that value, and decomposition continues.

Carbon behaves the same way: C(s) + CO₂(g) ⇌ 2CO(g) has K = (yCO P/P°)²/(yCO₂P/P°), since aC = 1. This Boudouard balance fixes gas composition in carburising furnaces and coal gasifiers.

K = Πᵢ (γᵢ xᵢ)νᵢ

for a reacting liquid mixture: γᵢ is the activity coefficient of species i, xᵢ its liquid mole fraction, νᵢ its stoichiometric coefficient; the product runs over liquid species. Used for esterification, hydrolysis and other liquid-phase syntheses.

Activity 1 belongs to a pure phase only. A solid in solid solution, or water acting as solvent in a concentrated solution, needs activity from its own standard state.

Several reactions at once

Real feeds crack, reform and shift together. Two reactions are independent when neither can be built from the other plus element balances. Write the atom matrix with elements as rows and species as columns; let C be its rank. Then r = N − C, where N is the number of species. Each independent reaction needs its own extent and K.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ

nᵢ is moles of species i at equilibrium; nᵢ₀ its initial moles; νᵢⱼ the coefficient of species i in reaction j, positive for products and negative for reactants; ξⱼ the extent of reaction j in mol. The sum runs over r independent reactions.

At equilibrium Qⱼ = Kⱼ for every j. The equations are coupled because all reactions share one total mole number and one set of mole fractions, so they are solved together.

  1. Write the stoichiometric matrix, find its rank, fix r.
  2. Express every nᵢ in the r extents with the mole balance.
  3. Write each Kⱼ as a function of ξ₁ … ξᵣ and pressure.
  4. Solve the r equations by Newton's method or successive substitution.
  5. Discard any root that makes a mole number negative.

The reacting phase rule counts what is free: F = N − π + 2 − r, with N species, π phases, r independent reactions and F degrees of freedom. Steam reforming with CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂ and CO + H₂O ⇌ CO₂ + H₂ has N = 5, π = 1 and r = 2, so F = 5 − 1 + 2 − 2 = 4: fixing T, P and the two independent element ratios fixes composition. Duhem's theorem says the same.

Worked example

Question. A feed of 1 mol pure A at 1 bar reacts in the gas phase by two independent reactions: A ⇌ B with K₁ = 2.00, and 2A ⇌ C with K₂ = 0.500. The gas is ideal and temperature is fixed. Find both extents and the equilibrium composition.

  1. Let ξ₁ mol of A ⇌ B and ξ₂ mol of 2A ⇌ C react. Then nA = 1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂, nB = ξ₁, nC = ξ₂, nT = 1 − ξ₂ mol.
  2. With P = P° = 1 bar and all φᵢ = 1: K₁ = yB/yA = ξ₁/(1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂) = 2.00.
  3. K₂ = yC/yA² = ξ₂(1 − ξ₂)/(1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂)² = 0.500, because nT cancels.
  4. Put D = 1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂. Then ξ₁ = K₁D and D = 1 − K₁D − 2ξ₂, so D = (1 − 2ξ₂)/(1 + K₁) = (1 − 2ξ₂)/3.
  5. Substitute in K₂: 0.500(1 − 2ξ₂)²/9 = ξ₂(1 − ξ₂), giving 1.2222ξ₂² − 1.2222ξ₂ + 0.05556 = 0.
  6. Root below 0.5: ξ₂ = (1.2222 − 1.1055)/2.4444 = 0.0477 mol. Then D = (1 − 0.0955)/3 = 0.3015 and ξ₁ = 2.00 × 0.3015 = 0.6030 mol.
  7. Moles: nA = 0.3015, nB = 0.6030, nC = 0.0477, nT = 0.9523 mol. Mole fractions: yA = 0.3166, yB = 0.6332, yC = 0.0501.

Exam traps

  • Putting a pure solid into K as a mole fraction. Its activity is 1, so CaCO₃ and CaO never appear in the calcination constant — only yCO₂P/P° does.

Takeaways

  1. A reacting mixture needs only stoichiometric coefficients and one extent variable per independent reaction to fix every mole number.
  2. At fixed T and P a closed reacting system stops changing when total Gibbs energy is minimum, which is the same statement as Σνᵢμᵢ = 0.
  3. The standard Gibbs energy change sets K through ΔG° = −RT ln K, and K itself depends only on temperature, never on pressure or initial composition.
  4. Van't Hoff's equation turns tabulated ΔH° data into K at any other temperature; endothermic reactions are favoured by heating, exothermic ones by cooling.
  5. K is a number, not a composition: converting it to conversions needs activities, mole balances and, for gases, the pressure and inert content.
  6. Q versus K decides feasibility at the current composition; equilibrium conversion and yield say how far the feed can actually go.
  7. Raising pressure helps only reactions that reduce the number of gas moles; inert dilution always works against them and is best removed by purge.
  8. Real problems stack nonideality, several phases and several reactions, so each reaction gets its own equilibrium relation over one shared set of mole balances.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to move from the Gibbs-minimisation criterion to the K expression, then apply van't Hoff, pressure and inert effects, and finish with numerical conversion or multireaction balances.

Understand Define the extent of reaction and show that a reacting mixture with N species and R independent reactions needs exactly R extent variables. How is R found from the atomic matrix? 10 marks

Points to cover. Write nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼνᵢⱼξⱼ for R reactions; State that each ξⱼ is zero at the start, positive when reaction j runs as written and negative when it runs backwards, bounded so that no nᵢ becomes negative; Build the element-by-species atomic matrix A and note that element balances conserve atoms for every ξ set; Independent reactions = N − rank(A); explain that dependent reactions add no new composition change; Give one worked count: steam reforming species CH₄, H₂O, CO, CO₂, H₂ (N = 5) contain C, H, O with rank 3, so R = 2.

Model answer.

  1. Extent. For one reaction written as Σνᵢ Aᵢ = 0, every mole number follows from one variable: nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ. ξ (mol) is the moles of reaction as written that have occurred; it is zero at the start, positive when the reaction runs forward and negative when it runs backwards, and bounded so that no nᵢ becomes negative.
  2. Several reactions. With R independent reactions, nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼνᵢⱼξⱼ. The vector of changes nᵢ − nᵢ₀ is a combination of the R reaction vectors νⱼ, so R extents describe every reachable composition.
  3. Why exactly R. Fewer than R extents cannot reach every composition the reactions allow. More than R adds a reaction vector that is a combination of the others: its extent can be absorbed into the others, so it adds no new composition change and makes the extents non-unique.
  4. Atom matrix. Write a matrix with one row per element and one column per species, each entry the number of atoms of that element in that species. Every reaction conserves atoms, so every reaction vector lies in the null space of this matrix. If its rank is rank(A), the null space has dimension N − rank(A), and R = N − rank(A).
  5. Worked count: steam reforming. Species CH₄, H₂O, CO, CO₂, H₂ (N = 5).
    ElementCH₄H₂OCOCO₂H₂
    C10110
    H42002

Understand Derive the equilibrium criterion Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 for a closed system held at fixed temperature and pressure, starting from dG. 12 marks

Points to cover. Write dG = −S dT + V dP + Σμᵢ dnᵢ; At fixed T and P the first two terms vanish, leaving dG = Σμᵢ dnᵢ; Substitute dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ to get dG = (Σνᵢμᵢ) dξ; At equilibrium G is minimum, so dG/dξ = 0, hence Σνᵢμᵢ = 0; second derivative positive for a stable minimum; Note the affinity A = −Σνᵢμᵢ drives the reaction and vanishes at equilibrium; Mention that at fixed T and V the Helmholtz energy is minimised instead; the criterion Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 is the same.

Model answer.

  1. Start. For a single phase, d(nG) = −(nS) dT + (nV) dP + Σμᵢ dnᵢ, where μᵢ = [∂(nG)/∂nᵢ] at fixed T, P and the other moles.
  2. Fixed T and P. With dT = 0 and dP = 0, d(nG) = Σμᵢ dnᵢ. For a closed system at fixed T and P, every spontaneous change lowers nG, so equilibrium is the state of minimum nG.
  3. Introduce the extent. In a closed system the moles change only by reaction: dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ. So d(nG) = (Σνᵢμᵢ) dξ and [∂(nG)/∂ξ]T,P = Σνᵢμᵢ = ΔGr, the reaction Gibbs energy.
  4. Criterion. At the minimum the slope is zero: Σνᵢμᵢ = 0. For stability the curvature is positive, ∂²(nG)/∂ξ² > 0. If Σνᵢμᵢ < 0 the forward reaction lowers G and proceeds; if > 0 the reverse does.
  5. Worked illustration (A ⇌ B, ideal gas, 500 K, 1 bar, ΔG° = −4.00 kJ/mol, 1 mol A fed, RT = 4157 J/mol):
    G − μA° = ξΔG° + RT[(1 − ξ)ln(1 − ξ) + ξ ln ξ]
dG/dξ   = ΔG° + RT ln[ξ/(1 − ξ)]
ξ = 0.25: −4000 − 4567 = −8567 J/mol  (forward)
ξ = 0.90: −4000 + 9134 = +5134 J/mol  (reverse)
minimum: ξ/(1 − ξ) = e0.9622 = 2.617 → ξ = 0.724 mol, G − μA° = −5345 J
  6. Affinity. A = −Σνᵢμᵢ is the driving force; it is positive while the forward reaction can proceed and zero at equilibrium.
  7. Link to K. Writing μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ turns Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 into ΔG° + RT ln Π aᵢνᵢ = 0, i.e. ΔG° = −RT ln K. In the example K = e0.9622 = 2.617 = yB/yA, consistent with ξ = 0.724.
  8. Other constraints. At fixed T and V the Helmholtz energy is minimised instead; since μᵢ is the same partial derivative, the criterion Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 is unchanged.
Remember Define standard state and activity, and derive K = Π aᵢνᵢ from μᵢ = G°ᵢ + RT ln aᵢ. 10 marks

Points to cover. Define standard state for gas, liquid and solid; aᵢ = 1 there; Write μᵢ = G°ᵢ + RT ln aᵢ and substitute into Σνᵢμᵢ = 0; Collect ΣνᵢG°ᵢ = ΔG° and Σνᵢ ln aᵢ = ln Π aᵢνᵢ; Obtain ΔG° = −RT ln K with K = Π aᵢνᵢ at equilibrium; Point out K is dimensionless and depends on T only, not on P or feed.

Model answer.

  1. Standard state. A chosen reference condition of each species at the system temperature: for a gas, the pure ideal gas at P° = 1 bar; for a liquid, the pure liquid at T (and 1 bar or the system pressure); for a solid, the pure solid. The standard chemical potential is G°ᵢ = μᵢ°.
  2. Activity. Defined by μᵢ = G°ᵢ + RT ln aᵢ, so aᵢ = f̂ᵢ/fᵢ°. In the standard state aᵢ = 1. Gas: aᵢ = φ̂ᵢyᵢP/P°. Liquid: aᵢ = γᵢxᵢ. Pure solid or liquid: aᵢ = 1.
  3. Substitute into the criterion. Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 becomes ΣνᵢG°ᵢ + RT Σνᵢ ln aᵢ = 0.
  4. Collect terms. ΣνᵢG°ᵢ = ΔG°, the standard Gibbs energy change, and Σνᵢ ln aᵢ = ln Π aᵢνᵢ. Hence ΔG° = −RT ln Π aᵢνᵢ.
  5. Define K. K = Π aᵢνᵢ (at equilibrium) = exp(−ΔG°/RT). ΔG° depends only on T (for fixed standard states), so K depends only on T, not on pressure or the feed.
  6. Worked numbers: ammonia at 298.15 K (ΔGf°(NH₃, g) = −16.45 kJ/mol):
    ΔG° = 2(−16.45) − 0 − 3(0) = −32.90 kJ/mol
ln K = 32 900/(8.314 × 298.15) = 13.272
K = 5.81 × 10⁵   (per mole NH₃: K′ = √K = 762)
  7. Dimensionless K. Each activity is a ratio (f̂ᵢ/P°, or γᵢxᵢ), so K has no units. A Kp built from partial pressures in bar equals K only because P° = 1 bar; in other units it carries (P°)Σν.
  8. Consistency. Changing a standard state changes G°ᵢ and therefore ΔG° and K, but the matching activities change too, so the predicted equilibrium composition is the same.

Apply For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, K = 5.81 × 10⁵ at 298.15 K and ΔH°₂₉₈ = −92.22 kJ/mol. Using van't Hoff's equation, obtain K at 500 K for (a) constant ΔH° and (b) ΔH° varying linearly with temperature, taking ΔCp° = −38.3 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ as constant. 12 marks

Points to cover. Identify two independent reactions; check R = N − rank(A) with species CO, H₂O, CO₂, H₂, CH₄; Introduce ξ₁ and ξ₂; write every nᵢ and total moles; Write two equilibrium relations K₁ = Π aᵢνᵢ¹ and K₂ = Π aᵢνᵢ² at the same T; Add carbon, hydrogen and oxygen balances; show only two are independent; Solve the coupled nonlinear equations in ξ₁, ξ₂ numerically; compare with Gibbs minimisation; Note Σν = 0 for the shift reaction, so pressure leaves that one unaffected.

Model answer.

  1. Species and reactions. CO, H₂O, CO₂, H₂ and CH₄ (N = 5) built from C, H and O. The atom matrix has rank 3, so R = 5 − 3 = 2 independent reactions: (1) CO + H₂O ⇌ CO₂ + H₂ and (2) CO + 3H₂ ⇌ CH₄ + H₂O.

    CO  = 1 − ξ₁ − ξ₂
H₂O = 2 − ξ₁ + ξ₂
CO₂ = ξ₁
H₂  = ξ₁ − 3ξ₂
CH₄ = ξ₂
nT = 3 − 2ξ₂

Analyze A gas mixture of 20 mol % NH₃, 20 mol % N₂ and 60 mol % H₂ is held at 500 K and 100 bar, where K = 0.100 for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Explain how you decide whether the forward reaction, the reverse reaction or nothing happens, and apply it. 12 marks

Points to cover. Compute the reaction quotient Q = Π aᵢνᵢ from the actual composition; Compare Q with K: Q < K forward feasible, Q > K reverse feasible, Q = K at equilibrium; Relate the sign of ΔG = RT ln(Q/K) to the direction; negative ΔG means the forward direction is spontaneous; State that K depends only on T, so temperature decides how far the system can go while Q sets the starting point; Mention that a large negative ΔG° does not fix the conversion — thermodynamics and kinetics are separate questions.

Model answer.

  1. Reaction quotient. Q = Π aᵢνᵢ evaluated at the actual composition; for ideal gases aᵢ = yᵢP/P°.
  2. Test. ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K). Q < K: ΔG < 0, forward. Q > K: ΔG > 0, reverse. Q = K: equilibrium, nothing happens.
  3. Apply (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, 500 K, 100 bar, K = 0.100):
    Q = [yNH₃²/(yN₂ yH₂³)](P/P°)⁻²
  = [0.20²/(0.20 × 0.60³)] × 100⁻²
  = (0.04/0.0432) × 10⁻⁴ = 9.26 × 10⁻⁵
Q/K = 9.26 × 10⁻⁴
ΔG = 8.314 × 500 × ln(9.26 × 10⁻⁴) = −29.0 kJ/mol
  4. Conclusion. Q is about a thousand times smaller than K, so ΔG = −29.0 kJ per mole of reaction and the forward reaction (more ammonia) is spontaneous. The mixture would move toward the equilibrium of the previous answers, with yNH₃ near 0.7 at this T and P.
  5. K versus Q. K depends only on T and sets where the mixture can end; Q depends on the present composition and pressure and sets the starting point. Changing P changes Q (through (P/P°)Σν) but not K.
  6. Thermodynamics is not kinetics. A negative ΔG says the reaction can go, not how fast. At 500 K ammonia synthesis is very slow even on a catalyst, which is why plants accept a lower K at 650–750 K to get a usable rate.
Evaluate Discuss the choice between Kp, Ky and Ka for gas-phase equilibria, and assess when fugacity coefficients can be ignored. 13 marks

Points to cover. Define f̂ᵢ = yᵢφᵢP and K = Π (f̂ᵢ/fᵢ°)νᵢ; At low pressure φᵢ → 1 so K ≈ Kp = Π (yᵢP/P°)νᵢ; Write K = Ky Kφ (P/P°)Σν to separate composition, nonideality and pressure; Judging rule: ignore φᵢ near 1 bar; at 100 bar and above use an equation of state or generalised charts; Example: ammonia synthesis at 200–300 bar has φᵢ far from unity; Conclusion: Ka is the rigorous constant; Kp and Ky are approximations valid in stated limits.

Model answer.

  1. Rigorous constant. K = Π(f̂ᵢ/fᵢ°)νᵢ with f̂ᵢ = φ̂ᵢyᵢP and fᵢ° = P° = 1 bar for every gas. This is Ka, the activity-based constant fixed by ΔG°.
  2. Splitting it. K = Ky Kφ (P/P°)Σν, where Ky = Πyᵢνᵢ carries the composition, Kφ = Πφ̂ᵢνᵢ the non-ideality and (P/P°)Σν the pressure.
  3. Kp. Kp = Π(yᵢP)νᵢ in pressure units equals Ky PΣν; it equals K numerically only for ideal gases with pressures in bar (P° = 1 bar). It is convenient at low pressure.
  4. Ky. Not a constant: at fixed T it changes with P (unless Σν = 0) and with non-ideality. It is the quantity actually solved for once K, P and Kφ are known.
  5. When φ can be ignored. Near 1 bar and for gases well above their critical temperatures at moderate pressure, φ̂ᵢ ≈ 1 and Kφ ≈ 1. At 100 bar and above, or near the dew point, compute φ̂ᵢ from a virial or cubic equation of state.
  6. Example. Ammonia at 500 K and 100 bar: the ideal-gas answer is 84.6 % conversion. At these conditions NH₃ has φ well below 1 while H₂ is slightly above 1, so Kφ < 1 and Ky = K/[Kφ(P/P°)Σν] is larger: the real conversion is somewhat higher than the ideal estimate. Ignoring Kφ in a 200–300 bar loop gives a noticeable error.
  7. Conclusion. Use K (Ka) as the defining constant; treat Kp as its low-pressure form and Ky as the derived composition product.
Evaluate For a heterogeneous reaction such as CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), derive the equilibrium relation and explain why solids drop out of K. 12 marks

Points to cover. Write K = aCO₂ aCaO / aCaCO₃ with each pure solid at unit activity; A pure solid at the system T and P is in its standard state (pressure effect negligible), so its activity is 1 and K = aCO₂ = f̂CO₂/P° ≈ pCO₂/P°; Hence the decomposition pressure is fixed by T alone, giving K = pCO₂/P° and pCO₂ = K P°; Show that adding or removing solid changes nothing at equilibrium as long as both phases persist; Distinguish cases where a solid is consumed completely, where the activity term no longer applies; Link to calcination temperature in lime kilns and to the phase rule for reacting systems.

Model answer.

  1. Equilibrium relation. CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): K = aCaO aCO₂/aCaCO₃.
  2. Why solids drop out. Each solid is a separate pure phase. Its standard state is the pure solid at T (and 1 bar); at the system pressure its chemical potential differs from μ° only by ∫V dP, which is negligible for a solid, so a = 1 whatever the amount. Hence K = aCO₂ = f̂CO₂/P° ≈ pCO₂/P°.
  3. Decomposition pressure. At each T, pCO₂ = KP° is fixed: the equilibrium CO₂ pressure over limestone. If the surrounding CO₂ pressure is below it, the solid decomposes; above it, CaO takes up CO₂.
  4. Amounts do not matter. Adding more CaCO₃ or CaO changes nothing as long as both solids are present, because neither appears in K. If one solid is used up, it is no longer a phase and the relation K = pCO₂/P° no longer applies; pCO₂ is then set by the material balance.
  5. Phase rule. N = 3 species, π = 3 phases, r = 1: F = 3 − 3 + 2 − 1 = 1. T alone fixes pCO₂, like a vapour-pressure curve.
  6. From data. ln K = −ΔG°/RT with ΔG° = ΔGf°(CaO) + ΔGf°(CO₂) − ΔGf°(CaCO₃) at T. The decomposition is strongly endothermic (ΔH° about +178 kJ/mol), so K rises steeply with T; pCO₂ reaches 1 bar at roughly 1170 K, which is why lime kilns run near 900 °C or above and sweep CO₂ away.
  7. Other examples. C(s) + CO₂ ⇌ 2CO: K = (yCOP/P°)²/(yCO₂P/P°); metal oxide reduction by H₂ or CO is handled the same way.
Analyze Compare the two routes to multireaction equilibrium — solving R equations Σνᵢⱼμᵢ = 0 with element balances, and direct Gibbs energy minimisation — and assess their merits. 15 marks

Points to cover. Route 1: choose R independent reactions, write R equilibrium constants plus element balances, solve for R extents; Route 2: minimise G = Σnᵢμᵢ(nᵢ) subject to element balances, using Lagrange multipliers; Show both use the same μᵢ model and give the same answer; Advantage of minimisation: no need to guess a reaction set; danger of local minima and numerical stiffness; Advantage of K-equations: physically transparent, easier to start, but needs known independent reactions; Mention extent bounds nᵢ ≥ 0 and that nonnegativity constraints matter in practical solvers.

Model answer.

  1. Route 1: reaction equations. Choose R = N − rank(A) independent reactions, write nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σνᵢⱼξⱼ, and solve the R equations Kⱼ = Π aᵢνᵢⱼ for the R extents. The element balances are satisfied automatically.
  2. Route 2: Gibbs minimisation. Minimise G = Σnᵢμᵢ(T, P, n) over all nᵢ ≥ 0 subject to the element balances Σnᵢaik = Ak. With Lagrange multipliers λk: μᵢ + Σk λk aik = 0 for each species, i.e. μᵢ/RT = ΔGf,i°/RT + ln(yᵢP/P°) = −Σλk aik/RT for ideal gases.
  3. Same answer. Both use the same μᵢ model and the same conservation laws; at the solution of route 2, Σνᵢⱼμᵢ = 0 holds for any reaction vector, which is route 1.
  4. Comparison.

  5. Example. For A ⇌ B (K₁ = 2.00) and 2A ⇌ C (K₂ = 0.500) at 1 bar from 1 mol A, route 1 gives ξ₁ = 0.603, ξ₂ = 0.0477 and yA = 0.317, yB = 0.633, yC = 0.050; minimising G over A, B and C with one balance on A-units gives the same.
  6. Practice. Hand calculations and small systems use route 1; reactor simulators (Gibbs reactors) use route 2 with bounds nᵢ ≥ 0, which avoids guessing reactions and handles solids and phase splits.
Evaluate Assess how temperature, pressure and inert level together set the equilibrium conversion in an industrial gas reaction with Σν < 0 and ΔH° < 0. 14 marks

Points to cover. ΔH° < 0 means K falls as T rises, so low T favours conversion but slows kinetics; Σν < 0 means high P favours conversion through Ky = K(P/P°)−Σν; Inerts dilute the mixture and cut conversion, so a purge stream is needed; Optimal design therefore compromises with a moderate T, high P and low inert level; Write the general K = Ky Kφ (P/P°)Σν relation and show each lever's role; Note that reaction kinetics, catalyst life and compression cost set the practical limits.

Model answer.

  1. Temperature. ΔH° < 0, so K falls as T rises (van't Hoff). For ammonia synthesis K drops from 5.81 × 10⁵ at 298 K to 0.100 at 500 K and 8.56 × 10⁻⁵ at 700 K. Low T favours conversion, but rates are too slow there, so a catalyst and a compromise temperature (about 650–750 K) are used.
  2. Pressure. Σν < 0, so Ky = K(P/P°)−Σν rises with P. At 500 K a stoichiometric feed converts 15.8 % of the N₂ at 1 bar but 84.6 % at 100 bar; at 700 K, 200 bar still gives 45.8 %.
  3. Inerts. They dilute the reactants and lower conversion for Σν < 0: 10 mol % argon at 500 K and 100 bar lowers the conversion from 84.6 % to 83.2 %. In a recycle loop inerts accumulate, so a purge is needed.
  4. All levers together. K = Ky Kφ (P/P°)Σν: T sets K; P enters through (P/P°)Σν; inerts change Ky through nT; non-ideality enters through Kφ.
  5. Summary.

    fewer inertshigher y of reactantspurge loss of reactants
    remove productQ kept below Kseparation and recycle cost
  6. Design. The result is a moderate temperature with a good catalyst, high pressure, product removal by condensation and recycle, and a controlled purge; kinetics, catalyst life and compression cost set the final choice.
Analyze State the phase rule and Duhem's theorem for reacting systems and explain what they allow you to fix when designing an equilibrium calculation. 10 marks

Points to cover. Degrees of freedom F = N − π + 2 − r for N species, π phases and r independent reactions; Explain why each independent reaction removes one degree of freedom: it adds a relation Σνᵢμᵢ = 0; Duhem's theorem: for a closed system made from given amounts, fixing two independent variables fixes the whole equilibrium state, reacting or not; when F = 1 one must be extensive; Consequence: composition at equilibrium follows from T and P plus feed and element balances; Example: CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g) has N = 3, π = 3, r = 1, so F = 1: T alone fixes the CO₂ pressure, and the amounts of the solids need an extensive specification.

Model answer.

  1. Reacting phase rule. F = N − π + 2 − r, with N chemical species, π phases and r independent reactions (plus any special constraint s, subtracted the same way).
  2. Why each reaction removes one freedom. The non-reacting count is F = N − π + 2. Each independent reaction adds one equation, Σνᵢⱼμᵢ = 0, among the same intensive variables, so it removes one degree of freedom.
  3. Duhem's theorem. For a closed system made from given amounts of species, the equilibrium state, intensive and extensive, is fixed by any two independent variables, whether or not reactions occur. When F ≥ 2 both may be intensive (T and P); when F = 1 one must be extensive.
  4. Examples.
    SystemNπrF
    ammonia synthesis gas3113
    steam reforming gas5124
    CaCO₃(s), CaO(s), CO₂(g)3311
  5. Reading F. For ammonia gas, F = 3: T, P and one composition variable, such as the N₂:H₂ ratio fixed by the feed. For steam reforming, T, P and two element ratios (for example C:H and O:H in the feed). For calcination, T alone fixes pCO₂.
  6. Design use. A single-phase reactor fed a known mixture: fixing T and P fixes the equilibrium composition (Duhem), which is how the K calculations are posed. For the three-phase calcination, T fixes pCO₂, and the extent of decomposition needs an extensive specification such as the amount of CO₂ removed.

Glossary

Activity
The dimensionless effective amount of a species relative to its standard state; equals 1 for a pure liquid or solid at 1 bar.
Affinity
A = −Σνᵢμᵢ in J/mol; positive when the forward reaction is spontaneous, zero at equilibrium.
Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs energy of a species, equal to the change in G when one mole is added at fixed T, P and other amounts.
Duhem's theorem
Fixing any two independent intensive properties of a closed system fixes all its remaining intensive properties.
Endothermic reaction
A reaction with ΔH° > 0; its equilibrium constant rises as temperature rises.
Equilibrium
The state in which no further net change of composition occurs at fixed temperature and pressure.
Equilibrium constant
The temperature-dependent number K = Π aᵢνᵢ evaluated at equilibrium; related to ΔG° by ΔG° = −RT ln K.
Equilibrium conversion
The fraction of the fed limiting reactant that has reacted when equilibrium is reached.
Exothermic reaction
A reaction with ΔH° < 0; its equilibrium constant falls as temperature rises.
Fugacity
An effective pressure that makes the ideal-gas chemical potential formula work for real gases.
Gibbs energy minimisation
The numerical method of finding equilibrium composition by minimising total G subject to element balances.
Heterogeneous reaction
A reaction involving more than one phase, so each pure condensed phase contributes unit activity.
Independent reactions
A minimal set of reactions whose extents can describe every possible composition change of the system.
Inert
A species present in the mixture that takes no part in the reaction but adds to the total moles.
Kp
The equilibrium constant expressed in partial pressures, Kp = Π(pᵢ/p°)νᵢ, valid for ideal gases.
Ky
The equilibrium constant expressed in mole fractions, Ky = Π yᵢνᵢ; carries all the composition dependence for gases.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that runs out first and therefore caps the maximum possible conversion.
Phase rule for reacting systems
F = N − π + 2 − r, where C counts components, P counts phases and R counts independent reactions.
Reaction extent
A single variable ξ in mol that measures how far a reaction has run; every mole number follows from nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ.
Reaction quotient
Q = Π aᵢνᵢ at the actual composition; comparison with K tells the direction of change.
Selectivity
The fraction of reacted feed that ends up as the desired product rather than a by-product.
Standard Gibbs energy of formation
ΔG°f of a compound: the change in Gibbs energy when one mole forms from elements in their standard states.
Standard state
The agreed reference state, at unit activity, against which Gibbs energies and activities are measured.
Stoichiometry
The fixed molar ratios in which species appear in a balanced chemical equation.
