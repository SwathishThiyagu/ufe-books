7.1 Reaction stoichiometry and extent of reaction

A reacting mixture changes composition as reactants turn into products. Instead of tracking every species separately, write the reaction as ΣνᵢAᵢ = 0, with negative coefficients for reactants and positive for products. Then one variable, the extent ξ, fixes every mole number through nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ. This bookkeeping is the base for equilibrium calculations in 7.2, reactor design, and material balances in process plants.

You need: Unit 1 mole balances and Unit 2 heat of reaction (see Unit 2, PVT Behaviour and Equations of State).

Stoichiometric coefficients carry a sign

A reaction such as N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3 gives the ratio in which molecules combine. To use it in a balance, move every species to one side: 0 = −N2 − 3H2 + 2NH3.

The number in front of each species is its stoichiometric coefficient νi. It is dimensionless. By convention νi is negative for a reactant, positive for a product, and zero for an inert.

Σᵢ νᵢ Aᵢ = 0

Aᵢ is the formula of species i and νᵢ its stoichiometric coefficient; the sum runs over every species in the mixture.

The sign convention turns the reaction into one linear equation. If species i is consumed, νᵢ < 0, so its moles fall as ξ grows; if it is formed, νᵢ > 0, so its moles rise.

Scaling the reaction multiplies every νᵢ. Writing 2N2 + 6H2 → 4NH3 doubles all coefficients. The physical reaction is the same, but the numerical value of ξ for a given change halves. Always state the reaction as written.

Extent of reaction ξ

One number tracks how far the reaction has gone. The extent of reaction ξ (unit: mol) is the moles of reaction as written that have occurred. Some syllabi call it the reaction coordinate.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢ ξ

nᵢ is the moles of species i at extent ξ (mol), nᵢ₀ is its initial moles (mol), νᵢ is its stoichiometric coefficient, and ξ is the extent (mol).

At the start ξ = 0. ξ is positive when the reaction proceeds as written and negative when it runs in reverse. The only firm limits are that no nᵢ may become negative. In the forward direction, a reactant needs ξ ≤ nᵢ₀/(−νᵢ), and a product that starts at zero needs ξ ≥ 0.

Adding the species balances gives the total moles:

n_T = n_T₀ + ξ Σᵢ νᵢ

n_T is the total moles at extent ξ (mol), n_T₀ is the initial total moles (mol), and Σᵢνᵢ is the sum of all stoichiometric coefficients.

For ammonia, Σν = −1 − 3 + 2 = −2, so total moles fall by 2 mol for each mole of reaction. The figure is the mole table for this case.

Several reactions, limiting reactant and conversion

A real feed may undergo more than one independent reaction, for example steam reforming followed by water-gas shift. Number them j = 1, ..., r. Each reaction has its own extent ξⱼ and its own coefficient νᵢⱼ for species i.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ

νᵢⱼ is the coefficient of species i in reaction j, ξⱼ is the extent of reaction j (mol), and the sum runs over r independent reactions.

Independent means no reaction in the set is a linear combination of the others. The count r equals the rank of the stoichiometric matrix. Using one ξ for two independent reactions is wrong; the mole balances cannot be satisfied.

The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. For one reaction, its maximum extent is ξmax = min over reactants of nᵢ₀/(−νᵢ).

For a single reaction, conversion of species i is the fraction of its initial moles that has reacted:

Xᵢ = (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀ = −νᵢ ξ/nᵢ₀

Xᵢ is the fractional conversion of species i (dimensionless), nᵢ₀ its initial moles (mol), nᵢ its moles at extent ξ, and νᵢ its coefficient.

With several reactions, Xᵢ = (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀ = −(Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ)/nᵢ₀.

An inert has νᵢ = 0, so nᵢ = nᵢ₀ at every extent. It still contributes to n_T. Equilibrium conversion, where ξ stops changing, belongs to 7.6.

Worked example

Question. A feed of 10 mol N₂ and 30 mol H₂ enters an ammonia reactor. The reaction is N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. At extent ξ = 4 mol, find n_N₂, n_H₂, n_NH₃ and the total moles n_T.

  1. Write the coefficients with the sign convention: ν(N₂) = −1, ν(H₂) = −3, ν(NH₃) = +2.
  2. Apply nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ to N₂: n_N₂ = 10 mol + (−1)(4 mol) = 10 − 4 = 6 mol.
  3. For H₂: n_H₂ = 30 mol + (−3)(4 mol) = 30 − 12 = 18 mol.
  4. For NH₃: n_NH₃ = 0 mol + (+2)(4 mol) = 0 + 8 = 8 mol.
  5. Add the species: n_T = 6 + 18 + 8 = 32 mol.
  6. Check with total moles: Σν = −1 − 3 + 2 = −2; n_T = n_T₀ + ξΣν = 40 mol + (4 mol)(−2) = 40 − 8 = 32 mol.

Answer. n_N₂ = 6 mol, n_H₂ = 18 mol, n_NH₃ = 8 mol, and n_T = 32 mol.

Exam traps

  • Using one extent ξ for two independent reactions. Each independent reaction needs its own ξⱼ; otherwise the mole balances conflict.
  • Taking conversion as ξ divided by total moles. Conversion is (nᵢ₀ − nᵢ)/nᵢ₀, that is ξ scaled by |νᵢ| and divided by the initial moles of that species.
  • Dropping the sign of νᵢ. A reactant has νᵢ < 0; a positive νᵢ with positive ξ increases that species.
  • Forgetting inerts. They have νᵢ = 0, stay constant, but appear in n_T and in n_T₀.
  • Doubling every coefficient without changing ξ. The reaction is scaled, so ξ must halve for the same physical change.

7.2 Equilibrium criteria for chemical reactions

At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed reacting mixture moves to the composition with the lowest total Gibbs energy. This is the chemical equilibrium criterion: no further change lowers G. The same idea decides whether a reaction goes forward, backward, or stands still, and it works for one reaction or several. Process engineers use it to check feasibility before building a reactor, to set operating temperature and pressure, and to predict equilibrium conversion. The key quantity is the reaction Gibbs energy, built from the chemical potentials of every species.

You need: Unit 4 Gibbs energy and Unit 5 chemical potential; Chapter 7.1 for extent ξ and stoichiometric coefficients.

Total Gibbs energy of a reacting mixture

For a closed mixture at fixed temperature T and pressure P, the total Gibbs energy is G = Σ nᵢ μᵢ. Here nᵢ is the number of moles of species i (mol) and μᵢ is its chemical potential (kJ/mol). The chemical potential μᵢ is the partial molar Gibbs energy of species i: μᵢ = (∂G/∂nᵢ) at fixed T, P and all other amounts. It equals the molar Gibbs energy of pure i only when i is pure.

At fixed T and P, the change in G when the amounts change is dG = Σ μᵢ dnᵢ. This comes from dG = −S dT + V dP + Σ μᵢ dnᵢ with dT = 0 and dP = 0.

Describe composition by the extent ξ (mol). From Chapter 7.1, dnᵢ = νᵢ dξ. The stoichiometric coefficient νᵢ is negative for a reactant and positive for a product. Substituting gives dG = (Σ νᵢ μᵢ) dξ.

Define the reaction Gibbs energy ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ. Then dG/dξ = ΔGr, and the affinity is A = −ΔGr. The slope of G against ξ is ΔGr. A negative slope means G falls as ξ increases, so the forward reaction is favoured. A positive slope means the reverse reaction is favoured.

G = Σ nᵢ μᵢ,  dG = Σ μᵢ dnᵢ = (Σ νᵢ μᵢ) dξ,  ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ

G is total Gibbs energy (kJ); nᵢ is moles of species i (mol); μᵢ is chemical potential (kJ/mol); ξ is extent (mol); νᵢ is stoichiometric coefficient (negative for reactants, positive for products); ΔGr is reaction Gibbs energy (kJ/mol).

Minimum and the equilibrium condition

At fixed T and P, equilibrium is the state of minimum total Gibbs energy. At a minimum the first derivative is zero, so dG/dξ = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0. This equation is the equilibrium criterion for a chemical reaction. Some syllabi call Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0 the chemical equilibrium criterion.

For a stable minimum the second derivative is positive: d²G/dξ² > 0. A zero first derivative alone does not prove equilibrium: the point could be a maximum or an inflection. d²G/dξ² > 0 identifies the stable minimum.

The sign of ΔGr gives direction. If ΔGr < 0, then dG/dξ < 0 and G decreases as ξ increases: the forward reaction proceeds. If ΔGr > 0, then dG/dξ > 0 and G decreases as ξ decreases: the reverse reaction proceeds. If ΔGr = 0, the system is at equilibrium. The affinity A = −ΔGr is positive for a forward tendency.

Do not set ΔG° = 0 at equilibrium. ΔG° is the standard Gibbs energy change, a fixed value at a chosen standard state. ΔGr uses the actual chemical potentials and changes with composition. Equilibrium requires ΔGr = 0, not ΔG° = 0. The link from ΔG° to the equilibrium constant is in Chapter 7.3.

Heterogeneous reactions and degrees of freedom

For a heterogeneous reaction, the same species may appear in more than one phase. At equilibrium its chemical potential must be equal in every phase where it appears: μᵢ^α = μᵢ^β for phases α and β. This is the phase equilibrium condition from Unit 6.

Write one reaction equilibrium equation Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0 for each independent reaction. Use the chemical potential of each species in any phase where it is present; equality of μ makes the choice irrelevant. Do not write a separate reaction equation for each phase for the same reaction.

Let N be the number of species, π the number of phases, and r the number of independent reactions. The reacting phase rule is F = N − π + 2 − r. F is the number of intensive degrees of freedom. Each independent reaction adds one equilibrium relation and removes one freedom.

Duhem's theorem applies with reactions. For specified initial amounts, fixing two intensive variables fixes every intensive variable of the equilibrium state, including the amount of each phase. In a reactor, fixing T and P therefore fixes the equilibrium composition and the size of each phase.

μᵢ^α = μᵢ^β, Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0, F = N − π + 2 − r

μᵢ^α is chemical potential of species i in phase α (kJ/mol); N is number of species; π is number of phases; r is number of independent reactions; F is degrees of freedom.

Worked example

Question. At 500 K and 1 bar, for A(g) → B(g), a trial mixture has μA = −12.0 kJ/mol and μB = −20.0 kJ/mol. Find ΔGr and state the direction in which the reaction proceeds.

  1. Write coefficients for A → B: νA = −1 and νB = +1.
  2. Use ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = νA μA + νB μB.
  3. Substitute: ΔGr = (−1)(−12.0) + (+1)(−20.0) = +12.0 − 20.0 = −8.0 kJ/mol.
  4. Negative ΔGr means dG/dξ < 0 at fixed T and P. G falls as ξ increases, so the forward reaction A → B proceeds.

Answer. ΔGr = −8.0 kJ/mol; the reaction proceeds forward from A to B until ΔGr = 0 at equilibrium.

Exam traps

  • Setting ΔG° = 0 at equilibrium. ΔG° is fixed by standard states; the equilibrium condition is ΔGr = Σ νᵢ μᵢ = 0.
  • Using ΔGr with standard chemical potentials only. Equilibrium uses actual μ at the mixture composition.
  • Reversing the sign convention for νᵢ. Reactants are negative and products positive; a sign slip flips ΔGr and the predicted direction.
  • For a heterogeneous reaction, writing one equilibrium equation per phase for the same reaction. Use one equation per independent reaction, plus μ equality for each species across phases.
  • Treating a zero first derivative as sufficient. A maximum or an inflection also has zero slope; stable equilibrium needs d²G/dξ² > 0.

7.3 Standard Gibbs energy change and the equilibrium constant

At fixed temperature and pressure, a reacting mixture moves toward Σνᵢμᵢ = 0. The standard Gibbs energy change removes the composition part and leaves a number fixed by temperature, the equilibrium constant K. Engineers use K to screen reactions before building a reactor: ammonia synthesis, methanol from syngas, SO₂ oxidation. This chapter connects standard formation data to K, and then to the activities that describe real gases and liquids.

You need: 7.1 for νᵢ and extent, 7.2 for Σνᵢμᵢ = 0, and Unit 5 for fugacity, activity and standard states.

Standard states and ΔG°

At equilibrium, 7.2 gives Σνᵢμᵢ = 0. μᵢ is the chemical potential of species i in the actual mixture. To get a number that depends only on temperature, split μᵢ into a standard part and a composition part. The standard chemical potential μᵢ° is the chemical potential of pure i in its standard state at reaction temperature T. For a gas the standard state is ideal gas at P° = 1 bar. For a liquid it is the pure liquid at 1 bar. Tables of ΔGf° use these conventions, so always record the standard state.

ΔG° = Σ νᵢ μᵢ° = Σ νᵢ ΔGf,i°

ΔG° is standard Gibbs energy change of reaction, J mol⁻¹; νᵢ is stoichiometric number, positive for products and negative for reactants; μᵢ° is standard chemical potential, J mol⁻¹; ΔGf,i° is standard Gibbs energy of formation, J mol⁻¹.

Formation data are the practical route. ΔGf° of an element in its stable standard state is zero. For A ⇌ 2B, ΔG° = 2μB° − μA° = 2ΔGf,B° − ΔGf,A°. Stoichiometric numbers carry the signs: positive for products, negative for reactants. No separate product-minus-reactant rule is needed.

Most tables are at 298.15 K. At another T, use ΔG° for that T; 7.4 gives the temperature correction.

Activity and the equilibrium constant

Activity is the composition variable that makes the chemical potential linear in a logarithm. Define aᵢ by μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ. Then aᵢ = exp[(μᵢ − μᵢ°)/(RT)].

K = Π aᵢνᵢ = exp(−ΔG°/(RT))

K is the equilibrium constant, dimensionless; aᵢ is activity, dimensionless; νᵢ is stoichiometric number; ΔG° is standard Gibbs energy change, J mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature, K.

For gases, aᵢ = fᵢ/fᵢ° ≈ φᵢ yᵢ P/P°. fᵢ is fugacity, fᵢ° is standard fugacity (equal to P° for ideal gas), φᵢ is fugacity coefficient, yᵢ is mole fraction, P is total pressure. For an ideal gas, φᵢ = 1 and aᵢ = yᵢP/P°. For a liquid with pure-liquid standard state, aᵢ = γᵢ xᵢ; γᵢ is activity coefficient and xᵢ is mole fraction. An ideal solution has γᵢ = 1.

K is dimensionless because every activity is a ratio to a standard state. The pair (standard state, K) must be consistent. Change the standard state and μᵢ° changes; ΔG° and K change with it. The physical equilibrium composition does not change when the matching activities are used.

K in mole fractions and concentrations

For an ideal-gas mixture, substitute aᵢ = yᵢP/P° into K. The pressure factors collect into one power.

K = Ky (P/P°)Σν, Ky = Π yᵢνᵢ

Ky is the mole-fraction product, dimensionless; yᵢ is mole fraction; P is total pressure, Pa or bar; P° is standard pressure, same unit; Σν = Σνᵢ is the sum of stoichiometric numbers.

Example: A ⇌ 2B has Σν = 2 − 1 = +1, so K = Ky(P/P°). Increasing P raises P/P° and lowers Ky at fixed K. That is the pressure effect on composition treated in 7.7.

Concentration form: for ideal gas pᵢ = cᵢRT and aᵢ = cᵢRT/P°. Substitution gives K = (RT/P°)Σν Kc, where Kc = Π cᵢνᵢ, cᵢ is concentration in mol m⁻³, R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, and P° is in Pa. Kc has units unless Σν = 0; K remains dimensionless.

  • Reversing a reaction replaces K by 1/K.
  • Doubling every stoichiometric coefficient replaces K by K².
  • Adding two reactions multiplies their K values.

Worked example

Question. For the gas reaction A(g) ⇌ 2B(g) at 400 K, ΔG° = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Calculate K. State whether reactants or products are favoured when every activity equals 1.

  1. Convert ΔG° to J mol⁻¹: ΔG° = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ = 5000 J mol⁻¹.
  2. Compute RT = 8.314 × 400 = 3325.6 J mol⁻¹.
  3. Apply ln K = −ΔG°/(RT) = −5000/3325.6 = −1.503.
  4. Exponentiate: K = e^−1.503 = 0.222.
  5. When every activity equals 1, Q = 1. ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = +5.0 kJ mol⁻¹ > 0, so the reverse direction is spontaneous and reactants are favoured.

Answer. K = 0.222 (dimensionless); at unit activities reactants are favoured.

Exam traps

  • Writing K as a product of partial pressures in bar and calling it dimensionless. Divide each pᵢ by P° first; a bar-based Kp is not K.
  • Using actual ΔG in K = exp(−ΔG/RT). At equilibrium ΔG = 0, so only ΔG° belongs in that formula.
  • Forgetting the (P/P°) power Σν. For A ⇌ 2B the exponent is +1; for the reverse it is −1.
  • Mixing standard states in one K. Gas, liquid and solute activities must each use the standard state that matches their μ°.
  • Sign slip: ΔG° negative gives K > 1; ΔG° positive gives K < 1. Check with ln K = −ΔG°/(RT).

7.4 Effect of temperature on equilibrium constant (van't Hoff)

The equilibrium constant is not frozen when temperature changes. For a reaction, the standard enthalpy of reaction tells the direction and size of the change. The van't Hoff equation converts ΔH° into the slope of ln K against 1/T. This slope lets you estimate K at a new temperature from one known value, or read ΔH° from experimental K data. Reactor designers use it to choose operating temperature: some reactions need heat to raise K, others need cooling to avoid losing conversion.

You need Chapter 7.3 for K and Unit 2 for the standard heat of reaction.

The van't Hoff slope

At equilibrium, K is fixed by the standard Gibbs energy change, as Chapter 7.3 shows. Temperature enters that relation twice: directly in T and inside ΔG°. Differentiating with respect to T at constant pressure removes the direct part and leaves the standard enthalpy. The result is the van't Hoff equation.

d ln K/dT = ΔH°/(R T²)

K is the thermodynamic equilibrium constant (dimensionless); T is absolute temperature in K; ΔH° is the standard enthalpy of reaction in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

The derivative is local: it gives the slope of ln K at one temperature. If ΔH° is nearly constant, integrate it to compare two temperatures. A positive ΔH° makes the right side positive, so ln K rises with T. A negative ΔH° makes it negative, so ln K falls with T. The next section states the same rule through Le Chatelier.

Sign rule and the plot

Le Chatelier's principle gives the sign without calculus. Heat can be treated as a product of an exothermic reaction and as a reactant of an endothermic reaction.

  • Endothermic, ΔH° > 0: raising T adds a reactant, shifts the reaction right, and K rises.
  • Exothermic, ΔH° < 0: raising T adds a product, shifts the reaction left, and K falls.

On a plot of ln K against 1/T, the slope is −ΔH°/R. The figure uses the worked example: K = 10.0 at 300 K and K = 0.571 at 350 K. The point at 1/350 = 0.002857 K⁻¹ has ln K = −0.560; the point at 1/300 = 0.003333 K⁻¹ has ln K = 2.303. Moving to the right means lower temperature and higher K for this exothermic reaction. At fixed T, pressure and inerts do not change K; they change composition only, as Chapter 7.7 explains.

Constant ΔH and changing ΔCp

With ΔH° constant, integrate between T₁ and T₂. The formula estimates a new K without ΔS° data. Use it for small temperature intervals, or when ΔCp° is close to zero.

ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

K₁ and K₂ are equilibrium constants at T₁ and T₂; ΔH° is the constant standard enthalpy of reaction in J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T₁ and T₂ are absolute temperatures in K.

For wide ranges, ΔH° and ΔS° change. Compute the standard heat capacity change ΔCp° = ΣνᵢCpᵢ° for products minus reactants. Then update enthalpy and entropy from a reference temperature T₀, and get ΔG°, then K.

ΔH°(T) = ΔH°(T₀) + ∫(T₀→T) ΔCp° dT;  ΔS°(T) = ΔS°(T₀) + ∫(T₀→T) (ΔCp°/T) dT;  ΔG°(T) = ΔH°(T) − TΔS°(T);  ln K(T) = −ΔG°(T)/(R T)

ΔH°(T) and ΔS°(T) are standard enthalpy and entropy at T; T₀ is a reference temperature; ΔCp° is products minus reactants in J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; ∫(T₀→T) means integrate from T₀ to T; ΔG°(T) is standard Gibbs energy at T; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Alternatively, integrate d ln K/dT numerically in small steps. At each step update ΔH°(T), then add ΔH°/(R T²) ΔT to ln K. A spreadsheet or short script handles this. Evaluation from formation data belongs to Chapter 7.5.

Worked example

Question. The standard enthalpy of reaction is ΔH° = −50.0 kJ/mol and is constant. At 300 K the equilibrium constant is K₁ = 10.0. Calculate K₂ at 350 K. Use R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

  1. T₁ = 300 K and T₂ = 350 K. ΔH° = −50.0 kJ/mol = −50 000 J/mol.
  2. ΔH°/R = −50 000 / 8.314 = −6014 K.
  3. 1/T₂ − 1/T₁ = 1/350 − 1/300 = 0.002857 − 0.003333 = −0.0004762 K⁻¹.
  4. ln(K₂/K₁) = −(ΔH°/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) = −(−6014)(−0.0004762) = −2.864.
  5. K₂ = K₁ e^(−2.864) = 10.0 × 0.0571 = 0.571.

Answer. K₂ = 0.571 at 350 K; the exothermic reaction loses equilibrium constant as temperature rises.

Exam traps

  • Using degrees Celsius for T or in 1/T. The van't Hoff equation needs kelvin.
  • Dropping the sign of ΔH°. Exothermic ΔH° is negative; K falls when T rises.
  • Reversing the temperature difference. Write 1/T₂ − 1/T₁ in the same order as K₂/K₁.
  • Reading the slope of ln K against 1/T as ΔH°/R instead of −ΔH°/R.
  • Using the constant-ΔH° integrated form over a wide range when ΔCp° is large. Then use ΔH°(T) and ΔS°(T), or integrate numerically.

7.5 Evaluation of equilibrium constants; relation to composition

The equilibrium constant K is a pure number fixed by temperature and standard-state Gibbs energies. To use it, convert it into a statement about what is actually in the reactor: mole fractions, total moles and extent of reaction. This chapter evaluates K from tabulated formation data or from ΔH° and ΔS°, corrects it to the operating temperature, then substitutes activities and mole balances to get one equation in ξ. That equation gives the composition of an equilibrium mixture, so it is used whenever a reactor design needs the maximum possible conversion.

You need: 7.1 mole balances, 7.3 K expression, and 7.4 van't Hoff.

Evaluating K from standard data

Evaluate K from standard-state data before you look at the reactor. At 298.15 K, add standard Gibbs energies of formation with stoichiometric signs. Products are positive, reactants negative. Tabulated values use pure ideal gas at 1 bar, pure liquid at 1 bar, and similar standard states. A large positive ΔG° gives a small K. If formation data are missing, use ΔG°rxn = ΔH°rxn − TΔS°rxn, where ΔH°rxn and ΔS°rxn are standard enthalpy and entropy changes. For another temperature, correct with van't Hoff: ln(K2/K1) = −(ΔH°rxn/R)(1/T2 − 1/T1). Use K1 at 298.15 K. The equation assumes constant ΔH°rxn; if heat capacities matter, integrate ΔCp (see Unit 2, heat effects). K at the reactor temperature is still one number.

ΔG°rxn = Σi νi ΔG°f,i,  K = exp(−ΔG°rxn / (R T))

νi is the stoichiometric coefficient of species i (positive for products, negative for reactants); ΔG°f,i is the standard Gibbs energy of formation at 298.15 K in J/mol; Σi means sum over all species; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; K is dimensionless.

From K to gas-phase composition

Write mole balances before substituting K. For one reaction, ni = ni0 + νi ξ and nT = nT0 + ξΣνi. Mole fractions are yi = ni/nT. Total moles change with ξ when Σνi ≠ 0: for A ⇌ 2B, nT = 1 + ξ, not 1. For an ideal gas, ai = yi P/P°. The standard state is pure ideal gas at P° = 1 bar. Then K = (P/P°)Σν Π (ni/nT)νi. This is one algebraic equation in ξ. For A ⇌ 2B with 1 mol pure A, nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ, nT = 1 + ξ, and P = P° = 1 bar, so K = yB²/yA = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). Keep (P/P°)Σν when P ≠ P°; it cancels when Σν = 0. Solve, then back-substitute to check Q = K. Some syllabi call this step the relation of K to composition.

ni = ni0 + νi ξ,  nT = nT0 + ξΣνi,  yi = ni / nT

ni0 is initial moles of species i; νi is its stoichiometric coefficient; ξ is extent of reaction in mol; nT is total moles; nT0 is initial total moles; yi is mole fraction; Σνi is the sum of stoichiometric coefficients. Π means product over all species.

Liquid solutions and the back-substitution check

For a liquid, ai = γi xi. The standard state is pure liquid at the system temperature and pressure, so pressure corrections are small. Then K = Π (γi xi)νi. For an ideal solution, γi = 1. Mole balances still use ξ; calculate nT = nT0 + ξΣνi even if the liquid volume is nearly constant. Nonideal systems need iteration. The γi depend on xi, so K is nonlinear in ξ. Start with γi = 1, solve for ξ, compute xi, update γi from a model (Margules, van Laar or Wilson, see Unit 5), and repeat. Check by back-substitution: compute Q from final xi and γi; Q must equal K. For several independent reactions, each has its own ξ and K, but all share ni and nT; solve simultaneously (see 7.8). The back-substitution check applies to every reaction.

ai = γi xi,  K = Π (γi xi)νi

ai is activity; γi is activity coefficient; xi is liquid mole fraction; νi is stoichiometric coefficient. For an ideal solution γi = 1.

Worked example

Question. At 400 K, A ⇌ 2B has K = 0.222. The feed is 1 mol pure A at P = 1 bar and P° = 1 bar. Assume ideal gas. Find the equilibrium extent ξ and mole fractions y_A and y_B.

  1. Stoichiometric table: n_A0 = 1 mol, n_B0 = 0; n_A = 1 − ξ, n_B = 2ξ; n_T = 1 − ξ + 2ξ = 1 + ξ.
  2. Activities: a_A = y_A P/P°, a_B = y_B P/P°. Since P/P° = 1, K = a_B²/a_A = y_B²/y_A.
  3. Substitute y_A = (1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ), y_B = 2ξ/(1 + ξ). K = [2ξ/(1+ξ)]² / [(1−ξ)/(1+ξ)] = 4ξ²/(1−ξ²).
  4. Solve 0.222 = 4ξ²/(1−ξ²). Multiply: 0.222 − 0.222ξ² = 4ξ², so 0.222 = 4.222ξ². ξ² = 0.222/4.222 = 0.05258. ξ = 0.2293 mol. Take the positive root; n_A ≥ 0 requires ξ ≤ 1.
  5. Mole fractions: y_A = (1 − 0.2293)/(1 + 0.2293) = 0.7707/1.2293 = 0.627. y_B = 2(0.2293)/(1.2293) = 0.4586/1.2293 = 0.373.
  6. Check: Q = 4(0.2293)²/(1 − 0.2293²) = 0.222, equal to K.

Answer. At equilibrium ξ = 0.229 mol, y_A = 0.627 and y_B = 0.373.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting that total moles change with ξ. For A ⇌ 2B, n_T = 1 + ξ, so y_i uses 1 + ξ in the denominator.
  • Using y_i directly in K when Σν ≠ 0 and P ≠ P°. The missing factor is (P/P°)Σν.
  • Using K at 298 K for a reactor at 400 K without van't Hoff. K is temperature-dependent.
  • Confusing Kp or Kc with the dimensionless activity-based K. Pressure and concentration forms carry extra factors.
  • Reversing stoichiometric signs in ΔG°rxn. Products positive, reactants negative; a sign slip gives 1/K.

7.6 Feasibility of reactions; equilibrium conversion and yield

A reaction can be thermodynamically possible and still stop far from complete conversion. At the current temperature, pressure and composition, compare the reaction quotient Q with the equilibrium constant K. The sign of ΔG = RT ln(Q/K) gives the spontaneous direction. Equilibrium conversion and yield then measure how far a given feed can go when Q reaches K. Engineers use this check to screen routes, size reactors and read plant data for ammonia, methanol and steam reforming.

You need: extent of reaction from 7.1, the equilibrium criterion from 7.2, K from 7.3 and composition from 7.5.

Reaction quotient decides direction

A reacting mixture has a composition at every instant, and that composition fixes Q. Q is not the equilibrium constant. Q changes as the reaction proceeds; K changes only with temperature.

Q = Π aᵢνᵢ

aᵢ is the activity of species i relative to its standard state; νᵢ is its stoichiometric number, positive for products and negative for reactants; Π means product over all species. Activities are dimensionless, so Q is dimensionless.

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = RT ln(Q/K)

ΔG is the Gibbs energy change at the current composition, J per mole of reaction as written; ΔG° is the standard Gibbs energy change, J/mol; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; Q and K are dimensionless.

  • Q < K gives ΔG < 0: forward reaction is spontaneous.
  • Q = K gives ΔG = 0: the mixture is at equilibrium.
  • Q > K gives ΔG > 0: backward reaction is spontaneous.

Feasibility here means direction from the present state. K > 1 only says standard-state products are favoured. It does not fix conversion: feed composition, stoichiometry, pressure and inerts enter through Q. Pressure and inert effects are in 7.7.

Equilibrium conversion from mole balances

For one reaction, write every mole number in terms of one extent ξ (mol). Set Q(ξ) = K and solve for ξ. Then convert extent to conversion.

X = (ni0 − ni) / ni0

X is the conversion of limiting reactant i, dimensionless and often reported as %; ni0 is its initial moles; ni is its moles at the chosen state. At equilibrium, Xeq uses ni at equilibrium.

For ideal gases, aᵢ = yᵢ P/P°, where yᵢ is mole fraction, P is total pressure and P° is standard pressure, 1 bar.

  1. Choose a basis, usually 1 mol of limiting reactant fed.
  2. Write nᵢ(ξ) = ni0 + νᵢξ from the stoichiometric table.
  3. Write total moles nT(ξ) and yᵢ = nᵢ/nT.
  4. Substitute activities into Q = Π aᵢνᵢ.
  5. Set Q(ξ) = K and solve for ξ, then Xeq.

For A ⇌ 2B with 1 mol A fed, no B and P = 1 bar: nA = 1 − ξ, nB = 2ξ, nT = 1 + ξ, so Q = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). The worked example solves this with K = 0.222.

Yield and selectivity

Conversion tells how much limiting reactant disappeared. Yield tells how much desired product appeared. Selectivity compares desired product with undesired product.

YD = nD,formed / nL0

YD is yield of desired product D, dimensionless or mol D per mol limiting reactant fed; nD,formed is moles of D formed; nL0 is moles of limiting reactant fed. Some texts divide by νD nL0, where νD is the stoichiometric number of D in the reaction as written, and call the result the fractional yield (fraction of the theoretical maximum amount of D).

SD/U = nD,formed / nU,formed

SD/U is selectivity of desired product D over undesired product U, mol/mol; nU,formed is moles of U formed. Name the undesired product when several exist.

High selectivity does not mean high yield. A reactor may make almost pure D but convert very little feed. For several simultaneous reactions, see 7.8.

Worked example

Question. For A ⇌ 2B, K = 0.222 at 400 K. A feed of 1 mol A at 1 bar contains no B. Take ideal gas behaviour and standard pressure 1 bar. (a) Derive Q as a function of extent ξ. (b) Compute Q at ξ = 0.10 mol and ξ = 0.30 mol; state the spontaneous direction. (c) Find equilibrium extent, conversion of A and yield of B.

  1. Stoichiometric table: nA = 1 − ξ mol, nB = 2ξ mol, total nT = 1 + ξ mol.
  2. Mole fractions: yA = (1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ), yB = 2ξ/(1 + ξ).
  3. At P = 1 bar and P° = 1 bar, ideal-gas activities are aA = yA and aB = yB.
  4. Q = aB²/aA = [(2ξ/(1 + ξ))²]/[(1 − ξ)/(1 + ξ)] = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²).
  5. At ξ = 0.10 mol: Q = 4(0.10)²/(1 − 0.10²) = 0.0404. This is below K = 0.222, so ΔG < 0 and the reaction goes forward.
  6. At ξ = 0.30 mol: Q = 4(0.30)²/(1 − 0.30²) = 0.3956 ≈ 0.396. This is above K, so ΔG > 0 and the reaction goes backward.
  7. At equilibrium: 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) = 0.222, so 4ξ² = 0.222 − 0.222ξ², giving 4.222ξ² = 0.222 and ξ² = 0.05258.
  8. ξeq = 0.2293 mol. Conversion of A: X = ξ/1 = 0.2293, or 22.9%.

Exam traps

  • Reporting K > 1 as high conversion. K fixes only the equilibrium activity ratio; stoichiometry, feed, pressure and inerts also matter.
  • Using ΔG° sign for feasibility at the plant composition. Feasibility uses ΔG = RT ln(Q/K), not ΔG° alone.
  • Treating Q as if it were K. Q can be evaluated at any composition and it changes as the reaction proceeds; only at equilibrium does Q equal K. K is the fixed value of Q at equilibrium for the given temperature.
  • Confusing yield with selectivity. Yield is desired product per limiting reactant fed; selectivity is desired product per undesired product.
  • Forgetting pressure in gas activities. aᵢ = yᵢP/P° for an ideal gas; yᵢ alone is correct only when P = P° = 1 bar.

7.7 Effect of pressure and inerts on conversion

At fixed temperature the equilibrium constant of a gas reaction is fixed. Pressure and inert gas change the mole fractions inside that constant without changing the constant itself. For an ideal gas K = K_y (P/P°)^Σν, so the sign of Σν tells which way the mixture moves. Industry uses the rule directly: ammonia synthesis runs at high pressure because it makes fewer moles, while cracking and dehydrogenation run at low pressure or with steam dilution because they make more moles.

You need: equilibrium constants and composition from 7.5, and equilibrium conversion from 7.6.

Pressure changes the split, not K

At fixed temperature, K for a gas reaction does not change when pressure changes. What changes is the way K is split between mole fractions and pressure. For an ideal gas, the activity of species i is a_i = y_i P/P°, where y_i is mole fraction, P is total pressure, and P° is standard pressure, 1 bar.

K = ∏ a_i^ν_i = [∏ y_i^ν_i] (P/P°)^Σν = K_y (P/P°)^Σν

a_i is activity, y_i is mole fraction, P is total pressure, P° is standard pressure (1 bar), ν_i is stoichiometric coefficient (positive for products, negative for reactants), Σν = Σν_i, and K_y = ∏ y_i^ν_i.

At fixed T, K is fixed. Rearranging gives K_y = K (P/P°)^−Σν. If Σν > 0, raising P lowers K_y; the mixture moves toward reactants to restore K_y to the value set by K. If Σν < 0, raising P favours products. If Σν = 0, pressure cancels and there is no ideal-gas shift.

Sign of ΣνRaising P at fixed TExample
Σν > 0favours reactants (fewer moles)A ⇌ 2B
Σν < 0favours products (fewer moles)N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3
Σν = 0no ideal-gas shiftH2 + I2 ⇌ 2HI

Inert gas dilutes the reacting mixture

An inert gas does not appear in the reaction stoichiometry. It still occupies volume and adds to total moles. For an ideal gas at total pressure P, the partial pressure of i is p_i = y_i P = n_i P / n_total. Adding inert raises n_total. At fixed T and P, p_i of every reactant and product falls.

This is dilution. It acts like lowering total pressure while keeping the same mole numbers of reactants and products. The equilibrium shifts toward the side with more moles. So inerts raise conversion when Σν > 0 and lower conversion when Σν < 0. When Σν = 0, inerts have no ideal-gas effect on composition. At fixed T and V, adding inert does not change p_i = n_i RT/V for the reacting species, so it does not dilute them; the effect is different.

Volume and condensed phases

At fixed T, changing volume changes the pressure of a gas by P = nRT/V. Halving V roughly doubles P. The composition shift follows the same Σν rule: compression acts like higher pressure. For a reaction that makes more moles, compression lowers conversion; for one that makes fewer moles, compression raises conversion.

For liquid-phase reactions, pressure has little effect on K and activity coefficients over ordinary pressures. The pressure effect becomes noticeable near the critical point or at very high pressures, where fugacity coefficients differ from unity. Then replace ideal-gas activities by a_i = y_i φ_i P/P°, with φ_i the fugacity coefficient. This is the link to nonideal gas treatment in 7.8.

  3. At P = 1 bar: 0.222 = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²). This gives 0.222 = 4.222ξ², ξ² = 0.05258, ξ = 0.2293. So X_A = 0.229.
  4. At P = 0.5 bar: 0.222 = 0.5 × 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) = 2ξ²/(1 − ξ²). This gives 0.222 = 2.222ξ², ξ² = 0.09991, ξ = 0.3161. So X_A = 0.316.
  5. At P = 2 bar: 0.222 = 2 × 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) = 8ξ²/(1 − ξ²). This gives 0.222 = 8.222ξ², ξ² = 0.02700, ξ = 0.1643. So X_A = 0.164.
  6. With 1 mol inert at 1 bar: n_total = 2 + ξ, y_A = (1 − ξ)/(2 + ξ), y_B = 2ξ/(2 + ξ). K = 4ξ²/[(2 + ξ)(1 − ξ)]. Substituting K = 0.222 gives 0.222(2 − ξ − ξ²) = 4ξ², which rearranges to 4.222ξ² + 0.222ξ − 0.444 = 0.
  7. The positive root is ξ = [−0.222 + √(0.222² + 4 × 4.222 × 0.444)]/(2 × 4.222). The square root is 2.7473, so ξ = 2.5253/8.444 = 0.2991. Thus X_A = 0.299.

Answer. At 0.5 bar X_A = 0.316; at 1 bar X_A = 0.229; at 2 bar X_A = 0.164; with 1 mol inert at 1 bar X_A = 0.299.

Exam traps

  • Saying pressure changes K. At fixed T, K is fixed; pressure changes activities and K_y.
  • Reversing the Σν rule. For A ⇌ 2B, Σν = +1, so higher pressure lowers conversion.
  • Forgetting that P/P° is dimensionless. Use P/P° in the formula, not P with units mixed in.
  • Putting inerts into the K expression. Inerts do not appear in K; they change n_total and hence mole fractions.
  • Confusing fixed P with fixed V when inert is added. At fixed P the inert dilutes reactants; at fixed V it does not change their partial pressures in an ideal gas.

7.8 Gas-phase, heterogeneous and simultaneous reactions

A real reactor rarely holds one ideal gas and one reaction. High pressure makes gases non-ideal, solids sit beside gases, and several reactions run together. The criterion is still Σνᵢμᵢ = 0 for each reaction; what changes is how activity is written and how many equations are solved at once. Fugacity coefficients handle high-pressure gases, pure solids and liquids have activity 1, and each independent reaction brings its own extent and K. Ammonia, methanol and steam-reforming reactors are designed this way.

You need: K from standard Gibbs energy, K to composition, fugacity and activity coefficients from Unit 5, and phase equilibrium from Unit 6.

Gas phase: fugacity coefficients inside K

At a few bar a gas is ideal and the fugacity of species i is its partial pressure, fᵢ = yᵢP. At 50–300 bar — ammonia synthesis at 100–300 bar, low-pressure methanol synthesis at 50–100 bar — molecules attract and repel, so fᵢ differs from yᵢP by a factor φᵢ. Equilibrium then uses fugacity, not partial pressure.

K = Πᵢ (φᵢ yᵢ P / P°)^νᵢ

K is the equilibrium constant at temperature T (dimensionless); φᵢ is the fugacity coefficient of species i in the mixture; yᵢ is its gas mole fraction; P is total pressure in bar; P° = 1 bar is standard pressure; νᵢ is the stoichiometric coefficient, positive for products and negative for reactants.

For an ideal gas every φᵢ = 1, giving K = Π(yᵢP/P°)^νᵢ. The general form matters because φᵢ depends on T, P and mixture composition, not on species alone.

  1. Choose an equation of state: virial at moderate pressure, cubic at high pressure.
  2. At the trial composition compute every φᵢ.
  3. Evaluate Q = Π(φᵢ yᵢ P/P°)^νᵢ and compare with K.
  4. Move composition along the extent, recompute φᵢ, repeat until Q = K.

The loop is unavoidable: φᵢ depends on the yᵢ you seek. At synthesis pressures, ignoring φᵢ shifts predicted conversion by several percentage points, so design calculations keep it.

Heterogeneous systems: pure solids and liquids drop out

The standard state of a pure solid or pure liquid is the pure condensed phase at system T and P. Its chemical potential equals the standard value, so its activity is exactly 1, whatever the amount. It enters K as 1^ν = 1. Only gases and dissolved species appear.

Limestone calcination, CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g): both solids have activity 1, so K = a_CO₂ = y_CO₂P/P°. At fixed T, equilibrium CO₂ pressure is fixed, whether the kiln holds a gram or a tonne of lime. In a cement kiln, flue gas sweeps CO₂ away, keeps y_CO₂ below that value, and decomposition continues.

Carbon behaves the same way: C(s) + CO₂(g) ⇌ 2CO(g) has K = (y_CO P/P°)²/(y_CO₂P/P°), since a_C = 1. This Boudouard balance fixes gas composition in carburising furnaces and coal gasifiers.

K = Πᵢ (γᵢ xᵢ)^νᵢ

for a reacting liquid mixture: γᵢ is the activity coefficient of species i, xᵢ its liquid mole fraction, νᵢ its stoichiometric coefficient; the product runs over liquid species. Used for esterification, hydrolysis and other liquid-phase syntheses.

Activity 1 belongs to a pure phase only. A solid in solid solution, or water acting as solvent in a concentrated solution, needs activity from its own standard state.

Several reactions at once

Real feeds crack, reform and shift together. Two reactions are independent when neither can be built from the other plus element balances. Write the atom matrix with elements as rows and species as columns; let C be its rank. Then r = N − C, where N is the number of species. Each independent reaction needs its own extent and K.

nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + Σⱼ νᵢⱼ ξⱼ

nᵢ is moles of species i at equilibrium; nᵢ₀ its initial moles; νᵢⱼ the coefficient of species i in reaction j, positive for products and negative for reactants; ξⱼ the extent of reaction j in mol. The sum runs over r independent reactions.

At equilibrium Qⱼ = Kⱼ for every j. The equations are coupled because all reactions share one total mole number and one set of mole fractions, so they are solved together.

  1. Write the stoichiometric matrix, find its rank, fix r.
  2. Express every nᵢ in the r extents with the mole balance.
  3. Write each Kⱼ as a function of ξ₁ … ξᵣ and pressure.
  4. Solve the r equations by Newton's method or successive substitution.
  5. Discard any root that makes a mole number negative.

The reacting phase rule counts what is free: F = N − P + 2 − r, with N species, P phases, r independent reactions and F degrees of freedom. Steam reforming with CH₄ + H₂O ⇌ CO + 3H₂ and CO + H₂O ⇌ CO₂ + H₂ has N = 5, P = 1 and r = 2, so F = 5 − 1 + 2 − 2 = 4: fixing T, P and the two independent element ratios fixes composition. Duhem's theorem says the same. Only a few universities teach the full multireaction count; exams usually ask for r in the K equations.

Worked example

Question. A feed of 1 mol pure A at 1 bar reacts in the gas phase by two independent reactions: A ⇌ B with K₁ = 2.00, and 2A ⇌ C with K₂ = 0.500. The gas is ideal and temperature is fixed. Find both extents and the equilibrium composition.

  1. Let ξ₁ mol of A ⇌ B and ξ₂ mol of 2A ⇌ C react. Then n_A = 1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂, n_B = ξ₁, n_C = ξ₂, n_T = 1 − ξ₂ mol.
  2. With P = P° = 1 bar and all φᵢ = 1: K₁ = y_B/y_A = ξ₁/(1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂) = 2.00.
  3. K₂ = y_C/y_A² = ξ₂(1 − ξ₂)/(1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂)² = 0.500, because n_T cancels.
  4. Put D = 1 − ξ₁ − 2ξ₂. Then ξ₁ = K₁D and D = 1 − K₁D − 2ξ₂, so D = (1 − 2ξ₂)/(1 + K₁) = (1 − 2ξ₂)/3.
  5. Substitute in K₂: 0.500(1 − 2ξ₂)²/9 = ξ₂(1 − ξ₂), giving 1.2222ξ₂² − 1.2222ξ₂ + 0.05556 = 0.
  6. Root below 0.5: ξ₂ = (1.2222 − 1.1055)/2.4444 = 0.0477 mol. Then D = (1 − 0.0955)/3 = 0.3015 and ξ₁ = 2.00 × 0.3015 = 0.6030 mol.
  7. Moles: n_A = 0.3015, n_B = 0.6030, n_C = 0.0477, n_T = 0.9523 mol. Mole fractions: y_A = 0.3166, y_B = 0.6332, y_C = 0.0501.
  8. Check: y_B/y_A = 2.000 and y_C/y_A² = 0.500.

Answer. ξ₁ = 0.6030 mol and ξ₂ = 0.0477 mol, giving n_A = 0.3015 mol, n_B = 0.6030 mol, n_C = 0.0477 mol with y_A = 0.3166, y_B = 0.6332 and y_C = 0.0501.

Exam traps

  • Putting a pure solid into K as a mole fraction. Its activity is 1, so CaCO₃ and CaO never appear in the calcination constant — only y_CO₂P/P° does.
  • Using one extent for two reactions. Each independent reaction needs its own extent and equilibrium equation.
  • Treating φᵢ as a pure-species constant. It depends on composition, so high-pressure equilibrium must be iterated.
  • Counting reactions from the feed list. Take the rank of the atom matrix; a reaction that is a sum of the others adds no extent.
  • Dropping standard pressure. In K = Π(yᵢP/P°)^νᵢ pressure enters as dimensionless P/P°, so P and P° need the same units.

Takeaways

  1. A reacting mixture needs only stoichiometric coefficients and one extent variable per independent reaction to fix every mole number.
  2. At fixed T and P a closed reacting system stops changing when total Gibbs energy is minimum, which is the same statement as Σνᵢμᵢ = 0.
  3. The standard Gibbs energy change sets K through ΔG° = −RT ln K, and K itself depends only on temperature, never on pressure or initial composition.
  4. Van't Hoff's equation turns tabulated ΔH° data into K at any other temperature; endothermic reactions are favoured by heating, exothermic ones by cooling.
  5. K is a number, not a composition: converting it to conversions needs activities, mole balances and, for gases, the pressure and inert content.
  6. Q versus K decides feasibility at the current composition; equilibrium conversion and yield say how far the feed can actually go.
  7. Raising pressure helps only reactions that reduce the number of gas moles; inert dilution always works against them and is best removed by purge.
  8. Real problems stack nonideality, several phases and several reactions, so each reaction gets its own equilibrium relation over one shared set of mole balances.

ΔH° < 0 means K falls as T rises, so low T favours conversion but slows kinetics; Σν < 0 means high P favours conversion through K_y = K(P/P°)^(−Σν); Inerts dilute the mixture and cut conversion, so a purge stream is needed; Optimal design therefore compromises with a moderate T, high P and low inert level; Write the general K = K_y K_φ (P/P°) ^Σν relation and show each lever's role; Note that reaction kinetics, catalyst life and compression cost set the practical limits.

Glossary

Activity
The dimensionless effective amount of a species relative to its standard state; equals 1 for a pure liquid or solid at 1 bar.
Affinity
A = −Σνᵢμᵢ in J/mol; positive when the forward reaction is spontaneous, zero at equilibrium.
Chemical potential
The partial molar Gibbs energy of a species, equal to the change in G when one mole is added at fixed T, P and other amounts.
Duhem's theorem
Fixing any two independent intensive properties of a closed system fixes all its remaining intensive properties.
Endothermic reaction
A reaction with ΔH° > 0; its equilibrium constant rises as temperature rises.
Equilibrium
The state in which no further net change of composition occurs at fixed temperature and pressure.
Equilibrium constant
The temperature-dependent number K = Π aᵢ^νᵢ evaluated at equilibrium; related to ΔG° by ΔG° = −RT ln K.
Equilibrium conversion
The fraction of the fed limiting reactant that has reacted when equilibrium is reached.
Exothermic reaction
A reaction with ΔH° < 0; its equilibrium constant falls as temperature rises.
Fugacity
An effective pressure that makes the ideal-gas chemical potential formula work for real gases.
Gibbs energy minimisation
The numerical method of finding equilibrium composition by minimising total G subject to element balances.
Heterogeneous reaction
A reaction involving more than one phase, so each pure condensed phase contributes unit activity.
Independent reactions
A minimal set of reactions whose extents can describe every possible composition change of the system.
Inert
A species present in the mixture that takes no part in the reaction but adds to the total moles.
K_p
The equilibrium constant expressed in partial pressures, K_p = Π(pᵢ/p°)^νᵢ, valid for ideal gases.
K_y
The equilibrium constant expressed in mole fractions, K_y = Π yᵢ^νᵢ; carries all the composition dependence for gases.
Limiting reactant
The reactant that runs out first and therefore caps the maximum possible conversion.
Phase rule for reacting systems
F = C − P + 2 − R, where C counts components, P counts phases and R counts independent reactions.
Reaction extent
A single variable ξ in mol that measures how far a reaction has run; every mole number follows from nᵢ = nᵢ₀ + νᵢξ.
Reaction quotient
Q = Π aᵢ^νᵢ at the actual composition; comparison with K tells the direction of change.
Selectivity
The fraction of reacted feed that ends up as the desired product rather than a by-product.
Standard Gibbs energy of formation
ΔG°f of a compound: the change in Gibbs energy when one mole forms from elements in their standard states.
Standard state
The agreed reference state, at unit activity, against which Gibbs energies and activities are measured.
Stoichiometry
The fixed molar ratios in which species appear in a balanced chemical equation.
