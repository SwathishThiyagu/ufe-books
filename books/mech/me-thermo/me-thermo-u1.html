1.1 Microscopic and macroscopic approaches

Thermodynamics studies energy, matter and their changes. You can follow every molecule, or you can measure bulk quantities. The microscopic view tracks molecules and uses statistics. The macroscopic view uses pressure p, volume V and temperature T that you can measure. Engineering uses the macroscopic view because a small sample holds an enormous number of molecules. Averages describe the sample well. The two views agree, and the bulk view is the practical one for design.

You need: school physics ideas of force, energy and density; systems and properties are defined next.

The two views

Thermodynamics is the study of energy, matter and the changes they undergo. It covers engines, refrigerators, steam plants, air conditioners and chemical reactions. Two points of view describe the same sample. The microscopic view follows individual molecules: where each one is, how fast it moves and how it collides. The macroscopic view ignores individual molecules and uses bulk properties that you can measure, such as pressure p, volume V and temperature T. Some syllabi call these the microscopic and macroscopic approaches. They are not rival theories. The microscopic model explains why the bulk properties behave as they do. The macroscopic model gives numbers you can use in design. Engineering calculations normally start from the macroscopic view and use the microscopic view only when a deeper reason is needed. The same copper block can be described by its mass, volume and temperature, or by the motion of its atoms. The first description is what an engineer measures. The second explains why the measurements follow the laws they do.

Following molecules

The microscopic view treats a sample as a very large number of molecules. A cubic centimetre of gas near room conditions holds about 10¹⁹ molecules. Tracking each molecule by hand is impossible. The method records molecular positions, speeds and collisions, then uses statistics to find averages. These averages predict bulk behaviour. For example, molecules strike the wall and transfer momentum; the average rate of momentum transfer per unit area is what we measure as pressure. Higher temperature means higher average molecular kinetic energy, so molecules hit harder and pressure rises if volume is fixed. Molecular kinetic theory and statistical mechanics build these links in detail; Unit 5 uses their results for gases. The microscopic view is powerful for explaining temperature, pressure and phase change. It is not convenient for routine calculations because the number of molecules is enormous and the detailed motion is not needed. The microscopic view does not replace measurements. It supplies a model that predicts what a gauge or thermometer will show. In a gas, the model treats collisions as random; in a solid, it treats atoms as vibrating about fixed positions. Both cases use averages, not individual fates.

Using bulk properties

The macroscopic view treats the whole sample as a continuous body with measurable properties. Pressure p is normal force per unit area: p = F / A, where p is pressure in pascal (Pa), F is normal force in newton (N), and A is area in square metre (m²). Volume V is the space occupied, in cubic metre (m³). Temperature T is the property that decides thermal equilibrium; the zeroth law defines it. Density ρ is mass per unit volume: ρ = m / V, where m is mass in kilogram (kg) and ρ is in kg/m³. A property measured for the whole sample, or for a uniform part of it, is macroscopic. The continuum idea assumes matter is continuous, so p, ρ and T vary smoothly from point to point. This works when the region of interest is much larger than molecular spacing, as in pipes, turbines and heat exchangers. It fails in a very thin gas layer near a surface. A property is macroscopic when it belongs to the sample or to a small continuum element, not to one molecule. Examples are p, V, T, density, internal energy and enthalpy. Internal energy and enthalpy are developed in Unit 2.

Worked example

Question. A copper block has mass 1.00 kg and volume 1.12×10⁻⁴ m³. Is density a microscopic or macroscopic property? Calculate its density.

  1. Density uses mass and volume of the whole block. Both are bulk measurements. So density is a macroscopic property.
  2. Use the formula ρ = m / V, where ρ is density in kg/m³, m is mass in kg and V is volume in m³.
  3. Substitute m = 1.00 kg and V = 1.12×10⁻⁴ m³.
  4. ρ = 1.00 / (1.12×10⁻⁴) = 8928.571... kg/m³.
  5. Round to three significant figures: ρ ≈ 8.93×10³ kg/m³.

Answer. Density is a macroscopic property; its value is 8.93×10³ kg/m³ (8928.57 kg/m³).

Exam traps

  • Pressure is not only microscopic. Molecular collisions explain it, but pressure is a bulk property measured with a gauge or transducer.
  • Density is not microscopic because copper contains atoms. Density uses total mass and total volume, so it is a macroscopic property.
  • Do not mix units: density from kg and m³ is kg/m³; from g and cm³ it is g/cm³. Convert before comparing.
  • The two views do not give different thermodynamics. They describe the same state; one follows molecules, the other measures averages.
  • The continuum is not valid at every scale. It fails when the flow region is comparable to molecular spacing or mean free path.

1.2 Systems: closed, open, isolated; control volume

A system is the part of the universe you choose to study. Everything outside it is the surroundings, and the boundary is the surface, real or imaginary, between them. The boundary decides what may cross: mass, heat, work, or none. Engineers use this choice to analyse an engine cylinder, a compressor, a pressure cooker, or a UPI server room. The same hardware can be a closed, open, or isolated system depending on where you draw the boundary.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the macroscopic view, where bulk properties describe the system.

System, surroundings and boundary

Pick a region in space or a fixed amount of matter. That region is the system. The surroundings are everything outside it that can interact with it. The boundary separates the two. The boundary may be the metal wall of a tank, the dashed surface around a turbine, or an imaginary surface drawn across a pipe.

Mass, heat and work cross only at the boundary. A boundary that lets nothing cross makes the system isolated. A boundary that lets heat cross is diathermic. A boundary that blocks heat is adiabatic. A boundary that cannot move is rigid, so it cannot transmit displacement work. A rigid boundary can still pass heat or shaft work.

The same physical object can be classified in more than one way when you move the boundary. A pressure cooker with the lid closed is a closed system. Draw the boundary around the cooker and the kitchen air, and the pair is isolated only if no heat, mass or work crosses the outer surface. In practice, the kitchen wall may pass heat, so the pair is not isolated.

Closed, open and isolated

Closed system (control mass): no mass crosses the boundary. Energy may cross as heat or work. A sealed piston–cylinder with gas is closed. The gas mass stays constant, but the piston can move and heat can enter.

Open system (control volume): mass and energy may cross. A compressor, a turbine, a radiator, and a human body are open systems. Some syllabi call an open system a control volume. The boundary is drawn around the device; water or air enters and leaves.

Isolated system: no mass, no heat, and no work crosses. A perfectly insulated rigid flask of gas is close to isolated. A truly isolated system is an ideal limit; real walls leak some heat.

m₂ = m₁ − mₑ

m₁ is the initial mass in kg; m₂ is the final mass in kg; mₑ is the mass that leaves in kg. Add mass that enters if any.

Classification depends on the boundary. A tank with a valve open is open while mass leaves. Close the valve and draw the boundary inside the closed tank: it becomes closed. It is isolated only if heat and work are also blocked.

Boundary types and steady running

Boundary names describe what crosses. A rigid boundary cannot move, so it does no p dV work. It may still pass heat or electrical work. An adiabatic boundary passes no heat, but work may cross. A diathermic boundary allows heat. An isolated system has a boundary that blocks mass, heat and work.

Steady state is a running condition: at any fixed point, properties do not change with time. Mass and energy may still flow through an open system at steady state. A steady-flow compressor can have constant inlet and outlet values for hours. Steady state is not the same as thermodynamic equilibrium; equilibrium is covered in Chapter 1.4.

Work and heat are transfers, not properties. Their sign convention is fixed in Chapter 1.6. For this chapter, only ask: does the boundary allow the transfer? Use this table.

Worked example

Question. A rigid tank holds 2.0 kg of air. A valve opens and 0.50 kg of air escapes while 30 kJ of heat leaves the tank. Take the tank as the system. Does mass cross the boundary? Does energy cross it? Is the system closed or open?

  1. System: the air inside the tank. Boundary: the tank inner wall plus the valve opening.
  2. Initial mass m₁ = 2.0 kg. Mass that leaves mₑ = 0.50 kg. Final mass m₂ = m₁ − mₑ = 2.0 − 0.50 = 1.5 kg.
  3. mₑ = 0.50 kg > 0, so mass crosses the boundary; 0.50 kg leaves.
  4. Heat Q = 30 kJ leaves, so energy crosses the boundary as heat. The tank is rigid, so no displacement work is done.
  5. Mass crosses, so the system is open (a control volume). It is not closed. It is not isolated because heat crosses.

Answer. The final mass is 1.5 kg, 0.50 kg of air crosses the boundary, 30 kJ of heat leaves, and the tank is an open system.

Exam traps

  • Closed is not isolated. A closed system can exchange heat and work; only an isolated system blocks both.
  • Open means mass crosses. It does not mean the flow is steady or that flow work must be calculated now.
  • Rigid does not mean adiabatic. A rigid tank can pass heat through its wall.
  • Steady state is not equilibrium. A steady-flow device can have gradients and still have constant readings at each point.
  • Do not call a system isolated just because it is sealed; heat can cross a metal wall.

1.3 Properties, state, path, process and cycle

A system is described by properties such as pressure, volume and temperature. Their values at one instant fix a state. Moving from one state to another along a chosen route is a process, and the route is the path. If the route returns to the starting state, it is a cycle. This chapter separates properties, which depend only on the state, from heat and work, which depend on the path. That separation is the basis for every energy balance in the rest of thermodynamics.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for system and boundary, and the idea of a property from Chapter 1.1.

Property, state and state point

A property is a measurable characteristic of a system. Pressure p, temperature T, volume V and mass m are properties. A state is the condition of the system when every property has a fixed value. A state point is that state drawn as one point on a property diagram, such as a p–V diagram. The system has no memory of how it reached the point.

Properties fall into three useful groups.

GroupDepends on mass?ExamplesSymbols
IntensiveNopressure, temperature, densityp, T, ρ
ExtensiveYesvolume, internal energy, mass, enthalpyV, U, m, H
SpecificNo (per unit mass)specific volume, specific internal energyv, u

v = V / m

v is specific volume in m³/kg; V is total volume in m³; m is mass in kg.

An intensive property does not change when you split the system into two equal parts. An extensive property halves. A specific property is an extensive property divided by mass, so it behaves like an intensive property. Some syllabi call specific properties per-unit-mass properties.

For a simple compressible system, two independent intensive properties fix the state. A simple compressible system has no electric, magnetic or surface effects that need extra variables. Choose p and v, or p and T, or T and v. Once two independent intensive properties are known, every other property is fixed. Use property tables or an equation of state (Unit 5) to find the rest. In a two-phase mixture p and T are not independent, so the pair p, T does not fix the state.

Path and process

A path is the sequence of states a system passes through. A process is a change from one state to another along a path. If the start and end states differ, the process is non-cyclic. If they are the same state, the process is cyclic, or a cycle. A path may be drawn as a line on a p–V diagram. A quasi-static path passes through near-equilibrium states (Chapter 1.4).

Properties depend only on state. The change in volume between state 1 and state 2 is ΔV = V₂ − V₁, whatever path is used. The same is true for pressure, temperature and internal energy. Heat Q and work W are not properties. They are energy transfers across the boundary, and their values depend on the path. Chapter 1.6 fixes signs; Chapter 1.7 gives W = ∫p dV for displacement work. Calling heat a property is a common exam error.

A process can be named by what stays fixed: isothermal (T constant), isobaric (p constant), isochoric (V constant), adiabatic (no heat transfer). These names describe the path, not a new property. Unit 2 applies them to gases.

Cycle and property changes

A cycle starts and ends at the same state. After one complete cycle the net change of every property is zero: ΔV = 0, Δp = 0, ΔU = 0. The system returns to its first state point. This does not mean the net heat and net work are zero. A heat engine repeats a cycle and produces net work, so work and heat are path-dependent transfers over the loop.

For the cycle in the figure, state 1 (p = 100 kPa, V = 0.20 m³) goes to state 2 (p = 200 kPa, V = 0.10 m³), then to state 3 (p = 150 kPa, V = 0.15 m³), then back to state 1. The volume changes are −0.10 m³, +0.05 m³ and +0.05 m³. They sum to zero. The pressure returns to 100 kPa. The worked example below asks for the net change.

Non-cyclic processes have a non-zero change in at least one property. A cycle is a closed path; a process is one segment of a path. Keep the words separate: a cycle can contain many processes.

Worked example

Question. A gas goes from state 1 (p₁ = 100 kPa, V₁ = 0.20 m³) to state 2 (p₂ = 200 kPa, V₂ = 0.10 m³), then to state 3 (p₃ = 150 kPa, V₃ = 0.15 m³), then back to state 1. Is 1→2→3→1 a cycle? What is the net change in volume after one complete cycle?

  1. List the given states: state 1 has p₁ = 100 kPa and V₁ = 0.20 m³; state 2 has p₂ = 200 kPa and V₂ = 0.10 m³; state 3 has p₃ = 150 kPa and V₃ = 0.15 m³.
  2. Check the start and end: the path starts at state 1 and ends at state 1 after processes A, B and the return. The start and end states are the same, so 1→2→3→1 is a cycle.
  3. Write the volume change for each segment: ΔV₁₂ = V₂ − V₁ = 0.10 − 0.20 = −0.10 m³. ΔV₂₃ = V₃ − V₂ = 0.15 − 0.10 = +0.05 m³. ΔV₃₁ = V₁ − V₃ = 0.20 − 0.15 = +0.05 m³.
  4. Add the segment changes: ΔV_net = (−0.10) + (+0.05) + (+0.05) = 0 m³.
  5. Use the property shortcut as a check: ΔV_net = V_final − V_initial = V₁ − V₁ = 0 m³. Volume is a property, so only the end states matter. The pressure also returns to 100 kPa, so its net change is zero.

Answer. Yes, 1→2→3→1 is a cycle because the system returns to state 1; the net change in volume after one complete cycle is 0 m³.

Exam traps

  • Calling heat or work a property. Heat Q and work W are path functions; they have no value at a state.
  • Mixing extensive and specific quantities. V is in m³; v is in m³/kg. Divide by mass before using a table entry for specific volume.
  • Using p and T as the two properties in a two-phase mixture. There p and T are not independent; use p and x (dryness fraction) or T and x.
  • Thinking a cycle has zero net work or zero net heat. A cycle has zero net change of every property, but net work and net heat can be non-zero.
  • Treating a process and a cycle as the same. A process is one path segment; a cycle is a closed path that returns to the start state.

1.4 Thermodynamic equilibrium and quasi-static process

Thermodynamics becomes calculable when a system has one pressure and one temperature. Equilibrium gives that condition: no temperature difference, no unbalanced force, no reaction still driving change. A quasi-static process keeps equilibrium while the state changes by moving in tiny steps. The process then forms a continuous line on a p–V diagram, so its area can be used. Turbine analysis, gas compression and steam plant design rest on this idealisation.

You need: 1.3 for state, path and process, and 1.2 for what may cross a boundary.

Three balances make one equilibrium

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when nothing inside it, and nothing across its boundary, is driving a change. Three balances must hold together.

Thermal equilibrium means no temperature difference. Every part and every body it can exchange heat with has the same temperature. Otherwise heat flows and the state drifts. The zeroth law turns this into the definition of temperature.

Mechanical equilibrium means no unbalanced force: pressure is the same at every point and the piston is not accelerating. For gas under a resting piston, p_gas × A = W_piston + p_atm × A with p in Pa, W_piston in N and A in m²; in kPa, p_gas = W_piston/(1000A) + p_atm, where p_gas is gas pressure in kPa, W_piston is piston weight in N, A is piston area in m² and p_atm is outside atmospheric pressure in kPa. Only if the outside of the piston is in vacuum does p_gas = W_piston/(1000A).

Chemical equilibrium means no reaction, mixing or diffusion is still changing composition.

With all three satisfied and the boundary fixed, p, V, T and composition stay constant. Constant readings are the practical test. Read, wait, read again; unchanged readings mean no change. They do not prove equilibrium, as the next section shows.

Equilibrium is not steady state

A steady state means properties at a fixed point do not change with time. It does not mean equilibrium. A running steam turbine shows the difference. Steam enters at 500 °C and leaves at 60 °C. The metal near inlet and outlet hold fixed temperatures, so readings are constant for hours. Yet a temperature gradient sits inside and heat keeps flowing down it.

Equilibrium has no gradients, no throughput, no driving force left. Steady state has gradients fixed in space, continuous flow and a driving force balanced by throughput.

A phone charger left plugged in warms for ten minutes, then stops changing. It is steady, not equilibrium: electrical work enters and heat leaves.

Exam question in disguise: constant properties do not prove equilibrium. Ask whether mass or energy is crossing the boundary.

Quasi-static: creeping through equilibrium states

Real changes are usually fast. Open a valve and gas rushes from high to low pressure. For a few seconds p and T differ from point to point. Such non-equilibrium conditions are not equilibrium states, so they have no single p or V and cannot be plotted as points on a p–V diagram.

A quasi-static process (some syllabi say quasi-equilibrium) is slow enough that at every instant the system sits infinitesimally away from equilibrium. It passes through a succession of well-defined equilibrium states. Each state has one p, one V and one T — a point on the p–V diagram.

  1. Create a driving force that is infinitesimal: add one grain of sand to the piston (a small compression), or lower the outside pressure by a tiny amount dp (a small change in pressure in kPa; a small expansion).
  2. Wait while the system settles into the new equilibrium state.
  3. Repeat until the final state is reached.
  4. In the limit of infinitely many infinitely small steps, the process is quasi-static.

A quasi-static process can follow any path. The worked example uses one particular path: if the product pV stays constant, then pV = C, where p is absolute pressure in kPa, V is volume in m³ and C is a constant in kPa·m³ = kJ. On a p–V diagram this path is a hyperbola.

pV = C

p is absolute pressure in kPa, V is volume in m³, and C is a constant in kPa·m³ = kJ. This is the particular path used in the worked example, not a rule for every quasi-static process.

The whole process is then a continuous line from state A to state B. Without a line there is no area, and without area displacement work W = ∫p dV cannot be computed (see 1.7). Infinite steps take infinite time, so no real process is exactly quasi-static; a slow real process is only a good approximation to it.

Slow does not mean reversible

Quasi-static is about speed, not direction. A gas can be stirred by a paddle wheel while a piston moves in slowly. Pressure stays uniform and the path is a clean line, yet the process is irreversible: paddle work has become internal energy and will not come back on its own.

Friction gives the same result. Push a piston slowly against friction and the state still passes through equilibrium states, but energy leaks to friction and cannot be recovered.

Every reversible process is quasi-static. Not every quasi-static process is reversible. Reversibility needs zero friction, zero viscosity and zero electrical resistance; Unit 3 defines it properly.

Worked example

Question. A gas goes from state 1 (p₁ = 100 kPa, V₁ = 0.20 m³) to state 2 (p₂ = 200 kPa, V₂ = 0.10 m³) along a quasi-static path on which the product pV stays constant. (a) Check whether p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. (b) Find the pressure at V = 0.15 m³. (c) Can this path be drawn as a continuous line on a p–V diagram?

  1. State 1: p₁V₁ = 100 kPa × 0.20 m³ = 20 kPa·m³ = 20 kJ.
  2. State 2: p₂V₂ = 200 kPa × 0.10 m³ = 20 kJ.
  3. The products are equal, so C = 20 kJ and p = C/V.
  4. At V = 0.15 m³: p = 20 kJ ÷ 0.15 m³ = 133.33 kPa.
  5. Every intermediate volume gives one finite pressure, so each state is one equilibrium point. Joining them gives an unbroken hyperbola from (0.20 m³, 100 kPa) to (0.10 m³, 200 kPa).
  6. Volume falls while pressure rises, so the gas is compressed. Answer to (c): yes.

Answer. p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ = 20 kJ, C = 20 kJ, p = 133.33 kPa at V = 0.15 m³, and the path is a continuous hyperbola on the p–V diagram.

Exam traps

  • Treating quasi-static as reversible. A slow process with friction or paddle-wheel stirring is quasi-static and still irreversible.
  • Treating constant readings as equilibrium. A turbine or heat exchanger holds steady readings while mass flows and internal gradients stay fixed. That is steady state.
  • Plotting a fast process as a line. A fast process passes through non-equilibrium conditions, where p and T differ from point to point. These are not equilibrium states, so they have no single p or V and cannot be plotted as points on a p–V diagram; ∫p dV is meaningless for it.
  • Checking only one balance. Gas at uniform pressure but with a hot end and cold end is not in thermodynamic equilibrium.
  • Unit slip: kPa × m³ = kJ. So 100 kPa × 0.20 m³ = 20 kJ = 20 000 J, not 20 J.

Thermal: no temperature difference. Mechanical: no unbalanced force. Chemical: no reaction or diffusion driving a change in composition.

Understand Why can a quasi-static process be drawn as a continuous line on a p–V diagram, while a fast expansion cannot? 3 marks

In a quasi-static process each intermediate state is an equilibrium state with a single p and a single V, so it is a point, and joined points make a line. In a fast expansion p differs from place to place, so no single p exists and no point can be plotted.

Analyze A steam turbine runs for hours with inlet temperature 500 °C and outlet temperature 60 °C, both constant. Is it in thermodynamic equilibrium? Justify. 4 marks

No. Readings at each point are constant with time, so it is in steady state. But a 440 °C difference exists inside, so thermal equilibrium fails, and mass flows through continuously. Equilibrium needs no gradients and no throughput.

Apply A gas goes from p₁ = 150 kPa, V₁ = 0.30 m³ to p₂ = 300 kPa along a quasi-static path with pV = constant. Find V₂ and the pressure at V = 0.20 m³. 4 marks

C = p₁V₁ = 150 × 0.30 = 45 kJ. V₂ = C/p₂ = 45/300 = 0.15 m³. At V = 0.20 m³, p = 45/0.20 = 225 kPa.

Analyze A piston is pushed in slowly while a paddle wheel stirs the gas, and pressure stays uniform throughout. Is the process quasi-static? Is it reversible? 5 marks

Quasi-static, yes: every intermediate state is an equilibrium state with one p and one T, so the path is a continuous line. Reversible, no: the paddle work has become internal energy through viscous dissipation and cannot be returned.

Understand Separate equilibrium from steady state in one line each, with an example. 3 marks

Equilibrium: no gradients, no flow, nothing driving — gas in a closed cylinder left alone. Steady state: gradients fixed in space, flow continuous — a running steam turbine with constant inlet and outlet temperatures.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Simulators use steady-state blocks with equilibrium flashes inside. Compressors and turbines are first sized as quasi-static, then corrected by efficiency. Finite-time thermodynamics studies machines running at finite rate. Search: endoreversible thermodynamics, quasi-static approximation in turbomachinery, equilibrium flash calculation.

1.5 Zeroth law, temperature and its measurement

Temperature is the property that two bodies share when no heat flows between them. The zeroth law makes this usable: if a thermometer agrees with body A and also with body B, then A and B agree with each other, so we can compare them without touching them. That rule underlies every fever thermometer, every server-room cooling alarm and every air-conditioning check in an Indian train coach. This chapter states the law, defines temperature, and shows how a thermometer turns a changing property into a number on the Celsius or Kelvin scale.

You need: equilibrium ideas from 1.4 Thermodynamic equilibrium and quasi-static process.

The zeroth law and thermal equilibrium

Two bodies are in thermal equilibrium when, after contact through a diathermal wall, no observable change occurs in either body. A diathermal wall lets heat pass; an adiabatic wall does not. The zeroth law states: if body A is in thermal equilibrium with body C, and body B is in thermal equilibrium with body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. C is usually a thermometer.

Use the law as a comparison rule. A thermometer placed in A reaches the same reading as in B only when A and B share the same temperature. Temperature is the property that is equal in thermal equilibrium. Heat flows only when temperatures differ; the direction is from higher temperature to lower temperature. Equal readings mean no net heat flow, not that the bodies hold equal internal energy.

Temperature, thermometric property and scales

A thermometer needs a thermometric property: a measurable property that changes with thermal state. Common examples are the length of a mercury column, the pressure of a gas held at fixed volume, the electrical resistance of a platinum wire, and the emf of a thermocouple.

The principle of thermometry chooses a property X and defines an empirical temperature θ by a linear relation.

θ = aX + b

θ is empirical temperature; X is the thermometric property; a and b are constants fixed by two fixed points.

The Celsius scale fixes two points at 1 atm: the ice point at 0 °C (ice and water in equilibrium) and the steam point at 100 °C (water boiling). The interval is divided into 100 equal parts. The Kelvin scale uses the same size degree and shifts the zero: 0 °C = 273.15 K and 100 °C = 373.15 K.

T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15

T(K) is absolute temperature in kelvin; t(°C) is Celsius temperature; 273.15 is the offset in kelvin.

A constant-volume gas thermometer measures the pressure of a fixed mass of gas at fixed volume. Its readings depend slightly on the gas, but as pressure tends to zero all gases agree. That limit gives the ideal gas temperature scale. The thermodynamic temperature scale is developed in Unit 3, Thermodynamic temperature scale. Some syllabi call this topic 'concept of temperature and principles of thermometry'.

Using the zeroth law in practice

  1. Place the thermometer in body A. Wait until the reading is steady; the thermometer is then in thermal equilibrium with A. Record θ_A.
  2. Place the same thermometer in body B. Wait for a steady reading and record θ_B.
  3. If θ_A = θ_B, the zeroth law says A and B are in thermal equilibrium. If the readings differ, connecting A and B causes net heat flow from the higher reading to the lower reading.

The waiting time matters. A thermometer shows its own temperature, not the body's, until equilibrium is reached. A clinical thermometer held under the tongue for too short a time reads low. A sensor strapped to a server rack gives the rack temperature only after its probe settles. Calibration fixes the scale; the zeroth law fixes the comparison.

Worked example

Question. A thermometer reads 25.0 °C in body A and 25.0 °C in body B. Body A and body B are then placed in contact. Using the zeroth law, find the direction of heat flow and convert 25.0 °C to kelvin.

  1. The two readings are equal: 25.0 °C in A and 25.0 °C in B.
  2. By the zeroth law, A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
  3. In thermal equilibrium there is no net heat flow when A and B are placed in contact. The direction is therefore 'no net flow'; the temperatures are equal.
  4. Convert using T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15 = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K.

Answer. No net heat flows between A and B; the temperature is 298.15 K.

Exam traps

  • Temperature is not heat. Equal thermometer readings mean no net heat flow; body A and body B can have different masses and different internal energies.
  • The Kelvin offset is 273.15, not 273. Use 273.15 for precise conversion; rounding early shifts the answer.
  • The ice point is equilibrium ice and water at 1 atm, not any mixture of ice and salt. The steam point is water boiling at 1 atm, not steam at any pressure.
  • A thermometer reads correctly only after it reaches thermal equilibrium with the body. A reading taken while the thermometer is still warming is not the body temperature.
  • The zeroth law gives equality of temperature. It does not by itself give an absolute zero or a heat quantity.

1.6 Work and heat: definitions and sign convention

Work and heat are not things stored inside a system. They are energy transfers across a boundary, and each is counted only while the transfer happens. A boundary that blocks heat is adiabatic; one that lets heat cross is diathermic. Before calculation, fix one sign convention. Here, heat entering a system is positive (Q > 0), and work done by the system is positive (W > 0). The surroundings see the opposite signs. This is the language of engines, refrigerators and power plants, where the direction of energy flow decides whether it is useful output or a cost.

You need: 1.2 for system and boundary, and 1.4 for equilibrium and quasi-static paths.

What work and heat are

Work is energy transfer by a force moving its point of application. In thermodynamics the force often comes from a pressure on a moving boundary; other modes, such as shaft work and electrical work, follow the same pattern. Work has symbol W and unit joule (J); 1 kJ = 1000 J. Work is not a property stored in the system. It exists only while the force acts and the boundary moves.

W = ∫ F dx

F is force in N, x is displacement of the point of application in m, and W is work in J.

Heat is energy transfer because of a temperature difference between system and surroundings. Heat has symbol Q and unit J or kJ. A hot vessel in a cooler room loses heat; the energy crossing is Q, not a property called heat inside the vessel. Heat transfer stops when temperatures equal. Small amounts are written δQ and δW; Q and W themselves are path functions, not state properties (1.3). Some syllabi call these heat and work interactions. Both are boundary phenomena: choose the boundary first, then Q and W have meaning.

Sign convention used in this book

Take heat entering the system as positive: Q > 0. Take work done by the system on the surroundings as positive: W > 0. Then Q < 0 means heat leaves the system, and W < 0 means work is done on the system. The surroundings get the opposite signs, because a transfer out of the system is a transfer into the surroundings. Engineering texts often use this convention because engines and turbines produce work; a positive W is useful output. Fix the convention before substituting numbers. A statement such as W = −20 kJ is meaningless until you say which side is positive.

Q = Q_in − Q_out, W = W_out − W_in

Q_in is heat entering in kJ, Q_out is heat leaving in kJ, W_out is work done by the system in kJ, and W_in is work done on the system in kJ.

TransferPositive whenSymbol
Heatenters systemQ > 0
Workleaves systemW > 0

The combination Q − W appears in the worked example as the net transfer into the system; Unit 2 connects it to internal energy.

Adiabatic and diathermic boundaries

An adiabatic boundary blocks heat transfer: Q = 0 across it. A thermos flask and a thick insulated steam pipe are approximations. Adiabatic does not mean isolated; work can still cross, and mass can cross if the boundary is open. An isolated system has no heat, no work and no mass crossing (1.2). A diathermic boundary allows heat transfer, but it does not force it. A metal wall between two regions at the same temperature is diathermic and still has Q = 0. The word tells you what may cross, not what does cross in a given process. A movable boundary lets displacement work cross; a rigid boundary blocks it. Check both labels: thermal (adiabatic or diathermic) and mechanical (rigid or movable).

Path dependence and exam discipline

Q and W depend on the path between the same two states. Heat a gas slowly at constant pressure, or heat it quickly and let the pressure change; the end states can match while Q and W differ. This is why Q and W are not properties and why a table of properties cannot list them. It is also why the sign convention must be written at the top of a solution. If a problem says “work done on the system is 20 kJ” and your convention is W > 0 for work by the system, enter W = −20 kJ. If it says “heat rejected is 30 kJ”, enter Q = −30 kJ. State the convention, convert each phrase to a signed number, then calculate. Do not change the convention halfway.

Worked example

Question. A closed system receives 50 kJ of heat from a hot reservoir and does 20 kJ of work on the surroundings. Take heat added to the system as positive and work done by the system as positive. (a) Write Q and W with signs. (b) Find the net energy transfer Q − W.

  1. Choose the convention: Q > 0 for heat into the system; W > 0 for work by the system on the surroundings.
  2. Heat received: 50 kJ enters, so Q = +50 kJ.
  3. Work done by the system: 20 kJ leaves as work, so W = +20 kJ.
  4. Substitute: Q − W = (+50 kJ) − (+20 kJ) = +30 kJ.
  5. The positive result means net energy enters the system by these two interactions: 30 kJ.

Answer. Q = +50 kJ, W = +20 kJ, and Q − W = +30 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Using W > 0 for work done on the system without saying so. In the convention here, work done on the system is W = −20 kJ, not +20 kJ.
  • Treating heat as a property stored in the system. Q is energy crossing the boundary; internal energy is the property, covered in Unit 2.
  • Writing Q = +30 kJ for “30 kJ of heat rejected”. Rejected heat leaves the system, so Q = −30 kJ in this convention.
  • Confusing adiabatic with isolated. An adiabatic boundary has Q = 0; an isolated system also has W = 0 and no mass crossing.
  • Forgetting units in the sign statement. A number like W = 20 is incomplete; write W = +20 kJ and state the convention.

1.7 Displacement work and other work modes

Work crosses a boundary when a force moves through a distance. In a gas, the force comes from pressure on a moving piston. This is displacement work, also called boundary work or p dV work. It appears in engines, compressors and steam turbines. The same force × displacement pattern covers a spring, a charged capacitor and a liquid film. This chapter shows how to compute each from a path, how to read the sign, and why unit consistency decides the marks.

You need: 1.4 for quasi-static paths and 1.6 for the work sign convention.

Displacement work is the area under the p–V path

Displacement work is the work done when a boundary moves against pressure. Take a gas in a piston–cylinder as the system. Let p be the absolute pressure in pascal (Pa) and V the volume in cubic metre (m³). In a quasi-static process the pressure is uniform inside at every instant, and the path can be drawn on a p–V diagram. The work done by the system is the area under that path.

W = ∫ p dV (from V₁ to V₂)

W is work done by the system in joule (J); p is absolute pressure in Pa; V is volume in m³; V₁ and V₂ are the initial and final volumes.

The area sign follows the direction. Expansion from left to right gives positive work by the system. Compression from right to left gives negative work by the system. A path that goes out and back encloses an area; the net work is the enclosed area with its sign. Work depends on the path, not only on the end states (see 1.3). For a constant-pressure process, p comes out of the integral: W = p(V₂ − V₁). If p is in kPa and V in m³, the answer is in kJ because 1 kPa × 1 m³ = 1 kJ.

Spring, electric and surface-tension work

Three non-flow work modes follow the force × displacement pattern. Spring or elastic work first. A spring with spring constant k, in N/m, is stretched from extension x₁ to x₂, in m. The work input to the spring is:

W = ½k(x₂² − x₁²)

W is work input to the spring in J; k is the spring constant in N/m; x₁ and x₂ are the initial and final extensions in m. Work done by the spring is −W.

Electric work. Move charge dq through a potential difference φ. The work input is:

W = ∫ φ dq

W is electric work input in J; φ is the electric potential difference in volt (V); q is charge in coulomb (C); dq is a small charge.

For a capacitor, q = Cφ, so charging from 0 to φ gives W = ½Cφ², where C is capacitance in farad (F). Surface-tension work. Stretch a liquid surface by dA at surface tension γ. The work input is:

W = ∫ γ dA

W is surface-tension work input in J; γ is surface tension in N/m; A is surface area in m²; dA is a small area change.

For a soap film, A must include both surfaces, so a factor 2 appears. Magnetic work appears in some syllabi as a field–magnetisation integral; this chapter only needs recognition. Compression is not a separate formula: it is displacement work with V₂ < V₁ and a negative sign.

Signs, units and a safe method

Use this order in an exam.

  1. Mark the system boundary and the process path.
  2. Write p as a function of V, or note that p is constant.
  3. Integrate from V₁ to V₂ for displacement work.
  4. Attach the sign: expansion positive, compression negative.
  5. Convert units before writing the final answer.

Table 1 lists the work modes. The area under a p–V line has units Pa × m³ = J. A common unit slip is p in kPa with V in m³; the number is then in kJ, not J. A second slip is using gauge pressure in p dV. Thermodynamic work uses absolute pressure. A third slip is treating the area of a closed loop as zero. A clockwise loop on a p–V diagram gives positive net work; an anticlockwise loop gives negative net work.

Work modeFormulaSymbols
DisplacementW = ∫ p dVp = pressure, V = volume
SpringW = ½k(x₂² − x₁²)k = spring constant, x = extension
ElectricW = ∫ φ dqφ = potential difference, q = charge
Surface tensionW = ∫ γ dAγ = surface tension, A = area

Worked example

Question. A gas expands quasi-statically at constant pressure 200 kPa from V₁ = 0.10 m³ to V₂ = 0.25 m³. Find the displacement work W = ∫ p dV and state its sign.

  1. Identify the path: p = 200 kPa at every state, so p is constant.
  2. Write the integral: W = ∫ p dV from V₁ to V₂.
  3. Take p outside: W = p(V₂ − V₁).
  4. Volume change: V₂ − V₁ = 0.25 − 0.10 = 0.15 m³.
  5. Work: W = 200 × 0.15 = 30 kPa·m³.
  6. Convert: 1 kPa·m³ = 1 kJ, so W = 30 kJ.
  7. Sign: volume increases, so the gas does work on the surroundings; W is positive.

Answer. W = +30 kJ; work is done by the system.

Exam traps

  • Using p in kPa and V in m³ and calling the result joule. 200 kPa × 0.15 m³ = 30 kJ, not 30 J.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure in ∫ p dV. Absolute pressure is required.
  • For compression, writing a positive number because the arithmetic is positive. p dV is negative when V falls; work done by the system is negative.
  • Treating W = p(V₂ − V₁) as valid for every process. It is valid only when p is constant.
  • Forgetting that a soap film has two surfaces when using W = ∫ γ dA; use total area.

Takeaways

  1. Thermodynamics can be built either from molecular behaviour (microscopic) or from bulk measurable properties (macroscopic); engineering uses the macroscopic route.
  2. The boundary you draw decides everything: whether mass crosses makes the system closed or open, whether heat crosses makes the wall diathermic or adiabatic.
  3. Intensive properties are size-independent, extensive ones scale with mass, and specific properties are extensive properties per kilogram.
  4. A state is a snapshot, fixed by enough properties; a path through states is a process, and a closed path is a cycle.
  5. Equilibrium means no unbalanced force, temperature difference or chemical tendency; only near-equilibrium states can be treated with simple property relations.
  6. A quasi-static process is an idealised slow process through near-equilibrium states, which is why p–V work can be integrated as ∫p dV.
  7. The zeroth law is what makes temperature a measurable, comparable quantity rather than a personal feeling.
  8. Every thermometer works by assuming one thermometric property varies linearly enough with temperature; each scale differs only in the fixed points and the property chosen.
  9. Work and heat are energy in transit across a boundary, not stored properties, so they depend on the path and their signs must be fixed before any calculation.
  10. Displacement work W = ∫p dV is only the most common work mode; shaft, spring, elastic, electric, magnetic and surface-tension work all follow force × displacement.

Apply

Apply A gas expands in a piston-cylinder from V₁ = 0.10 m³ to V₂ = 0.30 m³. Evaluate the displacement work if the path is (a) constant pressure p = 200 kPa, and (b) pV = constant with p₁ = 200 kPa. Comment on the two answers. 12 marks

W = ∫p dV for each path; (a) W = p(V₂ − V₁) = 200 × 10³ × 0.20 = 40 kJ; (b) W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = 20 000 × ln 3 ≈ 22 kJ; Same end states, different work: work is a path function; Units carried throughout.

Apply A gas in a cylinder is compressed with 40 kJ of work input while 25 kJ of heat leaves it to the surroundings. Fix a sign convention, write down the values of W and Q with signs, and state the net energy change of the gas. 12 marks

Convention stated: Q positive into system, W positive by system; W = −40 kJ, Q = −25 kJ; Energy balance ΔE = Q − W = −25 + 40 = +15 kJ; Physical meaning: internal energy rises by 15 kJ; Warning that reversing the convention flips both signs but not the physics.

Apply Write the expressions for work done in stretching an elastic rod, in blowing a soap bubble against surface tension, and in charging a capacitor or moving a charge through a potential difference. Explain the force × displacement pattern in each. 10 marks

Generalised work δW = F dx or Y dX; Elastic: δW = −σ dA or F dL with tension and length change; Surface tension: δW = σ dA, dA for a bubble film (two surfaces); Electrical: δW = V dq or E di dt; Identify the intensive property (force, surface tension, potential) and the conjugate displacement.

Apply Two bodies at 300 K and 400 K are separated first by a diathermic wall and then by an adiabatic wall. Describe what happens in each case, name the process in the diathermic case for the hotter body, and state the final condition. 10 marks

Diathermic: heat flows from 400 K body to 300 K body until temperatures are equal; Final temperature between 300 K and 400 K, value from energy balance; Thermal equilibrium reached, zeroth law applies; Adiabatic: no heat flow, temperatures stay different; Adiabatic wall makes the two parts separate systems.

Apply A constant-volume gas thermometer reads a pressure of 100.0 kPa at the ice point and 136.6 kPa at the steam point. Find the temperature when the pressure is 118.0 kPa, assuming p varies linearly with T. State the thermometric property used. 12 marks

Linear relation T = 273.15 (p/p_ice) or the two-point form; T = 273.15 + 100 × (118.0 − 100.0)/(136.6 − 100.0); ≈ 273.15 + 100 × 0.4918 ≈ 322 K; Thermometric property = pressure of fixed mass of gas at constant volume; Comment on ideal-gas temperature scale and why real gas readings need correction.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Using p–V diagrams, show that work and heat are path functions while pressure, volume and temperature are point functions. Explain why a cycle returns zero net change of properties but not necessarily zero net work or heat. 13 marks

Two different paths between the same states give different areas and hence different W; Properties depend only on the state, so ∮dp = ∮dV = ∮dT = 0; ∮δW = net area enclosed by the cycle, generally not zero; ∮δQ = ∮δW for a cycle; Use of exact versus inexact differential notation; Statement that heat and work exist only during a process.

Evaluate Assess why the zeroth law is stated as a law at all. Discuss what would fail in thermometry without transitivity of thermal equilibrium, and comment on the practical limits of a real thermometer. 13 marks

Law gives transitivity of 'is in equilibrium with'; Without it a temperature scale cannot be assigned consistently; Empirical temperature defined by a chosen thermometric property; Different thermometers agree only for ideal-gas scale; real gases deviate; Limits: finite thermometer disturbs the system, finite response time, limited range; Constant-volume gas thermometer as the practical standard.

Evaluate Contrast a quasi-static process with a real rapid process in an engine cylinder. Evaluate the error made in using W = ∫p dV for the real process, and state when the quasi-static assumption is acceptable. 13 marks

Quasi-static: infinite slowness, uniform properties, well-defined path; Real process: pressure gradients, turbulence, non-equilibrium states; W = ∫p dV uses the system pressure p, valid only if p is uniform; For rapid expansion against a known external pressure, use W = ∫p_ext dV; Error grows with speed of the process and with pressure non-uniformity; Acceptable when process time is much larger than relaxation time; uniform-property assumption stated explicitly.

Analyze Distinguish thermodynamic equilibrium from steady state using an open-system example such as a steam turbine or a running pump. Explain why a steady-flow device can be analysed with equilibrium property tables. 13 marks

Equilibrium: no change with time and no unbalanced driving force within the system; Steady state: properties at each point constant in time, but gradients and flow exist; Turbine inlet and outlet states steady while the flow is not in equilibrium internally; Local equilibrium assumption permits property tables at inlet and outlet; State the three parts of equilibrium: mechanical, thermal, chemical; Consequence: energy balance can be written between inlet and outlet only.

Glossary

Adiabatic wall
A boundary that prevents heat transfer completely.
Boundary
The surface, real or imaginary, that encloses the system and across which interactions are counted.
Control volume
A fixed region in space with an open boundary through which mass and energy flow; the basis of open-system analysis.
Cycle
A process or set of processes that returns the system to its initial state.
Diathermic wall
A boundary that permits heat transfer, allowing two bodies to reach the same temperature.
Displacement work
Work done as a system's volume changes against pressure, given by W = ∫p dV.
Empirical temperature
A temperature value assigned by a chosen thermometer and scale, valid through the thermometric property it uses.
Extensive property
A property that is proportional to the amount of matter, e.g. mass, volume, total energy.
Heat
Energy transferred across a boundary solely because of a temperature difference, measured in joules.
Intensive property
A property whose value does not change when the system is divided, e.g. pressure or temperature.
Path
The series of intermediate states passed through during a process.
Path function
A quantity whose value depends on the route taken between states, like heat and work.
Point function
A quantity whose change depends only on the end states, like a property; examples are p, V, T, U.
Process
A change of state, that is, the path taken from one equilibrium state to another.
Property
A characteristic of the system that can be measured or calculated, such as pressure, volume or temperature.
Quasi-static process
An idealised infinitely slow process in which every intermediate state is essentially in equilibrium.
State
The complete condition of a system described by its properties at one instant.
Steady state
A condition in which properties at every fixed point stay constant with time while mass continues to flow.
Surroundings
Everything outside the boundary of the system that can interact with it.
System
The fixed mass or fixed region chosen for study, separated from everything else by a boundary.
Temperature
The property that is equal for systems in thermal equilibrium; it decides the direction of heat flow.
Thermodynamic equilibrium
The condition in which mechanical, thermal and chemical driving forces are all balanced, so no change occurs with time.
Thermodynamics
The science of energy, its forms, its transformations, and the direction in which transformations can occur.
Thermometric property
Any property that changes in a known, repeatable way with temperature and is used to build a thermometer.
Work
Energy transferred across a boundary by an intensive force acting through a displacement, measured in joules.
Zeroth law
Two systems each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in thermal equilibrium with each other, which makes temperature a valid property.
