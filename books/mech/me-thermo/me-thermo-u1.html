1.1 Microscopic and macroscopic approaches

Thermodynamics can describe a gas in two ways. The microscopic approach follows the molecules: their positions, speeds and collisions, averaged with statistics. The macroscopic approach ignores single molecules and works with a few quantities a gauge or a thermometer can read: pressure p, volume V and temperature T. Engineering thermodynamics uses the macroscopic approach, and it rests on one assumption, the continuum: matter is treated as spread smoothly through space, so every small region has one density, one pressure and one temperature. This chapter shows why that works for a car tyre or a boiler drum and when it stops working.

You need: school physics ideas of force, pressure, energy and density. Systems and properties are defined in the next two chapters.

Two ways to describe the same gas

Take the air in a closed room. The microscopic approach (also called the statistical view) sees about 10²⁵ molecules in every cubic metre, each moving at a few hundred metres per second and colliding billions of times a second. It cannot follow each one, so it uses probability and averages.

The macroscopic approach (also called the classical view) treats the same air as a body with a handful of measurable properties: pressure p, volume V, temperature T, mass m and density ρ. It never asks what one molecule is doing. Its laws come from experiments on bulk matter.

The two are not rival theories. The microscopic view explains the meaning of the macroscopic quantities; the macroscopic view gives numbers you can design with.

From molecules to pressure and temperature

The link between the two views is an average. Pressure is the average rate at which molecules hand momentum to a wall, per unit area of wall. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the random molecular motion. A gauge on a tank cannot feel one molecule; it reads the steady average of an enormous number of impacts.

For a gas at ordinary conditions the number of molecules per cubic metre follows from p, T and the Boltzmann constant:

n = p / (kT)

n is the number density in molecules per m³; p is absolute pressure in Pa; k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; T is absolute temperature in K.

Between collisions a molecule travels on average a distance called the mean free path λ. For molecules modelled as hard spheres of diameter d:

λ = kT / (√2 π d² p)

λ is the mean free path in m; d is the effective molecular diameter in m; k, T and p as above. Halve the pressure and λ doubles.

The continuum and when it fails

Density at a point is defined by shrinking a small volume δV around the point and dividing the mass inside by the volume: ρ = lim δm/δV as δV shrinks towards δV′, the smallest volume that still holds enough molecules for a steady average. Below δV′ the count of molecules inside jumps up and down as molecules wander in and out, so δm/δV jumps too. Above it, the reading settles to one value. The continuum assumption says every region we care about is much larger than δV′, so properties vary smoothly from point to point and we can use calculus on them.

The size of the scatter follows from statistics: a cube holding N molecules on average gives a count that varies by about √N, a relative spread of 1/√N. Figure 1.1.1 plots this spread for room air.

The continuum fails when the length L of the region (a pipe diameter, a gap, a sensor) is not much larger than the mean free path. The ratio is the Knudsen number, Kn = λ / L. A common rule of thumb: Kn below about 0.01 means the continuum holds; Kn near 1 or above means the molecules hit the walls about as often as each other, and kinetic theory is needed. Vacuum chambers, the thin upper atmosphere and micro-channels are the usual cases.

Worked example

Question. Air in a room is at 27 °C and 101.325 kPa. Take R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K) for air and an effective molecular diameter d = 3.7 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Find (a) the density from the macroscopic relation ρ = p/(RT), (b) the number of molecules in 1 cm³, (c) the mean free path, and (d) the Knudsen number for a pipe of diameter 10 mm. Can the air in the pipe be treated as a continuum?

  1. Absolute temperature: T = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15 K; use T = 300 K, which changes no answer at three figures.
  2. (a) ρ = p/(RT) = 101.325 kPa ÷ (0.287 kJ/(kg·K) × 300 K) = 101.325 ÷ 86.1 = 1.177 kg/m³. The units work because kPa = kJ/m³.
  3. (b) n = p/(kT) = 101 325 Pa ÷ (1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K × 300 K) = 101 325 ÷ 4.1419 × 10⁻²¹ = 2.446 × 10²⁵ molecules per m³.
  4. 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³, so 1 cm³ holds 2.446 × 10²⁵ × 10⁻⁶ = 2.45 × 10¹⁹ molecules.
  5. (c) λ = kT/(√2 π d² p). Numerator: kT = 4.1419 × 10⁻²¹ J. Denominator: 1.4142 × 3.1416 × (3.7 × 10⁻¹⁰)² × 101 325 = 1.4142 × 3.1416 × 1.369 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 101 325 = 6.163 × 10⁻¹⁴ N.
  6. λ = 4.1419 × 10⁻²¹ ÷ 6.163 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 6.72 × 10⁻⁸ m = 67 nm.
  7. (d) Kn = λ/L = 6.72 × 10⁻⁸ m ÷ 0.010 m = 6.7 × 10⁻⁶.
  8. Kn is far below 0.01, so the air in the pipe is a continuum: p, ρ and T have one value at each point and the macroscopic approach applies.

Answer. ρ = 1.177 kg/m³; 2.45 × 10¹⁹ molecules per cm³; λ ≈ 67 nm; Kn ≈ 6.7 × 10⁻⁶, so the continuum assumption holds.

Worked example 2

Question. The same air is pumped down in a vacuum chamber to 1.0 Pa at 300 K and flows through a 10 mm tube. Find λ and Kn, and say whether the continuum still holds.

  1. λ is inversely proportional to p at fixed T, so λ = 6.72 × 10⁻⁸ m × (101 325 Pa ÷ 1.0 Pa) = 6.81 × 10⁻³ m = 6.8 mm.
  2. Kn = λ/L = 6.81 × 10⁻³ m ÷ 0.010 m = 0.68.
  3. Kn is close to 1, far above 0.01. A molecule crosses most of the tube before it meets another molecule, so a local density or pressure at a point is not well defined. The continuum fails; this flow needs the microscopic (kinetic) treatment.

Answer. λ ≈ 6.8 mm and Kn ≈ 0.68: the continuum assumption fails in the evacuated tube.

Exam traps

  • Pressure is caused by molecular impacts, but it is a macroscopic property: a gauge reads the average of an enormous number of impacts, never one molecule.
  • The continuum is not about the size of the object alone. A 10 mm tube is a continuum at 101 kPa (Kn ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁶) but not at 1 Pa (Kn ≈ 0.7): pressure matters through λ.
  • Use absolute temperature in K and absolute pressure in Pa in n = p/(kT) and λ = kT/(√2 π d² p). Writing 27 °C for T gives an answer eleven times too large.
  • Do not say the two approaches give different laws. They describe the same state; the macroscopic laws can be derived from the microscopic model, and they agree.

1.2 Systems: closed, open, isolated; control volume

Every thermodynamics problem starts by drawing a boundary. The system is what you choose to study; everything outside that can interact with it is the surroundings; the boundary is the surface, real or imaginary, fixed or moving, that separates them. What crosses the boundary decides the type of system: a closed system lets energy cross but no mass, an open system (control volume) lets both cross, and an isolated system lets neither. The same hardware, a pressure cooker or an LPG cylinder, can be any of these, depending on where you draw the line.

You need: the macroscopic view of a gas as a body with mass, volume and pressure.

System, surroundings and boundary

Choose a fixed amount of matter or a region in space; that is the system. The boundary may be a real wall (the steel shell of a boiler drum), a moving surface (the face of a piston), or an imaginary surface drawn across a pipe where steam enters a turbine. Mass, heat and work are counted only where they cross this boundary, so the boundary must be drawn and labelled before any calculation.

A boundary is described by what it lets through. A diathermic wall lets heat pass; an adiabatic wall does not. A rigid wall cannot move, so no work is done by moving it, though a shaft or a wire can still carry work across it. A movable wall, like a piston face, lets displacement work cross.

Closed, open and isolated systems

Closed system (control mass). No mass crosses the boundary, so the mass inside is fixed. Heat and work may cross, and the boundary may move. Gas in a sealed piston–cylinder is the standard example: the piston can rise and the cylinder can be heated, but the same kilograms of gas stay inside.

Open system (control volume). Mass crosses the boundary, and energy may cross both as heat and work and carried by the moving mass. The boundary, called the control surface, is usually fixed around a device: a compressor, a steam turbine, a car radiator, a boiler. Some syllabi use the words open system and control volume for the same thing.

Isolated system. No mass, no heat and no work crosses. It is an ideal limit: a well-insulated, rigid, sealed flask comes close. The system and its surroundings taken together form an isolated system, an idea the second law uses later.

The mass inside any system follows a simple balance over a period of time:

m₂ = m₁ + mᵢ − mₑ

m₁ and m₂ are the masses inside at the start and end, in kg; mᵢ is the mass that enters and mₑ the mass that leaves during the period, in kg. A closed system has mᵢ = mₑ = 0, so m₂ = m₁.

Choosing the boundary, and steady running

The classification belongs to the boundary, not to the hardware. When air leaks from a tank, a boundary around the tank is crossed by mass, so that system is open. A boundary drawn around only the air that will stay in the tank is crossed by no mass at all, so that system is closed, but its boundary moves as the air expands to fill the tank. Both choices are correct; pick the one that makes the calculation easiest. Figure 1.2.1 shows the two choices for the worked example.

Open systems are usually analysed at steady state: at each fixed point inside the control volume, the properties do not change with time, and the mass inside stays constant because the inflow equals the outflow. A running turbine is steady while its inlet and outlet readings hold for hours. Steady state is not equilibrium; equilibrium has no flows and no gradients at all. Filling or emptying a tank is unsteady because the mass inside changes; the energy balance for both cases comes with the first law for control volumes.

Worked example

Question. A rigid steel tank holds 2.0 kg of compressed air. A valve is opened until 0.50 kg of air has escaped, and during this time 30 kJ of heat leaves through the tank wall. (a) Take the tank (wall and valve opening) as the boundary: does mass cross, does energy cross, and what type of system is it? Find the final mass. (b) Now take as the system the air that is still in the tank at the end. What type of system is that? (c) Is either system isolated?

  1. (a) Boundary: the inner surface of the tank wall plus a surface across the valve opening. It is fixed in space.
  2. Mass balance: m₂ = m₁ + mᵢ − mₑ = 2.0 + 0 − 0.50 = 1.5 kg. Since mₑ = 0.50 kg is not zero, mass crosses the boundary.
  3. Energy crosses in two ways: 30 kJ of heat leaves through the wall, and the escaping 0.50 kg of air carries its own energy out through the valve. The tank is rigid, so no boundary work is done by its walls.
  4. Mass crosses, so the tank is an open system, a control volume. Its boundary is fixed but its mass changes from 2.0 kg to 1.5 kg, so the process is unsteady.
  5. (b) Boundary: a surface wrapped around the 1.5 kg of air that will remain. At the start this air fills three quarters of the tank (1.5 ÷ 2.0 = 0.75); at the end it fills the whole tank.
  6. No air crosses this surface, so the mass stays 1.5 kg: a closed system, a control mass. Its boundary moves outward as the air expands and pushes the escaping air ahead of it, so this system does work on the escaping air.
  7. (c) Neither system is isolated: heat leaves both, mass leaves the first and work leaves the second.

Answer. (a) Open (control volume), final mass 1.5 kg, with 0.50 kg and 30 kJ of heat crossing. (b) Closed (control mass) of 1.5 kg with a moving boundary. (c) Neither is isolated.

Exam traps

  • Closed is not isolated. A closed system can exchange heat and work; only an isolated system blocks mass, heat and work.
  • Rigid does not mean adiabatic. A rigid steel tank still passes heat through its wall; rigid only rules out boundary work by the wall.
  • In an open system the mass that crosses also carries energy. When the 0.50 kg escapes, heat is not the only energy that leaves.
  • A sealed system is closed, not isolated, unless its wall is also adiabatic and no work crosses.
  • Steady state is not equilibrium: a steady turbine has flow and temperature differences inside, constant in time.

1.3 Properties, state, path, process and cycle

A property is any quantity that has a definite value when the system is in a given condition, such as pressure, temperature or specific volume. The set of property values at one instant is the state. A change from one state to another is a process, the series of states passed through is its path, and a series of processes that brings the system back to its first state is a cycle. The state postulate says how many properties you must know to fix a state, an equation of state links them, and a state diagram turns every state into a point and every slow process into a line. Heat and work, unlike properties, depend on the path.

You need: systems and boundaries. Equilibrium, which a state needs, is the next chapter.

Properties: intensive, extensive, specific

Split a system in equilibrium into two equal halves. A property that keeps its value in each half is intensive: pressure p, temperature T, density ρ. A property that halves is extensive: mass m, volume V, internal energy U, enthalpy H. An extensive property divided by mass is a specific property, written in lower case, and behaves like an intensive one.

v = V / m = 1 / ρ

v is specific volume in m³/kg; V is volume in m³; m is mass in kg; ρ is density in kg/m³. In the same way u = U/m and h = H/m in kJ/kg.

A property depends only on the state, never on how the system got there. That gives a test: a quantity is a property if, and only if, its change between two states is the same for every path. Its change around any cycle is zero: ∮dX = 0.

The state postulate and the equation of state

How many properties fix a state? For a simple compressible system, one in which the only important way of doing work is volume change (no electric, magnetic, surface-tension or motion effects), the state postulate answers: two independent intensive properties fix the state. Once you know p and v, or p and T, or T and v, every other property has one value, which you read from a table or a chart or calculate.

Two properties are independent when one can change while the other is held constant. In a single phase, p and T are independent. During boiling they are not: water at 101.325 kPa boils at about 100 °C whatever its dryness, so p and T together do not fix how much is liquid and how much vapour. A second property such as specific volume or dryness fraction is needed; pure substances and steam tables develop this.

An equation of state is a formula that links p, v and T. The simplest is the ideal gas equation, good for air and most gases at low pressure and high temperature:

pv = RT, pV = mRT, R = R̄ / M

p is absolute pressure in kPa; v is specific volume in m³/kg; T is absolute temperature in K; R is the gas constant in kJ/(kg·K); R̄ = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K) is the universal gas constant; M is the molar mass in kg/kmol. For air M ≈ 28.97, so R = 8.314 ÷ 28.97 = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K).

Given any two of p, v and T, the equation gives the third: the state postulate at work.

State diagrams: states as points, processes as lines

Because two properties fix the state, a state can be drawn as a point on a graph of any two properties: a state diagram. The common ones are the p–v (or p–V) diagram, the T–v diagram and the p–T diagram; the T–s and h–s diagrams follow once entropy is defined. A state drawn this way is a state point.

A process made of equilibrium states, a quasi-static process, appears as a continuous line between two state points. Processes are named by the property held constant, and each has a typical line:

  • isochoric (constant volume): a vertical line on p–V;
  • isobaric (constant pressure): a horizontal line on p–V;
  • isothermal (constant temperature): for an ideal gas the curve pV = constant on p–V;
  • adiabatic (no heat transfer): a steeper curve on p–V for a slow ideal-gas process.

A cycle is a closed loop.

Path functions and cycles

Point functions (properties) change by the same amount on every path between two states. Path functions, heat Q and work W, do not: they are energy crossing the boundary during a process, and their amount depends on the route. Their small amounts are written δQ and δW, not dQ and dW, to show they are inexact differentials. On a p–V diagram the work of a slow process is the area under its line, so two different lines between the same states give different work (Figure 1.3.1).

After one full cycle every property is back to its first value, so ΔT = 0, Δp = 0, ΔV = 0 and ΔU = 0 over the cycle. The net work need not be zero: it equals the area enclosed by the loop, positive when the loop is traced clockwise on a p–V diagram. That non-zero net work is what every engine is built to deliver.

Worked example

Question. Air (R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K)) in a cylinder is taken round the cycle 1→2→3→4→1: state 1 is 100 kPa, 0.20 m³, 300 K; heating at constant volume to 200 kPa (state 2); expansion at constant pressure to 0.30 m³ (state 3); cooling at constant volume to 100 kPa (state 4); compression at constant pressure back to state 1. (a) Find the mass of air and its specific volume at state 1. (b) Find the temperature at states 2, 3 and 4. (c) Show that the net change of temperature over the cycle is zero. (d) Compare the work done along 1→2→3 and along 1→4→3, and find the net work of the cycle.

  1. (a) From pV = mRT at state 1: m = p₁V₁/(RT₁) = (100 × 0.20) ÷ (0.287 × 300) = 20 ÷ 86.1 = 0.2323 kg. Specific volume v₁ = V₁/m = 0.20 ÷ 0.2323 = 0.861 m³/kg. Check with pv = RT: 100 × 0.861 = 86.1 = 0.287 × 300.
  2. (b) The mass is fixed, so T = pV/(mR) with mR = 0.2323 × 0.287 = 0.06667 kJ/K. State 2 (200 kPa, 0.20 m³): T₂ = 40 ÷ 0.06667 = 600 K. State 3 (200 kPa, 0.30 m³): T₃ = 60 ÷ 0.06667 = 900 K. State 4 (100 kPa, 0.30 m³): T₄ = 30 ÷ 0.06667 = 450 K.
  3. Each state was fixed by two properties, p and V for the known mass, and the equation of state gave the third: the state postulate in use.
  4. (c) Temperature changes: 1→2 +300 K, 2→3 +300 K, 3→4 −450 K, 4→1 −150 K. Sum: 300 + 300 − 450 − 150 = 0 K. Temperature is a property, so its net change round a cycle is zero.
  5. (d) Work on each line, from the area under it (W = p ΔV for constant pressure, zero for constant volume): W₁₂ = 0; W₂₃ = 200 × (0.30 − 0.20) = 20 kJ; W₃₄ = 0; W₄₁ = 100 × (0.20 − 0.30) = −10 kJ.
  6. Path 1→2→3: W = 0 + 20 = 20 kJ. Path 1→4→3: W = W₁₄ + W₄₃ = 100 × (0.30 − 0.20) + 0 = 10 kJ. The same two end states, the same ΔT = 900 − 300 = 600 K on both paths, but different work: work is a path function.
  7. Net work of the cycle: 0 + 20 + 0 − 10 = 10 kJ, the area of the rectangle, 100 kPa × 0.10 m³. The loop runs clockwise on the p–V diagram, so the net work is done by the air.

Answer. m = 0.2323 kg and v₁ = 0.861 m³/kg; T₂ = 600 K, T₃ = 900 K, T₄ = 450 K; ∮dT = 0; W = 20 kJ on 1→2→3 and 10 kJ on 1→4→3; net cycle work 10 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Heat and work are not properties. A sentence such as 'the heat in the gas' is wrong; the gas has internal energy, and heat is energy crossing the boundary.
  • Two properties fix the state only if they are independent. During a phase change p and T are tied together, so use p and dryness fraction (or v) instead.
  • Specific volume v is in m³/kg and volume V is in m³; pv = RT uses v, pV = mRT uses V. Mixing them gives answers off by the mass.
  • Zero net change of every property over a cycle does not mean zero net work or heat: the cycle in Figure 1.3.1 delivers 10 kJ.

1.4 Thermodynamic equilibrium and quasi-static process

A system is in thermodynamic equilibrium when nothing inside it, or between it and its surroundings, is pushing for change: no unbalanced force, no temperature difference, no unfinished reaction. Only then does it have one pressure and one temperature, so only then can its state be drawn as a point. A quasi-static process keeps the system infinitely close to equilibrium all the time by changing it in tiny steps, so the process becomes a continuous line of state points on a p–V diagram and its work can be found as an area. Engine and compressor calculations begin with this idealisation and then correct for the real, faster process.

You need: state, path and state diagrams.

Three balances make one equilibrium

Thermodynamic equilibrium needs three kinds of balance at once.

  • Thermal equilibrium: one temperature throughout, equal to that of any surroundings it can exchange heat with. Otherwise heat flows and the state drifts.
  • Mechanical equilibrium: no unbalanced force. The pressure is uniform (apart from small hydrostatic differences) and any piston is at rest.
  • Chemical equilibrium: no reaction, mixing or diffusion still changing the composition. Some syllabi add phase equilibrium here: no net evaporation or melting.

For gas under a piston at rest, the forces on the piston balance. Upward is the gas pressure on the piston face; downward are the atmosphere, the weight of the piston and any load on it:

p A = pₐₜₘ A + (m + mₛ) g

p is the absolute gas pressure in Pa; A is the piston area in m²; pₐₜₘ is the atmospheric pressure in Pa; m is the piston mass and mₛ the load (sand) on it, in kg; g = 9.81 m/s². Leaving out pₐₜₘ is the most common mistake in these problems.

A practical test of equilibrium is to read the gauges, wait and read again: an isolated system whose readings do not change is in equilibrium. Constant readings alone are not enough when mass or energy is flowing, as the next section shows.

Equilibrium is not steady state

A steam turbine running at full load has steady readings: steam enters at, say, 500 °C and leaves at 60 °C, and each gauge holds still for hours. It is in steady state, not in equilibrium, because steam flows through it and a 440 °C temperature difference drives heat along its casing. Equilibrium has no flows and no gradients; steady state has flows and gradients that do not change with time. A useful exam check: if mass or energy is crossing the boundary, the system is not in equilibrium, however steady the readings.

Quasi-static: changing by tiny steps

Dump a heavy weight on a piston and the gas under it is squeezed fast: for a moment the gas next to the piston is at a higher pressure than the gas at the bottom, the piston overshoots and bounces, and eddies swirl. These in-between conditions are not equilibrium states. The gas has no single pressure, so they cannot be plotted as points and the process cannot be drawn as a line; only its start and end states, after everything settles, are known.

Now add the same load one grain of sand at a time, waiting after each grain. Each grain disturbs the gas by an infinitesimal amount, and the gas settles into a new equilibrium state before the next grain. The process passes through a continuous chain of equilibrium states. In the limit of infinitely small steps this is a quasi-static (quasi-equilibrium) process.

  1. Make the driving force infinitesimal: one more grain of sand to compress, or one grain removed (or the outside pressure lowered by a tiny dp) to expand.
  2. Wait until the system settles into a new equilibrium state.
  3. Repeat until the final state is reached; each settled state is one point on the p–V diagram.

Because every intermediate state has one p and one V, the process is a line, and the work done at the moving boundary is W = ∫p dV, the area under that line (see displacement work). Infinitely many steps would take infinite time, so a real process is only nearly quasi-static. It is a good approximation when the process is slow compared with the time the gas needs to settle. Pressure evens out at about the speed of sound, roughly 340 m/s in room air, so across a 0.1 m cylinder it takes about 0.3 ms; an engine stroke at 3000 rpm lasts 10 ms, which is why even engine strokes are first analysed as quasi-static.

Quasi-static is not the same as reversible

A process is reversible if system and surroundings can both be returned to their first states with no trace left anywhere. Every reversible process must be quasi-static, but a quasi-static process can still be irreversible. Push a piston in slowly against friction: the gas passes through equilibrium states, yet the friction turns work into heat that will not come back. Stir a gas slowly with a paddle while a piston moves: same result. Reversibility also needs no friction, no heat flow across a finite temperature difference, no unrestrained expansion and no mixing; the full treatment is in reversible and irreversible processes, with the second law.

1.5 Zeroth law, temperature and its measurement

Two bodies are in thermal equilibrium when, placed in contact through a wall that passes heat, neither changes any more. The zeroth law says that thermal equilibrium is shared: if body A and body B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. That makes temperature a property, the one quantity that is equal for bodies in thermal equilibrium, and it makes thermometers possible, since the thermometer is body C. This chapter states the law, shows how a thermometric property and fixed points turn it into a scale, and works two calibration problems that examiners like.

You need: thermal equilibrium and diathermic and adiabatic walls from systems.

The zeroth law and the idea of temperature

Zeroth law: when two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. It was named after the first and second laws had been numbered, because it is logically needed before them.

It sounds obvious, but it is an experimental fact, not a rule of logic: it is what lets a thermometer compare two bodies that never touch. Put the thermometer C in A and wait for its reading to stop changing; put it in B and wait again. If both readings are equal, the law says A and B would show no change if they were brought together, so no net heat would flow between them. The property that is equal is called temperature. If the readings differ, heat flows from the body with the higher reading to the one with the lower reading when they touch.

Equal temperatures do not mean equal energies. A bucket of water and a cup of water at 25 °C have the same temperature and very different internal energies.

Thermometric property and fixed points

A thermometer uses a thermometric property X, a quantity that changes steadily with how hot the body is: the length of a liquid column, the pressure of a gas held at constant volume, the resistance of a platinum wire, the emf of a thermocouple. The simplest scale assumes a straight-line link, θ = aX + b, and fixes a and b with two reproducible states, the fixed points. The traditional pair is the ice point (ice and air-saturated water at 1 atm, 0 °C) and the steam point (water boiling at 1 atm, 100 °C):

t = 100 × (X − Xᵢ) / (Xₛ − Xᵢ) °C

t is the temperature on this scale in °C; X is the property's reading; Xᵢ and Xₛ are its readings at the ice and steam points.

Different properties are not exactly linear in the same way, so two thermometers calibrated at the same fixed points agree at 0 and 100 °C but can disagree in between (second worked example). A scale that does not depend on the substance is needed.

Gas thermometers, the kelvin and the thermodynamic scale

A constant-volume gas thermometer comes closest. Its bulb is held at the triple point of water, the single state where ice, liquid water and vapour coexist (0.01 °C, about 0.61 kPa), and its pressure pₜₚ is read; then it is placed in the body and its pressure p is read. The ideal-gas temperature is:

T = 273.16 K × (p / pₜₚ)

T is in kelvin; p and pₜₚ are the gas pressures at the body and at the triple point of water, at the same volume. Strictly, the ratio is taken in the limit as the amount of gas, and so pₜₚ, tends to zero; in that limit every gas gives the same T.

The ideal-gas scale equals the thermodynamic (absolute) temperature scale, which the second law defines from the efficiency of reversible engines without any substance at all; that derivation sits with the thermodynamic temperature scale and the Carnot cycle. Since 2019 the kelvin has been defined by fixing the Boltzmann constant at exactly 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K, and the triple point of water is measured to be 273.16 K to within a fraction of a millikelvin. The Celsius scale is the kelvin scale shifted:

T (K) = t (°C) + 273.15,   t (°F) = 1.8 t (°C) + 32

a temperature difference of 1 K equals 1 °C; 1 °C of difference equals 1.8 °F.

1.6 Work and heat: definitions and sign convention

Energy crosses a system's boundary in two ways other than with flowing mass: as heat, driven by a temperature difference, and as work, every other kind of transfer, such as a moving piston, a turning shaft or an electric current. Neither is stored in the system; both exist only while they cross the boundary, and both depend on the path. Because energy can cross in either direction, every calculation must start with a sign convention. This book takes heat into the system as positive and work done by the system as positive, the usual engineering choice, since an engine's useful output then comes out positive.

You need: boundaries, path functions and temperature.

What work and heat are

In mechanics, work is a force moving through a distance, W = ∫F dx. Thermodynamics needs a definition that covers shafts and wires too: work is done by a system on its surroundings if the sole effect on everything outside the system could have been the raising of a weight. A turbine shaft could wind up a rope and lift a load; a battery could drive a motor that does the same. Neither is literally lifting a weight, but each could, so each is work.

Heat is energy that crosses the boundary because of a temperature difference between the system and its surroundings, from the hotter to the colder side. A cup of tea cooling in a room loses heat; once both are at the same temperature, heat transfer stops. Words such as 'the heat in the tea' are loose: the tea has internal energy, and heat is only the name of the transfer.

Both are measured in joules (kJ in most problems). Their rates are in watts: heat rate Q̇ in kW and power Ẇ in kW, with 1 kW = 1 kJ/s. Small amounts are written δQ and δW, not dQ and dW, because they are inexact differentials: ∫δW from state 1 to state 2 is W₁₂, the work of that path, not W₂ − W₁, since a system has no 'work at state 2'.

Adiabatic and diathermic boundaries; heat and work compared

An adiabatic boundary allows no heat transfer, Q = 0, though work can cross it; a thick lagged steam pipe or a vacuum flask comes close. A diathermic boundary allows heat transfer, but does not force it: a metal wall between two regions at the same temperature carries no heat. Adiabatic is not isolated: an isolated system also has W = 0 and no mass crossing.

The boundary decides which is which

Whether a transfer is heat or work depends on where the boundary is drawn. An electric heater immersed in a gas is the clearest case. If the boundary includes the heater, electricity crosses the boundary through the wire: that is electrical work done on the system. If the boundary encloses only the gas and leaves the heater outside, the hot heater surface warms the gas across a temperature difference: that is heat. 1.7 Displacement work and other work modes

Displacement work, also called boundary work, moving-boundary work or p dV work, is the work a gas does when it pushes a boundary such as a piston. For a quasi-static process it equals ∫p dV, the area under the process line on a p–V diagram, so it depends on the path. Engines, compressors and pumps run on it. Other work modes follow the same pattern of a driving force times a displacement: shaft work, electrical work, spring work, stretching a wire, surface-tension work, and the flow work that pushes mass across a control surface. This chapter gives the formula for each and works the common paths with numbers.

You need: the sign convention, quasi-static processes and the ideal gas equation.

Displacement work is the area under the p–V line

Let gas at absolute pressure p push a piston of area A through a small distance dx. The force is pA, so the work done by the gas is δW = pA dx = p dV, where dV = A dx is the change of volume. Adding up the small steps of a quasi-static process from state 1 to state 2:

W₁₂ = ∫ p dV (from V₁ to V₂)

W₁₂ is the work done by the system in kJ; p is absolute pressure in kPa; V is volume in m³ (1 kPa × 1 m³ = 1 kJ). The integral needs p as a function of V along the path, which exists only for a quasi-static process.

Expansion (V₂ > V₁) gives positive work, done by the gas; compression gives negative work, done on the gas. The area under the line from V₁ to V₂ is the work, so different paths between the same states give different work. Round a cycle, the net work is the enclosed area: positive for a clockwise loop on p–V, negative for an anticlockwise one.

For the polytropic path, p₂ = p₁(V₁/V₂)ⁿ. The exponent n covers the others as special cases: n = 0 is constant pressure, n = 1 is pV = C, and n → ∞ is constant volume. For a slow adiabatic process of an ideal gas, n equals the ratio of specific heats γ, about 1.4 for air; non-flow processes treat these paths with the first law.

In a free expansion, gas rushes from a tank into an evacuated space. The volume grows, but no surface is pushed against any resistance, so no work is done: W = 0 even though dV is not zero. ∫p dV does not apply because the process is not quasi-static; if a gas expands quickly against a known constant outside pressure pₑₓₜ, the work it does on the surroundings is pₑₓₜ (V₂ − V₁).

Worked example

Question. A gas at 800 kPa occupies 0.05 m³ in a cylinder. It expands quasi-statically to 0.20 m³. Find the work done by the gas if the path is (a) constant pressure, (b) pV = constant, (c) pV¹·³ = constant. Find the final pressure in (b) and (c) and comment.

  1. (a) W = p(V₂ − V₁) = 800 × (0.20 − 0.05) = 800 × 0.15 = 120 kJ.
  2. (b) p₁V₁ = 800 × 0.05 = 40 kJ, so p₂ = 40 ÷ 0.20 = 200 kPa. W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = 40 × ln 4 = 40 × 1.3863 = 55.45 kJ.
  3. (c) p₂ = p₁(V₁/V₂)¹·³ = 800 × (0.25)¹·³. ln(0.25) = −1.3863; × 1.3 = −1.8022; e⁻¹·⁸⁰²² = 0.16494. So p₂ = 800 × 0.16494 = 131.95 kPa.
  4. W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1) = (40 − 131.95 × 0.20) ÷ 0.3 = (40 − 26.39) ÷ 0.3 = 13.61 ÷ 0.3 = 45.37 kJ.
  5. Check (c) by summing: splitting 0.05 to 0.20 m³ into many thin strips and adding p ΔV gives 45.37 kJ, the same value.
  6. Comment: all three start at the same state and end at the same volume, but the work is 120, 55.45 and 45.37 kJ. The steeper the pressure falls, the smaller the area under the path (Figure 1.7.1). All are positive because the gas expands.

Answer. (a) 120 kJ; (b) 55.45 kJ with p₂ = 200 kPa; (c) 45.37 kJ with p₂ = 131.95 kPa.

Worked example 2

Question. Gas in a cylinder pushes a piston against the atmosphere (100 kPa) and a linear spring. As the volume grows from 0.040 m³ to 0.080 m³, the gas pressure rises linearly from 150 kPa to 350 kPa. Find the work done by the gas, the work done against the atmosphere and the work stored in the spring (neglect the piston's weight and friction).

  1. The pressure is linear in V, so W = ½(p₁ + p₂)(V₂ − V₁) = ½ × (150 + 350) × (0.080 − 0.040) = 250 × 0.040 = 10.0 kJ, done by the gas.
  2. Work against the atmosphere: pₐₜₘ ΔV = 100 × 0.040 = 4.0 kJ.
  3. The rest goes into the spring: 10.0 − 4.0 = 6.0 kJ.
  4. Check from the spring's side: the spring's share of the pressure is p − pₐₜₘ, rising linearly from 50 kPa to 250 kPa, so its work is ½ × (50 + 250) × 0.040 = 6.0 kJ. The two agree.

Answer. W = +10.0 kJ by the gas: 4.0 kJ against the atmosphere and 6.0 kJ stored in the spring.

Exam traps

  • kPa × m³ gives kJ, not J: 800 kPa × 0.15 m³ = 120 kJ.
  • W = p(V₂ − V₁) holds only at constant pressure; on a pV = C path the pressure falls and the work is smaller (55.45 kJ, not 120 kJ).
  • Use absolute pressure in ∫p dV. Gauge pressure gives only the work beyond what the atmosphere absorbs.
  • Free expansion does no work even though the volume increases; ∫p dV does not apply to it.
  • In (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1), find p₂ from pVⁿ = C first; do not use the isothermal p₂.

Takeaways

  1. Engineering thermodynamics uses the macroscopic approach, which rests on the continuum; the continuum holds when the Knudsen number λ/L is far below 0.01, as it is for room air in any ordinary pipe.
  2. Draw the boundary first: whether mass crosses decides closed or open, and the same tank can be either, depending on the boundary.
  3. A property depends only on the state; two independent intensive properties fix the state of a simple compressible system, and pv = RT links p, v and T for an ideal gas.
  4. On a state diagram a state is a point, a quasi-static process a line and a cycle a closed loop; every property returns to its first value round a cycle.
  5. Equilibrium needs thermal, mechanical and chemical balance; steady readings with flow mean steady state, not equilibrium.
  6. A quasi-static process passes through equilibrium states, so its work is the area ∫p dV; quasi-static does not mean reversible.
  7. The zeroth law makes temperature a property; thermometers need a thermometric property and fixed points, and the gas thermometer gives a scale independent of the substance.
  8. Heat and work are energy crossing the boundary, not stored in the system; fix the sign convention (Q in positive, W out positive) before any number.
  9. Displacement work depends on the path: from 800 kPa and 0.05 m³ to 0.20 m³ it is 120, 55.45 or 45.37 kJ along p = C, pV = C or pV¹·³ = C.

Points to cover. Definitions of system, boundary, surroundings; Closed: fixed mass, heat and work may cross (piston–cylinder); Open: mass and energy cross (turbine, compressor); Isolated: nothing crosses (ideal vacuum flask); Sketches with dashed boundaries and arrows; The boundary, not the hardware, decides the type.

Model answer.

  1. System, surroundings, boundary. A system is the quantity of matter or the region of space chosen for study. The surroundings are everything outside it that can interact with it. The boundary is the real or imaginary surface between them; it may be fixed (a tank wall) or moving (a piston face). Mass, heat and work are counted only where they cross the boundary.
  2. Closed system (control mass). No mass crosses the boundary, so the mass inside is constant; heat and work may cross and the boundary may move. Example: gas in a sealed piston–cylinder of an air compressor while both valves are shut. The gas can be heated (Q) and can push the piston (W), but its mass stays the same.

Example: a steam turbine, with steam entering at the inlet, leaving at the exhaust and shaft work leaving through the coupling. Other examples are a compressor, a boiler, a car radiator.

Understand Compare the microscopic and macroscopic approaches to thermodynamics, with the merits and limits of each. Explain the continuum assumption and show with numbers when it fails. 10 marks

  1. Microscopic approach. Matter is a collection of molecules.   2. Macroscopic approach. Matter is treated as a body described by a few quantities that instruments measure: pressure, volume, temperature, mass. No assumption about molecules is needed; the laws come from experiment. This is classical thermodynamics, the approach engineering uses.

Model answer.

  1. Statement. When two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. Thermal equilibrium means that, in contact through a diathermic wall, neither body changes any more.
  2. Temperature as a property. The law makes thermal equilibrium shared. All bodies in equilibrium with a given reference body can be given one label, and bodies with equal labels show no change when they touch. That label is temperature: the property that is equal for bodies in thermal equilibrium.
  3. The thermometer. The third body is the thermometer. Placed in body A until its reading stops changing, it is in equilibrium with A; placed in B, likewise.   3. Net energy change. The energy received by the gas through heat and work is Q − W = (−25) − (−40) = +15 kJ. With no other energy crossing, the energy stored in the gas, its internal energy, rises by 15 kJ (this is the first law for a closed system, ΔE = Q − W).
  4. Physical meaning. The piston pushed 40 kJ in; 25 kJ leaked out as heat because the compressed gas became hotter than the cylinder wall; 15 kJ stays in the gas, which ends hotter than it started.
  5. The other convention. Some books count work done on the system as positive and write ΔE = Q + W′. Then W′ = +40 kJ, Q = −25 kJ, and ΔE = −25 + 40 = +15 kJ. The numbers on the page change sign; the physics, a 15 kJ rise, does not.
  6. Summary.
    QuantityThis book (Q − W)Other convention (Q + W′)

    Heat 25 kJ outQ = −25 kJQ = −25 kJ

  7. Why the gas got hotter. For an ideal gas the internal energy depends on temperature only, so a 15 kJ rise in internal energy means a rise in temperature. Had the cylinder been perfectly lagged (adiabatic), all 40 kJ would have stayed in the gas and the temperature rise would have been larger.
  8. Where the boundary sits. The boundary is the gas; the piston face is part of it. The work input crosses at the moving piston face, and the heat crosses at the cylinder wall. Choosing the gas plus the cylinder metal as the system would change the heat figure (some energy would be stored in the warmed metal) but not the method.
  9. Exam habit. Write the convention in the first line, turn each phrase ('rejected', 'supplied', 'done on', 'done by') into a signed number, then substitute. Never change the convention part way.
  10. Check. If the gas had been compressed with no heat loss (adiabatic), ΔE would be +40 kJ; if all 40 kJ had leaked away as heat, ΔE would be 0. The answer +15 kJ lies between these limits, as it must.

Apply A constant-volume gas thermometer reads 100.0 kPa at the ice point and 136.6 kPa at the steam point. Find the temperature when the pressure is 118.0 kPa, on a linear scale. State the thermometric property, check the answer with the ideal-gas relation, and explain why gas thermometers are extrapolated to zero pressure. 12 marks

Points to cover. Thermometric property: pressure at constant volume; t = 100 (p − pᵢ)/(pₛ − pᵢ) = 49.18 °C = 322.33 K; Check: 273.15 × 118.0/100.0 = 322.32 K; Ratio 1.366 shows near-ideal behaviour; Extrapolation to zero pressure gives the ideal-gas scale.

Model answer.

  1. Thermometric property. The pressure of a fixed mass of gas held at constant volume. It rises steadily with temperature, which makes it a good thermometric property.
  2. The instrument. A glass or metal bulb holds a fixed mass of gas (often hydrogen, helium or nitrogen) and is joined by a thin tube to a mercury manometer. Before each reading the mercury reservoir is raised or lowered until the mercury touches a fixed mark, so the gas volume is the same at every reading; the manometer then gives the gas pressure.
  3. Linear two-point scale. Assume t = a p + b and fix a and b with the ice point (p = 100.0 kPa at 0 °C) and the steam point (p = 136.6 kPa at 100 °C): t = 100 × (p − pᵢ)/(pₛ − pᵢ).
  4. Substitute. t = 100 × (118.0 − 100.0) ÷ (136.6 − 100.0) = 100 × 18.0 ÷ 36.6 = 49.18 °C.
  5. In kelvin. T = 49.18 + 273.15 = 322.33 K.
  6. One-fixed-point check. For an ideal gas at constant volume p ∝ T, so T = Tᵢ × p/pᵢ = 273.15 × 118.0 ÷ 100.0 = 322.32 K. The two methods agree to 0.01 K.
  7. Is the gas nearly ideal? For an ideal gas the steam-to-ice pressure ratio should be 373.15 ÷ 273.15 = 1.3661. Here 136.6 ÷ 100.0 = 1.366, the same to four figures, so this thermometer behaves ideally over the range.
  8. Working.
    Pointp (kPa)t (°C)T (K)
    Ice point100.00273.15
    Unknown118.049.18322.33
    Steam point136.6100373.15
  9. Sensitivity. The pressure rises by 36.6 kPa over 100 °C, so 0.366 kPa per °C. A reading error of 0.1 kPa would shift the result by 0.1 ÷ 0.366 = 0.27 °C, which is why the volume is reset to the mark before each reading.
  10. Ideal-gas scale. Real gases deviate a little from p ∝ T, more at high pressure. Readings are therefore taken with less and less gas in the bulb and extrapolated to zero pressure; in that limit every gas gives the same temperature, the ideal-gas temperature, equal to the thermodynamic temperature. The modern reference point is the triple point of water, 273.16 K, with T = 273.16 K × p/pₜₚ.
  11. Answer. 49.18 °C (322.33 K).
Apply A frictionless piston of mass 30 kg and diameter 80 mm holds 0.500 litre of air in a vertical cylinder; the atmosphere is at 100 kPa. Find the air pressure. 10 kg of sand is then added grain by grain, the air staying at room temperature. Find the final pressure and volume, the piston drop and the work, and explain why the process is quasi-static. 12 marks

Points to cover. Force balance including the atmosphere: p₁ = 158.55 kPa; p₂ = 178.07 kPa; pV = C: V₂ = 0.4452 L, drop 10.9 mm; W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = −9.20 J; Each grain gives a new equilibrium state, so a line on p–V; Sudden loading would reach the same state by a different route.

Model answer.

  1. Forces on the piston. Up: the air pressure on the piston face, pA. Down: the atmosphere on the top face, pₐₜₘA, and the weight of the piston and anything on it, (m + mₛ)g. With no friction and the piston at rest, the two balance: pA = pₐₜₘA + (m + mₛ)g. Mechanical equilibrium fixes the air pressure; the volume then follows from the temperature.
  2. Piston area. A = πD²/4 = π × 0.080² ÷ 4 = 5.027 × 10⁻³ m².
  3. Equilibrium with the piston only. Forces on the piston at rest: pA = pₐₜₘA + mg, so p₁ = pₐₜₘ + mg/A = 100 + 30 × 9.81 ÷ (5.027 × 10⁻³ × 1000) = 100 + 58.55 = 158.55 kPa.
  4. With 10 kg of sand. p₂ = 100 + 40 × 9.81 ÷ (5.027 × 10⁻³ × 1000) = 100 + 78.07 = 178.07 kPa.
  5. Final volume. Sand is added grain by grain and the air stays at room temperature, so the process is quasi-static with pV constant: V₂ = V₁ p₁/p₂ = 0.500 × 158.55 ÷ 178.07 = 0.4452 L.
  6. Piston drop. ΔV = 0.500 − 0.4452 = 0.0548 L = 5.48 × 10⁻⁵ m³; drop = ΔV/A = 5.48 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 5.027 × 10⁻³ = 0.0109 m = 10.9 mm.

Apply Two copper blocks, 2.0 kg at 400 K and 3.0 kg at 300 K (c = 0.385 kJ/(kg·K)), are enclosed together in insulation. Describe what happens when they are separated by (a) a diathermic wall and (b) an adiabatic wall. Find the final temperature and the heat transferred in (a), and relate the result to the zeroth law. 10 marks

Points to cover. Diathermic: heat flows hot to cold until temperatures equal; Energy balance gives T = 340 K; Q = 46.2 kJ, checked from both blocks; Thermal equilibrium; zeroth law and a thermometer; Adiabatic: no heat flow, 400 K and 300 K stay.

Model answer.

  1. Diathermic wall. It lets heat pass. The 400 K block is hotter, so heat flows from it to the 300 K block until both reach one temperature. The hotter block cools and the colder one warms.
  2. Final temperature. The pair is enclosed by insulation and does no work, so the heat lost by the hot block equals the heat gained by the cold one: m₁c(T₁ − T) = m₂c(T − T₂). Same material, so c cancels: 2.0 × (400 − T) = 3.0 × (T − 300).
  3. Solve. 800 − 2.0T = 3.0T − 900, so 5.0T = 1700 and T = 340 K.
  4. Heat transferred. Q = m₁c(T₁ − T) = 2.0 × 0.385 × (400 − 340) = 46.2 kJ from the hot block to the cold block. Check: m₂c(T − T₂) = 3.0 × 0.385 × (340 − 300) = 46.2 kJ.
  5. Energy check for the pair. The two blocks inside the insulation form an isolated system: no heat or work crosses the outer boundary. The hot block's internal energy falls by 46.2 kJ and the cold block's rises by 46.2 kJ, so the total is unchanged, as it must be.
  6. Name of the final condition. Both blocks at 340 K: thermal equilibrium. By the zeroth law, a thermometer that reads 340 K (66.85 °C) in one block reads the same in the other.
  7. Process for the hotter block. It loses heat at nearly constant volume (a solid), so its process is a cooling at constant volume; for the hot block taken as the system, Q = −46.2 kJ and W ≈ 0.
  8. Why the final temperature is nearer 300 K. The cold block has more mass (3.0 kg against 2.0 kg) and the same specific heat, so it takes a larger heat capacity to warm; the common temperature is the mass-weighted average, (2.0 × 400 + 3.0 × 300) ÷ 5.0 = 340 K.
  9. Adiabatic wall. No heat crosses, so each block keeps its temperature: 400 K and 300 K indefinitely. They are not in thermal equilibrium with each other, yet nothing changes; an adiabatic wall makes them two separate systems, and the zeroth law says nothing about them.

Analyze Air (R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K)) at 150 kPa, 0.10 m³ and 300 K is taken round the cycle: heating at constant volume to 300 kPa, expansion at constant pressure to 0.25 m³, cooling at constant volume to 150 kPa, and compression at constant pressure back to the start. Using this cycle, show that temperature and volume are point functions while work and heat are path functions. 13 marks

Points to cover. Point and path functions defined; exact and inexact differentials; Mass 0.1742 kg; temperatures 300, 600, 1500, 750 K; ΔT and ΔV sum to zero round the cycle; Net work = enclosed area = 22.5 kJ; Two paths 1→3 give 45.0 and 22.5 kJ; ∮δQ = ∮δW = 22.5 kJ.

Evaluate Contrast a quasi-static process with a real rapid process in an engine cylinder. Evaluate, with numbers, the error made by using W = ∫p dV for a rapid process, and state when the quasi-static assumption is acceptable. 13 marks

Points to cover. Quasi-static: equilibrium states, a line on p–V; Rapid: no single pressure; use the pressure at the piston face; Numbers: 28.58 J slow against 31.23 J sudden, error 9.3 %; Free expansion: W = 0; Acceptable when the process is slow compared with pressure equalisation (about 0.3 ms against a 10 ms stroke).

Model answer.

  1. Quasi-static process. Infinitely slow: the system passes through equilibrium states, with one pressure and one temperature at every instant, so the path is a line on p–V and W = ∫p dV is the area under it.
  2. Real rapid process. In an engine cylinder at high speed, the gas near the piston is at a different pressure from the gas at the head, the charge swirls, and the piston may overshoot. These states have no single p, so ∫p dV along 'the' path has no meaning. The work actually done on the piston is ∫pₚ dV, where pₚ is the pressure on the piston face; for expansion against a constant outside pressure it is pₑₓₜ ΔV.
  3. Size of the error, with numbers. A piston (D = 100 mm, 50 kg) holds 1.000 L of air at 163.78 kPa with the atmosphere at 101.325 kPa. 25 kg of sand is added, and the air ends at 195.00 kPa and 0.8399 L at room temperature, by two routes:
    RouteWork on the airHow found
    Grain by grain (quasi-static)28.58 Jp₁V₁ ln(V₁/V₂) = 163.78 J × ln(1.000/0.8399) = 163.78 × 0.17448
    All 25 kg at once31.23 Jpₑₓₜ ΔV = 195.00 kPa × 0.16014 L
  4. Evaluate. Using ∫p dV for the sudden process would give 28.58 J, but 31.23 J was really supplied: an error of (31.23 − 28.58) ÷ 28.58 = 9.3 %. The extra 2.65 J was dissipated in the piston's bouncing and in eddies, and left as extra heat to the room.
  5. Free expansion: the extreme case. Gas expanding into a vacuum does no work at all, W = 0, while ∫p dV along any assumed path would give a positive number. The error is then 100 %.
  6. When the assumption is acceptable. When the process is slow compared with the time the gas needs to even out its pressure. That time is roughly the cylinder length divided by the speed of sound: 0.1 m ÷ 340 m/s ≈ 0.3 ms. An engine stroke at 3000 rpm takes 10 ms, about 30 times longer, so ideal cycle analysis is a fair first estimate; very fast processes such as a burst diaphragm are not.
  7. Practice. Engineers compute quasi-static work, then correct with measured efficiencies, or measure real indicator diagrams with fast pressure sensors and take their enclosed area.
  8. Conclusion. W = ∫p dV is exact only for quasi-static processes; for rapid ones use the pressure at the moving boundary, pₑₓₜ ΔV for a constant outside pressure, and expect the real work input to exceed the quasi-static value in compression (here by 9.3 %).
Analyze Distinguish thermodynamic equilibrium from steady state using a steam turbine as the example. Explain why a steady-flow device can still be analysed with equilibrium property tables. 13 marks

Points to cover. Equilibrium: thermal, mechanical, chemical; no change, no flow; Steady state: constant at each point, with flow and gradients; Turbine: steady but not in equilibrium; Comparison table; Local equilibrium at inlet and outlet allows tables; Balance written between inlet and outlet.

Model answer.

  1. Thermodynamic equilibrium. No unbalanced driving force inside the system or between it and its surroundings: thermal (one temperature), mechanical (no unbalanced force, uniform pressure) and chemical (no reaction or diffusion). Nothing changes with time and nothing flows.
  2. Steady state. At each fixed point inside the system the properties do not change with time, but mass and energy flow through and the properties differ from point to point. It is a balance of flows, not an absence of them.
  3. Example: a steam turbine at full load. Steam enters at 500 °C and leaves at 60 °C; each gauge holds its reading for hours. The readings are steady, so the turbine is in steady state. But steam crosses the boundary all the time and there is a 440 °C temperature difference inside, so thermal and mechanical equilibrium both fail: it is not in equilibrium.
  4. Comparison.

    Gradients of p and Tnonecan be large and fixed

  5. Why equilibrium tables still work. The steam at the inlet pipe is close to uniform over a small region: a small element of it is nearly in equilibrium even though the turbine as a whole is not. This local equilibrium assumption lets us read h, s and v from steam tables at the inlet and outlet sections.
  6. What the analysis then does. The energy balance is written between inlet and outlet only, without asking about the states inside, and with the mass inside constant (steady state), mass in equals mass out: ṁ₁ = ṁ₂.
  7. Which equilibrium fails in the turbine. Thermal: temperature falls from 500 °C to 60 °C along the flow. Mechanical: pressure falls from inlet to exhaust, and that unbalanced pressure is what drives the steam and turns the blades. Chemical: steam stays steam (some condenses near the exhaust, a phase change in progress). Two of the three fail, so the turbine is far from equilibrium even though it is perfectly steady.
  8. Check with numbers. A closed rigid tank of air at 500 kPa is left in a room at 30 °C. At first the air is hotter than the room and cools; heat flows, so it is not in equilibrium. After some hours the air is at 30 °C, its pressure has fallen to a steady value and no heat flows: equilibrium. Had it been the turbine, steady readings would never have meant equilibrium, because flow never stops.
  9. Conclusion. Constant readings are necessary but not sufficient for equilibrium. Ask whether anything crosses the boundary or whether gradients remain; if so, the system is at most in steady state.

Glossary

Boundary
The real or imaginary surface, fixed or moving, separating a system from its surroundings.
Continuum
The model of matter as continuous, so that properties have a value at every point.
Control mass
A fixed quantity of matter; a closed system.
Control volume
A region in space across whose boundary mass can flow; an open system.
Cycle
A series of processes that returns the system to its first state.
Displacement work
Work done by a moving boundary, W = ∫p dV for a quasi-static process.
Equation of state
A relation between p, v and T, such as pv = RT for an ideal gas.
Flow work
The work pV needed to push fluid across a control surface.
Heat
Energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference.
Knudsen number
Kn = λ/L, the mean free path divided by the size of the region; small values mean the continuum holds.
Macroscopic approach
A description of matter through a few measured bulk properties such as p, V and T.
Mean free path
The average distance a molecule travels between collisions.
Microscopic approach
A description of matter through its molecules and their statistics.
Path
The series of states a system passes through during a process.
Path function
A quantity, such as heat or work, whose amount depends on the path.
Point function
A quantity whose change depends only on the end states; a property.
Process
A change of a system from one state to another.
Property
A quantity with a definite value in each state, independent of the path.
Quasi-static process
A process slow enough that the system passes through a continuous chain of equilibrium states.
State diagram
A graph of two properties on which each state is a point, such as a p–v diagram.
State postulate
Two independent intensive properties fix the state of a simple compressible system.
Steady state
A condition in which properties at each point stay constant in time while mass and energy flow through.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system that can interact with it.
System
The matter or region chosen for study.
Thermodynamic equilibrium
Thermal, mechanical and chemical equilibrium at once, so that no property changes with time.
Thermodynamics
The science of energy, its transfer as heat and work, and the properties of the matter involved.
Thermometric property
A property that changes steadily with temperature and is used to measure it.
Triple point of water
The state where ice, liquid water and vapour coexist, at 273.16 K (0.01 °C).
Work
Energy crossing a boundary whose sole effect outside could be the raising of a weight.
Zeroth law
Two bodies each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
