6.1 Air standard cycles and their assumptions

A real engine is messy: fuel burns, the gas changes its make-up and heat leaks through the walls. An air standard cycle replaces all of that by a fixed mass of air that goes through a few simple, reversible processes and returns to its starting state. It gives the best efficiency an engine of that shape could reach. Every gas cycle in this unit starts from these assumptions; the Carnot cycle is the limit.

You need the ideal gas equation, the isentropic relation pvγ = constant and the heat transfer formulas of the topic Non-flow processes: isothermal, adiabatic, polytropic. Carnot's theorem comes from the topic Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem.

The air standard assumptions

An air standard analysis rests on four assumptions.

  1. Working fluid. A fixed mass of air is the working fluid all through the cycle. Air is an ideal gas, pv = RT, with R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K.
  2. Heat instead of combustion. Burning fuel is replaced by heat added from an outside hot source. The exhaust and suction strokes are replaced by heat rejected to an outside cold sink, so the same air returns to state 1 and the cycle is closed.
  3. Reversible processes. Every process is internally reversible. Compression and expansion are frictionless and adiabatic, so they are isentropic.
  4. Constant specific heats. cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K and γ = cp/cv = 1.4, the values for air near room temperature. With these room values the analysis is often called the cold air standard.

With these four, the cycle is a loop on the p-v and T-s diagrams, and the heat and work of each process follow from first-law formulas. The figure of merit is the air standard efficiency:

η = wnet/qin = 1 − qout/qin

Here wnet is the net work per kg of air in one cycle, qin is the heat added and qout the heat rejected, all in kJ/kg. It depends only on the shape and ratios of the cycle; a real engine reaches well below it. The ratio of the actual thermal efficiency to the air standard efficiency is called the relative efficiency (some syllabi say efficiency ratio).

Process relations used in every gas cycle

Four kinds of process build all the cycles of this unit. Per kg of air, with temperatures in kelvin:

  • Isentropic (reversible adiabatic): pvγ = constant, T₂/T₁ = (v₁/v₂)γ−1 = (p₂/p₁)(γ−1)/γ. No heat; work = cv(T₁ − T₂) in a closed system.
  • Constant volume: p/T = constant. Heat q = cv(T₂ − T₁); no displacement work.
  • Constant pressure: v/T = constant. Heat q = cp(T₂ − T₁); work = p(v₂ − v₁).
  • Isothermal: pv = constant. Heat = work = RT ln(v₂/v₁); entropy change Δs = R ln(v₂/v₁).

On a T-s diagram an isentropic process is a vertical line and an isothermal process a horizontal line. A constant-volume line rises more steeply than a constant-pressure line through the same point, because its slope is T/cv and cv is smaller than cp. That one fact explains most of the comparisons between cycles later in the unit.

The Carnot cycle as the reference

The Carnot gas cycle takes in all its heat at the highest temperature TH and rejects all of it at the lowest temperature TL. With state 1 the start of compression, its four processes are:

  1. 1→2 isentropic compression from TL to TH.
  2. 2→3 isothermal expansion at TH; heat qin = RTH ln(v₃/v₂) is taken in.
  3. 3→4 isentropic expansion from TH back to TL.
  4. 4→1 isothermal compression at TL; heat qout = RTL ln(v₄/v₁) is rejected.

The two isentropic steps have the same temperature ratio, so v₄/v₁ = v₃/v₂ and the logarithms cancel in qout/qin. On the T-s diagram the cycle is a rectangle, and

ηCarnot = 1 − TL/TH

No engine between the same two temperatures can beat it (Carnot's theorem), yet no engine runs on it, for three reasons. Isothermal heat transfer needs the gas to move slowly, which a piston at thousands of revolutions per minute cannot allow. The isentropic steps need a very large volume ratio and pressure ratio, so the cylinder is long and heavy for the work it gives. And the net work is a small difference between large positive and negative works, so friction in a real machine eats most of it. Two numbers measure this: the work ratio (net work divided by the positive work) and the mean effective pressure, defined in the chapter Air standard efficiency, mean effective pressure, comparison. Both are very low for the Carnot cycle, as the worked example shows.

Worked example

Question. An air standard Carnot cycle works between 300 K and 900 K. At the start of compression the air is at 1 bar and 300 K. During heat addition the volume doubles (v₃/v₂ = 2). Take R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K and γ = 1.4. Find the pressure and specific volume at each state, the heat added, the net work, the efficiency, the work ratio and the mean effective pressure.

  1. State 1: v₁ = RT₁/p₁ = 0.287 × 300/100 = 0.861 m³/kg (p in kPa).
  2. Isentropic 1→2: v₁/v₂ = (T₂/T₁)1/(γ−1) = 32.5 = 15.59, so v₂ = 0.861/15.59 = 0.0552 m³/kg. p₂ = p₁(T₂/T₁)γ/(γ−1) = 1 × 33.5 = 46.77 bar.
  3. Isothermal 2→3: v₃ = 2v₂ = 0.1105 m³/kg; p₃ = p₂/2 = 23.38 bar.
  4. Isentropic 3→4: the temperature ratio is again 3, so v₄ = 15.59 × v₃ = 1.722 m³/kg and p₄ = 23.38/46.77 = 0.50 bar. Check: v₄/v₁ = 2 = v₃/v₂.
  5. Heat added: qin = RTH ln 2 = 0.287 × 900 × 0.6931 = 179.0 kJ/kg. Heat rejected: qout = 0.287 × 300 × 0.6931 = 59.7 kJ/kg.
  6. Net work: wnet = 179.0 − 59.7 = 119.4 kJ/kg. Efficiency: η = 119.4/179.0 = 0.667, the same as 1 − 300/900.
  7. Positive work: 179.0 (isothermal) + cv(900 − 300) = 430.8 (isentropic), total 609.8 kJ/kg. Work ratio = 119.4/609.8 = 0.196.
  8. Mean effective pressure = wnet/(vmax − vmin) = 119.4/(1.722 − 0.0552) = 71.6 kPa = 0.716 bar.

Answer. p₂ = 46.77 bar, p₃ = 23.38 bar, p₄ = 0.50 bar; v₁ = 0.861, v₂ = 0.0552, v₃ = 0.1105, v₄ = 1.722 m³/kg; qin = 179.0 kJ/kg, wnet = 119.4 kJ/kg, η = 66.7%, work ratio 0.196, MEP 0.716 bar. The cycle needs a pressure ratio of about 94 and a volume ratio of about 31 to give a mean pressure below 1 bar: that is why no engine runs on it.

Exam traps

  • Temperatures in T₂/T₁ = (v₁/v₂)γ−1 and in 1 − TL/TH must be in kelvin. Writing 1 − 27/627 instead of 1 − 300/900 gives 95.7% instead of 66.7%.
  • The air standard efficiency is not the efficiency of the real engine. It is an upper limit for that cycle shape; the real value is lower because of friction, heat loss, variable specific heats and incomplete burning.
  • The Carnot efficiency does not depend on the working fluid or on the volume ratio during heat addition. Only TH and TL appear.
  • In the isothermal steps heat equals work, q = RT ln(v₂/v₁). Students often write cvΔT, which is zero there.
  • A favourite question: "Why is the Carnot cycle not used in practice?" Answer with the low work ratio, the low mean effective pressure and the impossibility of fast isothermal heat transfer.

6.2 Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles

The three piston-engine cycles differ in one thing only: how the heat goes in. The Otto cycle adds it at constant volume, as if a spark burned the charge instantly at top dead centre; it models the petrol engine. The Diesel cycle adds it at constant pressure while the piston moves out, as fuel injected into hot air burns steadily; it models the slow-speed compression-ignition engine. The Dual cycle adds part at constant volume and the rest at constant pressure, which is closer to a modern high-speed diesel. Each gives an efficiency formula in terms of the compression ratio and one or two more ratios.

You need the air standard assumptions and the process relations from Air standard cycles and their assumptions.

Otto cycle: heat added at constant volume

State 1 is the start of compression. The processes are 1→2 isentropic compression, 2→3 heat addition at constant volume, 3→4 isentropic expansion and 4→1 heat rejection at constant volume. The compression ratio is r = v₁/v₂, the total volume over the clearance volume; since v₃ = v₂ and v₄ = v₁, the expansion ratio is also r.

  1. Heat added qin = cv(T₃ − T₂); heat rejected qout = cv(T₄ − T₁).
  2. Isentropic steps: T₂ = T₁rγ−1 and T₃ = T₄rγ−1, so T₃ − T₂ = rγ−1(T₄ − T₁).
  3. η = 1 − qout/qin = 1 − (T₄ − T₁)/(T₃ − T₂) = 1 − 1/rγ−1.

ηOtto = 1 − 1/rγ−1. It depends only on r and γ, not on how much heat is added. Raising r raises η, but a petrol engine compresses the fuel-air mixture, and above r of about 10 to 12 the mixture can self-ignite before the spark (knock). That limit, not thermodynamics, keeps the petrol engine's r low.

Diesel cycle: heat added at constant pressure

The Diesel cycle keeps 1→2 isentropic compression, 3→4 isentropic expansion and 4→1 constant-volume rejection, but heat goes in at constant pressure from 2 to 3. The cut-off ratio is rc = v₃/v₂ = T₃/T₂, the volume at which fuel injection ends divided by the clearance volume. When cut-off is given as a fraction k of the stroke, v₃ − v₂ = k(v₁ − v₂), so rc = 1 + k(r − 1).

  1. qin = cp(T₃ − T₂) because the pressure is constant; qout = cv(T₄ − T₁).
  2. T₂ = T₁rγ−1, T₃ = rcT₂, and expansion from v₃ to v₄ = v₁ gives T₄ = T₃(rc/r)γ−1 = T₁rcγ.
  3. Substituting: η = 1 − (T₄ − T₁)/[γ(T₃ − T₂)] = 1 − (rcγ − 1)/[γ rγ−1(rc − 1)].

ηDiesel = 1 − (1/rγ−1) × (rcγ − 1)/[γ(rc − 1)]. The bracket is always greater than 1 when rc > 1, so at the same r a Diesel cycle is less efficient than an Otto cycle, and its efficiency falls as cut-off gets later (more load). A diesel engine compresses only air, so knock does not limit r; it runs at r of about 14 to 22 and ends up more efficient in practice.

Dual cycle: heat added in two parts

The Dual (mixed, or limited-pressure) cycle has five processes: 1→2 isentropic compression, 2→3 heat at constant volume, 3→4 heat at constant pressure, 4→5 isentropic expansion and 5→1 rejection at constant volume. It needs two more ratios: the pressure ratio rp = p₃/p₂ (also called the explosion ratio) and the cut-off ratio rc = v₄/v₃. Note that in the Dual cycle cut-off is measured from state 3, not state 2.

qin = cv(T₃ − T₂) + cp(T₄ − T₃), qout = cv(T₅ − T₁), with T₃ = rpT₂, T₄ = rcT₃ and T₅ = T₁rprcγ. The result is

ηDual = 1 − (1/rγ−1) × (rprcγ − 1)/[(rp − 1) + γrp(rc − 1)]

Put rc = 1 and the formula becomes the Otto formula; put rp = 1 and it becomes the Diesel formula. So the Dual cycle contains the other two as its limits, and its efficiency lies between them for the same r and heat input. A full Dual cycle calculation, with mean effective pressure, is worked in Air standard efficiency, mean effective pressure, comparison.

Worked example

Question. An air standard Otto cycle has a compression ratio of 8. At the start of compression the air is at 1 bar and 300 K, and 800 kJ/kg of heat is added per cycle. Take cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K and γ = 1.4. Find the temperatures and pressures at all states, the efficiency and the net work.

  1. rγ−1 = 80.4 = 2.297. T₂ = 300 × 2.297 = 689.2 K. p₂ = p₁rγ = 81.4 = 18.38 bar.
  2. Volumes: v₁ = 0.287 × 300/100 = 0.861 m³/kg and v₂ = v₁/8 = 0.1076 m³/kg.
  3. Heat at constant volume: T₃ = T₂ + qin/cv = 689.2 + 800/0.718 = 689.2 + 1114.2 = 1803.4 K. p₃ = p₂T₃/T₂ = 18.38 × 2.617 = 48.09 bar.
  4. Expansion: T₄ = T₃/rγ−1 = 1803.4/2.297 = 785.0 K. p₄ = p₁T₄/T₁ = 785.0/300 = 2.62 bar.
  5. qout = 0.718 × (785.0 − 300) = 348.2 kJ/kg; wnet = 800 − 348.2 = 451.8 kJ/kg.
  6. η = 451.8/800 = 0.565. Check with the formula: 1 − 1/2.297 = 0.565.

Answer. T₂ = 689.2 K, T₃ = 1803.4 K, T₄ = 785.0 K; p₂ = 18.38 bar, p₃ = 48.09 bar, p₄ = 2.62 bar; η = 56.5%, wnet = 451.8 kJ/kg.

Exam traps

  • Cut-off ratio is v₃/v₂ in the Diesel cycle but v₄/v₃ in the Dual cycle, because the Dual cycle's constant-pressure part starts at state 3. Read the state numbers before using a formula.
  • "Cut-off at 6% of the stroke" means rc = 1 + 0.06(r − 1), not 1.06 and not 0.06r.
  • Use cp for heat added at constant pressure and cv for heat at constant volume. Using cv for the Diesel heat input makes qin 29% too small.
  • Keep temperatures in kelvin. A question that gives 27 °C at the start of compression means T₁ = 300 K; putting 27 into T₂ = T₁rγ−1 gives 62 K instead of 689 K.
  • The Otto formula 1 − 1/rγ−1 is not the Diesel efficiency. For the same r the Diesel value is lower; the Diesel engine wins in practice only because it can use a higher r.

6.3 Brayton and Stirling cycles

The Brayton cycle (also called the Joule cycle) is the ideal cycle of the gas turbine: air is compressed, heated at constant pressure, expanded through a turbine and cooled at constant pressure. It runs in steady flow, so its key number is the pressure ratio, not a volume ratio. It models aircraft jet engines and the gas turbines of power stations. The Stirling cycle is a piston cycle with two isothermal and two constant-volume processes and a regenerator that stores heat inside the engine. With a perfect regenerator it reaches the Carnot efficiency, which is why it is studied for solar-dish and other external-heat engines.

You need the process relations from Air standard cycles and their assumptions and the steady flow energy equation from the topic Steady flow energy equation and its applications.

Brayton cycle: four steady-flow processes

State 1 is the compressor inlet. The processes are 1→2 isentropic compression, 2→3 heat addition at constant pressure (the combustor), 3→4 isentropic expansion in the turbine and 4→1 heat rejection at constant pressure. In a real open-cycle gas turbine, fresh air enters and exhaust leaves; the closed air standard cycle replaces the combustor by a heater and the atmosphere by a cooler.

The pressure ratio is rp = p₂/p₁ = p₃/p₄. Both isentropic steps work across it, so T₂/T₁ = T₃/T₄ = rp(γ−1)/γ. Each device is a steady-flow device, and the steady flow energy equation gives, per kg of air:

  • compressor work wc = cp(T₂ − T₁); turbine work wt = cp(T₃ − T₄);
  • heat added qin = cp(T₃ − T₂); net work wnet = wt − wc;
  • ηBrayton = 1 − 1/rp(γ−1)/γ, and the back work ratio wc/wt.

The efficiency formula looks like the Otto formula with rγ−1 replaced by rp(γ−1)/γ. It depends only on the pressure ratio. The back work ratio is large in a gas turbine (often 40 to 60% of the turbine work drives the compressor), so small losses in the compressor and turbine cut the net work sharply.

Pressure ratio for maximum net work

For fixed T₁ and T₃ (the turbine inlet temperature is limited by the blade material), η keeps rising with rp, but the net work does not. At rp = 1 nothing is compressed; at the rp where T₂ reaches T₃ no heat can be added. Between them wnet has a maximum. Writing x = rp(γ−1)/γ, wnet = cp[T₃(1 − 1/x) − T₁(x − 1)], and dwnet/dx = 0 gives x = √(T₃/T₁). So

rp,opt = (T₃/T₁)γ/[2(γ−1)], T₂ = T₄ = √(T₁T₃), wmax = cp(√T₃ − √T₁)²

Stirling cycle and the regenerator

With state 1 the start of compression at the largest volume, the processes are 1→2 isothermal compression at TL (heat rejected), 2→3 heating at constant volume, 3→4 isothermal expansion at TH (heat added) and 4→1 cooling at constant volume. The two constant-volume steps exchange the same amount of heat, cv(TH − TL).

The regenerator is a matrix of fine wire or metal mesh between the hot and cold spaces. In 4→1 the gas gives its heat to the matrix; in 2→3 the gas takes the same heat back. With a perfect regenerator, outside heat enters only at TH and leaves only at TL, so

ηStirling = R(TH − TL) ln r / [RTH ln r] = 1 − TL/TH, where r = v₁/v₂.

Without a regenerator the heat for 2→3 must come from outside too, and the efficiency drops far below Carnot. The Ericsson cycle is the same idea with two constant-pressure steps instead of constant-volume ones; it too reaches the Carnot efficiency with perfect regeneration.

  4. (a) η = 119.4/179.0 = 0.667, the Carnot value.
  5. (b) η = 119.4/(179.0 + 430.8) = 119.4/609.8 = 0.196.
  6. (c) Outside heat for 2→3 = 0.2 × 430.8 = 86.2 kJ/kg; η = 119.4/(179.0 + 86.2) = 0.450.

6.4 Air standard efficiency, mean effective pressure, comparison

Efficiency alone does not tell you how big an engine must be. Two cycles with the same efficiency can give very different work per stroke. The mean effective pressure (MEP) measures that: it is the steady pressure that, pushing the piston over one full stroke, would give the cycle's net work. A high MEP means a small, light engine for the power. This chapter defines the MEP, uses it to find engine power, and compares the Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles on the bases examiners use: the same compression ratio, the same peak pressure and temperature, and the same heat input.

You need the three cycles and their efficiency formulas from Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles.

Mean effective pressure and engine power

Per kg of air, the swept volume is v₁ − v₂ (largest minus smallest specific volume). The MEP is the net work divided by it:

pm = wnet/(v₁ − v₂) = Wnet/Vs

Here wnet is in kJ/kg and v in m³/kg, so pm comes out in kPa; Wnet is the net work per cycle in kJ and Vs the swept volume in m³. Since v₂ = v₁/r, the swept volume is v₁(1 − 1/r). For the Otto cycle this gives a closed formula:

pm,Otto = p₁ r (rγ−1 − 1)(rp − 1)/[(γ − 1)(r − 1)], with rp = p₃/p₂.

The MEP turns a cycle into engine power. Power = pm × Vs × (cycles per second) × (number of cylinders). A four-stroke engine completes one cycle in two revolutions, so cycles per second = N/120 with N in rpm; a two-stroke engine completes one per revolution, N/60.

Comparing Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles

Every comparison starts from one fact: η = 1 − qout/qin, and in all three cycles the heat is rejected along the same kind of constant-volume line from the end of expansion back to state 1. On the T-s diagram qout is the area under that line, so the cycle that ends its heat addition at the smaller entropy rejects less heat.

Same compression ratio and same heat input. States 1 and 2 are shared. A constant-volume line on T-s is steeper than a constant-pressure line, so to take in the same heat (the same area under the line) the Otto cycle climbs high with little entropy rise, and the Diesel cycle runs further to the right. The Diesel cycle then rejects more heat. Its temperature rises all through the constant-pressure heat addition (q = cpΔT with ΔT > 0), but it reaches a lower peak, so the mean temperature at which heat enters is lower. Result: ηOtto > ηDual > ηDiesel.

Same peak pressure and peak temperature. Now state 3 is shared and the heat rejected is the same. The Diesel cycle can reach that peak pressure with a much higher compression ratio, so it takes in more heat for the same rejection. Result: ηDiesel > ηDual > ηOtto. The same order holds for the same peak pressure and the same heat input. This is the fair comparison for real engines, because peak pressure is what the cylinder and bearings must withstand.

Basis (air from 1 bar, 300 K)OttoDualDiesel

The mean temperature idea gives the same answers in one line. Write Tm,in = qin/Δs for the heat addition and Tm,out = qout/Δs for the rejection, with the same Δs for both because the cycle closes; then η = 1 − Tm,out/Tm,in, and the cycle with the higher mean temperature of heat addition wins on that basis.

6.5 Rankine cycle and effect of pressure and temperature

A steam power plant runs on the Rankine cycle: a pump pushes water into a boiler, the boiler turns it into superheated steam, the steam expands through a turbine, and a condenser turns the exhaust back into water. The working fluid changes phase, so its properties come from steam tables, not from pv = RT. The Rankine cycle is the ideal model of every coal, nuclear and solar-thermal steam plant. This chapter fixes the four states, computes the work, heat, efficiency and steam rate, and shows how boiler pressure, steam temperature and condenser pressure change the result.

You need steam tables and the dryness fraction from the topics Steam tables and Mollier chart and Formation of steam, latent heat and dryness fraction, and the steady flow energy equation from the topic Steady flow energy equation and its applications.

The ideal Rankine cycle

Take state 1 as the turbine inlet. The four steady-flow processes are 1→2 isentropic expansion in the turbine, 2→3 condensation at constant pressure to saturated liquid, 3→4 isentropic pumping to boiler pressure, and 4→1 heating at constant pressure in the boiler (water heating, boiling and superheating). Each device obeys the steady flow energy equation with negligible kinetic and potential energy changes, so every work and heat is an enthalpy difference, in kJ/kg of steam:

  • turbine work wT = h₁ − h₂; pump work wP = h₄ − h₃ = vf(p₁ − p₂), with vf in m³/kg and p in kPa;
  • heat added qin = h₁ − h₄; heat rejected qout = h₂ − h₃;
  • ηRankine = (wT − wP)/qin; specific steam consumption SSC = 3600/wnet in kg/kWh.

To find h₂, use s₂ = s₁. If s₁ is less than sg at the condenser pressure, the exhaust is wet with dryness x₂ = (s₁ − sf)/sfg and h₂ = hf + x₂hfg. If s₁ is greater than sg, the exhaust is still superheated and h₂ comes from the superheat table. The pump work is tiny, often below 1% of the turbine work, because liquid water hardly changes volume.

Why not a Carnot cycle with steam? It would need a pump to compress a wet mixture up to saturated liquid, which a pump cannot do, and the turbine would expand very wet steam. The Rankine cycle condenses completely, so it pumps liquid only, and superheating keeps the turbine exhaust drier. The price is that part of the heat enters at low temperature, while the feedwater warms up in the boiler.

Effect of pressure and temperature

The efficiency follows the mean temperature of heat addition, Tm = qin/(s₁ − s₄), and the condenser temperature: η = 1 − Tcondenser/Tm, with both in kelvin. Each change below moves one of them. Values are for the worked example's plant (40 bar, 400 °C, 0.1 bar), changing one thing at a time.

ChangeEfficiencyTurbine exit dryness

Steam temperature 300 → 400 → 500 → 600 °C (40 bar)34.1 → 35.3 → 36.8 → 38.4%0.762 → 0.816 → 0.859 → 0.896
Condenser pressure 0.2 → 0.1 → 0.05 bar33.1 → 35.3 → 37.3%0.840 → 0.816 → 0.795

  • Higher superheat raises Tm and also dries the exhaust, so it helps on both counts; the limit is the creep strength of the superheater tubes and turbine inlet.
  • Lower condenser pressure lowers the temperature of heat rejection. Heat input rises slightly, the turbine work rises more, so η rises; the exhaust gets a little wetter and the condenser needs a good vacuum and cold cooling water.

Plants combine high pressure with high superheat, and reheat (next chapter) to keep the exhaust dryness above about 0.88 to 0.90.

The actual cycle

A real turbine and pump have friction, so entropy rises. Their isentropic efficiencies are ηT = (h₁ − h₂')/(h₁ − h₂) and ηP = (h₄ − h₃)/(h₄' − h₃), where the primed states are the actual ones. With ηT = 0.85 in the worked example's plant, h₂' = 3214.5 − 0.85 × 1070.0 = 2305.0 kJ/kg, the exhaust dryness becomes 0.883 and the cycle efficiency falls from 35.3% to 30.0%. Pressure drops in the boiler and pipes and heat loss lower it further.

6.6 Reheat and regenerative Rankine cycles

The simple Rankine cycle has two weak points. High boiler pressure raises the efficiency but makes the turbine exhaust too wet, and the cold feedwater takes in much of its heat at low temperature. Reheat fixes the first: the steam expands part-way, goes back to the boiler to be heated again, and then finishes its expansion, so the exhaust is drier. Regeneration fixes the second: a little steam is bled from the turbine to warm the feedwater, so the boiler adds heat at a higher mean temperature. Nearly every large steam plant uses both. This chapter works one example of each with the same steam conditions so the gains can be compared.

You need the simple cycle, its state numbering and the worked example of Rankine cycle and effect of pressure and temperature.

Reheat cycle

In the reheat cycle the states are: 1 high-pressure (HP) turbine inlet, 2 HP turbine exit, 3 reheater exit (low-pressure turbine inlet), 4 LP turbine exit, 5 condenser exit, 6 pump exit. Steam expands isentropically 1→2 to the reheat pressure, is reheated at constant pressure 2→3, usually back to about the initial steam temperature, and expands isentropically 3→4 to the condenser pressure.

  • wT = (h₁ − h₂) + (h₃ − h₄); wP = h₆ − h₅ = vf(p₁ − p₅);
  • qin = (h₁ − h₆) + (h₃ − h₂), the boiler plus the reheater;
  • η = (wT − wP)/qin.

The main gain is dryness: the LP expansion starts at a higher entropy, so the exhaust lies further right. The net work per kg also rises, which lowers the steam rate. The efficiency gain is small and depends on the reheat pressure; a reheat pressure of about a fifth to a quarter of the boiler pressure is a common choice. In current plants the reheat temperature is set equal to or close to the main steam temperature, about 540 to 600 °C.

Regenerative cycle with an open feedwater heater

In the regenerative cycle a fraction m of each kg of steam is bled from the turbine and mixed with the feedwater in an open feedwater heater, a mixing chamber at the bleed pressure. The states are: 1 turbine inlet, 2 bleed point, 3 turbine exit, 4 condenser exit, 5 exit of pump 1 (raised to heater pressure), 6 heater exit (saturated liquid at the bleed pressure), 7 exit of pump 2 (raised to boiler pressure). An open heater works at its own pressure, so each open heater needs a pump after it.

  1. Heater energy balance (1 kg leaves as saturated liquid): m h₂ + (1 − m) h₅ = h₆, so m = (h₆ − h₅)/(h₂ − h₅).
  2. Turbine work: wT = (h₁ − h₂) + (1 − m)(h₂ − h₃); only 1 − m kg expands to the condenser.
  3. Pump work: wP = (1 − m)(h₅ − h₄) + (h₇ − h₆).
  4. Heat added: qin = h₁ − h₇. Heat rejected: qout = (1 − m)(h₃ − h₄).

The boiler now receives water at about the saturation temperature of the bleed pressure instead of the condenser temperature. Heat that the simple cycle added at low temperature is now supplied by bled steam inside the cycle, so the mean temperature of heat addition rises. The net work per kg falls a little, because the bled steam does not finish its expansion. For one heater, a good bleed point is where the saturation temperature lies roughly halfway between the boiler and condenser saturation temperatures. With many heaters the cycle approaches the Carnot efficiency between the boiler and condenser temperatures (in kelvin).

A closed feedwater heater is a shell-and-tube exchanger: the feedwater passes through tubes and the bled steam condenses outside them, so the two streams do not mix and can be at different pressures. Its drain is either throttled back to a lower-pressure heater or the condenser, or pumped forward into the feed line. Plants use several closed heaters and one open heater that also removes dissolved gases (the deaerator).

6.7 Vapour compression refrigeration cycle

A refrigerator moves heat from a cold space to warmer surroundings, and it needs work to do so. The vapour compression refrigeration cycle does it with a refrigerant that boils at a low temperature in the evaporator, taking heat from the cold space, and condenses at a higher temperature in the condenser, giving heat to the room or the outside air. A compressor raises the pressure between the two, and an expansion valve lowers it again. Domestic refrigerators, air conditioners, water coolers and cold storages all run on it. Its performance is measured by the coefficient of performance, computed from refrigerant tables or a pressure-enthalpy chart.

You need refrigerators, heat pumps and their COP from the topic Heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps, and the steady flow energy equation (throttling: h constant).

From the reversed Carnot cycle to the practical cycle

The ideal refrigerator is the reversed Carnot cycle, with COPCarnot = TL/(TH − TL) (temperatures in kelvin). Run inside the vapour dome it would need isentropic compression of a wet mixture and an expansion engine for a wet liquid. Both are impractical: liquid droplets in a compressor cause slugging and valve damage, and an expansion engine for a small flow of liquid would give very little work for its cost.

The practical cycle makes two changes. It compresses dry vapour (saturated or slightly superheated), and it replaces the expansion engine by a throttle valve. With state 1 at the compressor inlet, the processes are:

  1. 1→2 isentropic compression of saturated vapour from evaporator pressure to condenser pressure; the vapour leaves superheated.
  2. 2→3 constant-pressure heat rejection in the condenser: the superheated vapour cools to saturation, then condenses to saturated liquid.
  3. 3→4 throttling in the expansion valve: h₄ = h₃; pressure and temperature fall and some liquid flashes to vapour.
  4. 4→1 constant-pressure boiling in the evaporator, taking heat from the cold space.

Capacity is often given in tonnes of refrigeration: 1 TR = 211 kJ/min = 3.517 kW, the rate of heat removal that freezes one tonne of water at 0 °C in a day.

Energy terms and the diagrams

Per kg of refrigerant, from the steady flow energy equation:

  • refrigerating effect qL = h₁ − h₄; compressor work w = h₂ − h₁; condenser heat qH = h₂ − h₃ = qL + w;
  • COPR = (h₁ − h₄)/(h₂ − h₁); as a heat pump, COPHP = (h₂ − h₃)/(h₂ − h₁) = COPR + 1;
  • mass flow ṁ = capacity/qL; compressor power = ṁw.

On the T-s diagram the compression 1→2 is a vertical line that ends outside the dome, in the superheated region. The condenser line 2→3 first falls as the vapour desuperheats, then runs horizontally inside the dome while it condenses. The throttling 3→4 is drawn dashed, because it is irreversible and not a quasi-static path. The p-h diagram is the refrigeration engineer's chart: both heat exchanges are horizontal lines, throttling is a vertical line (h constant), and each energy term is read as a horizontal length.

Subcooling, superheating and operating pressures

  • Subcooling the liquid below the condensing temperature lowers h₃ = h₄, so the refrigerating effect rises with the same compressor work: COP rises.
  • Superheating the vapour before the compressor protects the compressor from liquid. If the superheat comes from the cold space it adds to the refrigerating effect; if it comes from the suction line outside, it only raises the work.
  • A lower evaporator temperature (a freezer) or a higher condenser temperature (a hot day) widens the pressure ratio, cuts the refrigerating effect and raises the work, so COP falls.

In a real plant the compressor is not isentropic, there are pressure drops in the pipes and heat exchangers, and the evaporator and condenser must run several kelvin colder and hotter than the space and the air they exchange heat with. Common refrigerants are R-134a (refrigerators, car air conditioners), R-32 and R-410A (room air conditioners), R-290 propane and R-600a isobutane (newer refrigerators) and R-717 ammonia (cold storages). Properties come from the refrigerant's own tables or chart, not from steam tables.

6.8 Vapour absorption refrigeration

A vapour absorption refrigerator gets its cooling from heat instead of shaft work. It keeps the condenser, expansion valve and evaporator of the vapour compression cycle, but replaces the compressor by a liquid loop: an absorber where the refrigerant vapour dissolves in a liquid absorbent, a pump that raises the pressure of that liquid, and a generator where heat drives the refrigerant out again at high pressure. Pumping a liquid takes very little work, so the main input is heat at a moderate temperature: low-pressure steam, hot water, engine exhaust, solar heat. Its COP is lower than a compressor system's, but the heat is often free.

You need the vapour compression cycle from Vapour compression refrigeration cycle and the Carnot efficiency and COP from the topic Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem.

The ammonia-water system

In the ammonia-water system, ammonia is the refrigerant and water is the absorbent; water dissolves ammonia readily, more so when cold. The parts are named by what happens in them:

  1. Evaporator: liquid ammonia boils at low pressure, taking heat QE from the cold space.
  2. Absorber: the ammonia vapour dissolves in weak solution coming back from the generator, forming strong solution. Dissolving releases heat, so the absorber is cooled with water (heat QA out).
  3. Pump: raises the strong solution to the condenser pressure; a liquid needs little work.
  4. Generator: heat QG boils ammonia vapour out of the strong solution. An analyser and rectifier above it condense the water vapour that comes off with it, so nearly pure ammonia goes on.
  5. Condenser: the ammonia vapour condenses, giving heat QC to the cooling water.
  6. Expansion valve: the liquid ammonia is throttled back to the evaporator pressure.
  7. The weak solution left in the generator returns to the absorber through a solution heat exchanger, where it warms the strong solution going the other way, and a pressure-reducing valve.

The energy balance of the whole plant is QG + QE + Wpump = QC + QA, and COP = QE/(QG + Wpump) ≈ QE/QG.

The ideal COP: three temperatures

An absorption plant takes heat at the generator temperature TG, takes heat from the cold space at TE and rejects heat at the absorber and condenser temperature T0, near ambient. Think of it as a reversible heat engine between TG and T0 driving a reversible refrigerator between TE and T0. The engine turns QG into work QG(1 − T0/TG), and the refrigerator turns that work into cooling at COP TE/(T0 − TE). So

COPmax = (1 − T0/TG) × TE/(T0 − TE), all temperatures in kelvin.

The same result follows from the second law: with no entropy generation, QG/TG + QE/TE = (QG + QE)/T0. The formula has three temperatures; the two-temperature expression TE/(TG − TE) is not the absorption limit. A hotter generator or a colder heat sink raises the COP. Real single-stage plants reach well under half of this limit.

Lithium bromide-water systems and the comparison

In the lithium bromide-water system, water is the refrigerant and LiBr solution the absorbent. Water cannot be cooled below 0 °C without freezing, so these units chill water to about 5 to 7 °C for air conditioning, and their evaporator runs at a deep vacuum. LiBr does not evaporate, so no rectifier is needed. Single-effect LiBr chillers driven by hot water or low-pressure steam have COPs of about 0.7; double-effect units, with a second generator, reach about 1.35 to 1.4. Ammonia-water units are used for cold stores and ice plants below 0 °C.

The Electrolux (three-fluid) refrigerator adds hydrogen to the ammonia-water pair so that the total pressure is the same everywhere; it needs no pump at all, only a small heater, and runs silently in hotel minibars and caravan fridges.

Exam traps

  • The ideal absorption COP needs three temperatures: (1 − T0/TG) × TE/(T0 − TE). TE/(TG − TE) is a Carnot refrigerator COP between the evaporator and the generator, which is a different machine.
  • In ammonia-water the refrigerant is ammonia; in LiBr-water the refrigerant is water. Swapping them is a common slip.
  • COP below 1 does not mean the plant is badly designed: its input is heat, not work, and the reversible limit at these temperatures is itself only about 1.4 to 1.5.
  • The heat rejected is QG + QE (plus pump work), shared between the absorber and condenser. Forgetting the absorber's share undersizes the cooling water.
  • A LiBr-water machine cannot make ice: its refrigerant, water, freezes at 0 °C.

Takeaways

  1. Every air standard cycle is a fixed mass of ideal air in reversible processes; its efficiency depends only on the cycle's ratios, never on the fuel.
  2. The Carnot cycle sets the limit but has a tiny work ratio and mean effective pressure, so no engine runs on it.
  3. Otto, Diesel and Dual differ only in how heat goes in; at equal compression ratio and heat input Otto is the most efficient, at equal peak pressure Diesel is.
  4. The Brayton efficiency depends only on the pressure ratio; the net work peaks at T₂ = T₄ = √(T₁T₃).
  5. A perfect regenerator gives the Stirling cycle the Carnot efficiency; without one it falls far below.
  6. In steam and refrigerant cycles every work and heat is an enthalpy difference read from tables; check whether each state is wet or superheated first.
  7. Higher boiler pressure, higher superheat and lower condenser pressure all raise the Rankine efficiency by raising the mean temperature of heat addition or lowering that of rejection.
  8. Reheat keeps the turbine exhaust dry; regeneration raises the efficiency by heating the feedwater with bled steam.
  9. The vapour compression COP is (h₁ − h₄)/(h₂ − h₁); it falls as the evaporator gets colder or the condenser hotter.
  10. An absorption plant trades the compressor for an absorber, pump and generator driven by heat; its COP is below 1 but its input can be waste heat.

  1. Properties (R-134a tables, IIR reference; another reference shifts every h by the same constant). Evaporator −20 °C: p = 1.327 bar; saturated vapour h₁ = 386.55 kJ/kg, s₁ = 1.7413 kJ/kg·K. Condenser 35 °C: p = 8.870 bar.
  2. Compressor exit: at 8.870 bar with s = 1.7413, the superheat table gives h₂ = 426.10 kJ/kg (about 43 °C).
  3. Liquid subcooled to 30 °C at 8.870 bar: h₃ ≈ hf at 30 °C = 241.72 kJ/kg. Throttling: h₄ = h₃ = 241.72 kJ/kg.

qH = h₂ − h₃ = 426.10 − 241.72 = 184.38 kJ/kg

Analyze Compare the Otto, Diesel and Dual cycles (a) for the same compression ratio and heat input and (b) for the same maximum pressure and temperature, using T-s diagrams. Support each comparison with numbers for air starting at 1 bar and 300 K, and say which comparison matters for real engine design. 15 marks

Points to cover. η = 1 − qout/qin; qout is the area under the constant-volume line to state 1; case (a): shared 1-2, slopes T/cv and T/cv, numbers; case (b): shared state 3 and equal heat rejected, numbers; mean temperature argument; which basis is fair.

Model answer.

  1. Basis: η = 1 − qout/qin. In all three cycles heat is rejected along a constant-volume line from the end of expansion to state 1, so qout is the area under that line on the T-s diagram. On T-s, a constant-volume line has slope T/cv and a constant-pressure line T/cp, so the constant-volume line is steeper.
  2. (a) Same r and same qin. States 1 and 2 are common. To add the same heat (equal areas under the heating lines), the steep constant-volume line of Otto needs less entropy rise than the flatter constant-pressure line of Diesel. The Diesel cycle therefore ends heat addition at a larger entropy, and its rejection line 4′→1 is longer, with more area under it.
  3. Numbers for r = 14, qin = 1200 kJ/kg (T₂ = 300 × 140.4 = 862.1 K):
    CyclePeak temperature (K)Heat rejected (kJ/kg)Efficiency
    Otto2533.4417.665.2%
    Dual (half at constant volume)2294.8431.464.1%
    Diesel2056.2511.957.3%
  4. Working for the Diesel line: T₃ = 862.1 + 1200/1.005 = 2056.2 K, rc = 2056.2/862.1 = 2.385, T₄ = 300 × 2.3851.4 = 1013.0 K, qout = 0.718 × 713.0 = 511.9 kJ/kg. Result (a): ηOtto > ηDual > ηDiesel.
  5. (b) Same peak pressure and peak temperature. Now state 3 is common, so the expansion 3→4 and the heat rejected 4→1 are the same for all three. The Diesel cycle reaches the peak pressure by compression alone, so it has the highest compression ratio and takes in the most heat; the Otto cycle has the lowest r and the least heat input.
  6. Numbers for a peak of 60 bar and 2000 K:
    Otto: r = v₁/v₃ = (0.287 × 300/100)/(0.287 × 2000/6000) = 9.0
      T₂ = 300 × 90.4 = 722.4 K,  qin = 0.718 × (2000 − 722.4) = 917.3 kJ/kg
      η = 1 − 380.9/917.3 = 58.5%

      qin = 1.005 × (2000 − 966.4) = 1038.8 kJ/kg
      η = 1 − 380.9/1038.8 = 63.3%
(qout = 380.9 kJ/kg in both, from the shared state 3)
  7. Result (b): ηDiesel > ηDual > ηOtto. The same order holds for the same peak pressure and the same heat input.
  8. Mean temperature view: η = 1 − Tm,out/Tm,in. In (a) Otto has the highest mean temperature of heat addition; in (b) all three reject at the same mean temperature and Diesel has the highest mean temperature of addition because its heating starts from the hottest state 2.
  9. Evaluation: the cylinder, head and bearings are designed for a maximum pressure, and the materials for a maximum temperature, so comparison (b) is the fair one for engine design. It explains why compression-ignition engines, with their higher compression ratios, are more efficient in practice, even though comparison (a) favours Otto.
Evaluate A cold store in Coimbatore needs 50 kW of cooling at −10 °C, with heat rejected at about 30 °C. Low-pressure steam at 120 °C is available as waste heat from a process. Evaluate a vapour compression system (COP 4.03, from the R-134a cycle between −10 °C and 40 °C) against an ammonia-water absorption system (actual COP 0.55). Compare energy input, heat rejected and the thermodynamic quality of each, and recommend one. 13 marks

Points to cover. Input power or heat for each; heat rejected for each; Carnot and ideal absorption COPs as benchmarks; fraction of ideal achieved; cost of the energy, not just its amount; recommendation with conditions.

Model answer.

  1. Vapour compression: electric input = 50/4.03 = 12.4 kW; heat rejected at the condenser = 50 + 12.4 = 62.4 kW.
  2. Absorption: generator heat = 50/0.55 = 90.9 kW of steam; heat rejected in the condenser and absorber = 50 + 90.9 = 140.9 kW (pump work of a few hundred watts neglected).
  3. Benchmarks. The compression cycle's limit is the reversed Carnot COP between its evaporator and condenser, 263.15/(313.15 − 263.15) = 5.26, so it reaches 4.03/5.26 = 77%. The absorption limit with TG = 393.15 K, T0 = 303.15 K, TE = 263.15 K is
    COPmax = (1 − 303.15/393.15) × 263.15/40 = 0.2289 × 6.579 = 1.51
reached: 0.55/1.51 = 36%
  4. Comparing the inputs fairly: a kilowatt of electricity is worth far more than a kilowatt of 120 °C heat. The most work 90.9 kW of heat at 120 °C could give with the surroundings at 30 °C is 90.9 × (1 − 303.15/393.15) = 20.8 kW. So, by work potential, the absorption system uses 20.8 kW-equivalent against 12.4 kW of electricity for compression: compression is the better machine thermodynamically.
  5. But the steam is waste heat. If it would otherwise be thrown away, the absorption plant's running energy cost is close to zero (only the pump and cooling-water pumps), while the compression plant buys 12.4 kW of electricity: about 12.4 × 24 = 298 kWh a day.
  6. Penalties of absorption: the cooling tower and water flow must handle 140.9 kW instead of 62.4 kW, more than twice as much; the plant is larger and costs more to buy; ammonia needs leak precautions (as it does in large compression cold stores too).
  7. Comparison:

Choose vapour compression if the steam is intermittent, cooling water is scarce, or the process may later use the steam itself. A common answer is both: absorption for the base load and a compression unit as standby.
Analyze The Carnot cycle has the highest efficiency between two temperatures, yet power plants use the Brayton, Stirling and Rankine cycles. Using work ratio, mean effective pressure and mean temperature of heat addition, analyse why, with the numbers from this unit's examples. 13 marks

Points to cover. Carnot limit and its two practical faults; work ratio and MEP of the Carnot example; Brayton: η and work, back work ratio; Stirling: regenerator recovers Carnot efficiency; Rankine: isothermal heat in the dome, mean temperature of heat addition; verdict.

Model answer.

  1. The limit: any engine between TH and TL has η ≤ 1 − TL/TH. A practical cycle must also give a lot of net work per kg and per litre of machine, and must keep that net work large compared with the losses. Two measures capture this: the work ratio (net work/positive work) and the mean effective pressure.
  2. Carnot with air between 300 K and 900 K (volume ratio 2 during heat addition): η = 66.7%, but wnet = 119.4 kJ/kg out of 609.8 kJ/kg of positive work, a work ratio of 0.196, and an MEP of only 0.716 bar despite a pressure ratio of 94. A 10% friction loss on the positive work (61.0 kJ/kg) would halve the output. Isothermal heat transfer at engine speed is also impossible.
  3. Brayton (rp = 8, 300 to 1200 K): η = 44.8%, lower than Carnot for the same extremes (75%), because heat enters along a rising constant-pressure line from 543 K, not all at 1200 K. Its back work ratio is 0.453, so its work ratio is 0.547, far better than Carnot's, and it is a compact steady-flow machine. The gas-turbine problem example showed the cost of the remaining back work: with 85% and 88% component efficiencies η fell from 48.2% to 33.2%.
  4. Stirling (300 to 900 K, volume ratio 2): the isothermal steps are the same as Carnot's, but the isentropic steps are replaced by constant-volume steps whose heat is stored and returned by the regenerator. With a perfect regenerator η = 66.7%, exactly Carnot, and the constant-volume steps cost no compression work. Without the regenerator η falls to 19.6%; with 80% recovery, 45.0%. The cycle's quality rests on the regenerator.
  5. Rankine (40 bar, 400 °C, 0.1 bar): inside the dome, boiling and condensing are naturally isothermal, so the steam cycle gets Carnot-like heat transfer for free. The pump handles liquid, so wP = 4.03 kJ/kg against wT = 1070.0 kJ/kg: a work ratio of 0.996. Its efficiency, 35.3%, is below the Carnot value of 52.6% between 400 °C and 45.8 °C because the mean temperature of heat addition is only 493 K; feedwater heating and superheating happen at lower temperatures.
  6. Summary:
    Cycle (example)EfficiencyWork ratioPractical strength
    Carnot, 300-900 K66.7%0.196none: a reference only
    Brayton, rp = 844.8%0.547compact, steady flow, high power
    Stirling with regenerator66.7%highany external heat source
    Rankine, 40 bar 400 °C35.3%0.996liquid pumping, isothermal boiling
  7. Verdict: a practical cycle gives up some ideal efficiency to gain a high work ratio and MEP. Engineers then recover efficiency by raising the mean temperature of heat addition: regeneration and reheat in the Rankine cycle, regenerators and intercooling in gas turbines, and combined gas-steam cycles.

Glossary

Absorber
The vessel of an absorption plant where refrigerant vapour dissolves in the absorbent, releasing heat.
Air standard cycle
An ideal cycle in which a fixed mass of air, treated as an ideal gas, goes through reversible processes with heat added and rejected from outside.
Back work ratio
Compressor work divided by turbine work, in a gas turbine or steam plant.
Closed feedwater heater
A shell-and-tube exchanger in which bled steam heats feedwater without mixing.
Coefficient of performance (COP)
Useful heat moved divided by the energy input: cooling over work for a refrigerator, heating over work for a heat pump.
Compression ratio
r = v₁/v₂, the largest volume of the cylinder divided by the clearance volume.
Cut-off ratio
The volume at the end of constant-pressure heat addition divided by the volume at its start (v₃/v₂ in Diesel, v₄/v₃ in Dual).
Deaerator
The open feedwater heater of a large plant, which also removes dissolved gases from the water.
Dryness fraction
The mass of vapour divided by the total mass of a wet steam mixture.
Generator (absorption plant)
The vessel where heat drives the refrigerant out of the strong solution at high pressure.
Knock
Self-ignition of part of the fuel-air charge before the flame reaches it, which limits the compression ratio of petrol engines.
Mean effective pressure
The constant pressure that would do the cycle's net work in one stroke: wnet/(v₁ − v₂).
Mean temperature of heat addition
qin divided by the entropy rise during heat addition; the higher it is, the higher the cycle efficiency.
Open feedwater heater
A mixing chamber in which bled steam mixes with feedwater at the bleed pressure; it needs a pump after it.
Pressure ratio (explosion ratio)
In the Dual cycle, p₃/p₂ during constant-volume heat addition; in the Brayton cycle, the compressor exit over inlet pressure.
Rectifier
The part above the generator of an ammonia-water plant that condenses water vapour out of the ammonia vapour.
Refrigerating effect
Heat taken from the cold space per kg of refrigerant, h₁ − h₄.
Regenerator
A heat store inside a cycle that takes heat from the working fluid in one process and gives it back in another, as in the Stirling engine.
Reheat
Heating steam again at an intermediate pressure between two turbine stages, to keep the exhaust drier.
Relative efficiency
The actual thermal efficiency of an engine divided by its air standard efficiency.
Specific steam consumption
Steam needed per kWh of net work, 3600/wnet kg/kWh.
Subcooling
Cooling the condensed liquid below its saturation temperature before the expansion valve.
Throttling
An irreversible pressure drop through a valve at constant enthalpy.
Tonne of refrigeration (TR)
A cooling rate of 211 kJ/min, or 3.517 kW.
Work ratio
Net work divided by the positive (expansion) work of a cycle; low values make a cycle sensitive to losses.
