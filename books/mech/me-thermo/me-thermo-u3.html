About this unit

This unit starts from a simple question: why does heat never flow uphill on its own? 3.1 Heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps

A heat engine sits between a hot body and a cold body. It takes heat Q₁ from the hot body, turns part of it into work W, and dumps the rest Q₂ into the cold body. Run the same device backwards and it becomes a refrigerator or a heat pump: now you pay work to carry heat from cold to hot. This is the machine behind every power plant, every air conditioner on a wall and every cold storage. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and the energy accounting for all of them. The second-law limit on how good they can be comes later in the unit.

You need: heat and work with their sign convention, and the first law applied to a cycle.

Source, sink and a cyclic device

A thermal reservoir is a body so large that adding or removing heat does not change its temperature. A lake, the atmosphere and a large furnace are treated as reservoirs in problems. The hot reservoir at T₁ is the source. The cold reservoir at T₂ is the sink. Temperatures are in kelvin, and T₁ is greater than T₂.

Every device in this chapter has the same three parts: a source, a sink, and a device placed between them. The device works in cycles, so it returns to its own state at the end of each cycle. Its internal energy therefore returns to the same value, and the net change over one cycle is zero. The first law for a cycle then connects only the heat and the work crossing the boundary.

Q₁ = W + Q₂

Q₁ is the heat taken from the source, in kJ; W is the net work delivered by the device, in kJ; Q₂ is the heat given to the sink, in kJ. Heat into the device is positive and work done by the device is positive, the usual sign convention.

The same balance serves engines and reversed devices. Only the direction of the arrows changes, and with it which quantity you pay for and which one you want. That is why one chapter covers power plants, room air conditioners and hot-water heat pumps together.

Heat engine and thermal efficiency

A heat engine takes heat Q₁ from the source, converts part of it into work W, and rejects the rest Q₂ to the sink. The working fluid may be steam, air or burnt gas. The accounting is the same in every case.

η = W / Q₁ = (Q₁ − Q₂) / Q₁ = 1 − Q₂ / Q₁

η (Greek eta) is the thermal efficiency, a pure fraction; multiply by 100 for per cent. W is the net work output in kJ, Q₁ the heat supplied from the source in kJ, and Q₂ the heat rejected to the sink in kJ.

Efficiency is what you get divided by what you paid for. In an engine the payment is the heat Q₁ drawn from the source, so η is always less than 1 whenever Q₂ is greater than zero. A claim of 100% efficiency means Q₂ = 0, an engine that dumps no heat at all. That machine is forbidden by the second law, and the Kelvin-Planck statement states the ban precisely.

Watch the denominator. It is Q₁, not Q₂. Dividing work by the rejected heat measures nothing useful.

Running it backwards: refrigerator and heat pump

Reverse every arrow. Work W now goes in, heat Q₂ is pulled out of the cold space, and Q₁ = Q₂ + W is pushed into the warm room. The hardware is the same. What changes is which heat you want: the cold-side heat for a refrigerator, the hot-side heat for a heat pump.

COPR = Q₂ / W and COPHP = Q₁ / W
COP is the coefficient of performance, a pure number. Q₂ is the heat removed from the cold space in kJ, Q₁ the heat delivered to the warm space in kJ, and W the work input in kJ. Some syllabi call the COP the performance factor.

Divide Q₁ = Q₂ + W by W and you get COPHP = COPR + 1. A device with COPR = 1.667 becomes a heat pump of COP 2.667 on the same work.

A COP above 1 surprises students. It is not free energy. The COP compares heat moved with work paid, not heat with heat. Most of the heat moved was already sitting in the cold space and was only carried uphill. Energy in still equals energy out.

Room air conditioners are sold in tons of cooling. One ton of refrigeration is 3.517 kW of heat removed, so a 1.5 ton split unit removes 1.5 × 3.517 ≈ 5.3 kW of heat. If its compressor draws 1.5 kW of electricity, its COP is 5.3 / 1.5 ≈ 3.5.

Worked example

Question. A cyclic engine receives 800 kJ of heat from a reservoir at 600 K and rejects 500 kJ to a reservoir at 300 K. (a) Find the work output and the thermal efficiency. (b) A refrigerator is driven by 300 kJ of work and removes 500 kJ of heat from a cold store at 270 K; the room around it is at 300 K. Find the heat it rejects to the room, its COP as a refrigerator, and its COP if used as a heat pump. Compare with the reversible limit T₂/(T₁ − T₂).

  1. (a) Work output from the cycle energy balance: W = Q₁ − Q₂ = 800 kJ − 500 kJ = 300 kJ.
  2. Thermal efficiency: η = W / Q₁ = 300 kJ / 800 kJ = 0.375, that is 37.5%.
  3. The reservoir temperatures are not needed for η; they set only the reversible limit 1 − 300/600 = 0.5 (see the Carnot cycle).
  4. (b) Energy balance for the refrigerator cycle: Q₁ = Q₂ + W = 500 kJ + 300 kJ = 800 kJ rejected to the room.
  5. COP as a refrigerator: COPR = Q₂ / W = 500 kJ / 300 kJ = 1.667.
  6. COP as a heat pump (the wanted heat is now Q₁): COPHP = Q₁ / W = 800 kJ / 300 kJ = 2.667. Check: COPR + 1 = 1.667 + 1 = 2.667.
  7. Reversible limit: 270 / (300 − 270) = 9. The actual 1.667 is far below it: allowed, but a poor, irreversible machine.

Answer. (a) W = 300 kJ and η = 37.5%. (b) 800 kJ is rejected to the room; COPR = 1.667 and COPHP = 2.667, well below the reversible refrigerator limit of 9 between 270 K and 300 K.

Exam traps

  • Efficiency with the wrong denominator. η = W/Q₁, the heat taken from the source. Writing W/Q₂ gives 0.6 in the worked example, which means nothing.
  • Forgetting COPHP = COPR + 1, and quoting the same COP for a fridge and for a heat pump built on the same cycle.
  • Reading a COP of 3 as an efficiency of 300% or as free energy. The COP counts heat moved, not heat created; Q₁ = Q₂ + W still holds.
  • Confusing a ton of refrigeration with electrical power. One ton is 3.517 kW of heat removed; a 1.5 ton unit removes about 5.3 kW of heat but draws far less than that from the socket.
  • Feeding the reservoir temperatures into 1 − T₂/T₁ here. That formula belongs to a reversible engine; it gives 0.5 for the 600 K and 300 K reservoirs while the actual engine in the example gives 0.375.

3.2 Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements

The first law balances energy but cannot fix the direction of flow. The second law does, in two equivalent forms. Kelvin-Planck speaks of engines; Clausius speaks of refrigerators. Each forbids one impossible machine. Engineers use them to reject impossible claims and to set plant efficiency limits.

You need: heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps for the energy-flow pictures, and the first-law energy balance for a cycle.

Kelvin-Planck: no engine on one reservoir

An engine takes heat Q₁ from a hot reservoir at T₁, rejects Q₂ to a cold reservoir at T₂, and gives work W = Q₁ − Q₂.

η = W / Q₁ = (Q₁ − Q₂) / Q₁

η is thermal efficiency; W is net work per cycle in kJ; Q₁ and Q₂ are heat taken and rejected per cycle in kJ.

Kelvin-Planck statement: it is impossible for any device working in a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and produce net work.

In a cycle means it returns to its starting state. Single reservoir means no other body. Then Q₂ = 0 and η = 100%. Such a machine is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM2.

A PMM2 obeys the first law: 1000 kJ in, 1000 kJ out. What it breaks is the direction rule: a ship could then run on the heat of the sea alone. Real engines always reject heat: a coal plant to a river, a car through its radiator. Compare the perpetual motion machine of the first kind, PMM1, which would give work with no energy input at all and so breaks the first law. The first law alone cannot rule out a PMM2; that is its main limitation, and the reason the second law is needed.

Clausius: heat does not climb by itself

A refrigerator runs backwards: takes Q₂ from a cold body at T₂, receives work W, delivers Q₁ = Q₂ + W to a hot body at T₁.

Clausius statement: it is impossible to construct a device working in a cycle whose only effect is to transfer heat from a lower temperature to a higher temperature.

Only effect carries the weight: no work, no fuel, no change in device or surroundings. Refrigeration is not forbidden. A kitchen fridge moves heat from 4 °C to 35 °C using wall-socket work. Hot tea cools and never heats itself; a sealed box that chills inside and warms the room with no plug violates Clausius.

The two statements are one law

Neither follows from the first law; each is a postulate. Accepting one forces the other. Both proofs use contradiction.

If a PMM2 existed, Clausius would fail. Let a 100% engine take Q from the hot body at T₁ and give W = Q, rejecting nothing. Let that work drive a reversible refrigerator between T₁ and the colder T₂: it removes Q₂ from T₂ and delivers Q₂ + W to T₁. The hot body loses Q and gains Q₂ + Q, a net gain of Q₂. The cold body loses Q₂. The work made equals the work used. So heat Q₂ has climbed from cold to hot with no work from outside: Clausius is broken.

If Clausius failed, a PMM2 would exist. Suppose a device lifts Q₂ from T₂ to T₁ with no work. Run an ordinary engine between the same bodies: it takes Q₁ from T₁, gives W = Q₁ − Q₂ and rejects Q₂ to T₂. The work-free device carries that Q₂ straight back up. Now the hot body loses Q₁ − Q₂ = W, the cold body is unchanged, and W of work appears. The combination is a cycle that makes work from one reservoir: a PMM2.

Both directions hold; the statements stand or fall together.

What follows at once

  • Heat crosses a temperature difference only from hot to cold, unless work is spent.
  • A cycle on one reservoir cannot produce net work: no engine has η = 100%.
  • No real engine reaches 100%; the Carnot cycle gives the limit for a given pair of temperatures.
  • A real refrigerator needs work, so COP = Q₂/W is finite.

Worked example

Question. An inventor claims an engine that takes 1000 kJ from a reservoir at 500 K, rejects 400 kJ to a reservoir at 300 K, and delivers 600 kJ of work. (a) Check the claim against the first law and against a reversible engine between the same two reservoirs. (b) Combine the claim with a reversed reversible engine to show it violates the Kelvin-Planck statement. (c) Use the excess work in a reversible refrigerator to show it violates the Clausius statement too.

  1. First law: W = 1000 − 400 = 600 kJ. Claim matches; first law accepts it.
  2. ηclaim = 600/1000 = 60%.
  3. Reversible engine: ηrev = 1 − 300/500 = 40%. With Q₁ = 1000 kJ, Wrev = 400 kJ, Q₂,rev = 600 kJ.
  4. Excess work = 600 − 400 = 200 kJ.
  5. Reverse the reversible engine: driven by 400 kJ, it removes 600 kJ from 300 K and delivers 1000 kJ to 500 K.
  6. Combine. Hot reservoir: −1000 + 1000 = 0. Cold body: +400 − 600 = −200 kJ, so 200 kJ leaves. Work: 600 − 400 = 200 kJ net out.
  7. This cyclic pair takes 200 kJ from one 300 K reservoir and makes 200 kJ work. PMM2; Kelvin-Planck violated. First law still balances.
  8. Spend 200 kJ in a reversible refrigerator between 500 K and 300 K. COP = 300/(500 − 300) = 1.5. It removes 300 kJ from 300 K and delivers 500 kJ to 500 K.
  9. Add to step 7: cold body loses 200 + 300 = 500 kJ; 500 K reservoir gains 500 kJ; work in equals work out. Heat climbs 300 K to 500 K with no work input: Clausius violated.

Answer. The claim obeys the first law but gives 200 kJ extra work; combined with reversible devices it moves 500 kJ from 300 K to 500 K with zero work input, breaking both statements.

Exam traps

  • Kelvin-Planck is about a cyclic device, not one process. Isothermal expansion of an ideal gas is legal; repeating it needs compression work.
  • The statements are one law in two forms. A violation of one violates the other.
  • A PMM2 obeys conservation of energy. It breaks direction. Calling it a first-law violation is wrong; that is PMM1.
  • Clausius does not ban refrigerators. It bans heat uphill with no work and no other change.
  • For a reversible refrigerator, COP = T₂/(T₁ − T₂); cold temperature on top.

3.3 Reversible and irreversible processes

A process is reversible only if it can be run backwards and leave absolutely no trace — in the gas, in the cylinder, in the room. Real engines never manage this, but the idea earns its place: it marks the best that any device could ever do between the same two states, so the gap between it and a real machine measures what we are wasting. This chapter defines the reversible process, lists what spoils it, and shows how the same gas expanding the same way can deliver work or deliver nothing.

You need: the quasi-static process, state and path, and the isothermal process of an ideal gas, all from the earlier topics.

Two conditions, together

A process is reversible if the system and its surroundings can both be returned to their exact starting states, leaving no change anywhere. Nothing may be left behind: no warm spot on the cylinder, no stirred-up oil, no worn bearing.

Two conditions must hold at the same time.

  • Quasi-static: at every instant the driving force exceeds the resisting force by only an infinitesimal amount. The piston moves because the gas pressure is p + dp, not p + 2 bar. Heat flows because the gas sits at T + dT, not at T + 200 K.
  • No dissipative effect: no friction, no viscosity, no electrical resistance, no plastic deformation, no mixing of two different fluids.

The first condition removes finite gradients inside and across the boundary. The second rules out dissipative effects. A process is reversible only when the first holds and the second is met: no finite gradient anywhere inside or across the boundary, and no dissipation.

W = ∫₁² p dV

p is the pressure at the moving boundary in kPa, dV is the small volume change in m³, and W is the work in kJ. For a reversible path, p must be the gas pressure itself, uniform and known at every instant.

A reversible process creates no entropy, so it is the yardstick for everything else. The Carnot engine is only as good as this limit allows.

What makes a process irreversible

Every real process carries at least one of these. Each has the same two signatures: a finite gradient, or a one-way conversion of work into internal energy.

  • Friction in a pump or under a piston: shaft work heats the oil and the metal, and that heat never climbs back into the shaft by itself.
  • Unrestrained expansion: gas in one flask is connected to an evacuated flask. Nothing pushes back, so W = 0, yet the gas spreads over both flasks and cannot be gathered into one without spending work.
  • Heat transfer across a finite temperature difference: a hot vessel left in a room cools to room temperature. The same heat will not flow back up the same difference.
  • Mixing of two streams: hot and cold water joining in a shower line. Separating them again needs a heat pump and work.
  • Current through a resistance: all the electrical work becomes heat in the wire.
  • Plastic deformation: bending a steel rod past its yield point leaves it permanently bent and slightly warm.

Entropy and the Clausius inequality turn these signatures into a number.

Internal and external reversibility

Internal reversibility concerns only what happens inside the boundary: the gas has no pressure gradient, no temperature gradient and no internal friction, so at every instant it is uniform and in equilibrium.

External reversibility concerns the link with the surroundings: heat crosses only to a reservoir at the same temperature, and work is delivered to a load that is raised with no outside friction. A process may be internally reversible and externally irreversible. Standard texts define a reversible (totally reversible) process as one that is both internally and externally reversible. Many numerical problems only need internal reversibility, so check whether the surroundings are also idealised before assuming total reversibility.

A limit, drawn not reached

No real process is reversible. We draw the reversible path the way a mathematician draws a smooth curve through measured points: it gives the best number possible, and the difference from reality is lost work, priced as exergy in the topic of available energy.

Slowness alone does not buy reversibility. A piston pushed very slowly against dry friction is quasi-static and still irreversible, because heat keeps appearing in the cylinder.

Worked example

Question. 0.5 kg of air at 300 K expands at constant temperature from V₁ = 0.2 m³ to V₂ = 0.4 m³. Take R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). (a) Find the work delivered if the expansion is reversible and isothermal. (b) The same air is instead allowed to expand freely into an evacuated flask until it occupies 0.4 m³. Find the work delivered and the work lost.

  1. Data: m = 0.5 kg, T = 300 K, V₁ = 0.2 m³, V₂ = 0.4 m³, R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K).
  2. For an ideal gas at constant temperature, pV = mRT, so p = mRT/V.
  3. Reversible work: Wrev = ∫ p dV = mRT ∫ dV/V = mRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  4. mRT = 0.5 × 0.287 × 300 = 43.05 kJ. So p₁ = 43.05/0.2 = 215.3 kPa and p₂ = 43.05/0.4 = 107.6 kPa.
  5. V₂/V₁ = 0.4 / 0.2 = 2, and ln 2 = 0.6931.
  6. Wrev = 43.05 × 0.6931 = 29.84 kJ.
  7. Free expansion: the gas pushes against nothing, so W = 0 kJ. The end state is the same, 0.4 m³ at 300 K, because an ideal gas’s internal energy depends only on T and Q = W = 0 leaves T unchanged.
  8. Work lost = 29.84 − 0 = 29.84 kJ. No energy is destroyed; what is lost is the work the reversible path could have delivered. In entropy terms the gas gains mR ln 2 = 0.5 × 0.287 × 0.6931 = 0.0995 kJ/K with no heat in, and T₀ × 0.0995 = 300 × 0.0995 = 29.84 kJ, the same number.

Answer. The reversible isothermal expansion delivers Wrev = 29.84 kJ; the free expansion delivers W = 0 kJ and loses all 29.84 kJ of possible work.

Exam traps

  • Quasi-static is necessary but not sufficient. A slow process with friction, or with a finite temperature difference across the wall, is still irreversible.
  • Reversible gives maximum work out of an expansion and minimum work into a compression. Do not apply the maximum rule to a compressor.
  • Free expansion gives W = 0 but is not reversible. For an ideal gas in an insulated flask the temperature also stays the same, which fools students into calling it isothermal and reversible.
  • Internally reversible is weaker than reversible. The boundary may still lose work to outside friction.
  • The entropy of the system may fall in an irreversible process, as when a hot block cools. The second law is applied to system plus surroundings together.

3.4 Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem

The Carnot cycle is a closed loop of four reversible steps run between a hot reservoir at T₁ and a cold reservoir at T₂. It is the best engine possible between those two temperatures. Every real engine, from a backup generator to a coal power plant, is judged against it. Its efficiency depends only on T₁ and T₂, not on the gas, the size of the machine or the pressures used.

You need: reversible processes, and the isothermal and adiabatic work formulas for an ideal gas.

The four reversible steps

Take m kg of ideal gas in a piston-cylinder. The hot reservoir is at T₁ and the cold reservoir at T₂. Every step is reversible: no friction, no finite temperature gap, no unrestrained expansion. Define Q₁ as heat taken in and Q₂ as heat rejected, both positive magnitudes.

  1. 1→2 isothermal expansion at T₁. The gas touches the hot reservoir, takes Q₁, and does work. T stays T₁.
  2. 2→3 adiabatic expansion. The gas is insulated. It expands further, does work, and T falls from T₁ to T₂. A reversible adiabatic step is isentropic (see entropy).
  3. 3→4 isothermal compression at T₂. The gas touches the cold reservoir. Work goes in and the gas rejects Q₂ at constant T₂.
  4. 4→1 adiabatic compression. The gas is insulated. Work goes in and T rises from T₂ back to T₁. The gas returns to state 1.

On a p-V diagram the path is two isotherms cut by two adiabats. The enclosed area is the net work per cycle.

Efficiency from the gas

For an ideal gas, the two isothermal heat transfers have fixed forms. The two adiabatic steps link their volumes.

Q₁ = m R T₁ ln(V₂/V₁), Q₂ = m R T₂ ln(V₃/V₄)

m is mass in kg; R is the gas constant in kJ/(kg·K); T is absolute temperature in K; V₁ to V₄ are total volumes in m³; Q₂ is the positive magnitude rejected.

The adiabatic steps obey TVγ−1 = constant, where γ = cp/cv is the ratio of specific heats: T₁V₂γ−1 = T₂V₃γ−1 for 2→3 and T₁V₁γ−1 = T₂V₄γ−1 for 4→1. Divide the first by the second: (V₂/V₁)γ−1 = (V₃/V₄)γ−1, so V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄. The logarithms in Q₁ and Q₂ are then equal and cancel in Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁.

η = W/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁ = 1 − T₂/T₁

η is thermal efficiency; W=Q₁−Q₂ is net work per cycle; T₁ and T₂ must be in kelvin.

The result holds for every working substance, not only a gas. A derivation on one substance is enough because Carnot's theorem says the reversible efficiency is fixed by the reservoirs.

Carnot's theorem

Carnot's theorem has two parts.

  • No engine working between two fixed reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine working between the same two reservoirs.
  • All reversible engines working between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever the working substance, the size of the cycle or the pressures used.

Some syllabi call the whole result Carnot’s principle and list part 2 as a corollary. Both parts are proved by contradiction. Part 1. Suppose an engine X between the two reservoirs were more efficient than a reversible engine R. Let both take the same Q₁ from the hot reservoir, so X gives more work: WX > WR. Run R backwards as a refrigerator, driven by WR taken from X’s output: it puts exactly Q₁ back into the hot reservoir. The hot reservoir is now unchanged, and the pair delivers the surplus WX − WR as work, all drawn from the cold reservoir. That is a PMM2, forbidden by the Kelvin-Planck statement. Part 2. Let X be a second reversible engine. The same argument shows X cannot beat R; X is reversible too, so running X backwards shows R cannot beat X. Their efficiencies are equal.

A real engine between T₁ and T₂ has irreversibilities and lower efficiency. The Carnot value 1−T₂/T₁ is the upper limit, not a promise. It is the benchmark used to judge power plants and refrigerators.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of air (R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K), γ = 1.4) runs a Carnot cycle between 900 K and 300 K. The isothermal expansion at 900 K takes the volume from 0.1 m³ to 0.2 m³. Find Q₁, Q₂, W and η, and the volumes at the other two corners of the cycle.

  1. Data: m = 1 kg, R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K), T₁ = 900 K, T₂ = 300 K, V₁ = 0.1 m³, V₂ = 0.2 m³. For the two adiabatic steps, V₃/V₄ = V₂/V₁ = 0.2/0.1 = 2.
  2. Q₁ = mRT₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = 1 × 0.287 × 900 × ln 2 = 258.3 × 0.6931 = 179.04 kJ ≈ 179.0 kJ.
  3. Q₂ = mRT₂ ln(V₃/V₄) = 1 × 0.287 × 300 × ln 2 = 86.1 × 0.6931 = 59.68 kJ ≈ 59.7 kJ.
  4. W = Q₁ − Q₂ = 179.04 − 59.68 = 119.36 kJ ≈ 119.4 kJ.
  5. η = W/Q₁ = 119.36/179.04 = 0.6667, and the check η = 1 − 300/900 = 0.6667 agrees: 66.7%.
  6. Corner volumes from TVγ−1 = constant: V₄ = V₁(T₁/T₂)1/(γ−1) = 0.1 × 32.5 = 0.1 × 15.59 = 1.559 m³, and V₃ = V₂ × 15.59 = 3.118 m³ (check V₃/V₄ = 2).
  7. Pressures from p = mRT/V: p₁ = 258.3/0.1 = 2583 kPa and p₃ = 86.1/3.118 = 27.6 kPa. The gas swells about 31 times and the pressure falls about 94 times for only 119.4 kJ of work, about 39.6 kJ per m³ swept. This is why no practical engine runs a Carnot cycle on a gas.

Answer. Q₁ = 179.0 kJ, Q₂ = 59.7 kJ, W = 119.4 kJ and η = 66.7%; the cycle reaches V₃ = 3.118 m³ and V₄ = 1.559 m³.

Exam traps

  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. Using °C in 1−T₂/T₁ gives a meaningless number. Convert first.
  • Efficiency is a ratio of energies per cycle. It does not depend on m or on the cycle's size; do not multiply η by mass.
  • Q₂ in 1−Q₂/Q₁ is the positive heat rejected. If your first-law sign convention makes Q₂ negative, do not subtract a negative by mistake.
  • An efficiency above 1−T₂/T₁ is impossible for any engine between those reservoirs. Equality means reversible; a real engine is lower.
  • The Carnot cycle is not a real Rankine or Brayton cycle. Those cycles come later in this subject; do not use their practical efficiencies as Carnot limits.

3.5 Thermodynamic temperature scale

A thermometer needs a scale, and gas thermometers using different gases disagree slightly. The thermodynamic temperature scale removes the working substance from the definition. Carnot's theorem says every reversible engine between the same two reservoirs has the same heat ratio, so that ratio can define temperature. We set T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂. This makes Carnot efficiency exactly 1 − T₂/T₁ for every substance. The scale is used whenever second-law calculations need absolute temperatures, such as entropy and exergy, and it fixes the Kelvin scale.

You need: Carnot’s theorem, and temperature and the zeroth law from the basic concepts.

Why a gas scale is not enough

A gas thermometer reads temperature by a gas property, for example pressure at fixed volume. It assumes p/ptr = T/Ttr, where p is the gas pressure, ptr is the pressure at the triple point, T is the temperature to be found, and Ttr is 273.16 K. Different gases do not follow this relation exactly. Readings from helium, nitrogen and argon agree closely near the ideal-gas range and drift apart outside it. A fundamental scale cannot depend on which gas is inside the bulb.

Carnot’s theorem removes the gas. Every reversible engine working between the same two reservoirs has the same thermal efficiency η, where η = W/Q₁, W is work output and Q₁ is heat taken from the hot reservoir. The theorem holds for any working substance: gas, steam, or a magnetic material. So the heat ratio Q₁/Q₂ of a reversible engine is a property of the two reservoirs only, not of the engine. That ratio is the raw material for a substance-free temperature scale.

The practical problem is small but serious. A thermometer calibrated with one gas can disagree with a thermometer calibrated with another gas by amounts that matter in careful work. Metrology needs one scale that every thermometer realises, not a family of gas-dependent scales.

Defining temperature by a reversible engine

Take a reversible engine between a hot reservoir at thermodynamic temperature T₁ and a cold reservoir at T₂. Let Q₁ be heat taken from the hot reservoir and Q₂ be heat rejected to the cold reservoir, both as positive magnitudes in joule. Carnot's theorem says Q₁/Q₂ is fixed by the two reservoirs. Define temperature so that the same ratio holds:

T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂

T₁ and T₂ are thermodynamic temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs, in kelvin; Q₁ is heat taken from the hot reservoir by a reversible engine, in joule; Q₂ is heat rejected to the cold reservoir, in joule.

This is the thermodynamic temperature scale. It is also called the Kelvin scale. The definition uses only heat quantities, which are measured with a calorimeter or from work and first-law balances. It never asks what the engine contains. If a reversible engine takes 600 J from a hot reservoir and rejects 300 J to a cold reservoir, then T₁/T₂ = 600/300 = 2. The hot reservoir is twice as hot as the cold one on this scale.

  • Only ratios are fixed. Multiplying every T by a constant leaves every Q₁/Q₂ unchanged.
  • One fixed point is needed. The triple point of water was assigned exactly 273.16 K. Since 2019 the kelvin is fixed through the Boltzmann constant instead, and the triple point still sits at 273.16 K to within a fraction of a millikelvin.
  • With this fixed point, the scale is fixed completely and is written in kelvin, symbol K.

The scale coincides with the ideal-gas scale. For an ideal gas, a reversible Carnot cycle gives Q₁/Q₂ = T₁/T₂ when T is the ideal-gas temperature. So a constant-volume gas thermometer reads thermodynamic temperature once its readings are extrapolated to zero pressure, where every real gas behaves as an ideal gas. In that limit the agreement is exact, and it is the reason the Kelvin scale looks familiar.

Efficiency and the absolute zero

For a reversible engine, the first law gives W = Q₁ − Q₂. Thermal efficiency is η = W/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁. Insert the defining ratio Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁:

ηrev = 1 − T₂/T₁

ηrev is the thermal efficiency of a reversible engine, dimensionless; T₁ is the hot-reservoir temperature, in kelvin; T₂ is the cold-reservoir temperature, in kelvin.

This result is exact for every reversible engine, whatever the working substance. For a real engine between the same reservoirs, η is lower. The reversible value is the yardstick. It is not a prediction of a real engine’s efficiency; the Clausius inequality shows how irreversibility enters.

η = 1 would need T₂/T₁ = 0, so T₂ = 0 K. Absolute zero is not available. The third law says it cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. Every real engine with a sink above 0 K rejects some heat, so its efficiency is below 100%.

The definition also sets the zero: T = 0 K is the lower limit of the scale. It is approached, never reached. Temperatures in °C are shifted Kelvin values: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15. Use kelvin in every second-law formula.

Worked example

Question. Two reversible engines work in series. The first takes heat from a source at T₁ and rejects it at T₂. The second takes that heat at T₂ and rejects it at 300 K. Each engine has a thermal efficiency of 20%. Find T₁ and T₂ on the thermodynamic scale.

  1. Write the reversible efficiency for the second engine: η₂ = 1 − TL/TH = 1 − 300 K / T₂.
  2. Set η₂ = 0.20: 0.20 = 1 − 300/T₂. Hence 300/T₂ = 0.80, so T₂ = 300 K / 0.80 = 375 K.
  3. Write the reversible efficiency for the first engine: η₁ = 1 − T₂/T₁ = 1 − 375 K / T₁.
  4. Set η₁ = 0.20: 0.20 = 1 − 375/T₁. Hence 375/T₁ = 0.80, so T₁ = 375 K / 0.80 = 468.75 K.
  5. Check the heat ratios. For engine 1, Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁ = 375/468.75 = 0.80. For engine 2, Q₃/Q₂ = 300/375 = 0.80, where Q₃ is heat rejected to 300 K. Both engines reject 80% of the heat they receive, as a 20% efficiency requires.
  6. Overall check with 100 kJ taken from the source: engine 1 gives 20 kJ and passes 80 kJ to the 375 K reservoir; engine 2 gives 0.2 × 80 = 16 kJ and rejects 64 kJ at 300 K. Together they give 36 kJ from 100 kJ, η = 0.36, and one reversible engine between 468.75 K and 300 K gives 1 − 300/468.75 = 0.36. The scale is consistent.

Answer. T₂ = 375 K and T₁ = 468.75 K on the thermodynamic scale; the pair together works like one reversible engine of 36% efficiency.

  • Use absolute temperatures in kelvin. Writing the Celsius value 27 where 300 K belongs gives a meaningless answer.
  • The definition fixes only ratios. Without the triple-point value 273.16 K, all temperatures could be multiplied by an arbitrary constant.
  • Efficiency 100% needs T₂ = 0 K exactly. A very small sink temperature still gives η below 1.
  • Do not use the Carnot formula to predict a real engine's efficiency. It is the upper limit, not the actual value.

3.6 Clausius inequality

The Clausius inequality turns the second law into one number for a whole cycle: add up δQ/T for every small heat transfer around the cycle, and the total cannot be positive. Heat entering is positive, heat leaving is negative, and T is the boundary temperature in kelvin where the heat crosses. Equality means the cycle is reversible; less than zero means it is irreversible. It matters because it gives the sign test for real engines, refrigerators and power plants, and it is the step that leads to entropy.

You need: Carnot’s theorem and the thermodynamic temperature scale for the reversible reference engine and the kelvin.

The inequality

For a cycle, follow every small heat transfer δQ across the boundary of the system. Divide each δQ by the absolute temperature T at which that heat crosses. Add the terms around the closed cycle. The Clausius inequality says the total cannot be positive. For an engine, the hot-stream term is positive and the cold-stream term is negative. For a refrigerator, the same rule applies: heat taken in by the system is positive.

∮ δQ/T ≤ 0

δQ is the small heat transfer in kJ, positive into the system and negative out of it; T is the boundary temperature in kelvin at which that heat crosses; ∮ means the sum around one complete cycle.

For a cycle with a few separate heat transfers, use Σ Qi/Ti ≤ 0 with the same sign rule. If the heat comes from a reservoir at Tr, use Tr for that term. The temperature inside the system may vary; it is not used unless the boundary itself is at that temperature.

Why a positive total is impossible

Suppose a cycle gave a positive total, ∮δQ/T > 0. Wherever a small heat δQ crosses the boundary at temperature T, place a small reversible engine between one fixed reservoir at T₀ and that part of the boundary. It supplies exactly the δQ the system receives there, and, being reversible, it draws δQ₀ = δQ T₀/T from the T₀ reservoir. The actual cycle and all the small engines together form one device that returns to its starting state and exchanges heat with the single reservoir at T₀ only.

  1. Sum the T₀ heat: Q₀,total = Σ δQ T₀/T = T₀ ∮δQ/T.
  2. The first law for the combined device gives Wtotal = Q₀,total, because it has no net change of state.
  3. A positive integral makes Q₀,total positive. Then Wtotal is positive: work from one reservoir with no other change. That is exactly the Kelvin-Planck forbidden machine.
  4. So the positive case is impossible. The total must be zero or negative.

The argument works for any T₀ > 0. It does not assume the actual cycle is reversible. It only uses a reversible reference unit, whose behaviour is fixed by the thermodynamic temperature scale.

The three cases and the Kelvin-Planck link

  • ∮δQ/T = 0: the cycle is reversible. Every heat transfer can be reversed with no trace.
  • ∮δQ/T < 0: the cycle is irreversible. Friction, finite temperature difference or unrestrained expansion is present.
  • ∮δQ/T > 0: no cycle can have this sign. It would break the second law.

For a cycle that exchanges heat with one reservoir only, at T₀, the integral is Qin/T₀ − Qout/T₀ = (Qin − Qout)/T₀ = W/T₀. The inequality says W/T₀ ≤ 0, so W ≤ 0. A one-reservoir cycle cannot deliver work. That is the Kelvin-Planck statement. The zero case leads to entropy as a property; the negative case becomes the principle of increase of entropy. The zero case is an ideal limit that real machines approach but never reach.

Worked example

Question. A cycle receives 500 kJ of heat at 600 K, rejects 300 kJ at 300 K, and delivers 200 kJ of work. Evaluate ∮δQ/T. Is the cycle reversible, irreversible or impossible? Find its efficiency and compare it with the Carnot efficiency between the same two temperatures.

  1. Use heat entering positive: Q₁ = +500 kJ at T₁ = 600 K; Q₂ = −300 kJ at T₂ = 300 K.
  2. First term: Q₁/T₁ = 500/600 = 0.8333 kJ/K. Second term: Q₂/T₂ = −300/300 = −1.0000 kJ/K.
  3. Cyclic integral: ∮δQ/T = 0.8333 − 1.0000 = −0.1667 kJ/K ≈ −0.167 kJ/K.
  4. The result is less than zero, so the cycle is irreversible. It is not impossible, because an impossible cycle would give a positive value.
  5. Check with first law: W = Q₁ + Q₂ = 500 − 300 = 200 kJ, matching the stated work. Efficiency η = W/Q₁ = 200/500 = 0.40 = 40%.
  6. Carnot efficiency for 600 K and 300 K: ηC = 1 − T₂/T₁ = 1 − 300/600 = 0.50 = 50%. The actual 40% is below 50%, as expected for an irreversible cycle.

Answer. ∮δQ/T = −0.167 kJ/K; the cycle is irreversible; η = 40%, below the Carnot value of 50%.

  • Use kelvin. A term like 300/27 is meaningless; convert 27°C to 300 K first.
  • T is the boundary or reservoir temperature where the heat crosses, not the gas temperature inside during an irreversible process. For a reservoir, use the reservoir temperature.
  • Equality means reversible, not 'no heat transfer'. A reversible cycle can exchange plenty of heat and still have zero integral.
  • A positive integral is impossible, not 'very irreversible'. If your calculation gives >0, check signs, units and temperatures before calling the cycle real.

3.7 Entropy: concept, T-s diagram and entropy change

Entropy is a property that measures how heat and temperature set the direction of a process. For a reversible path it is defined by dS = δQrev/T. Because the Clausius inequality gives zero around any reversible cycle, the integral between two states is the same for every reversible route. That is why entropy belongs to the state, not the path. Engineers use it on the T-s diagram to see heat as area and to compute ideal-gas entropy changes in compressors, turbines and heat exchangers.

You need: the Clausius inequality, and ideal-gas processes and specific heats from the first-law topics.

Entropy as a property

The Clausius inequality says ∮δQ/T ≤ 0 for any cycle; the value is zero for a reversible cycle. Take two reversible paths between state 1 and state 2. Go from 1 to 2 along path A, then return from 2 to 1 along path B reversed. This closed cycle is reversible, so its cyclic integral is zero. Hence the integral of δQ/T from 1 to 2 along A equals the integral along B. The value depends only on the end states. That is the definition of a property.

dS = δQrev/T

δQrev is the heat transfer in a reversible process, in kJ; T is absolute temperature, in K; dS is the entropy change, in kJ/K.

S is extensive: 2 kg has twice the entropy of 1 kg at the same state. Specific entropy s = S/m has units kJ/kg·K. For an irreversible process, ΔS is still fixed by the end states; calculate it along any convenient reversible path, not along the real path. The real path gives only ∫δQ/T, which is less than ΔS; the gap is the entropy generated inside the system (see entropy generation).

The T-s diagram

On a temperature-entropy diagram, plot T in K against s in kJ/kg·K. For a reversible process, δQrev = T dS, so Qrev = ∫T dS. The signed area under the path between two entropies is the heat transfer. If T is in K and S in kJ/K, the area is in kJ.

An isothermal reversible process is a horizontal line. A reversible adiabatic (isentropic) process is a vertical line. A Carnot cycle between two temperatures is therefore a rectangle: two horizontal isotherms and two vertical isentropics. The area inside the rectangle is the net work of the cycle; it equals Qin − Qout.

Slopes. At constant pressure, T ds = dh = cp dT, so dT/ds = T/cp. At constant volume, T ds = du = cv dT, so dT/ds = T/cv. Since cv < cp, T/cv > T/cp: through any point the constant-volume line is steeper than the constant-pressure line. The figure draws both for the worked example’s air, from 300 K.

Entropy change of an ideal gas

For an ideal gas with constant specific heats, integrate ds = cv dT/T + R dv/v and ds = cp dT/T − R dp/p. For mass m:

ΔS = m[cᵥ ln(T₂/T₁) + R ln(v₂/v₁)]

m is the mass in kg; cv is the specific heat at constant volume in kJ/kg·K; T1, T2 are absolute temperatures in K; R is the gas constant in kJ/kg·K; v1, v2 are specific volumes in m³/kg; ΔS is in kJ/K.

ΔS = m[cₚ ln(T₂/T₁) − R ln(p₂/p₁)]

cp is the specific heat at constant pressure in kJ/kg·K; p1, p2 are pressures in the same unit, usually kPa; the other symbols are as above.

3.8 Principle of increase of entropy

Energy is conserved; entropy is not. In every real process the total entropy of the system plus its surroundings rises, and the amount it rises by is the entropy generated, Sgen. This one inequality decides which processes are possible, separates the best possible machine from the actual one, and shows where a plant is destroying quality of energy. Engineers use it to judge heat exchanger designs, steam plant performance and any claim of a new, super-efficient engine.

You need: entropy as a property for dS = δQrev/T, and reversible and irreversible processes for what makes a process irreversible.

The principle written as an equation

For any process between state 1 and state 2, reversible or not, the entropy account reads as follows.

ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings = Sgen ≥ 0
ΔSsystem is the entropy change of the system in kJ/K, equal to S₂ − S₁; ΔSsurroundings is the entropy change of everything outside the system, in kJ/K; Sgen is the entropy generated during the process, in kJ/K; the sum is never negative.

The equality holds only for a fully reversible process. Every real process gives a strict greater-than. Sgen is not a property of the system; it is a quantity produced along the path, and it depends on how the process is carried out.

Seal the system and its surroundings into one box and you have an isolated system. For it the statement becomes: the entropy of an isolated system increases in every real process and stays constant only in a reversible one. It never falls. That is the second law stated in entropy words.

Compare this with the first law. The first law says energy is conserved, so the energy account always balances to zero. The second law says the entropy account does not balance to zero — it always leaves a positive remainder. Nature keeps books that never close.

Sgen measures how irreversible the process is

Sgen is a quantitative measure of irreversibility. Large Sgen means a badly irreversible process; zero means a reversible one. Four sources account for almost every case you will meet.

Heat through a finite temperature difference is the commonest. When Q kilojoules cross from a body at Thot to a body at Tcold, both in kelvin:

Sgen = Q (1/Tcold − 1/Thot)
Q is the heat in kJ passing from the hot body to the cold body; Tcold and Thot are in K; Sgen is in kJ/K. The bracket is positive because Tcold < Thot, so a wider gap or a larger Q creates more entropy.

A reversible adiabatic process has Q = 0 and no friction or mixing, so Sgen = 0 and ΔS = 0. Such a process is called isentropic — same entropy. On a T-s diagram the ideal gas turbine or compressor is drawn as a vertical line.

The criterion for a spontaneous change

The same inequality answers a different question: will this change happen on its own? For a closed system held at constant internal energy U and constant volume V, with no help from outside, the answer is yes only if dS ≥ 0. Entropy climbs to a maximum, and that maximum is the equilibrium state.

This form is asked less often. At constant temperature and volume the criterion is written with the Helmholtz function, and at constant temperature and pressure with the Gibbs function; both belong to the topic of Gibbs and Helmholtz functions.

A positive ΔS says the process can happen, not that it happens quickly. The conversion of diamond to graphite has a positive ΔS at room conditions and you will not see it happen in your lifetime. Thermodynamics gives direction, never rate.

Every kJ/K of entropy generated makes T₀ × Sgen of work unavailable, where T₀ is the temperature of the surroundings in K. This lost work, Wlost = T₀Sgen, is the Gouy-Stodola relation; the topic of available energy turns it into exergy, the part of energy still useful. For flowing streams the same account is written per second in entropy generation in control volumes.

Worked example

Question. In an insulated vessel, 2 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 3 kg of water at 20 °C. Take cp = 4.187 kJ/kg·K and treat it as constant. Find the final mixture temperature and the total entropy change, and state whether the mixing is reversible. (8 marks)

  1. Convert to kelvin: T₁ = 80 + 273 = 353 K, T₂ = 20 + 273 = 293 K.
  2. The vessel is insulated and no work crosses the boundary, so the heat given up by the hot water equals the heat taken by the cold water: m₁cp(T₁ − Tf) = m₂cp(Tf − T₂). The cp cancels.
  3. Solve for Tf: Tf = (m₁T₁ + m₂T₂)/(m₁ + m₂) = (2 × 353 + 3 × 293)/5 = (706 + 879)/5 = 1585/5 = 317 K = 44 °C.
  4. Entropy change of the hot water: ΔS₁ = m₁cp ln(Tf/T₁) = 2 × 4.187 × ln(317/353) = 8.374 × (−0.10757) = −0.9008 kJ/K.
  5. Entropy change of the cold water: ΔS₂ = m₂cp ln(Tf/T₂) = 3 × 4.187 × ln(317/293) = 12.561 × (0.07873) = +0.9889 kJ/K.
  6. The vessel is insulated, so the surroundings change by zero. Total: ΔStotal = −0.90076 + 0.98892 = +0.0882 kJ/K = Sgen.
  7. The total is positive, so the mixing is irreversible and allowed by the second law. Warm water giving heat to cold water is exactly the finite-temperature-gap source of entropy.

3.9 Entropy generation and second-law analysis of control volumes

The principle of increase of entropy tells you whether a process can happen. An engineer needs more: how much entropy a compressor, a turbine, a throttle valve or a heat exchanger generates, so that the losses can be ranked and priced. This chapter writes entropy as a balance, like mass and energy: entropy enters and leaves with heat and with flowing mass, and irreversibility generates more inside. It ends with the molecular picture behind entropy, S = k ln W, and shows that it gives the same number as the heat-over-temperature definition.

You need: the principle of increase of entropy, the entropy change of an ideal gas, and the steady flow energy equation from the first-law topics.

The entropy balance: transfer plus generation

Entropy crosses a boundary in only two ways: with heat and with flowing mass. Work carries no entropy. Inside the boundary, friction, mixing, unrestrained expansion and heat flow across a finite temperature difference create entropy, and nothing can destroy it. For a closed system going from state 1 to state 2:

S₂ − S₁ = Σ Qⱼ/Tⱼ + Sgen

S₂ − S₁ is the change in the system’s entropy, in kJ/K; Qⱼ is a heat transfer in kJ (positive into the system) that crosses the boundary where the boundary temperature is Tⱼ in K; Qⱼ/Tⱼ is the entropy that heat carries; Sgen ≥ 0 is the entropy generated inside, zero only for an internally reversible process.

For an isolated system no heat and no mass cross, so S₂ − S₁ = Sgen ≥ 0: the principle of increase of entropy is the special case of this balance.

Control volumes: the rate form and steady flow

A control volume also has streams of mass, and each kilogram brings its specific entropy s with it. Written per second:

dScv/dt = Σ Q̇ⱼ/Tⱼ + Σ ṁᵢsᵢ − Σ ṁₑsₑ + Ṡgen
dScv/dt is the rate of change of entropy inside, in kW/K; Q̇ⱼ is a heat rate in kW crossing where the boundary is at Tⱼ; ṁᵢ and ṁₑ are the mass flow rates in kg/s at each inlet and exit; sᵢ and sₑ are the specific entropies there in kJ/(kg·K); Ṡgen ≥ 0 is the rate of entropy generation in kW/K.

In steady flow nothing inside changes, so dScv/dt = 0. With one inlet 1, one exit 2 and a single mass flow ṁ:

Ṡgen = ṁ(s₂ − s₁) − Σ Q̇ⱼ/Tⱼ ≥ 0

  • Adiabatic device (an insulated turbine, compressor, nozzle or throttle): Q̇ = 0, so s₂ ≥ s₁. The exit entropy equals the inlet entropy only for the reversible, isentropic device.
  • Device that loses heat to surroundings at T₀: the heat term is −Q̇out/T₀, so the stream may even leave with less entropy than it brought, provided the heat carries enough away.
  • Throttle valve on an ideal gas: h₂ = h₁, so T₂ = T₁ and Ṡgen = ṁR ln(p₁/p₂). All of it is generation; pressure is lost and nothing is gained.

Each kW/K generated costs T₀Ṡgen kW of work, where T₀ is the temperature of the surroundings in K (the Gouy-Stodola relation). Adding these lost-work figures over a plant shows which component to fix first: the second-law analysis that the topic of available energy and exergy carries further.

Entropy on the molecular scale

A macrostate is what we measure: p, V, T. A microstate is one exact arrangement of every molecule’s position and energy that gives that macrostate. Boltzmann linked the two:

S = k ln W

k = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K is Boltzmann’s constant, the gas constant per molecule (R̄ divided by Avogadro’s number); W is the number of microstates that give the same macrostate. More ways of arranging the molecules means more entropy.

Let a gas double its volume freely, as in the free-expansion example of reversible and irreversible processes. Each of the N molecules now has twice the room, so W grows by a factor of 2N and ΔS = k ln 2N = Nk ln 2. For 0.5 kg of air, N = (0.5/28.97) kmol × 6.022 × 1026 per kmol = 1.04 × 1025 molecules, and Nk = 0.1435 kJ/K, which is exactly mR = 0.5 × 0.287. So ΔS = 0.1435 × 0.6931 = 0.0995 kJ/K, the same as mR ln 2 from the gas formula.

Heat opens more energy arrangements to the molecules, and the same heat added at a low temperature, where few were open, raises W by a larger factor: that is why dS = δQrev/T has T at the bottom. Work moves the molecules together and adds no arrangements, so it carries no entropy.

Worked example

Question. Air flows steadily through a compressor at 0.5 kg/s. It enters at 100 kPa and 300 K and leaves at 400 kPa and 480 K. 3.10 Third law of thermodynamics

The third law fixes the zero of entropy. At absolute zero, a perfect crystal at equilibrium has zero entropy, so entropy can be listed as an absolute value and not only as a change. It also explains why 0 K is a limit, not a target you can hit. Cryogenics, low-temperature physics and the absolute entropies in chemical data tables all rest on it. Steam tables, met later with pure substances, instead take an arbitrary zero at the triple point of water, which is enough because engineering problems need only changes of entropy.

You need: entropy as a property and the molecular view of entropy, S = k ln W.

Nernst theorem and the third law

Cool a substance and its entropy falls. Early measurements could give only ΔS between two temperatures. Nernst asked what happens as T → 0 K. His heat theorem says that for a condensed system at equilibrium, the entropy change of any isothermal process tends to zero as T tends to zero. In symbols, ΔS → 0 as T → 0 K.

From this, Planck proposed a fixed zero: for a perfect crystal at 0 K, S = 0. This is the third law. A perfect crystal has every atom on its proper lattice site, with no mixing and no frozen disorder. There is only one way to arrange it, so the statistical entropy S = kB ln W gives S = kB ln 1 = 0. Here kB is Boltzmann's constant (1.381 × 10−23 J/K) and W is the number of equally likely arrangements.

The law does not say that every real material has zero entropy at 0 K. It says a perfect crystal at equilibrium does. A glass or a crystal with trapped defects does not. That leftover is residual entropy.

Absolute entropy from heat capacity

With a zero at 0 K, entropy at any T is an absolute value. Heat the sample reversibly from 0 K; at each step dS = δQrev/T = cp dT/T. Integrate:

S(T) = ∫₀ᵀ (cₚ/T) dT

S(T) is absolute entropy at temperature T in J/(mol·K); cp is heat capacity at constant pressure in J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K. This holds when no phase change happens on the way up; if the solid melts, boils or changes crystal form at Ttr, add ΔHtr/Ttr for each change, where ΔHtr is the heat of that change in J/mol.

Near 0 K the heat capacity is too small to measure over the last part. Debye's model gives cp = aT3, where a is a constant for the material with units J/(mol·K4). Put this into the integral from 0 to a low measured temperature T1:

∫ (aT³/T) dT from 0 to T₁ = aT₁³/3

a is the Debye constant in J/(mol·K4); T1 is the lowest measured temperature in K; the result is in J/(mol·K).

Above T1 you add the measured ∫(cp/T)dT pieces. If cp were constant, S = cp ln T + constant. As T → 0, ln T → −∞, so S would run to minus infinity. That cannot match S = 0. Real cp falls to zero as T → 0 (as aT³ in an insulator, roughly in proportion to T in a metal), so cp/T stays finite and the integral converges. The third law therefore forces cp → 0 as T → 0 K. Most exam questions ask for the statement and the unattainability argument; numerical absolute entropies appear only occasionally.

Absolute zero cannot be reached

Cooling by adiabatic demagnetisation uses a magnetic salt. Magnetise it isothermally, releasing heat. Then isolate it and remove the field adiabatically. The spins disarrange and the sample cools. Repeat with a new starting temperature. Each step lowers T, but the entropy curves for different field strengths approach each other near 0 K. The temperature drop per step shrinks.

Unattainability statement: no finite sequence of processes can bring a system to exactly 0 K. You can approach it, never arrive. This is equivalent to the third law for equilibrium systems. The unattainability form is often easier to apply than the Nernst form. It forbids a machine that reaches 0 K in finite steps. It does not forbid getting very close: laboratories reach microkelvin or nanokelvin. The last fraction of a kelvin is paid for with many steps.

The reason is the Nernst theorem itself: as T → 0, the entropy change of the isothermal magnetising step shrinks towards zero, so the entropy gap that each adiabatic demagnetisation can use, and with it the temperature drop per step, shrinks too. Together with cp → 0, that is the thermodynamic signature of the third law.

Worked example

Question. A non-metallic solid follows the Debye law below 10 K, with cp = aT3, where a = 0.002 J/(mol·K4). Take S = 0 at 0 K. Find the absolute entropy at 10 K.

  1. Write the heat capacity: cp = 0.002 T3 J/(mol·K), with T in K.
  2. For reversible heating at constant pressure: dS = cp dT/T = (aT3/T) dT = aT2 dT.
  3. Integrate from 0 K to 10 K: S = ∫010 aT2 dT = a[T3/3]010.
  4. Put in a = 0.002 J/(mol·K4) and T = 10 K: S = 0.002 × 103/3 J/(mol·K).
  5. S = 0.002 × 1000/3 = 2/3 = 0.667 J/(mol·K).

Answer. The absolute entropy at 10 K is 0.667 J/(mol·K).

Exam traps

  • S = 0 at 0 K is for a perfect crystal at equilibrium. Glasses, solid solutions and crystals with frozen-in defects keep residual entropy, so do not apply zero blindly.
  • Absolute zero is unattainable, not just difficult. A finite number of adiabatic demagnetisation steps never reaches exactly 0 K; microkelvin is not zero.
  • Do not integrate S = cp ln T down to 0 K. Use cp = aT3 and the integral aT3/3 over the low-temperature range.
  • cp and cv tend to zero as T → 0 K. Treating them as constant near absolute zero gives a divergent, negative entropy.
  • Entropy change between two states needs no third law. Absolute entropy, as in tables, does need the third-law zero.

If cp is constant, S = cp ln T + constant. As T → 0, ln T → −∞, so S → −∞, which contradicts S = 0. Real cp → 0 as T → 0, so cp/T stays finite and the integral converges.

Understand Why can a finite number of adiabatic demagnetisation steps not reach 0 K? 3 marks

Each step removes the field adiabatically, but the entropy curves for different fields merge near 0 K. The temperature drop per step shrinks, so finite steps approach 0 K but never reach it.

Apply A solid has cp = aT3 with a = 0.003 J/(mol·K4) below 5 K. Taking S = 0 at 0 K, find S at 5 K. 4 marks

S = ∫05 (aT3/T) dT = ∫05 aT2 dT = aT3/3 = 0.003 × 125/3 = 0.125 J/(mol·K).

Analyze A glass is cooled to 0 K. Does the third law force its entropy to zero? Explain. 4 marks

No. A glass is not a perfect crystal at equilibrium. Its frozen disorder gives residual entropy at 0 K, so S(0) > 0. The third law sets zero only for a perfect crystal at equilibrium.

Analyze Show why the third law implies cp → 0 as T → 0 K. 5 marks

S(0) is finite. For heating from 0 K, S(T) − S(0) = ∫0T (cp/T) dT must converge. If cp tended to a nonzero constant, the integral would diverge as ln T. Therefore cp must tend to zero; the Debye form cp = aT3 is one model that does this.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Absolute entropy is tabulated for refrigerants and cryogenic fluids, and the third law underpins low-temperature thermometry and adiabatic demagnetisation refrigerators. In materials science, residual entropy reveals frozen disorder in glasses and spin ice. In quantum computing, cooling to millikelvin is routine but exact 0 K remains a limit. Search for Nernst heat theorem, Debye T cubed law and residual entropy of glasses to go further.

Takeaways

  1. The first law balances energy; the second law decides which energy transfers are possible and in which direction they occur.
  2. A cyclic heat engine must reject some heat to a sink, so its thermal efficiency is always less than 100%.

  4. The Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements are equivalent, and each forbids a different version of a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind.

Understand Define a heat engine, a refrigerator and a heat pump. Draw their energy-flow diagrams and show that COPHP = COPR + 1 when both work between the same two reservoirs. 10 marks

Points to cover. Define each device and label Q₁, Q₂ and W; Draw source, sink and work arrows for engine, refrigerator and heat pump; Use Q₁ = Q₂ + W from the first law for a cycle; Write COPR = Q₂/W and COPHP = Q₁/W; Substitute to get COPHP = COPR + 1, with a numerical check.

Model answer.

  1. Common parts. A source (hot reservoir) at T₁, a sink (cold reservoir) at T₂ < T₁, and a device that works in a cycle between them. A thermal reservoir is a body so large that taking or adding heat does not change its temperature. Over one cycle the device returns to its starting state, so its internal energy change is zero and the first law reduces to a balance of heat and work: Q₁ = Q₂ + W, with all three taken as positive magnitudes.
  2. Heat engine. A cyclic device that takes heat Q₁ from the source, converts part of it into net work W and rejects the rest, Q₂, to the sink. Its figure of merit is the thermal efficiency η = W/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁, always below 1 because Q₂ > 0.
  3. Refrigerator. The same kind of device run backwards: work W is supplied, heat Q₂ is removed from the cold space, and Q₁ = Q₂ + W is rejected to the warmer surroundings. The wanted effect is the cooling Q₂, so COPR = Q₂/W.
  4. Heat pump. The same reversed cycle, but the wanted effect is the heat Q₁ delivered to the warm space (a room in winter, a water tank). So COPHP = Q₁/W.
  5. Energy-flow diagrams. The engine’s arrows run down from source to sink with work out; the refrigerator and heat pump have the same reversed arrows, and differ only in which heat is wanted.
  6. Derivation. For the reversed device the first law for a cycle gives Q₁ = Q₂ + W. Divide every term by W:
    Q₁/W = Q₂/W + W/W
COPHP = COPR + 1
  7. Meaning. For the same machine, the same two reservoirs and the same work input, the heat pump always delivers exactly one unit of heat per unit of work more than the refrigerator removes, because the work itself also ends up as heat on the warm side.
  8. Numerical check. A machine takes W = 4 kW and removes Q₂ = 10 kW from a cold space.
    quantityworkingvalue
    COPRQ₂/W = 10/42.5
    Q₁Q₂ + W = 10 + 414 kW
    COPHPQ₁/W = 14/43.5

  9. Why the two COPs behave differently. COPR can be below 1 when the temperature lift is large (a freezer in a hot room), because the work can exceed the heat removed. COPHP can never be below 1: even if the machine moved no heat at all, the work itself would arrive as heat, giving COPHP = 1.
  10. Closing points. η is always below 1, but a COP is usually above 1: it compares heat moved with work paid, so it is not an efficiency and creates no energy. The relation holds for real and reversible machines alike, since it uses only the first law.
Remember State the Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements of the second law. Explain why each statement rules out a different impossible machine, and show that they are equivalent. 10 marks

Points to cover. State the Kelvin-Planck statement; Explain that it forbids a PMM2; State the Clausius statement; Explain that it forbids heat flow from cold to hot with no work input; Prove the equivalence: a violation of either gives a violation of the other.

Model answer.

  1. Kelvin-Planck statement. It is impossible for any device that operates in a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and produce an equal amount of net work. Every heat engine must reject some heat to a colder body, so η = W/Q₁ < 1.
  2. The machine it forbids. A perpetual motion machine of the second kind (PMM2): a cyclic engine with Q₂ = 0 and η = 100%, for example a ship running on the heat of the sea alone. A PMM2 would obey the first law (1000 kJ in, 1000 kJ of work out), so the first law cannot rule it out; this is the main limitation of the first law.
  3. Clausius statement. It is impossible to construct a device that operates in a cycle and whose only effect is to transfer heat from a body at a lower temperature to a body at a higher temperature.
  4. The machine it forbids. A work-free refrigerator: a sealed box that cools its inside and warms the room with no power supply. “Only effect” matters: a kitchen fridge moves heat from 4 °C to 35 °C, but it uses work from the socket, so it is allowed.
  5. Different focus. Kelvin-Planck speaks about engines (heat into work); Clausius speaks about refrigerators (heat uphill). Both are postulates drawn from experience, and neither follows from the first law.
  6. If Kelvin-Planck fails, Clausius fails. Let a PMM2 take Q from the hot body at T₁ and give W = Q. Use W to drive a reversible refrigerator that takes Q₂ from the cold body and gives Q₂ + W to the hot body. Net: the hot body gains (Q₂ + Q) − Q = Q₂, the cold body loses Q₂, and no outside work is used. Heat has gone uphill with no other effect.
  7. If Clausius fails, Kelvin-Planck fails. Let a work-free device lift Q₂ from T₂ to T₁. Run an engine between the same bodies: it takes Q₁, gives W = Q₁ − Q₂ and rejects Q₂, which the device carries straight back. Net: the cold body is unchanged, the hot body loses Q₁ − Q₂, and the same amount appears as work. That is a PMM2.
  8. Numerical illustration (the chapter’s example, a reversible refrigerator of COP 1.5 between 500 K and 300 K driven by a 200 kJ PMM2):
    bodyPMM2refrigeratornet
    cold body, 300 K−200 kJ−300 kJ−500 kJ
    hot body, 500 K0+500 kJ+500 kJ
    work+200 kJ−200 kJ0
  9. Conclusion. Each statement implies the other, so they are two forms of one law: the second law of thermodynamics.
Understand Define reversible and irreversible processes. List four causes of irreversibility, give one engineering example of each, and distinguish internal from external reversibility. 12 marks

Points to cover. Define a reversible process: no trace left in system or surroundings; State the two conditions: quasi-static and no dissipation; List friction, unrestrained expansion, heat transfer across a finite temperature difference and mixing, each with an example; Distinguish internal and external reversibility; Explain that all real processes are irreversible and why the reversible process is still useful.

Model answer.

  1. Reversible process. A process after which the system and the surroundings can both be returned exactly to their initial states, leaving no change anywhere: no warmed oil, no worn bearing, no extra heat in the atmosphere.
  2. Irreversible process. A process after which this full restoration is impossible: undoing it leaves a permanent change somewhere, usually work converted to internal energy or heat spread to a colder body.
  3. Conditions for reversibility. (i) Quasi-static: the driving and resisting forces differ only infinitesimally (gas pressure p + dp against the piston, heat across T + dT), so the system passes through equilibrium states. (ii) No dissipative effects: no friction, viscosity, electrical resistance or plastic deformation. Both must hold; slowness alone is not enough.
  4. Causes of irreversibility with engineering examples.
    causewhy it is irreversibleengineering example
    Frictionwork becomes internal energy and never returns to the shaft by itselfpiston rings rubbing in an engine cylinder; bearings of a pump
    Unrestrained (free) expansiongas does no work as it spreads and will not gather back without worka pressure vessel bursting; a throttle valve in a refrigerator

    Mixing of different fluids or statesseparating them again needs workfeedwater heater mixing steam with water; hot and cold water in a shower line
  5. Further causes (also accepted): current through a resistance, plastic deformation, combustion and other fast chemical reactions, viscous flow through pipes.
  6. Internal reversibility. No irreversibility inside the system boundary: no internal friction, no pressure or temperature gradients in the gas, so it is uniform at every instant.
  7. External reversibility. No irreversibility at the boundary or in the surroundings: heat is exchanged only with a body at the same temperature, and work goes to a load with no outside friction. Understand Explain the microscopic interpretation of entropy. Use Boltzmann’s relation to find the entropy change when 1 kg of nitrogen (R = 0.2968 kJ/(kg·K), molar mass 28.013 kg/kmol) doubles its volume at constant temperature, and check the answer with the macroscopic formula. 12 marks

Points to cover. Distinguish macrostate and microstate; State S = k ln W with symbols and units; Explain why more arrangements means more entropy, and why heat (not work) carries entropy; Count microstates for a doubling of volume: W rises by 2ᴺ; Compute N, Nk and ΔS, and check against mR ln(V₂/V₁).

Model answer.

  1. Two levels of description. A macrostate is the state we measure: p, V, T, the mass. A microstate is one exact arrangement of every molecule’s position and energy. A huge number of microstates W give the same macrostate, because swapping or shifting molecules does not change the measured p, V and T.
  2. Boltzmann’s relation. S = k ln W, where k = 1.381 × 10−23 J/K is Boltzmann’s constant (the universal gas constant per molecule, R̄/NA) and W is the number of microstates. Entropy is a measure of the number of ways the molecules can be arranged consistent with what we measure.
  3. Why it matches thermodynamics. A system left alone moves to the macrostate with the most microstates, simply because that macrostate is overwhelmingly the most probable; this is the increase of entropy. Heat added to a body opens more energy arrangements, and the same heat added at a lower temperature opens relatively more, which is why dS = δQrev/T. Work moves molecules together in an ordered way and opens no new arrangements, so work carries no entropy.
  4. Counting for a doubling of volume. At constant temperature the energy arrangements do not change; only the room for each molecule doubles. Each molecule has twice as many places to be, so with N molecules W₂ = 2NW₁.
  5. Entropy change from Boltzmann.
    ΔS = k ln W₂ − k ln W₁ = k ln(W₂/W₁) = k ln 2N = Nk ln 2

Analyze Prove Carnot’s theorem: no engine between two reservoirs is more efficient than a reversible engine, and all reversible engines between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency. 13 marks

Points to cover. Assume an engine X more efficient than a reversible engine R; Give both the same Q₁ and run R backwards on part of X’s work; The hot reservoir is unchanged; the surplus work comes from the cold reservoir alone; This is a PMM2, violating Kelvin-Planck; Repeat with two reversible engines both ways to prove equality; state the consequences.

Model answer.

  1. Statement. (1) No heat engine working between two given reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine working between the same reservoirs. (2) All reversible engines working between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever their working substance.
  2. Set-up for part 1. Let R be a reversible engine and X any engine, both between a hot reservoir at T₁ and a cold reservoir at T₂. Suppose, against the theorem, ηX > ηR. Let both take the same heat Q₁ from the hot reservoir. Then WX = ηXQ₁ > WR = ηRQ₁, and X rejects less heat: Q₁ − WX < Q₁ − WR.
  3. Reverse R. Because R is reversible, it can run as a refrigerator with every quantity the same size and reversed: driven by WR, it takes Q₁ − WR from the cold reservoir and delivers Q₁ to the hot reservoir. Drive it with part of X’s work.
  4. Net effect of X plus reversed R (with numbers for ηX = 0.5, ηR = 0.4, Q₁ = 100 kJ):
    generalnumbers
    hot reservoir−Q₁ + Q₁ = 0−100 + 100 = 0
    cold reservoir+(Q₁ − WX) − (Q₁ − WR) = −(WX − WR)+50 − 60 = −10 kJ
    net work outWX − WR > 050 − 40 = 10 kJ

  6. Part 2. Let A and B be two reversible engines between the same reservoirs. Applying part 1 with X = A and R = B gives ηA ≤ ηB. Both are reversible, so the roles can be swapped: X = B and R = A gives ηB ≤ ηA. Hence ηA = ηB.

(iii) The working fluid, size and pressures of a reversible engine do not matter, so a Carnot cycle on an ideal gas gives the result for every substance.

Model answer.

  2. Third law (Planck’s form). The entropy of a perfect crystal in equilibrium is zero at absolute zero. On the molecular scale a perfect crystal at 0 K has a single arrangement, W = 1, so S = k ln 1 = 0.
  3. Absolute entropy. Chemical data tables list such absolute (standard) entropies. (Steam tables, by contrast, use an arbitrary zero at the triple point, which is enough for changes.)

The reversible cycle made of two isothermal and two adiabatic processes between two reservoirs.
Carnot theorem
No engine between two fixed reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine.
Clausius inequality
For any cycle, ∮δQ/T ≤ 0, with equality only for a reversible cycle.
Clausius statement
Heat cannot move from a cold body to a hot body in a cycle without work input.
Coefficient of performance
The ratio of useful heat transfer to work input for a refrigerator or heat pump.
Entropy
A thermodynamic property whose change for a reversible path is dS = δQrev/T.
Entropy balance
Change of entropy = entropy carried in by heat and mass − entropy carried out + entropy generated.
Entropy generation
The extra entropy created by irreversibilities within a process.
Heat engine
A cyclic device that converts part of the heat taken from a source into work.
Heat pump
A reversed heat engine used to deliver heat to a warm space.
Internally reversible process
A process with no irreversibility inside the system boundary; any irreversibility lies in the surroundings.
Irreversible process
A real process that leaves a permanent change and cannot be fully reversed.
Kelvin-Planck statement
No cyclic device can produce work by exchanging heat with only one reservoir.
Lost work
The work made unavailable by irreversibility, T₀ times the entropy generated.
Microstate
One exact arrangement of the molecules’ positions and energies; many microstates make one measured macrostate.
PMM2
A perpetual-motion machine of the second kind, which would convert all heat from one reservoir into work in a cycle.
Principle of increase of entropy
The total entropy of an isolated system never decreases.
Refrigerator
A device that uses work to remove heat from a cold region and reject it to a warmer region.
Reversible process
An ideal process that can be reversed without leaving any net change in system or surroundings.
Sink
A thermal reservoir at low temperature that receives rejected heat.
Source
A thermal reservoir at high temperature that supplies heat to a device.
T-s diagram
A plot of temperature against entropy used to show heat transfer and cycle performance.
Thermal efficiency
The ratio of work output to heat input of a heat engine, η = W/Q₁.
Thermal reservoir
A body large enough that its temperature remains constant when heat is added or removed.
Thermodynamic temperature scale
A temperature scale based on reversible heat transfers, T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂.
Third law of thermodynamics
The entropy of a perfect crystal is zero at absolute zero.
Ton of cooling
A refrigeration capacity equal to 3.517 kW of heat removal.
Unattainability of absolute zero
Absolute zero cannot be reached in a finite number of cooling steps.
