3.1 Heat engines, refrigerators and heat pumps

A heat engine sits between a hot body and a cold body. It takes heat Q₁ from the hot body, turns part of it into work W, and dumps the rest Q₂ into the cold body. Run the same device backwards and it becomes a refrigerator or a heat pump: now you pay work to carry heat from cold to hot. This is the machine behind every power plant, every air conditioner on a Chennai wall and every cold storage. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and the energy accounting for all of them. The second-law limit on how good they can be comes later in the unit.

You need Unit 1 (heat, work and the sign convention) and Unit 2 (the first law applied to a cycle).

Source, sink and a cyclic device

A thermal reservoir is a body so large that adding or removing heat does not change its temperature. A lake, the atmosphere and a large furnace are treated as reservoirs in problems. The hot reservoir at T₁ is the source. The cold reservoir at T₂ is the sink. Temperatures are in kelvin, and T₁ is greater than T₂.

Every device in this chapter has the same three parts: a source, a sink, and a device placed between them. The device works in cycles, so it returns to its own state at the end of each cycle. Its internal energy therefore returns to the same value, and the net change over one cycle is zero. The first law for a cycle then connects only the heat and the work crossing the boundary.

Q₁ = W + Q₂

Q₁ is the heat taken from the source, in kJ; W is the net work delivered by the device, in kJ; Q₂ is the heat given to the sink, in kJ. Heat into the device is positive and work done by the device is positive, as in Unit 1.

The same balance serves engines and reversed devices. Only the direction of the arrows changes, and with it which quantity you pay for and which one you want. That is why one chapter covers power plants, room air conditioners and hot-water heat pumps together.

Heat engine and thermal efficiency

A heat engine takes heat Q₁ from the source, converts part of it into work W, and rejects the rest Q₂ to the sink. The working fluid may be steam, air or burnt gas. The accounting is the same in every case.

η = W / Q₁ = (Q₁ − Q₂) / Q₁ = 1 − Q₂ / Q₁

η (Greek eta) is the thermal efficiency, a pure fraction; multiply by 100 for per cent. W is the net work output in kJ, Q₁ the heat supplied from the source in kJ, and Q₂ the heat rejected to the sink in kJ.

Efficiency is what you get divided by what you paid for. In an engine the payment is the heat Q₁ drawn from the source, so η is always less than 1 whenever Q₂ is greater than zero. A claim of 100% efficiency means Q₂ = 0, an engine that dumps no heat at all. That machine is forbidden by the second law, and 3.2 states the ban precisely.

Watch the denominator. It is Q₁, not Q₂. Dividing work by the rejected heat measures nothing useful.

Running it backwards: refrigerator and heat pump

Reverse every arrow. Work W now goes in, heat Q₂ is pulled out of the cold space, and Q₁ = Q₂ + W is pushed into the warm room. The hardware is the same. What changes is which heat you want: the cold-side heat for a refrigerator, the hot-side heat for a heat pump.

COP_R = Q₂ / W and COP_HP = Q₁ / W

COP is the coefficient of performance, a pure number. Q₂ is the heat removed from the cold space in kJ, Q₁ the heat delivered to the warm space in kJ, and W the work input in kJ. Some syllabi call the COP the performance factor.

Divide Q₁ = Q₂ + W by W and you get COP_HP = COP_R + 1. A device with COP_R = 1.667 becomes a heat pump of COP 2.667 on the same work.

A COP above 1 surprises students. It is not free energy. The COP compares heat moved with work paid, not heat with heat. Most of the heat moved was already sitting in the cold space and was only carried uphill. Energy in still equals energy out.

Room air conditioners are sold in tons of cooling. One ton of refrigeration is 3.517 kW of heat removed, so a 1.5 ton split unit removes 1.5 × 3.517 ≈ 5.3 kW of heat. If its compressor draws 1.5 kW of electricity, its COP is 5.3 / 1.5 ≈ 3.5.

Worked example

Question. A cyclic engine receives 800 kJ of heat from a reservoir at 600 K and rejects 500 kJ to a reservoir at 300 K. Find the work output and the thermal efficiency. The same device is then run backwards as a refrigerator, driven by 300 kJ of work. Find its COP as a refrigerator and its COP as a heat pump.

  1. Work output comes from the cycle energy balance: W = Q₁ − Q₂ = 800 kJ − 500 kJ = 300 kJ.
  2. Thermal efficiency: η = W / Q₁ = 300 kJ / 800 kJ = 0.375, that is 37.5%.
  3. The temperatures 600 K and 300 K are not used here. They only set the best efficiency a reversible engine could reach between these reservoirs, which is a second-law question answered in 3.4.
  4. Run the device backwards as an energy-balance exercise: 300 kJ of work goes in, Q₂ = 500 kJ is taken from the cold space, and Q₁ = Q₂ + W = 800 kJ is delivered to the warm room.
  5. COP as a refrigerator: COP_R = Q₂ / W = 500 kJ / 300 kJ = 1.667. Take the cold space at 270 K and the room at 300 K: the reversible limit there is 270/(300 − 270) = 9, so 1.667 is allowed but poor, as expected from an irreversible machine.
  6. COP as a heat pump: COP_HP = Q₁ / W = 800 kJ / 300 kJ = 2.667. The check COP_R + 1 = 1.667 + 1 = 2.667 agrees.

Answer. Work output 300 kJ and thermal efficiency 37.5%; the same device, working between a 270 K cold space and a 300 K room, gives COP 1.667 as a refrigerator and COP 2.667 as a heat pump.

Exam traps

  • Efficiency with the wrong denominator. η = W/Q₁, the heat taken from the source. Writing W/Q₂ gives 0.6 in the worked example, which means nothing.
  • Forgetting COP_HP = COP_R + 1, and quoting the same COP for a fridge and for a heat pump built on the same cycle.
  • Reading a COP of 3 as an efficiency of 300% or as free energy. The COP counts heat moved, not heat created; Q₁ = Q₂ + W still holds.
  • Confusing a ton of refrigeration with electrical power. One ton is 3.517 kW of heat removed; a 1.5 ton unit removes about 5.3 kW of heat but draws far less than that from the socket.
  • Feeding the reservoir temperatures into 1 − T₂/T₁ here. That formula belongs to a reversible engine; it gives 0.5 for the 600 K and 300 K reservoirs while the actual engine in the example gives 0.375.

3.2 Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements

The first law balances energy but cannot fix the direction of flow. The second law does, in two equivalent forms. Kelvin-Planck speaks of engines; Clausius speaks of refrigerators. Each forbids one impossible machine. Engineers use them to reject impossible claims and to set plant efficiency limits.

You need 3.1 for engine and refrigerator pictures; Unit 2 for energy balance.

Kelvin-Planck: no engine on one reservoir

An engine takes heat Q₁ from a hot reservoir at T₁, rejects Q₂ to a cold reservoir at T₂, and gives work W = Q₁ − Q₂.

η = W / Q₁ = (Q₁ − Q₂) / Q₁

η is thermal efficiency; W is net work per cycle in kJ; Q₁ and Q₂ are heat taken and rejected per cycle in kJ.

Kelvin-Planck statement: it is impossible for any device working in a cycle to receive heat from a single reservoir and produce net work.

In a cycle means it returns to its starting state. Single reservoir means no other body. Then Q₂ = 0 and η = 100%. Such a machine is a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM2.

A PMM2 obeys the first law: 1000 kJ in, 1000 kJ out. It breaks direction. A ship could run on sea heat. Real engines reject heat: a coal plant to a river, a car through its radiator.

Clausius: heat does not climb by itself

A refrigerator runs backwards: takes Q₂ from a cold body at T₂, receives work W, delivers Q₁ = Q₂ + W to a hot body at T₁.

Clausius statement: it is impossible to construct a device working in a cycle whose only effect is to transfer heat from a lower temperature to a higher temperature.

Only effect carries the weight: no work, no fuel, no change in device or surroundings. Refrigeration is not forbidden. A kitchen fridge moves heat from 4 °C to 35 °C using wall-socket work. Hot tea cools and never heats itself; a sealed box that chills inside and warms the room with no plug violates Clausius.

The two statements are one law

Neither follows from the first law; each is a postulate. Accepting one forces the other. Both proofs use contradiction.

Assume a PMM2, break Clausius. A 100% engine takes Q from T₁ and gives W = Q, rejecting nothing. Drive a reversible refrigerator between T₁ and colder T₂: it removes Q₂ from T₂ and delivers Q₂ + W to T₁. Hot reservoir loses Q, gains Q₂ + Q, net gain Q₂. Cold body loses Q₂. Work in equals work out. Heat Q₂ has climbed cold to hot with no work input — Clausius broken.

Assume a free refrigerator, break Kelvin-Planck. A device lifts Q₂ from T₂ to T₁ with no work. Run an engine between the same bodies: takes Q₁ from T₁, gives W = Q₁ − Q₂, rejects Q₂ to T₂. The free device carries Q₂ back up. Hot loses Q₁ − Q₂ = W, cold unchanged, W work appears. A cycle made work from one reservoir — a PMM2.

Both directions hold; the statements stand or fall together.

What follows at once

  • Heat crosses a temperature difference from hot to cold only unless work is spent.
  • A cycle on one reservoir cannot produce net work: no engine has η = 100%.
  • No real engine reaches 100%; 3.4 gives the limit for a given pair.
  • A real refrigerator needs work, so COP = Q₂/W is finite.

Worked example

Question. An inventor claims an engine that takes 1000 kJ from a reservoir at 500 K, rejects 400 kJ to a reservoir at 300 K, and delivers 600 kJ of work. (a) Check the claim against the first law and against a reversible engine between the same two reservoirs. (b) Combine the claim with a reversed reversible engine to show it violates the Kelvin-Planck statement. (c) Use the excess work in a reversible refrigerator to show it violates the Clausius statement too.

  1. First law: W = 1000 − 400 = 600 kJ. Claim matches; first law accepts it.
  2. η_claim = 600/1000 = 60%.
  3. Reversible engine: η_rev = 1 − 300/500 = 40%. With Q₁ = 1000 kJ, W_rev = 400 kJ, Q₂_rev = 600 kJ.
  4. Excess work = 600 − 400 = 200 kJ.
  5. Reverse the reversible engine: driven by 400 kJ, it removes 600 kJ from 300 K and delivers 1000 kJ to 500 K.
  6. Combine. Hot reservoir: −1000 + 1000 = 0. Cold body: +400 − 600 = −200 kJ, so 200 kJ leaves. Work: 600 − 400 = 200 kJ net out.
  7. This cyclic pair takes 200 kJ from one 300 K reservoir and makes 200 kJ work. PMM2; Kelvin-Planck violated. First law still balances.
  8. Spend 200 kJ in a reversible refrigerator between 500 K and 300 K. COP = 300/(500 − 300) = 1.5. It removes 300 kJ from 300 K and delivers 500 kJ to 500 K.
  9. Add to step 7: cold body loses 200 + 300 = 500 kJ; 500 K reservoir gains 500 kJ; work in equals work out. Heat climbs 300 K to 500 K with no work input: Clausius violated.

Answer. The claim obeys the first law but gives 200 kJ extra work; combined with reversible devices it moves 500 kJ from 300 K to 500 K with zero work input, breaking both statements.

Exam traps

  • Kelvin-Planck is about a cyclic device, not one process. Isothermal expansion of an ideal gas is legal; repeating it needs compression work.
  • The statements are one law in two forms. A violation of one violates the other.
  • A PMM2 obeys conservation of energy. It breaks direction. Calling it a first-law violation is wrong; that is PMM1.
  • Clausius does not ban refrigerators. It bans heat uphill with no work and no other change.
  • For a reversible refrigerator, COP = T₂/(T₁ − T₂); cold temperature on top.

3.3 Reversible and irreversible processes

A process is reversible only if it can be run backwards and leave absolutely no trace — in the gas, in the cylinder, in the room. Real engines never manage this, but the idea earns its place: it marks the best that any device could ever do between the same two states, so the gap between it and a real machine measures what we are wasting. This chapter defines the reversible process, lists what spoils it, and shows how the same gas expanding the same way can deliver work or deliver nothing.

You need: quasi-static process and path from Unit 1 (Unit 1), and the isothermal process of an ideal gas from Unit 2.

Two conditions, together

A process is reversible if the system and its surroundings can both be returned to their exact starting states, leaving no change anywhere. Nothing may be left behind: no warm spot on the cylinder, no stirred-up oil, no worn bearing.

Two conditions must hold at the same time.

  • Quasi-static: at every instant the driving force exceeds the resisting force by only an infinitesimal amount. The piston moves because the gas pressure is p + dp, not p + 2 bar. Heat flows because the gas sits at T + dT, not at T + 200 K.
  • No dissipative effect: no friction, no viscosity, no electrical resistance, no plastic deformation, no mixing of two different fluids.

The first condition removes finite gradients inside and across the boundary. The second rules out dissipative effects. A process is reversible only when the first holds and the second is met: no finite gradient anywhere inside or across the boundary, and no dissipation.

W = ∫₁² p dV

p is the pressure at the moving boundary in kPa, dV is the small volume change in m³, and W is the work in kJ. For a reversible path, p must be the gas pressure itself, uniform and known at every instant.

A reversible process creates no entropy, so it is the yardstick for everything else. The Carnot engine in 3.4 is only as good as this limit allows.

What makes a process irreversible

Every real process carries at least one of these. Each has the same two signatures: a finite gradient, or a one-way conversion of work into internal energy.

  • Friction in a pump or under a piston: shaft work heats the oil and the metal, and that heat never climbs back into the shaft by itself.
  • Unrestrained expansion: gas in one flask is connected to an evacuated flask. Nothing pushes back, so W = 0, yet the gas spreads over both flasks and cannot be gathered into one without spending work.
  • Heat transfer across a finite temperature difference: a hot vessel left in a room cools to room temperature. The same heat will not flow back up the same difference.
  • Mixing of two streams: hot and cold water joining in a shower line. Separating them again needs a heat pump and work.
  • Current through a resistance: all the electrical work becomes heat in the wire.
  • Plastic deformation: bending a steel rod past its yield point leaves it permanently bent and slightly warm.

Entropy (see 3.7) and the Clausius inequality (see 3.6) turn these signatures into a number.

Internal and external reversibility

Internal reversibility concerns only what happens inside the boundary: the gas has no pressure gradient, no temperature gradient and no internal friction, so at every instant it is uniform and in equilibrium.

External reversibility concerns the link with the surroundings: heat crosses only to a reservoir at the same temperature, and work is delivered to a load that is raised with no outside friction. A process may be internally reversible and externally irreversible. Standard texts define a reversible (totally reversible) process as one that is both internally and externally reversible. Many numerical problems only need internal reversibility, so check whether the surroundings are also idealised before assuming total reversibility.

A limit, drawn not reached

No real process is reversible. We draw the reversible path the way a mathematician draws a smooth curve through measured points: it gives the best number possible, and the difference from reality is lost work, priced as exergy in Unit 4.

Slowness alone does not buy reversibility. A piston pushed very slowly against dry friction is quasi-static and still irreversible, because heat keeps appearing in the cylinder.

Worked example

Question. 0.5 kg of air at 300 K expands at constant temperature from 0.2 m³ to 0.4 m³. Take R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). (a) Find the work delivered if the expansion is reversible and isothermal. (b) The same air is instead allowed to expand freely into an evacuated flask until it occupies 0.4 m³. Find the work delivered and the work lost.

  1. Data: m = 0.5 kg, T = 300 K, v₁ = 0.2 m³, v₂ = 0.4 m³, R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K).
  2. For an ideal gas at constant temperature, p V = m R T, so p = m R T / V.
  3. Reversible work: W_rev = ∫ p dV = m R T ∫ dV/V = m R T ln(v₂/v₁).
  4. m R T = 0.5 × 0.287 × 300 = 43.05 kJ.
  5. v₂/v₁ = 0.4 / 0.2 = 2, and ln 2 = 0.6931.
  6. W_rev = 43.05 × 0.6931 = 29.84 kJ.
  7. Free expansion: the gas pushes against nothing, so W = 0 kJ.
  8. Work lost = 29.84 − 0 = 29.84 kJ. For an ideal gas in free expansion the temperature does not change and no heat is absorbed, so no energy is destroyed; what is lost is the 29.84 kJ of work that the reversible path could have delivered. That lost work equals T₀ × entropy generated = 300 × 0.0995 ≈ 29.84 kJ.

Answer. The reversible isothermal expansion delivers W_rev = 29.84 kJ; the free expansion delivers W = 0 kJ and loses all 29.84 kJ of possible work.

Exam traps

  • Quasi-static is necessary but not sufficient. A slow process with friction, or with a finite temperature difference across the wall, is still irreversible.
  • Reversible gives maximum work out of an expansion and minimum work into a compression. Do not apply the maximum rule to a compressor.
  • Free expansion gives W = 0 but is not reversible. For an ideal gas in an insulated flask the temperature also stays the same, which fools students into calling it isothermal and reversible.
  • Internally reversible is weaker than reversible. The boundary may still lose work to outside friction.
  • The entropy of the system may fall in an irreversible process, as when a hot block cools. The second law is applied to system plus surroundings together.

3.4 Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem

The Carnot cycle is a closed loop of four reversible steps run between a hot reservoir at T₁ and a cold reservoir at T₂. It is the best engine possible between those two temperatures. Every real engine, from a Chennai backup generator to a coal power plant, is judged against it. Its efficiency depends only on T₁ and T₂, not on the gas, the size of the machine or the pressures used.

You need: 3.3 reversible processes and Unit 2, the isothermal and adiabatic work formulas for an ideal gas.

The four reversible steps

Take m kg of ideal gas in a piston-cylinder. The hot reservoir is at T₁ and the cold reservoir at T₂. Every step is reversible: no friction, no finite temperature gap, no unrestrained expansion. Define Q₁ as heat taken in and Q₂ as heat rejected, both positive magnitudes.

  1. 1→2 isothermal expansion at T₁. The gas touches the hot reservoir, takes Q₁, and does work. T stays T₁.
  2. 2→3 adiabatic expansion. The gas is insulated. It expands further, does work, and T falls from T₁ to T₂. A reversible adiabatic step is isentropic (3.7).
  3. 3→4 isothermal compression at T₂. The gas touches the cold reservoir. Work goes in and the gas rejects Q₂ at constant T₂.
  4. 4→1 adiabatic compression. The gas is insulated. Work goes in and T rises from T₂ back to T₁. The gas returns to state 1.

On a p-V diagram the path is two isotherms cut by two adiabats. The enclosed area is the net work per cycle.

Efficiency from the gas

For an ideal gas, the two isothermal heat transfers have fixed forms. The two adiabatic steps link their volumes.

Q₁ = m R T₁ ln(V₂/V₁), Q₂ = m R T₂ ln(V₃/V₄)

m is mass in kg; R is the gas constant in kJ/(kg·K); T is absolute temperature in K; V₁ to V₄ are total volumes in m³; Q₂ is the positive magnitude rejected.

The adiabatic steps obey T₁V₂^(γ−1)=T₂V₃^(γ−1) and T₁V₁^(γ−1)=T₂V₄^(γ−1), where γ=c_p/c_v is the specific heat ratio. Divide the first by the second: V₂/V₁ = V₃/V₄. The volume ratios cancel in Q₂/Q₁.

η = W/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁ = 1 − T₂/T₁

η is thermal efficiency; W=Q₁−Q₂ is net work per cycle; T₁ and T₂ must be in kelvin.

The result holds for every working substance, not only a gas. A derivation on one substance is enough because Carnot's theorem says the reversible efficiency is fixed by the reservoirs.

Carnot's theorem

Carnot's theorem has two parts.

  • No engine working between two fixed reservoirs can be more efficient than a reversible engine working between the same two reservoirs.
  • All reversible engines working between the same two reservoirs have the same efficiency, whatever the working substance, the size of the cycle or the pressures used.

Some syllabi call the whole result Carnot's principle and list part 2 as a corollary. The proof is by contradiction. If a claimed engine were better, couple it to a reversible Carnot refrigerator. The pair would move heat from cold to hot with no net work input, which violates the Clausius statement (3.2). Hence no such engine exists.

A real engine between T₁ and T₂ has irreversibilities and lower efficiency. The Carnot value 1−T₂/T₁ is the upper limit, not a promise. It is the benchmark used to judge power plants and refrigerators.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of air with R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K) runs a Carnot cycle between 900 K and 300 K. The isothermal expansion at 900 K takes the volume from 0.1 m³ to 0.2 m³. Find Q₁, Q₂, W and η.

  1. Data: m=1 kg, R=0.287 kJ/(kg·K), T₁=900 K, T₂=300 K, V₁=0.1 m³, V₂=0.2 m³. For the two adiabatic steps, V₃/V₄ = V₂/V₁ = 0.2/0.1 = 2.
  2. Q₁ = m R T₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = 1×0.287×900×ln2 = 258.3×0.6931 = 179.04 kJ ≈ 179.0 kJ.
  3. Q₂ = m R T₂ ln(V₃/V₄) = 1×0.287×300×ln2 = 86.1×0.6931 = 59.68 kJ ≈ 59.7 kJ.
  4. W = Q₁ − Q₂ = 179.04 − 59.68 = 119.36 kJ ≈ 119.4 kJ.
  5. η = W/Q₁ = 119.36/179.04 = 0.6667 = 66.67%, or η = 1−300/900 = 66.67% ≈ 66.7%.

Answer. Q₁ = 179.0 kJ, Q₂ = 59.7 kJ, W = 119.4 kJ and η = 66.7%.

Exam traps

  • Temperatures must be in kelvin. Using °C in 1−T₂/T₁ gives a meaningless number. Convert first.
  • Efficiency is a ratio of energies per cycle. It does not depend on m or on the cycle's size; do not multiply η by mass.
  • Q₂ in 1−Q₂/Q₁ is the positive heat rejected. If your first-law sign convention makes Q₂ negative, do not subtract a negative by mistake.
  • An efficiency above 1−T₂/T₁ is impossible for any engine between those reservoirs. Equality means reversible; a real engine is lower.
  • The Carnot cycle is not a real Rankine or Brayton cycle. Those are Unit 6; do not use their practical efficiencies as Carnot limits.

3.5 Thermodynamic temperature scale

A thermometer needs a scale, and gas thermometers using different gases disagree slightly. The thermodynamic temperature scale removes the working substance from the definition. Carnot's theorem says every reversible engine between the same two reservoirs has the same heat ratio, so that ratio can define temperature. We set T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂. This makes Carnot efficiency exactly 1 − T₂/T₁ for every substance. The scale is used whenever second-law calculations need absolute temperatures, such as entropy and exergy, and it fixes the Kelvin scale.

You need: 3.4 Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem and Unit 1, Foundations: Systems, Properties and Zeroth Law (temperature and the zeroth law).

Why a gas scale is not enough

A gas thermometer reads temperature by a gas property, for example pressure at fixed volume. It assumes p/ptr = T/Ttr, where p is the gas pressure, ptr is the pressure at the triple point, T is the temperature to be found, and Ttr is 273.16 K. Different gases do not follow this relation exactly. Readings from helium, nitrogen and argon agree closely near the ideal-gas range and drift apart outside it. A fundamental scale cannot depend on which gas is inside the bulb.

Carnot's theorem, from 3.4, removes the gas. Every reversible engine working between the same two reservoirs has the same thermal efficiency η, where η = W/Q₁, W is work output and Q₁ is heat taken from the hot reservoir. The theorem holds for any working substance: gas, steam, or a magnetic material. So the heat ratio Q₁/Q₂ of a reversible engine is a property of the two reservoirs only, not of the engine. That ratio is the raw material for a substance-free temperature scale.

The practical problem is small but serious. A thermometer calibrated with one gas can disagree with a thermometer calibrated with another gas by amounts that matter in careful work. Metrology needs one scale that every thermometer realises, not a family of gas-dependent scales.

Defining temperature by a reversible engine

Take a reversible engine between a hot reservoir at thermodynamic temperature T₁ and a cold reservoir at T₂. Let Q₁ be heat taken from the hot reservoir and Q₂ be heat rejected to the cold reservoir, both as positive magnitudes in joule. Carnot's theorem says Q₁/Q₂ is fixed by the two reservoirs. Define temperature so that the same ratio holds:

T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂

T₁ and T₂ are thermodynamic temperatures of the hot and cold reservoirs, in kelvin; Q₁ is heat taken from the hot reservoir by a reversible engine, in joule; Q₂ is heat rejected to the cold reservoir, in joule.

This is the thermodynamic temperature scale. It is also called the Kelvin scale. The definition uses only heat quantities, which are measured with a calorimeter or from work and first-law balances. It never asks what the engine contains. If a reversible engine takes 600 J from a hot reservoir and rejects 300 J to a cold reservoir, then T₁/T₂ = 600/300 = 2. The hot reservoir is twice as hot as the cold one on this scale.

  • Only ratios are fixed. Multiplying every T by a constant leaves every Q₁/Q₂ unchanged.
  • One fixed point is needed. The triple point of water is assigned 273.16 K exactly.
  • With this fixed point, the scale is fixed completely and is written in kelvin, symbol K.

The scale coincides with the ideal-gas scale. For an ideal gas, a reversible Carnot cycle gives Q₁/Q₂ = T₁/T₂ when T is the ideal-gas temperature. So gas thermometer readings need no correction when they are treated as thermodynamic temperatures. The agreement is exact for an ideal gas and is the reason the Kelvin scale looks familiar.

Efficiency and the absolute zero

For a reversible engine, the first law gives W = Q₁ − Q₂. Thermal efficiency is η = W/Q₁ = 1 − Q₂/Q₁. Insert the defining ratio Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁:

ηrev = 1 − T₂/T₁

ηrev is the thermal efficiency of a reversible engine, dimensionless; T₁ is the hot-reservoir temperature, in kelvin; T₂ is the cold-reservoir temperature, in kelvin.

This result is exact for every reversible engine, whatever the working substance. For a real engine between the same reservoirs, η is lower. The reversible value is the yardstick. It is not a prediction of a real engine's efficiency; see 3.6 for how irreversibility enters.

η = 1 would need T₂/T₁ = 0, so T₂ = 0 K. Absolute zero is not available. The third law says it cannot be reached in a finite number of steps. Every real engine with a sink above 0 K rejects some heat, so its efficiency is below 100%.

The definition also sets the zero: T = 0 K is the lower limit of the scale. It is approached, never reached. Temperatures in °C are shifted Kelvin values: T(K) = t(°C) + 273.15. Use kelvin in every second-law formula.

Worked example

Question. Two reversible engines work in series. The first takes heat from a source at T₁ and rejects it at T₂. The second takes that heat at T₂ and rejects it at 300 K. Each engine has a thermal efficiency of 20%. Find T₁ and T₂ on the thermodynamic scale.

  1. Write the reversible efficiency for the second engine: η₂ = 1 − TL/TH = 1 − 300 K / T₂.
  2. Set η₂ = 0.20: 0.20 = 1 − 300/T₂. Hence 300/T₂ = 0.80, so T₂ = 300 K / 0.80 = 375 K.
  3. Write the reversible efficiency for the first engine: η₁ = 1 − T₂/T₁ = 1 − 375 K / T₁.
  4. Set η₁ = 0.20: 0.20 = 1 − 375/T₁. Hence 375/T₁ = 0.80, so T₁ = 375 K / 0.80 = 468.75 K.
  5. Check the heat ratios. For engine 1, Q₂/Q₁ = T₂/T₁ = 375/468.75 = 0.80. For engine 2, Q₃/Q₂ = 300/375 = 0.80, where Q₃ is heat rejected to 300 K. Both engines reject 80% of the heat they receive, as a 20% efficiency requires.

Answer. T₂ = 375 K and T₁ = 468.75 K on the thermodynamic scale.

Exam traps

  • T₁/T₂ = Q₁/Q₂ applies only to a reversible engine. A real engine's heat ratio is not a temperature ratio.
  • Use absolute temperatures in kelvin. Substituting 27 for 300 K in a second-law formula gives a meaningless answer.
  • The definition fixes only ratios. Without the triple-point value 273.16 K, all temperatures could be multiplied by an arbitrary constant.
  • Efficiency 100% needs T₂ = 0 K exactly. A very small sink temperature still gives η below 1.
  • Do not use the Carnot formula to predict a real engine's efficiency. It is the upper limit, not the actual value.

3.6 Clausius inequality

The Clausius inequality turns the second law into one number for a whole cycle: add up δQ/T for every small heat transfer around the cycle, and the total cannot be positive. Heat entering is positive, heat leaving is negative, and T is the boundary temperature in kelvin where the heat crosses. Equality means the cycle is reversible; less than zero means it is irreversible. It matters because it gives the sign test for real engines, refrigerators and power plants, and it is the step that leads to entropy in 3.7.

You need: 3.4 Carnot cycle and Carnot's theorem and 3.5 Thermodynamic temperature scale for the reversible reference cycle and the kelvin scale.

The inequality

For a cycle, follow every small heat transfer δQ across the boundary of the system. Divide each δQ by the absolute temperature T at which that heat crosses. Add the terms around the closed cycle. The Clausius inequality says the total cannot be positive. For an engine, the hot-stream term is positive and the cold-stream term is negative. For a refrigerator, the same rule applies: heat taken in by the system is positive.

∮ δQ/T ≤ 0

δQ is the small heat transfer in kJ, positive into the system and negative out of it; T is the boundary temperature in kelvin at which that heat crosses; ∮ means the sum around one complete cycle.

For a cycle with a few separate heat transfers, use Σ Q_i/T_i ≤ 0 with the same sign rule. If the heat comes from a reservoir at T_r, use T_r for that term. The temperature inside the system may vary; it is not used unless the boundary itself is at that temperature.

Why a positive total is impossible

Suppose a cycle gave a positive total, ∮δQ/T > 0. Build a reversible Carnot unit for each heat transfer. The unit works between a fixed reservoir at T₀ and the boundary at T. It exchanges −δQ at T, cancelling the actual heat there, and takes Q₀ = δQ T₀/T from the T₀ reservoir. The actual cycle and all the reversible units together form a device that returns to its starting state and exchanges heat only with the single reservoir at T₀.

  1. Sum the T₀ heat: Q₀,total = Σ δQ T₀/T = T₀ ∮δQ/T.
  2. The first law for the combined device gives W_total = Q₀,total, because it has no net change of state.
  3. A positive integral makes Q₀,total positive. Then W_total is positive: work from one reservoir with no other change. That is exactly the Kelvin-Planck forbidden machine.
  4. So the positive case is impossible. The total must be zero or negative.

The argument works for any T₀ > 0. It does not assume the actual cycle is reversible. It only uses a reversible reference unit, whose behaviour is fixed by the thermodynamic temperature scale in 3.5.

The three cases and the Kelvin-Planck link

  • ∮δQ/T = 0: the cycle is reversible. Every heat transfer can be reversed with no trace.
  • ∮δQ/T < 0: the cycle is irreversible. Friction, finite temperature difference or unrestrained expansion is present.
  • ∮δQ/T > 0: no cycle can have this sign. It would break the second law.

For a cycle that exchanges heat with one reservoir only at T₀, the integral is Q_in/T₀ − Q_out/T₀ = (Q_in − Q_out)/T₀ = W/T₀. The inequality says W/T₀ ≤ 0, so W ≤ 0. A one-reservoir cycle cannot deliver work. That is the 3.2 Kelvin-Planck statement. The zero case is the seed of 3.7 entropy; the negative case becomes the 3.8 increase of entropy. The zero case is not a small effect; it is the limit that real machines approach but never reach.

Worked example

Question. A cycle receives 500 kJ of heat at 600 K, rejects 300 kJ at 300 K, and delivers 200 kJ of work. Evaluate ∮δQ/T. Is the cycle reversible, irreversible or impossible? Find its efficiency and compare it with the Carnot efficiency between the same two temperatures.

  1. Use heat entering positive: Q₁ = +500 kJ at T₁ = 600 K; Q₂ = −300 kJ at T₂ = 300 K.
  2. First term: Q₁/T₁ = 500/600 = 0.8333 kJ/K. Second term: Q₂/T₂ = −300/300 = −1.0000 kJ/K.
  3. Cyclic integral: ∮δQ/T = 0.8333 − 1.0000 = −0.1667 kJ/K ≈ −0.167 kJ/K.
  4. The result is less than zero, so the cycle is irreversible. It is not impossible, because an impossible cycle would give a positive value.
  5. Check with first law: W = Q₁ + Q₂ = 500 − 300 = 200 kJ, matching the stated work. Efficiency η = W/Q₁ = 200/500 = 0.40 = 40%.
  6. Carnot efficiency for 600 K and 300 K: η_C = 1 − T₂/T₁ = 1 − 300/600 = 0.50 = 50%. The actual 40% is below 50%, as expected for an irreversible cycle.

Answer. ∮δQ/T = −0.167 kJ/K; the cycle is irreversible; η = 40%, below the Carnot value of 50%.

Exam traps

  • Use kelvin. A term like 300/27 is meaningless; convert 27°C to 300 K first.
  • T is the boundary or reservoir temperature where the heat crosses, not the gas temperature inside during an irreversible process. For a reservoir, use the reservoir temperature.
  • Equality means reversible, not 'no heat transfer'. A reversible cycle can exchange plenty of heat and still have zero integral.
  • A positive integral is impossible, not 'very irreversible'. If your calculation gives >0, check signs, units and temperatures before calling the cycle real.

3.7 Entropy: concept, T-s diagram and entropy change

Entropy is a property that measures how heat and temperature set the direction of a process. For a reversible path it is defined by dS = δQ_rev/T. Because the Clausius inequality gives zero around any reversible cycle, the integral between two states is the same for every reversible route. That is why entropy belongs to the state, not the path. Engineers use it on the T-s diagram to see heat as area and to compute ideal-gas entropy changes in compressors, turbines and heat exchangers.

You need: the Clausius inequality from 3.6 and ideal-gas processes and specific heats from Unit 2.

Entropy as a property

The Clausius inequality says ∮δQ/T ≤ 0 for any cycle; the value is zero for a reversible cycle (see 3.6). Take two reversible paths between state 1 and state 2. Go from 1 to 2 along path A, then return from 2 to 1 along path B reversed. This closed cycle is reversible, so its cyclic integral is zero. Hence the integral of δQ/T from 1 to 2 along A equals the integral along B. The value depends only on the end states. That is the definition of a property.

dS = δQ_rev/T

δQ_rev is the heat transfer in a reversible process, in kJ; T is absolute temperature, in K; dS is the entropy change, in kJ/K.

S is extensive: 2 kg has twice the entropy of 1 kg at the same state. Specific entropy s = S/m has units kJ/kg·K. For an irreversible process, ΔS is still fixed by the end states; calculate it along any convenient reversible path, not along the real path. The real path gives only ∫δQ/T, which is less than ΔS; the gap is the entropy generated inside the system (see 3.8).

The T-s diagram

On a temperature-entropy diagram, plot T in K against s in kJ/kg·K. For a reversible process, δQ_rev = T dS, so Q_rev = ∫T dS. The signed area under the path between two entropies is the heat transfer. If T is in K and S in kJ/K, the area is in kJ.

An isothermal reversible process is a horizontal line. A reversible adiabatic (isentropic) process is a vertical line. A Carnot cycle between two temperatures is therefore a rectangle: two horizontal isotherms and two vertical isentropics. The area inside the rectangle is the net work of the cycle; it equals Q_in − Q_out.

Slopes. At constant pressure, T ds = dh = cp dT, so dT/ds = T/cp. At constant volume, T ds = du = cv dT, so dT/ds = T/cv. Since cv < cp, T/cv > T/cp, so the constant-volume line is steeper on the T-s diagram. The figure shows both curves starting at 300 K.

3.8 Principle of increase of entropy

Energy is conserved; entropy is not. In every real process the total entropy of the system plus its surroundings rises, and the amount it rises by is the entropy generated, S_gen. This one inequality decides which processes are possible, separates the best possible machine from the actual one, and shows where a plant is destroying quality of energy. Engineers use it to judge heat exchanger designs, steam plant performance and any claim of a new, super-efficient engine.

You need: 3.7 for dS = δQ_rev/T, and 3.3 for what makes a process irreversible.

The principle written as an equation

For any process between state 1 and state 2, reversible or not, the entropy account reads as follows.

ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings = S_gen ≥ 0

ΔS_system is the entropy change of the system in kJ/K, equal to S₂ − S₁; ΔS_surroundings is the entropy change of everything outside the system, in kJ/K; S_gen is the entropy generated during the process, in kJ/K; the sum is never negative.

The equality holds only for a fully reversible process. Every real process gives a strict greater-than. S_gen is not a property of the system; it is a quantity produced along the path, and it depends on how the process is carried out.

Seal the system and its surroundings into one box and you have an isolated system. For it the statement becomes: the entropy of an isolated system increases in every real process and stays constant only in a reversible one. It never falls. That is the second law stated in entropy words.

Compare this with the first law. The first law says energy is conserved, so the energy account always balances to zero. The second law says the entropy account does not balance to zero — it always leaves a positive remainder. Nature keeps books that never close.

S_gen measures how irreversible the process is

S_gen is a quantitative measure of irreversibility. Large S_gen means a badly irreversible process; zero means a reversible one. Four sources account for almost every case you will meet.

Heat through a finite temperature difference is the commonest. When Q kilojoules cross from a body at T_hot to a body at T_cold, both in kelvin:

S_gen = Q (1/T_cold − 1/T_hot)

Q is the heat in kJ passing from the hot body to the cold body; T_cold and T_hot are in K; S_gen is in kJ/K. The bracket is positive because T_cold < T_hot, so a wider gap or a larger Q creates more entropy.

A reversible adiabatic process has Q = 0 and no friction or mixing, so S_gen = 0 and ΔS = 0. Such a process is called isentropic — same entropy. On a T-s diagram the ideal gas turbine or compressor is drawn as a vertical line.

The criterion for a spontaneous change

The same inequality answers a different question: will this change happen on its own? For a closed system held at constant internal energy U and constant volume V, with no help from outside, the answer is yes only if dS ≥ 0. Entropy climbs to a maximum, and that maximum is the equilibrium state.

Only a few universities ask for this form. At constant temperature and volume the criterion is written with the Helmholtz function, at constant temperature and pressure with the Gibbs function; both are built from entropy and belong to Unit 4.

A positive ΔS says the process can happen, not that it happens quickly. The conversion of diamond to graphite has a positive ΔS at room conditions and you will not see it happen in your lifetime. Thermodynamics gives direction, never rate.

Every kJ/K of entropy generated makes T₀ × S_gen of work unavailable, where T₀ is the surroundings temperature in K. Unit 4 turns this into lost work, W_lost = T₀ S_gen, and into exergy, the part of energy still useful.

Worked example

Question. In an insulated vessel, 2 kg of water at 80 °C is mixed with 3 kg of water at 20 °C. Take cp = 4.187 kJ/kg·K and treat it as constant. Find the final mixture temperature and the total entropy change, and state whether the mixing is reversible. (8 marks)

C program.

#include <stdio.h>
#include <math.h>

int main(void) {
    double m1 = 2.0, T1 = 353.0;
    double m2 = 3.0, T2 = 293.0;
    double cp = 4.187;
    double Tf = (m1*T1 + m2*T2) / (m1 + m2);
    double dS = m1*cp*log(Tf/T1) + m2*cp*log(Tf/T2);
    printf("%.2f K\n", Tf);
    printf("%.4f kJ/K\n", dS);
    return 0;
}
  1. Convert to kelvin: T₁ = 80 + 273 = 353 K, T₂ = 20 + 273 = 293 K.
  2. The vessel is insulated and no work crosses the boundary, so the heat given up by the hot water equals the heat taken by the cold water: m₁cp(T₁ − T_f) = m₂cp(T_f − T₂). The cp cancels.
  3. Solve for T_f: T_f = (m₁T₁ + m₂T₂)/(m₁ + m₂) = (2 × 353 + 3 × 293)/5 = (706 + 879)/5 = 1585/5 = 317 K = 44 °C.
  4. Entropy change of the hot water: ΔS₁ = m₁cp ln(T_f/T₁) = 2 × 4.187 × ln(317/353) = 8.374 × (−0.10757) = −0.9008 kJ/K.
  5. Entropy change of the cold water: ΔS₂ = m₂cp ln(T_f/T₂) = 3 × 4.187 × ln(317/293) = 12.561 × (0.07873) = +0.9889 kJ/K.
  6. The vessel is insulated, so the surroundings change by zero. Total: ΔS_total = −0.9008 + 0.9889 = +0.0882 kJ/K = S_gen.
  7. The total is positive, so the mixing is irreversible and allowed by the second law. Warm water giving heat to cold water is exactly the finite-temperature-gap source of entropy.

Exam traps

  • ΔS of the system alone may be negative — freezing water and compressing gas both do it. Only system plus surroundings is guaranteed non-negative, so write the full account before you conclude anything.
  • S_gen is in kJ/K, not kJ. Dividing Q by a temperature in °C gives nonsense; convert to kelvin first.
  • A reversible adiabatic process is isentropic (ΔS = 0). A reversible isothermal process is not: ΔS = Q/T is finite. Do not label every reversible process isentropic.
  • When the vessel is not insulated, the surroundings' entropy change must be added with its sign. Many answers lose marks by silently dropping it.
  • A positive ΔS says the process can happen, not that it happens quickly. Direction comes from thermodynamics; rate comes from kinetics.

3.9 Third law of thermodynamics

The third law fixes the zero of entropy. At absolute zero, a perfect crystal at equilibrium has zero entropy, so entropy can be listed as an absolute value and not only as a change. It also explains why 0 K is a limit, not a target you can hit. Cryogenics, low-temperature physics and steam tables all use this idea; see Unit 5 for steam tables that carry absolute entropy.

You need entropy as a property (3.7) and the state-function view of entropy (3.8).

Nernst theorem and the third law

Cool a substance and its entropy falls. Early measurements could give only ΔS between two temperatures. Nernst asked what happens as T → 0 K. His heat theorem says that for a condensed system at equilibrium, the entropy change of any isothermal process tends to zero as T tends to zero. In symbols, ΔS → 0 as T → 0 K.

From this, Planck proposed a fixed zero: for a perfect crystal at 0 K, S = 0. This is the third law. A perfect crystal has every atom on its proper lattice site, with no mixing and no frozen disorder. There is only one way to arrange it, so the statistical entropy S = kB ln W gives S = kB ln 1 = 0. Here kB is Boltzmann's constant (1.381 × 10−23 J/K) and W is the number of equally likely arrangements.

The law does not say that every real material has zero entropy at 0 K. It says a perfect crystal at equilibrium does. A glass or a crystal with trapped defects does not. That leftover is residual entropy.

Absolute entropy from heat capacity

With a zero at 0 K, entropy at any T is an absolute value. Heat the sample reversibly from 0 K; at each step dS = δQrev/T = cp dT/T. Integrate:

S(T) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>T</sup> (c<sub>p</sub>/T) dT

S(T) is absolute entropy at temperature T in J/(mol·K); cp is heat capacity at constant pressure in J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K.

Near 0 K the heat capacity is too small to measure over the last part. Debye's model gives cp = aT3, where a is a constant for the material with units J/(mol·K4). Put this into the integral from 0 to a low measured temperature T1:

∫<sub>0</sub><sup>T<sub>1</sub></sup> (aT<sup>3</sup>/T) dT = aT<sub>1</sub><sup>3</sup>/3

a is the Debye constant in J/(mol·K4); T1 is the lowest measured temperature in K; the result is in J/(mol·K).

Above T1 you add the measured ∫(cp/T)dT pieces. If cp were constant, S = cp ln T + constant. As T → 0, ln T → −∞, so S would run to minus infinity. That cannot match S = 0. Real cp falls to zero faster than T, so cp/T stays finite and the integral converges. The third law therefore forces cp → 0 as T → 0 K. Most exam questions ask for the statement and the unattainability argument; numerical absolute entropies appear only occasionally.

Absolute zero cannot be reached

Cooling by adiabatic demagnetisation uses a magnetic salt. Magnetise it isothermally, releasing heat. Then isolate it and remove the field adiabatically. The spins disarrange and the sample cools. Repeat with a new starting temperature. Each step lowers T, but the entropy curves for different field strengths approach each other near 0 K. The temperature drop per step shrinks.

Unattainability statement: no finite sequence of processes can bring a system to exactly 0 K. You can approach it, never arrive. This is equivalent to the third law for equilibrium systems. The unattainability form is often easier to apply than the Nernst form. It forbids a machine that reaches 0 K in finite steps. It does not forbid getting very close: laboratories reach microkelvin or nanokelvin. The last fraction of a kelvin is paid for with many steps.

At 0 K any reversible isothermal process has ΔS = 0, so entropy cannot be used to cool the last bit. Also cp → 0, so the heat capacity vanishes. That is the thermodynamic signature of the third law.

Worked example

Question. A non-metallic solid follows the Debye law below 10 K, with cp = aT3, where a = 0.002 J/(mol·K4). Take S = 0 at 0 K. Find the absolute entropy at 10 K.

  1. Write the heat capacity: cp = 0.002 T3 J/(mol·K), with T in K.
  2. For reversible heating at constant pressure: dS = cp dT/T = (aT3/T) dT = aT2 dT.
  3. Integrate from 0 K to 10 K: S = ∫010 aT2 dT = a[T3/3]010.
  4. Put in a = 0.002 J/(mol·K4) and T = 10 K: S = 0.002 × 103/3 J/(mol·K).
  5. S = 0.002 × 1000/3 = 2/3 = 0.667 J/(mol·K).

Answer. The absolute entropy at 10 K is 0.667 J/(mol·K).

Exam traps

  • S = 0 at 0 K is for a perfect crystal at equilibrium. Glasses, solid solutions and crystals with frozen-in defects keep residual entropy, so do not apply zero blindly.
  • Absolute zero is unattainable, not just difficult. A finite number of adiabatic demagnetisation steps never reaches exactly 0 K; microkelvin is not zero.
  • Do not integrate S = cp ln T down to 0 K. Use cp = aT3 and the integral aT3/3 over the low-temperature range.
  • cp and cv tend to zero as T → 0 K. Treating them as constant near absolute zero gives a divergent, negative entropy.
  • Entropy change between two states needs no third law. Absolute entropy, as in tables, does need the third-law zero.
