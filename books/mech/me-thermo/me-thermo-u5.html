5.1 Pure substance, phase change and p-v-T surface

Water in a piston–cylinder at constant pressure, heated slowly, does not warm up steadily from ice-cold to red-hot. It warms, then boils at a fixed temperature while swelling enormously, then warms again. The same five states — compressed liquid, saturated liquid, wet mixture, saturated vapour, superheated vapour — appear in every pure substance, and their names, volumes and masses follow simple rules. Boiler designers, refrigeration engineers and steam-table users all work inside these states daily. This chapter names them, draws the constant-pressure path on the T-v and p-v planes, and collects every equilibrium state onto one surface in p-v-T space.

You need: system, property and state, and specific volume as an intensive property, from the basic concepts of thermodynamics.

Pure substance, phase and saturation

A pure substance is matter whose chemical composition is the same everywhere in the system. Water is a pure substance; so is nitrogen. A glass of ice and liquid water is still a pure substance, because both phases are H₂O. Wet air is not, because the liquid water and the moist gas have different compositions.

A phase is a quantity of matter uniform in chemical composition and physical structure. Ice is one phase, liquid water a second, water vapour a third. A system may hold one, two or three of them at once.

Saturation is the state at which a pure substance can change phase with no change in temperature or pressure. At 101.325 kPa water boils at 100 °C; that is a saturation pair. Inside saturation, pressure and temperature are not independent — fix one and the other is fixed (the Clausius–Clapeyron equation, met with the thermodynamic relations, gives the slope of this link).

Five names cover the behaviour. Compressed liquid, also called subcooled liquid: colder than saturation at that pressure. Saturated liquid: exactly at saturation, about to boil, quality x = 0. Wet mixture: liquid and vapour together, 0 < x < 1. Saturated vapour: all vapour, about to condense, x = 1. Superheated vapour: hotter than saturation at that pressure.

Heating at constant pressure: the five states in order

Take 2 kg of water in a piston–cylinder at 101.325 kPa and 20 °C, and add heat slowly while the piston holds the pressure constant.

  1. 20 °C to 100 °C: compressed liquid. The water expands only slightly, from vf = 0.001002 m³/kg to 0.001043 m³/kg. The heat added raises temperature; it is sensible heat.
  2. At 100 °C: saturated liquid, x = 0. Any further heat starts boiling and the temperature stops rising.
  3. Boiling: the temperature stays at 100 °C while the volume grows about 1600 times, from 0.001043 to 1.673 m³/kg. This is the wet region; the heat added is latent heat.
  4. At 1.673 m³/kg the last droplet evaporates: saturated vapour, x = 1. More heat now raises the temperature again and the vapour becomes superheated.

v = vf + x (vg − vf)

v is the specific volume of the wet mixture in m³/kg; vf and vg are the saturated liquid and saturated vapour specific volumes at the same pressure, in m³/kg; x is the dryness fraction, x = mg / m, the vapour mass divided by the total mass, with 0 ≤ x ≤ 1.

Figure 5.1.1 draws this path on the T-v plane, with v on a log scale so that 0.001 and 1.7 m³/kg fit on one page. The boiling stretch is the horizontal line at Tsat = 100 °C; on the p-v plane the same stretch is a horizontal line at p = 101.325 kPa. Both stretches end on the saturation dome, the boundary of the wet region. Heat accounting for the three stages is in 5.4; the two ends of the dome are in 5.2.

The p-v-T surface and the triple line

The state postulate says that for a simple compressible substance two independent intensive properties fix the state. So all equilibrium states of a pure substance lie on one surface in p-v-T space. Compressed liquid and superheated vapour are single-phase sheets. The two-phase region is a ruled surface that joins the saturated liquid line to the saturated vapour line. Inside it, p and T are not independent, so the second property must be v or x.

Flatten the surface onto the p-v plane and the wet region becomes the saturation dome, crossed by a horizontal line for each constant-pressure heating. On the T-v plane the wet region is bounded by the saturated liquid and saturated vapour lines; each constant-pressure heating is a horizontal segment at that pressure's Tsat. It is one object seen twice.

Ice, liquid water and vapour meet at the triple point of water: 0.01 °C and 0.6117 kPa. In p-v-T space the three two-phase surfaces meet along the triple line at that fixed temperature and pressure, because the volume may take any value between the three saturated volumes. Below the triple pressure water sublimes: ice turns straight to vapour.

  • Steam plants: the boiler drum and the condenser hold wet mixtures, so quality x sets what the plant can extract.
  • Refrigeration: the throttling valve leaves a wet mixture; the evaporator boils it off, usually to saturated or superheated vapour. The quality after throttling sets cooling capacity.
  • LNG storage: cryogenic tanks hold a boiling liquid near atmospheric pressure; a pressure drop causes flash boiling.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of water at 101.325 kPa has dryness fraction x = 0.25. At this pressure vf = 0.001043 m³/kg and vg = 1.673 m³/kg. Find the specific volume of the mixture, the total volume, and the mass of liquid and of vapour.

  1. Given: m = 2 kg, x = 0.25, vf = 0.001043 m³/kg, vg = 1.673 m³/kg at 101.325 kPa. Since 0 < x < 1, the water is a wet mixture.
  2. Specific volume of the mixture: v = vf + x (vg − vf).
  3. Latent part: vg − vf = 1.673 − 0.001043 = 1.671957 m³/kg.
  4. x (vg − vf) = 0.25 × 1.671957 = 0.417989 m³/kg.
  5. v = 0.001043 + 0.417989 = 0.419032 m³/kg.
  6. Total volume: V = m v = 2 × 0.419032 = 0.838064 m³.
  7. Vapour mass: mg = x m = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 kg. Liquid mass: mf = (1 − x) m = 1.5 kg.
  8. Check: mf + mg = 1.5 + 0.5 = 2 kg, which matches the given mass.

Answer. v = 0.4190 m³/kg, V = 0.8381 m³, with 1.5 kg of liquid and 0.5 kg of vapour.

Exam traps

  • Writing v = x vg for a wet mixture. Quality multiplies only the latent part, so use v = vf + x (vg − vf); the first term is small but it is never zero.
  • Thinking a two-phase system cannot be a pure substance. Ice plus water is a pure substance; moist air is not, because the two phases differ in composition.
  • Calling x = 1 superheated. x = 1 is saturated vapour, still on the boundary of the dome; superheat starts only above Tsat.
  • Quoting both p and T inside the wet region as if they were independent. There T is fixed by p, so an extra temperature is either redundant or a contradiction.
  • Treating x as a volume fraction. It is a mass fraction, x = mg / m; the volume share of vapour is much larger because vg is hundreds of times vf.

5.2 Triple point and critical point

The triple point and critical point are the two endpoints of the liquid-vapour saturation curve on a pressure-temperature diagram. At the triple point, solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium. At the critical point, the liquid and vapour phases become identical. These two points bound the range of conditions where ordinary boiling and condensation occur. Engineers use them to decide whether a substance is a liquid, gas or supercritical fluid in power plants, refrigerators and chemical processes.

You need: phase names and saturation from 5.1, and equilibrium and property diagrams from the basic concepts of thermodynamics.

Phase boundaries and the triple point

On a pressure-temperature (p-T) diagram, a pure substance shows three phase boundaries. The sublimation curve separates solid and vapour. The melting curve separates solid and liquid. The vaporisation curve separates liquid and vapour. These curves meet at one point: the triple point.

At the triple point, solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium. For water, the triple point is at T = 273.16 K (0.01°C) and p = 0.6117 kPa. This pressure is much lower than atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa). Until 2019 the triple point of water defined the kelvin. The kelvin is now defined through the Boltzmann constant, and the triple point remains a fixed, reproducible reference state at 273.16 K.

Below the triple-point pressure, liquid water cannot exist. If you heat ice at a pressure below 0.6117 kPa, it sublimes: it turns directly into vapour without melting. The sublimation curve lies below the triple point on the p-T diagram. At pressures above the triple point and below the critical pressure, heating ice first melts it to liquid, then boils the liquid to vapour.

Figure 5.2.1 draws the three curves on a p-T diagram (not to scale) with the two heating paths of the worked example: one at 0.4 kPa, below the triple point, and one at atmospheric pressure. Water’s melting curve leans slightly to the left.

The critical point

The vaporisation curve ends at the critical point. For water, the critical temperature is Tc = 647.1 K (373.95°C) and the critical pressure is pc = 22.064 MPa. At the critical point, the saturated liquid and saturated vapour states become identical. The latent heat of vaporisation falls to zero.

Above the critical temperature, no amount of pressure can liquefy the substance. It remains a gas or a supercritical fluid. A supercritical fluid has properties between those of a liquid and a gas; it can dissolve materials like a liquid and diffuse like a gas.

On a p-v diagram, the critical point is where the saturated liquid line and saturated vapour line meet. The dome-shaped two-phase region shrinks to a point. The isotherm through the critical point has a horizontal inflection: (∂p/∂v)T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)T = 0 at the critical point. This is the critical isotherm. Below Tc, each isotherm has a flat horizontal segment inside the dome, where liquid and vapour coexist. Above Tc, the isotherms are smooth curves with no flat part.

The triple and critical points of a few common fluids are worth knowing, because the compressibility chart uses the critical values. Carbon dioxide has a triple-point pressure of about 5 atm, so at atmospheric pressure solid CO₂ (dry ice) cannot melt: it sublimes at about −78 °C.

Reading the diagrams and practical use

Engineers read the p-T diagram to choose the right phase for a process. A steam power plant using a Rankine cycle operates below the critical pressure: water boils in the boiler at a pressure well under 22.064 MPa. A supercritical boiler runs above the critical pressure, so the water turns into steam without a distinct boiling point. The steam is a single dense phase.

The triple point warns you about low-pressure processes. Freeze drying and low-pressure vacuum drying run below 0.6117 kPa, so water sublimes there; ordinary vacuum drying at a few kPa still has liquid water. If you assume the wrong phase, your energy balance will be wrong.

When you read a p-T or p-v diagram, check the pressure scale: 0.6117 kPa is not 0.6117 MPa. That factor of 1000 changes the phase. Also use absolute pressure, not gauge pressure. A gauge pressure of zero means atmospheric pressure, 101.325 kPa, which is far above the triple point.

Worked example

Question. A piston–cylinder holds 1 kg of ice at −20 °C. The piston keeps the pressure constant. (a) The ice is heated slowly to 20 °C at 0.4 kPa. Describe what happens. (b) Repeat at 101.325 kPa. (c) Water at 25 MPa is heated from 300 °C to 450 °C. Does it ever boil? Data: triple point 0.01 °C and 0.6117 kPa; the ice–vapour saturation pressure is 0.402 kPa at −5 °C; psat of water at 20 °C is 2.339 kPa; critical point 373.95 °C and 22.064 MPa.

  1. (a) Compare the pressure with the triple point: 0.4 kPa < 0.6117 kPa. Below the triple-point pressure no liquid can exist, so the ice cannot melt.
  2. The ice warms as a solid until its own vapour pressure reaches 0.4 kPa. The data give 0.402 kPa at −5 °C, so sublimation starts at about −5 °C.
  3. At −5 °C the ice turns straight into vapour at constant temperature, the way water boils at constant temperature. This is the step across the sublimation curve.
  4. The vapour then warms to 20 °C. Check: psat at 20 °C is 2.339 kPa, larger than 0.4 kPa, so the vapour at 20 °C and 0.4 kPa is superheated.
  5. (b) 101.325 kPa is far above 0.6117 kPa, so the path crosses the melting curve. The ice warms to 0 °C, melts at 0 °C, and the liquid warms to 20 °C.
  6. Check the end state: p = 101.325 kPa is larger than psat(20 °C) = 2.339 kPa, so the water at 20 °C is a compressed liquid. Boiling would need 100 °C.
  7. (c) 25 MPa > 22.064 MPa, the critical pressure. Above pc there is no liquid–vapour boundary to cross, so the water never boils.
  8. As T rises past 373.95 °C the density falls smoothly and fast, with no bubbles and no constant-temperature stage. At 450 °C and 25 MPa the water is a supercritical fluid.

Answer. (a) At 0.4 kPa the ice sublimes at about −5 °C and ends as superheated vapour at 20 °C, with no liquid at any stage. (b) At 101.325 kPa it melts at 0 °C and ends as compressed liquid at 20 °C. (c) At 25 MPa there is no boiling: the water passes smoothly into a supercritical fluid.

Exam traps

  • Reading 0.6117 kPa as 0.6117 MPa. The triple-point pressure of water is about 0.006 atm, not 6 atm. A factor of 1000 puts you above atmospheric pressure and gives a completely different phase.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure. Gauges read zero at atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa). The triple point and critical point are absolute pressures.
  • Thinking any gas can be liquefied by squeezing it. Above the critical temperature no pressure produces a separate liquid; carbon dioxide above 304.1 K stays one fluid however hard it is compressed.
  • Confusing the critical point with the triple point. The triple point has three phases; the critical point has one phase where liquid and vapour are indistinguishable.
  • Drawing water’s melting curve leaning to the right. Ice is less dense than liquid water, so water’s melting curve leans to the left: more pressure lowers the melting point slightly. Most other substances lean to the right.

5.3 Gibbs phase rule

The Gibbs phase rule counts how many intensive properties you may choose freely before a system in equilibrium is fixed. For a system without chemical reactions it reads F = C − P + 2, where C is the number of components and P the number of phases present. One line explains why boiling water at a given pressure has only one possible temperature, why the triple point of a pure substance is a single point, and why no pure substance shows four phases together.

You need: phases, saturation and the triple line from 5.1, and the triple point from 5.2.

Components, phases and degrees of freedom

A component is one of the smallest set of chemical species needed to make up every phase of the system. Water, as ice, liquid or vapour, is one component. Moist air is usually counted as two: dry air (its make-up does not change) and water. The number of components is C.

A phase is a region uniform in composition and physical structure, as in 5.1. Ice, liquid water and water vapour are three phases; a mixture of gases is always one phase, because gases mix completely. The number of phases present together is P.

The degrees of freedom F are the number of intensive properties (temperature, pressure and the composition of each phase) that can be changed independently without making a phase appear or disappear. Some syllabi call F the variance of the system.

F = C − P + 2

F is the number of degrees of freedom; C is the number of components; P is the number of phases in equilibrium; the 2 stands for the two intensive properties every phase shares, temperature and pressure. The rule holds for a system in equilibrium with no chemical reactions.

The rule counts intensive properties only. It says nothing about how much of each phase is present. That is why the dryness fraction of wet steam can still change while F = 1: x measures amounts, not an intensive property of either phase.

Where the rule comes from

Count the unknowns and the equations. Every phase has the same T and p in equilibrium: that is 2 unknowns. Each phase also has C mole fractions, but they add to 1, so only C − 1 of them are free; for P phases that makes P(C − 1) more unknowns.

Equilibrium between phases needs one more condition for each component: it must have the same chemical potential, the Gibbs function per mole, in every phase. Otherwise that component would move from one phase to another. With P phases this gives P − 1 equations per component, C(P − 1) in all.

unknowns   = 2 + P(C − 1)
equations  = C(P − 1)
F = unknowns − equations
  = 2 + PC − P − CP + C
  = C − P + 2

Each equation removes one free choice, so the degrees of freedom are the unknowns left over.

For a pure substance C = 1, so F = 3 − P. Because F cannot be negative, P can be at most 3: a pure substance never has four phases in equilibrium. Water has several crystal forms of ice and therefore several triple points, but no state where four phases meet.

Using the rule, and how it fits the state postulate

Superheated steam in a pipe has F = 2, so you may pick 10 bar and 250 °C independently and look up the state. Wet steam in a boiler drum has F = 1: pick 10 bar and the temperature must be 179.9 °C. At the triple point F = 0: nothing can be chosen, which is why it serves as a fixed calibration point.

With two components the count grows. A mixture of nitrogen and oxygen gas has C = 2 and P = 1, so F = 3: pressure, temperature and one mole fraction. Moist air standing over liquid water has C = 2 and P = 2, so F = 2. Fix the total pressure and the temperature, and the vapour pressure in the air is fixed at psat(T). That is why a psychrometric chart drawn for one total pressure has just one saturation line (see 5.10).

The phase rule and the state postulate answer different questions. The phase rule counts the free intensive properties of the phases. The state postulate counts the properties needed to fix the whole state, including how much of each phase there is. For wet steam F = 1, yet two properties are still needed: p fixes T, and x then fixes v, h and s through v = vf + x(vg − vf).

Worked example

Question. Use the phase rule to find the degrees of freedom, and say what you may choose, for: (a) superheated steam in a pipe; (b) wet steam in a boiler drum at 10 bar; (c) ice, water and vapour in a sealed triple-point cell; (d) moist air standing over liquid water in a closed bottle at 30 °C and 101.325 kPa, with psat(30 °C) = 4.247 kPa. Find the humidity ratio of the air in (d). (e) Could ice, a second form of ice, liquid water and vapour coexist?

  1. (a) One component (water), one phase (vapour): F = 1 − 1 + 2 = 2. Pressure and temperature may both be chosen, for example 10 bar and 250 °C.
  2. (b) C = 1, P = 2 (liquid and vapour): F = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1. Choosing p = 10 bar fixes T = Tsat = 179.9 °C. The dryness fraction is still free, because it is an amount, not an intensive property.
  3. (c) C = 1, P = 3: F = 1 − 3 + 2 = 0. Nothing can be chosen; the cell sits at 0.01 °C and 0.6117 kPa.
  4. (d) C = 2 (dry air and water), P = 2 (the gas phase and liquid water): F = 2 − 2 + 2 = 2. Choosing T = 30 °C and p = 101.325 kPa uses both freedoms, so the composition of the air is fixed: the air is saturated and pv = psat(30 °C) = 4.247 kPa.
  5. Humidity ratio of that saturated air: w = 0.622 pv/(p − pv) = 0.622 × 4.247/(101.325 − 4.247) = 2.6416/97.078 = 0.0272 kg of vapour per kg of dry air.
  6. (e) C = 1, P = 4: F = 1 − 4 + 2 = −1. A negative F is impossible, so four phases of a pure substance cannot coexist in equilibrium.

Answer. (a) F = 2: choose p and T. (b) F = 1: choose p, T follows (179.9 °C at 10 bar). (c) F = 0: nothing to choose. (d) F = 2: T and p fix the air as saturated, w = 0.0272 kg/kg dry air. (e) No: F would be −1.

Exam traps

  • Counting the amount of a phase, or the dryness fraction, as a degree of freedom. F counts intensive properties of the phases only.
  • Swapping the signs: F = C + P − 2 is wrong. Test any formula on the triple point of water, where it must give F = 0.
  • Counting a gas mixture as several phases. Gases always mix, so nitrogen plus oxygen is one phase, not two.
  • Saying p alone fixes the state of wet steam. p fixes T only; x or v is still needed to fix v, h and s.

Unknowns: T and p, plus one free mole fraction in each of the two phases: 2 + 2 × 1 = 4. Equations: each of the two components has equal chemical potential in both phases: 2 × 1 = 2. F = 4 − 2 = 2, and C − P + 2 = 2 − 2 + 2 = 2.

5.4 Formation of steam, latent heat and dryness fraction

Water heated at constant pressure passes through three stages: liquid warms to saturation, liquid boils at fixed temperature, and steam superheats. The heat taken in each stage has a name and a symbol. Dryness fraction x tells how much of a wet mixture is vapour, so one number fixes enthalpy, volume and entropy between saturated liquid and saturated vapour. Boiler operators, turbine designers and examiners use these ideas to price steam, check coal use and avoid blade erosion.

You need: phase names and the p-v-T surface from 5.1, and enthalpy h = u + pv from the first law.

Three stages of steam formation

Heat 1 kg of water at 20 °C and 1 atm. The temperature rises to 100 °C. The heat added here is sensible heat, because it changes temperature. At 100 °C the water is a saturated liquid: liquid at its boiling point for that pressure. Its specific enthalpy is hf = 419 kJ/kg. Specific enthalpy h = u + pv (kJ/kg) adds the specific internal energy u to the flow work pv, pressure times specific volume.

More heat at 1 atm does not raise the temperature. Water boils into vapour at 100 °C. This hidden heat is the latent heat of vaporisation hfg. At 1 atm hfg = 2257 kJ/kg. Most of it pulls the molecules apart (ufg ≈ 2087 kJ/kg); the rest, p·vfg = 101.325 × 1.672 ≈ 169 kJ/kg, pushes the atmosphere back as the volume grows. Neither part raises the temperature, so the thermometer stays at 100 °C. When the last liquid disappears, the state is saturated vapour, with hg = 2676 kJ/kg. Further heat gives superheated steam: steam above its saturation temperature. At 150 °C the enthalpy is about 2776 kJ/kg.

hfg = hg − hf

hf is the specific enthalpy of saturated liquid (kJ/kg); hg is the specific enthalpy of saturated vapour (kJ/kg); hfg is the latent heat of vaporisation (kJ/kg). At 1 atm, 2257 = 2676 − 419.

Dryness fraction and mixture rules

Wet steam is saturated liquid and saturated vapour together. Dryness fraction x is the mass fraction that is vapour. Some syllabi call x quality; a percentage must be divided by 100 before use.

x = mg / (mf + mg)

mg is the mass of vapour (kg); mf is the mass of liquid (kg); x is the dryness fraction, no unit. x = 0 is saturated liquid; x = 1 is saturated vapour; 0 < x < 1 is wet steam.

Take 1 kg of wet steam. It contains x kg vapour and (1 − x) kg liquid. The liquid part carries hf per kg. The vapour part carries hfg above hf per kg. So the mixture enthalpy is a straight-line rule between the two saturation values.

h = hf + x hfg

h is the specific enthalpy of wet steam (kJ/kg); x is dryness fraction; hf and hfg are taken at the same pressure. For m kg, total enthalpy H = m h (kJ).

Specific volume v and specific entropy s follow the same linear rule, because each is a mass-weighted property in the wet region.

v = vf + x (vg − vf) and s = sf + x (sg − sf)

v is specific volume (m³/kg); s is specific entropy (kJ/(kg·K)); subscript f means saturated liquid; subscript g means saturated vapour.

Sketches and the critical point

A T-v sketch shows a saturation dome. Under the dome is wet steam. A constant-pressure line is horizontal while boiling, and v grows steadily from vf to vg as x goes from 0 to 1. A T-h sketch has a sloped liquid line, a flat boiling line of length hfg, and a sloped superheat line. The flat line is short at high pressure and disappears at the critical point.

The critical point is about 22.1 MPa and 374 °C for water; see 5.2. There hfg = 0, so there is no latent heat and no flat boiling line. Liquid and vapour have the same density. Below the critical point, hfg falls as pressure rises: 2257 kJ/kg at 1 atm, 2015 kJ/kg at 10 bar, zero at critical.

An h-s sketch is the Mollier diagram. In the wet region, lines of constant x lie under the saturation curve. Reading the chart is a skill for 5.5. A throttling calorimeter finds x from a measured temperature, as 5.6 shows. Vapour power cycles use these same numbers; they are taught with the cycles.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of wet steam at 1 atm has dryness fraction 0.9. At 1 atm hf = 419 kJ/kg and hfg = 2257 kJ/kg. Find the specific enthalpy h, the total enthalpy H, and the masses of liquid and vapour.

  1. Write the data: m = 2 kg, x = 0.9, hf = 419 kJ/kg, hfg = 2257 kJ/kg.
  2. Use the wet-steam rule: h = hf + x hfg = 419 + 0.9 × 2257.
  3. The latent part is 0.9 × 2257 = 2031.3 kJ/kg. So h = 419 + 2031.3 = 2450.3 kJ/kg.
  4. Total enthalpy: H = m h = 2 × 2450.3 = 4900.6 kJ.
  5. Mass of vapour: mg = x m = 0.9 × 2 = 1.8 kg.
  6. Mass of liquid: mf = m − mg = 2 − 1.8 = 0.2 kg. Check: (1 − x)m = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 kg.

Answer. The specific enthalpy is 2450.3 kJ/kg, the total enthalpy is 4900.6 kJ, and the mixture contains 1.8 kg vapour and 0.2 kg liquid.

5.5 Steam tables and Mollier chart

Steam tables and the Mollier chart are lookup tools. The tables list measured specific volume v (m³/kg), enthalpy h (kJ/kg) and entropy s (kJ/kg·K) for water and steam, arranged by pressure or by temperature. The Mollier chart draws the same data on an h–s plane. Boiler designers and turbine testers use both to find every property from a known pair.

You need: 5.1 for the five states of a pure substance, 5.4 for hf, hfg and dryness fraction, and gauge versus absolute pressure from the basic concepts of thermodynamics.

What the tables hold, and which pressure to use

A steam table lists measured properties of water and steam, because no single simple equation covers compressed liquid, wet steam and superheated steam. Each row gives v in m³/kg, h in kJ/kg and s in kJ/kg·K.

Tables use absolute pressure. Absolute pressure is gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure; atmospheric pressure is about 1.013 bar. A boiler gauge showing 10 bar means about 11.01 bar absolute, and 11 bar is what you look up.

pabs = pgauge + patm

pabs is absolute pressure, pgauge the gauge reading, patm the local atmospheric pressure (1.013 bar at sea level); all three in the same unit, bar or kPa.

The saturated table lists pressure with Tsat or temperature with psat. Both carry vf, vg, hf, hfg, hg, sf, sfg, sg. Subscript f marks saturated liquid, g saturated vapour, fg the change during boiling. So hg = hf + hfg and sfg = hfg / Tsat, with Tsat in kelvin.

Inside the dome, pressure and temperature are not independent, so a table row alone cannot fix the state. The dryness fraction x does, through the mixture rules of 5.4: h = hf + x hfg, and the same for v and s.

Reading the superheated table

Above Tsat the steam is superheated and the saturated table stops helping. The superheated table puts pressure down the rows and temperature across the columns. It has entries only where T is above Tsat at that pressure. Each cell gives v, h and s for that (p, T).

  1. Look up Tsat at the given absolute pressure in the saturated table.
  2. If T > Tsat, the state is superheated; open the superheated table.
  3. If T = Tsat, the steam is saturated; use the saturated row with x.
  4. If T < Tsat, the water is a compressed liquid; use the compressed liquid table, or h ≈ hf at that temperature when pressure is small.
  5. Find the pressure row, then the temperature column, and read v, h and s.

Printed tables jump in steps, often 50 K, so a given temperature may fall between two columns. Linear interpolation is accurate enough because each property varies smoothly with T at constant pressure.

y = y₁ + [(T − T₁)/(T₂ − T₁)]·(y₂ − y₁)

y is the property wanted (h, v or s); (T₁, y₁) and (T₂, y₂) are the two table entries either side; T is the given temperature in °C.

The h-s (Mollier) chart

The Mollier chart, also called the h-s diagram, plots enthalpy h in kJ/kg upward and entropy s in kJ/kg·K across. A state is one point and a process is a line, so you read with a ruler instead of a table.

The saturation dome sits in the middle. The left curve is saturated liquid (x = 0), the right curve is saturated vapour (x = 1), and they meet at the critical point near 221 bar and 374 °C.

  • Constant-pressure lines: straight inside the dome, where they are also isotherms, because at constant p the slope dh/ds equals T. In superheated steam they curve upward and steepen.
  • Constant-temperature lines: in superheated steam they bend towards the horizontal at low pressure, where steam behaves like an ideal gas and h depends on T alone.
  • Constant-dryness lines: curves for x = 0.9, 0.95 and so on inside the dome; they place a wet state without a table.

Reading a point on a printed chart by eye is less precise than the table: expect an error of several kJ/kg. That is fine for a sketch or a quick check of an expansion; give final exam answers from the tables. Figure 5.5.1 draws the worked expansion on the chart.

5.6 Steam calorimeters

A throttling calorimeter measures steam dryness without collecting water. Wet steam at pressure p₁ passes through a throttle to a lower pressure p₂ and leaves superheated. The steady flow energy equation, with no work, little heat loss and small kinetic and potential energy changes, gives h₁ = h₂. The steam tables turn the outlet p₂ and T₂ into h₂, and with hf and hfg at the inlet pressure p₁ that gives the dryness x₁. Boiler trials and steam laboratories use it to check the steam sent to engines and turbines. Steam calorimetry belongs to steam laboratory work.

You need dryness fraction, the steam tables, and the steady flow energy equation from the first law for open systems.

Throttling calorimeter

Wet steam at p₁ enters a throttle, a small orifice or valve. It leaves at lower p₂. The process is throttling: no work, negligible heat transfer, small kinetic and potential energy changes. The steady flow energy equation gives h₁ = h₂. h₁ is inlet specific enthalpy, kJ/kg; h₂ is outlet specific enthalpy, kJ/kg.

x₁ = (h₂ − hf1)/hfg1

hf1 is saturated liquid enthalpy at inlet pressure p₁, kJ/kg; hfg1 is latent heat at p₁, kJ/kg; x₁ is inlet dryness fraction.

To use this, the outlet must be superheated. Then p₂ and T₂ fix h₂ in the superheated table. At inlet, h₁ = hf1 + x₁ hfg1. Substituting h₁ = h₂ gives the formula above.

  1. Read inlet pressure p₁, outlet pressure p₂ and outlet temperature T₂.
  2. Confirm the outlet is superheated at p₂ and T₂.
  3. Find h₂ from the superheated steam table.
  4. Find hf1 and hfg1 at p₁ from the saturated steam table.
  5. Compute x₁ = (h₂ − hf1)/hfg1.

If the outlet is still wet, h₂ depends on an unknown outlet dryness x₂. The equation h₁ = h₂ then has two unknowns, so the throttling calorimeter is invalid. The limit is easy to compute. With p₂ = 1 bar the outlet is just dry when h₂ = hg = 2675.0 kJ/kg, so at p₁ = 10 bar the wettest steam the instrument can read is x₁ = (2675.0 − 762.5)/2014.6 = 0.949. Steam wetter than about 95 per cent needs the separating calorimeter.

Separating calorimeter and limits

A separating calorimeter works mechanically. Steam passes through baffles that turn it sharply; water droplets hit the walls and drain into a measuring vessel. The steam that leaves is nearly dry; it is condensed and weighed. Let mw be the separated water mass and ms the mass of steam that passed, over the same time. Then xs = ms/(ms + mw).

This instrument suits very wet steam, but it is less exact: some fine droplets pass with the steam, so xs overstates the dryness. A combined separating and throttling calorimeter first removes most of the water; the throttling part then measures the dryness xt of the steam that passed. The dryness of the main steam is x = xs × xt.

  • Throttling calorimeter: outlet must be superheated; needs accurate p₁, p₂ and T₂.
  • Separating calorimeter: direct mass measurement; used when steam is very wet.
  • Combined unit: separating part handles bulk water, throttling part gives fine dryness.
  • Both need steady flow and a representative steam sample.

Worked example

Question. Wet steam at 10 bar is throttled to 1 bar and 130 °C. At 10 bar, hf = 762.5 kJ/kg and hfg = 2014.6 kJ/kg. At 1 bar and 130 °C, h = 2736.7 kJ/kg. Find the inlet dryness fraction x₁.

  1. Check the outlet state: at 1 bar the saturation temperature is 99.6 °C, so 130 °C is superheated by about 30 K.

  • Applying h₁ = h₂ with large kinetic or potential energy changes. The throttle must be well insulated and changes small.

5.7 Ideal and real gases; equations of state

The ideal gas law pv = RT is the simplest equation of state: it links pressure, molar volume and temperature through one constant. It is accurate for air, nitrogen and carbon dioxide near room temperature and atmospheric pressure. It fails near saturation and at high pressure, where molecules crowd together and pull on each other. Real-gas equations of state repair that with a molecular volume and an attraction term.

You need: state, property and equilibrium from the basic concepts, pv = RT and specific heats from the first law, and the phase-change picture in 5.1.

The ideal gas model

An equation of state is any relation among pressure, volume and temperature for a fixed mass. The ideal gas is the simplest such model. Its molecules are treated as point masses: they occupy no volume of their own, they exert no force on one another except at the instant of a collision, and every collision is perfectly elastic. A gas behaves this way because at ordinary pressure it is mostly empty space.

pv = Ru T

p is absolute pressure in Pa; v is molar volume V/n in m³/mol; Ru is the universal gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K, never °C.

Engineers usually work per kilogram, not per mole. Dividing Ru by molar mass M gives the specific gas constant R, and the law becomes pV = mRT. For air M = 0.02897 kg/mol, so R = 8.314/0.02897 = 287 J/(kg·K) = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). For carbon dioxide M = 0.044 kg/mol, so R = 8.314/0.044 = 188.9 J/(kg·K).

pV = mRT, R = Ru/M

V is total volume in m³; m is mass in kg; R is the specific gas constant in J/(kg·K); Ru = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the universal gas constant; M is molar mass in kg/mol.

The same law defines a temperature scale. In a constant-volume gas thermometer p rises in proportion to T, so T = 273.16 × (p/ptr), with ptr measured at the triple point of water (see 5.2). At low pressure this scale agrees with the Kelvin scale of the second law.

Why real gases deviate

Two facts spoil the model. First, a molecule has a finite size, so the space available to it is less than the container volume. Second, molecules attract each other at short range, so a molecule about to strike the wall is pulled back and hits with less force. The first effect raises pressure; the second lowers it.

Deviations grow as the gas is compressed towards saturation, or pushed to high pressure at a temperature well above the critical temperature. At 1 atm and 300 K, carbon dioxide is within a fraction of a percent of ideal; at 5 MPa it is off by about 30 percent (Z ≈ 0.7). Near the saturated vapour line the ideal law can be wrong by tens of percent, because molecules are close to condensing.

A real gas, also called a non-ideal gas in some syllabi, therefore needs a better equation of state. The measure of the error is the compressibility factor Z = pv/(Ru T), covered in 5.8. For an ideal gas Z = 1 at every state.

The van der Waals equation

Van der Waals repaired the ideal law with two constants. The constant b subtracts the volume the molecules themselves occupy, so the free volume becomes (v − b). The term a/v² restores the pressure lost because attraction pulls the molecules inward, away from the wall. Adding a/v² to the measured p gives the pressure the gas would exert if attraction were absent.

(p + a/v²)(v − b) = Ru T

p is pressure in Pa; v is molar volume in m³/mol; T is in K; Ru = 8.314 J/(mol·K); a is the attraction constant in Pa·m⁶/mol²; b is the excluded volume in m³/mol, roughly four times the molecular volume.

Rearranged for pressure it is easier to use: p = Ru T/(v − b) − a/v². The constants can be fitted to measured data, or found from the critical point, where the critical isotherm has a horizontal inflection: (∂p/∂v)T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)T = 0. Solving these two conditions with the equation itself gives vc = 3b and the two results below.

a = 27 Ru² Tc² / (64 pc), b = Ru Tc / (8 pc)

Tc and pc are the critical temperature (K) and pressure (Pa); Ru = 8.314 J/(mol·K). For CO₂, Tc = 304.1 K and pc = 7.38 MPa give a = 0.3654 Pa·m⁶/mol² and b = 4.28 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol, close to the fitted values in the table. The same algebra predicts the critical compressibility pc vc/(Ru Tc) = 3/8 = 0.375 for every gas.

The virial form Z = 1 + B(T)/v + C(T)/v² + … is a third family: a power series whose coefficients come from data. Examinations usually ask for van der Waals with its constants supplied.

Worked example

Question. One mole of carbon dioxide is held at 300 K in a tank of volume 0.024 m³. Take Ru = 8.314 J/(mol·K), a = 0.3640 Pa·m⁶/mol² and b = 4.267 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol. Find the pressure from the ideal gas law and from the van der Waals equation, in kPa.

  1. Molar volume: v = V/n = 0.024/1 = 0.024 m³/mol.
  2. Ideal gas law: p = RuT/v = (8.314 × 300)/0.024 = 2494.2/0.024 = 103 925 Pa.
  3. Van der Waals, first term: RuT/(v − b) = 2494.2/(0.024 − 0.00004267) = 2494.2/0.02395733 = 104 110.1 Pa.
  4. Attraction term: a/v² = 0.3640/(0.024)² = 0.3640/0.000576 = 631.9 Pa.
  5. Van der Waals pressure: p = 104 110.1 − 631.9 = 103 478.2 Pa.

Answer. Ideal p = 103.93 kPa and van der Waals p = 103.48 kPa; attraction lowers the pressure by about 0.45 kPa.

Exam traps

  • Using the total volume V in place of v in the van der Waals equation. There v is molar volume V/n, so for 4 mol in 0.024 m³ you must put v = 0.006 m³/mol.
  • Mixing units of a, b and R. If a is in Pa·m⁶/mol² and b in m³/mol, R must be 8.314 J/(mol·K) and p comes out in Pa, not kPa.
  • Putting T in °C. Both ideal and van der Waals equations need absolute temperature, so add 273.15 before substituting.
  • Using Ru where R is wanted. Divide by molar mass for per-kilogram work; 8.314 in pV = mRT with m in kg gives wrong numbers.
  • Claiming the van der Waals equation is exact near the critical point. It is a two-constant model, good for trends, and the compressibility chart of 5.8 is preferred for accurate work.

5.8 Compressibility factor and compressibility chart

Real gases do not obey pv = RT exactly. The compressibility factor Z = pv/RT measures the correction: Z is 1 for an ideal gas and moves away from 1 near the critical point and at high pressure. Engineers read Z from a generalized chart using reduced pressure and temperature, then get v = ZRT/p. This matters for filling a CNG cylinder at 200 bar, sizing gas pipelines, and checking when the ideal-gas answer is safe.

You need: 5.7 ideal and real gases and 5.2 critical properties.

Z and reduced coordinates

A real gas approaches ideal behaviour only at low pressure and high temperature. The compressibility factor Z measures the gap. At a given pressure and temperature, Z = pv/(RT). At the same p and T, an ideal gas has videal = RT/p, so Z = vactual/videal. For an ideal gas, Z = 1. For steam near saturation, Z can be well below 1. For nitrogen at ordinary conditions, Z is close to 1.

Z = pv/(RT) = v/videal

p is absolute pressure in Pa or kPa; v is specific volume in m³/kg; R is the gas constant in J/(kg·K) or kJ/(kg·K); T is absolute temperature in kelvin; videal = RT/p.

To compare gases, scale by critical properties: pR = p/pc, TR = T/Tc, and vR = v/vc. Here pc and Tc are the critical pressure and temperature from 5.2, and vc is the critical specific volume. These ratios are dimensionless. Some charts use pseudoreduced volume vR′ = v/(RTc/pc) instead. The principle of corresponding states says gases with the same pR and TR have approximately the same Z. That is why one chart works for many gases. Some syllabi call it the generalized compressibility chart.

Written with vR = v/vc, the van der Waals equation of 5.7 loses its constants: (pR + 3/vR²)(3vR − 1) = 8TR. This reduced equation of state is the simplest argument for corresponding states. It also predicts the same critical compressibility Zc = pcvc/(RTc) = 0.375 for every gas; measured values are lower, about 0.29 for nitrogen and methane, 0.27 for carbon dioxide and 0.23 for water, which is why charts built from data beat the reduced van der Waals equation.

Reading the chart

The chart plots pR on the horizontal axis and Z on the vertical axis. Curves are lines of constant TR. At very low pR, every curve meets Z = 1: the gas is ideal. At high pR, the curves spread apart. Near TR = 1 the curve dips sharply to low Z because attractive forces are strong close to the critical point. For TR below 1 the dip ends where the saturation line cuts the curve. At high TR, Z stays near 1 or rises above 1 as pressure grows. The chart is approximate; read Z to two or three significant figures.

  1. Find pc and Tc for the gas.
  2. Use absolute pressure and kelvin temperature.
  3. Compute pR = p/pc and TR = T/Tc.
  4. Locate the point on the chart and read Z; interpolate between TR curves if needed.
  5. Compute v = ZRT/p with matching units.

Do not use gauge pressure or Celsius in pR and TR. The chart does not need the gas identity beyond pc and Tc, but it is less accurate for polar gases such as water and ammonia. For those, use tables or a suitable equation of state. Mixtures need pseudocritical properties (Kay’s rule, in 5.9).

Using Z and its limits

Once Z is known, v = ZRT/p. Use p in kPa with R in kJ/(kg·K), because 1 kJ = 1 kPa·m³. For total volume, V = m v. The same correction appears as pV = mZRT. If Z > 1, the gas is less compressible than ideal (it occupies more volume, repulsive forces dominate). If Z < 1, it is more compressible than ideal (it occupies less volume, attractive forces dominate). At low pR or high TR, the ideal-gas answer is close. At pR near 1 and TR near 1, the ideal-gas law can be badly wrong.

v = ZRT/p

v is specific volume in m³/kg; Z is the compressibility factor from the chart; R is the gas constant in kJ/(kg·K); T is kelvin; p is absolute pressure in kPa.

The chart is a hand tool for quick estimates. Detailed process design uses cubic equations of state or reference databases, as noted in 5.7. The worked examples apply the chart route to a CNG cylinder, where Z is well below 1, and to nitrogen, where it is close to 1.

5.9 Gas mixtures: Dalton's and Amagat's laws

In an ideal gas mixture the molecules ignore each other: no attraction, no reaction, and each gas fills the whole vessel at the mixture temperature. Dalton splits the total pressure into partial pressures pi = yip; Amagat splits the total volume into partial volumes Vi = yiV. Both use the mole fraction yi as the share. Engineers need this for air, combustion products, LPG–air and natural gas lines, where a compressor or a pipe must be sized for the mixture, not for one gas.

You need: the ideal gas law pV = nRuT and the kmol from 5.7 Ideal and real gases, plus pressure and the mole concept from the basic concepts.

Mole fraction and mixture molar mass

An ideal gas mixture is a set of gases sharing one vessel at the same temperature T and pressure p, with no chemical reaction and no forces between molecules. Amounts are in kilomoles: one kmol of gas i has mass Mi kg, and the total is n = n1 + n2 + ….

The mole fraction of component i is yi = ni/n. It is dimensionless, and Σ yi = 1. The mass fraction wi = mi/m is a different number; for air, yO2 ≈ 0.21 but wO2 ≈ 0.23. The two are linked by wi = yiMi/Mmix.

A mixture behaves as one gas with molar mass Mmix = m/n = Σ yiMi and gas constant Rmix = Ru/Mmix, where Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K) is the universal gas constant.

Mmix = Σ yiMi Rmix = Ru/Mmix

yi = ni/n is the mole fraction, dimensionless; Mi is the molar mass of component i in kg/kmol; Mmix is the mixture molar mass in kg/kmol; Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K); Rmix is the mixture gas constant in kJ/(kg·K).

Dalton's law: partial pressures

Dalton's model takes the mixture apart. Put component i alone in the same volume V at the same temperature T. It behaves as an ideal gas and presses on the walls with the partial pressure pi = niRuT/V. Dalton's law states that the mixture pressure is the sum of these partial pressures: p = Σ pi.

Divide pi by p = nRuT/V and the temperature and volume cancel: pi = yip. The partial pressure is a share of the total, and the share is the mole fraction. Using the mass fraction here is the most common error in this chapter.

The V and T in p = nRuT/V belong to the whole mixture, with Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K) giving p in kPa. In the example below, oxygen and nitrogen share one 2 m³ tank; nitrogen is not squeezed into a smaller volume of its own.

Amagat's law: partial volumes

Amagat's model keeps the mixture pressure p and temperature T and asks a different question: what volume Vi would component i occupy alone at p and T? For an ideal gas, Vi = niRuT/p = yiV. Amagat's law states that the partial volumes add to the vessel volume: V = Σ Vi.

The same mole fraction appears in both laws: pi/p = Vi/V = yi. Dalton and Amagat are two ways of bookkeeping the same ideal-gas model, not two different physical pictures. Use Dalton when a pressure gauge is read. Use Amagat when a gas analyser reports percentages by volume, because for ideal gases volume per cent equals mole per cent.

Mixture properties follow the Gibbs–Dalton rule: umix = Σ yiui and hmix = Σ yihi per kmol, each component property taken at the mixture temperature; per kg the weights are the mass fractions, cp,mix = Σ wicp,i. Mixing is irreversible, so entropy rises even when T and p do not change. For ideal gases that start separate at the same T and p, ΔS = −Ru Σ ni ln yi, which is positive because every yi < 1.

Some old notes write Amalgam's law for Amagat's law; the correct name is Amagat. Both laws assume ideal gases. At 200 bar, or near condensation, they fail, and a real mixture needs the compressibility factor of 5.8. Kay's rule gives the pseudocritical values to enter the chart with: T′c = Σ yiTc,i and p′c = Σ yipc,i.

Worked example

Question. A rigid tank of 2 m³ holds 0.8 kg of oxygen and 2.8 kg of nitrogen at 300 K. Take Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K), MO2 = 32 kg/kmol and MN2 = 28 kg/kmol.   1. Moles: nO2 = 0.8/32 = 0.025 kmol and nN2 = 2.8/28 = 0.100 kmol, so n = 0.125 kmol.
  2. Mole fractions: yO2 = 0.025/0.125 = 0.2 and yN2 = 0.100/0.125 = 0.8; check 0.2 + 0.8 = 1.00. (Mass fractions are different: wO2 = 0.8/3.6 = 0.222.)
  3. Total pressure: p = nRuT/V = 0.125 × 8.314 × 300/2 = 311.78/2 = 155.9 kPa.
  4. Dalton: pO2 = 0.2 × 155.9 = 31.2 kPa and pN2 = 0.8 × 155.9 = 124.7 kPa; 31.2 + 124.7 = 155.9 kPa.
  5. Amagat: VO2 = 0.2 × 2 = 0.4 m³ and VN2 = 0.8 × 2 = 1.6 m³; together 2 m³.

Exam traps

  • pi = yip, never pi = wip. Air is 0.21 oxygen by mole but 0.23 by mass, so the mass fraction overstates pO2 by about 10 %.
  • Partial pressure uses the mixture volume V and the mixture temperature T. Putting a component's own volume into piV = niRuT is the classic mistake.
  • Mixing unit sets. Ru = 8.314 works with kPa, m³ and kmol, or with Pa, m³ and mol. Putting MPa with m³, or mol with kmol, shifts the answer by a factor of 1000.
  • The mole fractions must add to 1.00. If an analysis sums to 0.95, a component is missing, not a rounding error.
  • Amagat's partial volume is not the volume of the gas before mixing. It is the volume that gas would occupy alone at the final p and T.

5.10 Psychrometry: humidity and psychrometric chart

Moist air is a mixture of dry air and water vapour. Its state decides whether a room feels comfortable, whether clothes dry on a line, and how much water a cooling tower evaporates. Psychrometry gives four linked measures: dry-bulb temperature, humidity ratio, relative humidity and dew point. The psychrometric chart plots them together so heating, cooling and drying paths can be read without solving equations each time.

You need 5.9 Dalton's law for partial pressures and 5.5 steam tables for psat(T).

Dry air, vapour and humidity ratio

Moist air is dry air plus water vapour. At ordinary pressures both behave as ideal gases, so Dalton's law applies: the total pressure p is the sum of the dry-air partial pressure pa and the vapour partial pressure pv. The dry-bulb temperature T is the temperature read by an ordinary thermometer in the air, with no water on its bulb.

φ = pv / psat(T)

φ is relative humidity, from 0 to 1; pv is the water-vapour partial pressure in kPa; psat(T) is the saturation pressure of water at the dry-bulb temperature T, read from steam tables.

Relative humidity is a ratio of pressures, not a ratio of masses. It says how close the vapour is to condensing. φ = 1 means saturated air.

w = 0.622 pv / (p − pv)

w is the humidity ratio in kg water vapour per kg dry air; 0.622 = 18.015/28.97 is the molar mass ratio; p is total pressure in kPa; pv is vapour partial pressure in kPa.

Here p − pv is pa, the dry-air pressure. The factor 0.622 comes from the molar masses: 18.015 g/mol for water and 28.97 g/mol for dry air. Using total p in the denominator instead of pa is a common error.

h = 1.005 T + w (2500 + 1.86 T)

h is the enthalpy of moist air in kJ per kg dry air; T is dry-bulb temperature in °C; 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) is the specific heat of dry air; 2500 kJ/kg is the latent heat of water at 0 °C; 1.86 kJ/(kg·K) is the specific heat of water vapour.

The enthalpy of moist air is quoted per kg of dry air. The first term is the dry-air enthalpy; the bracket is the vapour enthalpy. This approximation is accurate enough for comfort air-conditioning.

Two more measures appear in exam questions. The degree of saturation μ = w/ws compares w with the humidity ratio of saturated air at the same temperature; it is close to φ but not equal. The specific volume of moist air, per kg of dry air, is v = RaT/pa with Ra = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K) and T in kelvin.

Dew point and wet-bulb temperature

The dew point Tdp is the temperature at which the vapour just becomes saturated at the same pv. It satisfies psat(Tdp) = pv. If moist air is cooled at constant pressure and constant w, the first change is a fall in T; the dew point is reached when the relative humidity becomes 100%. Further cooling makes liquid water condense. This is why a cold water bottle sweats in a Chennai room.

The wet-bulb temperature Twb is read by a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in a wet cloth and exposed to moving air. Water evaporates from the cloth and cools the bulb. The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the lower the wet-bulb reading. For unsaturated air, Tdp < Twb < Tdb. At saturation all three are equal.

Wet-bulb and dew point answer different questions. Dew point is reached by cooling without evaporation. Wet-bulb is reached by evaporating water into the air and cooling it. For air-water vapour the wet-bulb temperature is close to the adiabatic saturation temperature, which is why the chart uses one line family for both.

Reading the psychrometric chart

The chart plots humidity ratio w on the vertical axis against dry-bulb temperature T on the horizontal axis, at one fixed total pressure, usually 101.325 kPa. The curved saturation line is φ = 100%. Above it is fog or condensation; below it is unsaturated moist air.

Constant relative humidity lines curve down from the saturation line; the 60% line lies below the 100% line. Constant enthalpy and constant wet-bulb lines run nearly parallel, sloping down to the right. The state point fixes all properties: read w on the vertical scale and T on the horizontal scale.

Sensible heating moves the state point horizontally to the right: w stays fixed, T rises, and φ falls. Cooling moves it horizontally left; when the saturation line is reached, further cooling follows the curve and water condenses out. Adding water vapour moves the point upward: with a fine water spray the air also cools slightly as the water evaporates, while adding steam raises the temperature. The chart turns these paths into straight-line steps, which is why it survives in air-conditioning work.

Worked example

Question. Moist air at 30 °C dry-bulb and 60% relative humidity flows at a total pressure of 101.325 kPa. The saturation pressure of water is 4.247 kPa at 30 °C, 2.488 kPa at 21 °C and 2.645 kPa at 22 °C. Find the vapour partial pressure, the humidity ratio, the degree of saturation, the dew point, the enthalpy and the specific volume per kg of dry air.

  1. Vapour partial pressure: pv = φ × psat(30 °C) = 0.60 × 4.247 = 2.548 kPa.
  2. Dry-air partial pressure: pa = p − pv = 101.325 − 2.548 = 98.777 kPa.
  3. Humidity ratio: w = 0.622 × 2.548/98.777 = 0.01605 kg/kg dry air.
  4. Saturated air at 30 °C: ws = 0.622 × 4.247/(101.325 − 4.247) = 0.02721 kg/kg, so μ = 0.01605/0.02721 = 0.590, a little below φ = 0.60.
  5. Dew point: psat(Tdp) = 2.548 kPa lies between 21 °C and 22 °C: Tdp = 21 + (2.548 − 2.488)/(2.645 − 2.488) = 21 + 0.060/0.157 = 21.4 °C.
  6. Enthalpy: h = 1.005 × 30 + 0.01605 × (2500 + 1.86 × 30) = 30.15 + 0.01605 × 2555.8 = 30.15 + 41.02 = 71.2 kJ/kg dry air.
  7. Specific volume: v = RaT/pa = 0.287 × 303.15/98.777 = 0.881 m³/kg dry air.

Answer. pv = 2.548 kPa, w = 0.0160 kg/kg dry air, μ = 0.590, Tdp ≈ 21.4 °C, h ≈ 71.2 kJ/kg dry air and v ≈ 0.881 m³/kg dry air.

Exam traps

  • Using total pressure p instead of dry-air pressure p − pv in the humidity-ratio formula. The error is small at low humidity but large near saturation.
  • Treating relative humidity as a mass ratio. φ compares pv with psat at the same dry-bulb temperature.
  • Taking the wet-bulb temperature as the dew point. They coincide only at saturation.
  • Using psat at the wet-bulb or dew-point temperature when the question gives φ at the dry-bulb temperature. Use psat(Tdb).
  • Forgetting that humidity ratio is per kg of dry air, not per kg of moist air.

Takeaways

  1. All equilibrium states of a pure substance lie on its p-v-T surface, and the two-phase region is only a surface or a line, not a volume.
  2. The triple point is a single point for a given substance; the critical point closes the liquid-vapour curve and removes the difference between liquid and vapour.
  3. The Gibbs phase rule F = C − P + 2 counts the free intensive properties: two for one phase of water, one on a saturation curve, none at the triple point.
  4. Inside the wet region pressure and temperature are not independent — fixing one fixes the other, so a second property such as x is needed to fix the state.
  5. Enthalpy of wet steam follows h = hf + x·hfg, and the same mixing rule applies to v, s and u with vf, vg and sf, sg.
  6. Steam tables and the Mollier chart are read, not solved; they replace the missing equation of state for water in exam problems.
  7. A throttling calorimeter relies on the fact that h is constant across a throttle, and it works only when the steam is dry enough to leave superheated.
  8. Real gases depart from pv = RT near saturation and at high pressure; Z = pv/RT, read from the generalised chart with reduced properties, quantifies that departure.
  9. In an ideal gas mixture each component behaves as if alone in the vessel, so the mixture calculations reduce to weighted sums using mole fractions.
  10. Moist air is treated as an ideal gas mixture of dry air and water vapour, which is why the psychrometric chart carries two independent temperatures.

Analyze Explain physically why a real gas deviates from pv = RT. Derive the van der Waals constants a and b in terms of critical properties and show how the equation predicts the critical point. 12 marks

Points to cover. Two assumptions break down: molecules have finite volume, and they attract each other; b corrects the volume, a/v² corrects the pressure; At the critical point the isotherm has an inflection: (∂p/∂v)T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)T = 0; Solving gives vc = 3b, Tc = 8a/(27Rb), pc = a/(27b²); Therefore a = 27R²Tc²/(64pc) and b = RTc/(8pc); Predicted Zc = 3/8 = 0.375 for all substances, against about 0.23 to 0.29 measured.

Model answer.

  1. Why real gases deviate: the ideal model treats molecules as points that do not attract. At high density both assumptions fail. Molecules fill part of the volume, so the free volume is less than v (this raises p), and neighbours pull a molecule back as it approaches the wall (this lowers p).
  2. van der Waals’ corrections, per mole: replace v by (v − b) and add a/v² to the measured pressure: (p + a/v²)(v − b) = RT, or p = RT/(v − b) − a/v².
  3. Critical conditions: the critical isotherm has a horizontal inflection at the critical point, so (∂p/∂v)T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)T = 0 there.
  4. Differentiate at constant T:
    (∂p/∂v)T  = −RT/(v − b)² + 2a/v³ = 0      … (1)
(∂²p/∂v²)T = 2RT/(v − b)³ − 6a/v⁴ = 0     … (2)
  5. From (1): RT = 2a(v − b)²/v³. Put this in (2): 4a/[v³(v − b)] = 6a/v⁴, so 4v = 6(v − b) and vc = 3b.
  6. Then RTc = 2a(2b)²/(27b³) = 8a/(27b), so Tc = 8a/(27Rb).
  7. And pc = RTc/(vc − b) − a/vc² = [8a/(27b)]/(2b) − a/(9b²) = 4a/(27b²) − 3a/(27b²) = a/(27b²).
  8. Solve for the constants from the measured Tc and pc: from Tc/pc = 8b/R, b = RTc/(8pc); then a = 27b²pc = 27R²Tc²/(64pc).
  9. For CO₂ (Tc = 304.1 K, pc = 7.38 MPa, R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)): b = 8.314 × 304.1/(8 × 7.38 × 10⁶) = 4.28 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol and a = 27 × 8.314² × 304.1²/(64 × 7.38 × 10⁶) = 0.3654 Pa·m⁶/mol², close to the fitted 4.267 × 10⁻⁵ and 0.3640.
  10. Predicted critical compressibility: Zc = pcvc/(RTc) = [a/(27b²)] × 3b/[8a/(27b)] = 3/8 = 0.375 for every gas. Measured values are lower (0.29 for nitrogen, 0.27 for CO₂, 0.23 for water), a known weakness of the two-constant equation near the critical point.
  11. Check: putting the CO₂ values back, Tc = 8a/(27Rb) = 8 × 0.3654/(27 × 8.314 × 4.28 × 10⁻⁵) = 2.923/0.009608 = 304.2 K, which returns the critical temperature.

Glossary

Amagat's law
The volume of an ideal gas mixture equals the sum of the volumes each component would occupy at the mixture pressure and temperature.
Component
One of the smallest set of chemical species needed to make up every phase of a system; water is one component, moist air two.
Degree of saturation
μ = w/ws, the humidity ratio divided by that of saturated air at the same temperature and pressure.
Degree of superheat
The difference between the actual temperature of superheated steam and the saturation temperature at the same pressure.
Degrees of freedom
The number of intensive properties that can be varied independently without changing the number of phases; F in the Gibbs phase rule.
Dew-point temperature
The temperature at which moist air becomes saturated when cooled at constant pressure and humidity ratio.
Dry air
Air with the water vapour removed; it is the reference mass for humidity ratio calculations.
Dry saturated steam
Steam with x = 1, containing no liquid droplets, at the saturation temperature for its pressure.
Dry-bulb temperature
The ordinary temperature of moist air measured by a thermometer with a dry bulb.
Enthalpy of evaporation
hfg, the heat needed to convert 1 kg of saturated liquid into saturated vapour at constant pressure.
Partial pressure
The pressure a single component of a gas mixture would exert if it alone filled the same volume at the same temperature.
Principle of corresponding states
All substances at the same reduced pressure and temperature have nearly the same compressibility factor Z.
Quality
Another name for dryness fraction; the vapour mass fraction in a saturated liquid-vapour mixture.
Real gas
A gas whose behaviour departs from pv = RT because its molecules have volume and interact.
Reduced pressure
pr = p/pc, the pressure divided by the critical pressure of the substance.
Reduced temperature
Tr = T/Tc, the absolute temperature divided by the critical temperature.
Relative humidity
φ = pv/psat at the dry-bulb temperature, the ratio of actual vapour pressure to the saturation value.
Saturation pressure
The pressure at which a pure substance boils at a given temperature.
Saturation temperature
The boiling or condensing temperature that belongs to a given pressure for a pure substance.
Sensible heat
Heat that changes the temperature of a single phase without changing the phase, equal to m·c·ΔT for a constant specific heat.
Subcooling
The amount by which a liquid is cooler than its saturation temperature at the given pressure.
Supercritical fluid
A substance above both its critical temperature and critical pressure, where liquid and vapour cannot be told apart.
Superheated steam
Steam above its saturation temperature for the given pressure; it behaves roughly as a gas.
Throttling
A steady-flow expansion through a restriction with no work and negligible heat transfer, so enthalpy stays constant.
Wet steam
A mixture of saturated liquid droplets and saturated vapour in equilibrium, with 0 < x < 1.
Wet-bulb temperature
The temperature reached by a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in wet cloth and exposed to moving air.
