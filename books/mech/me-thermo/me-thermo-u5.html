5.1 Pure substance, phase change and p-v-T surface

Water in a piston–cylinder at constant pressure, heated slowly, does not warm up steadily from ice-cold to red-hot. It warms, then boils at a fixed temperature while swelling enormously, then warms again. The same five states — compressed liquid, saturated liquid, wet mixture, saturated vapour, superheated vapour — appear in every pure substance, and their names, volumes and masses follow simple rules. Boiler designers, refrigeration engineers and steam-table users all work inside these states daily. This chapter names them, draws the constant-pressure path on the T-v and p-v planes, and collects every equilibrium state onto one surface in p-v-T space.

You need: system, property and state, and specific volume as an intensive property, from Unit 1.

Pure substance, phase and saturation

A pure substance is matter whose chemical composition is the same everywhere in the system. Water is a pure substance; so is nitrogen. A glass of ice and liquid water is still a pure substance, because both phases are H₂O. Wet air is not, because the liquid water and the moist gas have different compositions.

A phase is a quantity of matter uniform in chemical composition and physical structure. Ice is one phase, liquid water a second, water vapour a third. A system may hold one, two or three of them at once.

Saturation is the state at which a pure substance can change phase with no change in temperature or pressure. At 101.325 kPa water boils at 100 °C; that is a saturation pair. Inside saturation, pressure and temperature are not independent — fix one and the other is fixed (see Unit 4, Clausius–Clapeyron equation).

Five names cover the behaviour. Compressed liquid, also called subcooled liquid: colder than saturation at that pressure. Saturated liquid: exactly at saturation, about to boil, quality x = 0. Wet mixture: liquid and vapour together, 0 < x < 1. Saturated vapour: all vapour, about to condense, x = 1. Superheated vapour: hotter than saturation at that pressure.

Heating at constant pressure: the five states in order

Take 2 kg of water in a piston–cylinder at 101.325 kPa and 20 °C, and add heat slowly while the piston holds the pressure constant.

  1. 20 °C to 100 °C: compressed liquid. The water expands only slightly, from v_f = 0.001002 m³/kg to 0.001043 m³/kg. The heat added raises temperature; it is sensible heat.
  2. At 100 °C: saturated liquid, x = 0. Any further heat starts boiling and the temperature stops rising.
  3. Boiling: the temperature stays at 100 °C while the volume grows about 1600 times, from 0.001043 to 1.673 m³/kg. This is the wet region; the heat added is latent heat.
  4. At 1.673 m³/kg the last droplet evaporates: saturated vapour, x = 1. More heat now raises the temperature again and the vapour becomes superheated.

v = v_f + x (v_g − v_f)

v is the specific volume of the wet mixture in m³/kg; v_f and v_g are the saturated liquid and saturated vapour specific volumes at the same pressure, in m³/kg; x is the dryness fraction, x = m_g / m, the vapour mass divided by the total mass, with 0 ≤ x ≤ 1.

On the T-v plane the boiling stretch is a horizontal line at T_sat; on the p-v plane it is a horizontal line at p_sat. Both stretches end on the saturation dome, the boundary of the wet region. Heat accounting for the three stages is in 5.3; the two ends of the dome are in 5.2.

The p-v-T surface and the triple line

A state needs two independent intensive properties, so all equilibrium states of a pure substance lie on one surface in p-v-T space. Compressed liquid and superheated vapour are single-phase sheets. The two-phase region is a ruled surface that joins the saturated liquid line to the saturated vapour line.

Flatten the surface onto the p-v plane and the wet region becomes the saturation dome, crossed by a horizontal line for each constant-pressure heating. On the T-v plane the wet region is bounded by the saturated liquid and saturated vapour lines; each constant-pressure heating is a horizontal segment at that pressure's T_sat. It is one object seen twice.

Ice, liquid water and vapour meet at the triple point of water: 0.01 °C and 0.6117 kPa. In p-v-T space the three two-phase surfaces meet along the triple line at that fixed temperature and pressure, because the volume may take any value between the three saturated volumes. Below the triple pressure water sublimes: ice turns straight to vapour.

  • Steam plants: the boiler drum and the condenser hold wet mixtures, so quality x sets what the plant can extract.
  • Refrigeration: the throttling valve leaves a wet mixture; the evaporator boils it off, usually to saturated or superheated vapour. The quality after throttling sets cooling capacity.
  • LNG storage: cryogenic tanks hold a boiling liquid near atmospheric pressure; a pressure drop causes flash boiling.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of water at 101.325 kPa has dryness fraction x = 0.25. At this pressure v_f = 0.001043 m³/kg and v_g = 1.673 m³/kg. Find the specific volume of the mixture, the total volume, and the mass of liquid and of vapour.

  1. Given: m = 2 kg, x = 0.25, v_f = 0.001043 m³/kg, v_g = 1.673 m³/kg at 101.325 kPa. Since 0 < x < 1, the water is a wet mixture.
  2. Specific volume of the mixture: v = v_f + x (v_g − v_f).
  3. Latent part: v_g − v_f = 1.673 − 0.001043 = 1.671957 m³/kg.
  4. x (v_g − v_f) = 0.25 × 1.671957 = 0.417989 m³/kg.
  5. v = 0.001043 + 0.417989 = 0.419032 m³/kg.
  6. Total volume: V = m v = 2 × 0.419032 = 0.838064 m³.
  7. Vapour mass: m_g = x m = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 kg. Liquid mass: m_f = (1 − x) m = 1.5 kg.
  8. Check: m_f + m_g = 1.5 + 0.5 = 2 kg, which matches the given mass.

Answer. v = 0.4190 m³/kg, V = 0.8381 m³, with 1.5 kg of liquid and 0.5 kg of vapour.

Exam traps

  • Writing v = x v_g for a wet mixture. Quality multiplies only the latent part, so use v = v_f + x (v_g − v_f); the first term is small but it is never zero.
  • Thinking a two-phase system cannot be a pure substance. Ice plus water is a pure substance; moist air is not, because the two phases differ in composition.
  • Calling x = 1 superheated. x = 1 is saturated vapour, still on the boundary of the dome; superheat starts only above T_sat.
  • Quoting both p and T inside the wet region as if they were independent. There T is fixed by p, so an extra temperature is either redundant or a contradiction.
  • Treating x as a volume fraction. It is a mass fraction, x = m_g / m; the volume share of vapour is much larger because v_g is hundreds of times v_f.

5.2 Triple point and critical point

The triple point and critical point are the two endpoints of the liquid-vapour saturation curve on a pressure-temperature diagram. At the triple point, solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium. At the critical point, the liquid and vapour phases become identical. These two points bound the range of conditions where ordinary boiling and condensation occur. Engineers use them to decide whether a substance is a liquid, gas or supercritical fluid in power plants, refrigerators and chemical processes.

You need: phase names and saturation from 5.1, and equilibrium and property diagrams from Unit 1.

Phase boundaries and the triple point

On a pressure-temperature (p-T) diagram, a pure substance shows three phase boundaries. The sublimation curve separates solid and vapour. The melting curve separates solid and liquid. The vaporisation curve separates liquid and vapour. These curves meet at one point: the triple point.

At the triple point, solid, liquid and vapour coexist in equilibrium. For water, the triple point is at T = 273.16 K (0.01°C) and p = 0.6117 kPa. This pressure is much lower than atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa). Until 2019 the triple point of water defined the kelvin. The kelvin is now defined through the Boltzmann constant, and the triple point remains a fixed, reproducible reference state at 273.16 K.

Below the triple-point pressure, liquid water cannot exist. If you heat ice at a pressure below 0.6117 kPa, it sublimes: it turns directly into vapour without melting. The sublimation curve lies below the triple point on the p-T diagram. At pressures above the triple point and below the critical pressure, heating ice first melts it to liquid, then boils the liquid to vapour.

The figure plots log10(p/kPa) against T for water. The sublimation curve runs from low temperature up to the triple point. The vaporisation curve runs from the triple point to the critical point. The melting curve is nearly vertical and is not shown.

The critical point

The vaporisation curve ends at the critical point. For water, the critical temperature is Tc = 647.1 K (373.95°C) and the critical pressure is pc = 22.064 MPa. At the critical point, the saturated liquid and saturated vapour states become identical. The latent heat of vaporisation falls to zero.

Above the critical temperature, no amount of pressure can liquefy the substance. It remains a gas or a supercritical fluid. A supercritical fluid has properties between those of a liquid and a gas; it can dissolve materials like a liquid and diffuse like a gas.

On a p-v diagram, the critical point is where the saturated liquid line and saturated vapour line meet. The dome-shaped two-phase region shrinks to a point. The isotherm through the critical point has a horizontal inflection: (∂p/∂v)T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)T = 0 at the critical point. This is the critical isotherm. Below Tc, each isotherm has a flat horizontal segment inside the dome, where liquid and vapour coexist. Above Tc, the isotherms are smooth curves with no flat part.

Some syllabi call the critical state the critical point. The two terms mean the same state: the highest temperature and pressure at which a liquid and its vapour can coexist.

Reading the diagrams and practical use

Engineers read the p-T diagram to choose the right phase for a process. A steam power plant using a Rankine cycle operates below the critical pressure: water boils in the boiler at a pressure well under 22.064 MPa. A supercritical boiler runs above the critical pressure, so the water turns into steam without a distinct boiling point. The steam is a single dense phase.

The triple point warns you about low-pressure processes. Freeze drying and low-pressure vacuum drying run below 0.6117 kPa, so water sublimes there; ordinary vacuum drying at a few kPa still has liquid water. If you assume the wrong phase, your energy balance will be wrong.

When you read a p-T or p-v diagram, check the pressure scale: 0.6117 kPa is not 0.6117 MPa. That factor of 1000 changes the phase. Also use absolute pressure, not gauge pressure. A gauge pressure of zero means atmospheric pressure, 101.325 kPa, which is far above the triple point.

Worked example

Question. A piston-cylinder contains water at 0.5 kPa and 0°C. The piston moves freely so the pressure stays at 0.5 kPa. (a) Is liquid water stable at this state? (b) What is the minimum pressure required for liquid water to exist at 0°C? (c) Describe what happens when the water is slowly heated at constant pressure to 20°C. Use the triple-point values for water: Tt = 273.16 K, pt = 0.6117 kPa.

  1. The triple point of water is Tt = 273.16 K and pt = 0.6117 kPa. At this point ice, liquid water and water vapour coexist.
  2. The given pressure is p = 0.5 kPa and the given temperature is 0°C = 273.15 K. At 0°C the sublimation pressure (ice-vapour equilibrium) is about 0.6117 kPa, essentially the triple-point pressure.
  3. Compare p with pt: 0.5 kPa < 0.6117 kPa. The pressure is 0.6117 − 0.5 = 0.1117 kPa below the triple-point pressure.
  4. (a) At 0°C and 0.5 kPa, liquid water is not stable. The pressure is below the triple-point pressure, so no liquid can exist. Any liquid present would evaporate; ice would sublime. The stable phase is vapour.
  5. (b) The minimum pressure for liquid water at 0°C is about 1 atm (≈101 kPa), the melting pressure at 0°C. The melting curve of water slopes backwards, so at 0°C ice melts only near 101 kPa. At pressures between 0.6117 kPa and about 101 kPa, ice is stable; below 0.6117 kPa, no liquid can exist at all.
  6. (c) On slow heating at constant pressure 0.5 kPa, any ice sublimes directly to vapour. The sublimation temperature at 0.5 kPa is slightly below 0°C. No liquid phase appears. Once all ice has turned to vapour, further heating raises the vapour temperature toward 20°C.

Answer. Liquid water is not stable at 0.5 kPa and 0°C because the pressure is 0.1117 kPa below the triple-point pressure of 0.6117 kPa; at 0°C liquid water needs about 1 atm (≈101 kPa). On heating at 0.5 kPa, ice sublimes directly to vapour without melting.

Exam traps

  • Reading 0.6117 kPa as 0.6117 MPa. The triple-point pressure of water is about 0.006 atm, not 6 atm. A factor of 1000 puts you above atmospheric pressure and gives a completely different phase.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure. Gauges read zero at atmospheric pressure (101.325 kPa). The triple point and critical point are absolute pressures.
  • Thinking that above the critical pressure a gas can be liquefied by pressure alone. Above the critical temperature, no pressure works. Above the critical pressure but below the critical temperature, liquid can exist if you compress enough.
  • Confusing the critical point with the triple point. The triple point has three phases; the critical point has one phase where liquid and vapour are indistinguishable.
  • Forgetting that the melting curve is not vertical for all substances. For water, the melting curve slopes backwards (ice is less dense than liquid water), so increasing pressure lowers the melting point slightly. The triple point is the only point where all three curves meet.

5.3 Formation of steam, latent heat and dryness fraction

Water heated at constant pressure passes through three stages: liquid warms to saturation, liquid boils at fixed temperature, and steam superheats. The heat taken in each stage has a name and a symbol. Dryness fraction x tells how much of a wet mixture is vapour, so one number fixes enthalpy, volume and entropy between saturated liquid and saturated vapour. Boiler operators, turbine designers and examiners use these ideas to price steam, check coal use and avoid blade erosion.

You need: phase names and the p-v-T surface from 5.1, and enthalpy h = u + pv from Unit 2.

Three stages of steam formation

Heat 1 kg of water at 20 °C and 1 atm. The temperature rises to 100 °C. The heat added here is sensible heat, because it changes temperature. At 100 °C the water is a saturated liquid: liquid at its boiling point for that pressure. Its specific enthalpy is hf = 419 kJ/kg. Specific enthalpy h = u + pv (kJ/kg) counts internal energy and flow work.

More heat at 1 atm does not raise the temperature. Water boils into vapour at 100 °C. This hidden heat is the latent heat of vaporisation hfg. At 1 atm hfg = 2257 kJ/kg. The energy breaks liquid bonds; it does not raise molecular kinetic energy, so the thermometer stays at 100 °C. When the last liquid disappears, the state is saturated vapour, with hg = 2676 kJ/kg. Further heat gives superheated steam: steam above its saturation temperature. At 150 °C the enthalpy is about 2776 kJ/kg.

hfg = hg − hf

hf is the specific enthalpy of saturated liquid (kJ/kg); hg is the specific enthalpy of saturated vapour (kJ/kg); hfg is the latent heat of vaporisation (kJ/kg). At 1 atm, 2257 = 2676 − 419.

A boiler burns coal or gas to supply these three buckets of heat. The liquid stage and the superheat stage change temperature. The boiling stage does not.

Dryness fraction and mixture rules

Wet steam is saturated liquid and saturated vapour together. Dryness fraction x is the mass fraction that is vapour. Some syllabi call x quality; a percentage must be divided by 100 before use.

x = m<sub>g</sub> / (m<sub>f</sub> + m<sub>g</sub>)

mg is the mass of vapour (kg); mf is the mass of liquid (kg); x is the dryness fraction, no unit. x = 0 is saturated liquid; x = 1 is saturated vapour; 0 < x < 1 is wet steam.

Take 1 kg of wet steam. It contains x kg vapour and (1 − x) kg liquid. The liquid part carries hf per kg. The vapour part carries hfg above hf per kg. So the mixture enthalpy is a straight-line rule between the two saturation values.

h = hf + x hfg

h is the specific enthalpy of wet steam (kJ/kg); x is dryness fraction; hf and hfg are taken at the same pressure. For m kg, total enthalpy H = m h (kJ).

Specific volume v and specific entropy s follow the same linear rule, because each is a mass-weighted property in the wet region.

v = v<sub>f</sub> + x (v<sub>g</sub> − v<sub>f</sub>)   and   s = s<sub>f</sub> + x (s<sub>g</sub> − s<sub>f</sub>)

v is specific volume (m³/kg); s is specific entropy (kJ/(kg·K)); subscript f means saturated liquid; subscript g means saturated vapour.

Sketches and the critical point

A T-v sketch shows a saturation dome. Under the dome is wet steam. A constant-pressure line is horizontal while boiling, and v jumps from vf to vg as x rises. A T-h sketch has a sloped liquid line, a flat boiling line of length hfg, and a sloped superheat line. The flat line is short at high pressure and disappears at the critical point.

The critical point is about 22.1 MPa and 374 °C for water; see 5.2. There hfg = 0, so there is no latent heat and no flat boiling line. Liquid and vapour have the same density. Below the critical point, hfg falls as pressure rises: 2257 kJ/kg at 1 atm, 2015 kJ/kg at 10 bar, zero at critical.

An h-s sketch is the Mollier diagram. In the wet region, lines of constant x lie under the saturation curve. Reading the chart is a skill for 5.4. A throttling calorimeter finds x from a measured temperature, as 5.5 shows. Keep cycle analysis for Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of wet steam at 1 atm has dryness fraction 0.9. At 1 atm h_f = 419 kJ/kg and h_fg = 2257 kJ/kg. Find the specific enthalpy h, the total enthalpy H, and the masses of liquid and vapour.

  1. Write the data: m = 2 kg, x = 0.9, h_f = 419 kJ/kg, h_fg = 2257 kJ/kg.
  2. Use the wet-steam rule: h = h_f + x h_fg = 419 + 0.9 × 2257.
  3. The latent part is 0.9 × 2257 = 2031.3 kJ/kg. So h = 419 + 2031.3 = 2450.3 kJ/kg.
  4. Total enthalpy: H = m h = 2 × 2450.3 = 4900.6 kJ.
  5. Mass of vapour: m_g = x m = 0.9 × 2 = 1.8 kg.
  6. Mass of liquid: m_f = m − m_g = 2 − 1.8 = 0.2 kg. Check: (1 − x)m = 0.1 × 2 = 0.2 kg.

Answer. The specific enthalpy is 2450.3 kJ/kg, the total enthalpy is 4900.6 kJ, and the mixture contains 1.8 kg vapour and 0.2 kg liquid.

Exam traps

  • Using h_fg without multiplying by x: for x = 0.9 the latent part is 0.9 × 2257 = 2031.3 kJ/kg, not 2257 kJ/kg.
  • Reading 90% quality as x = 90. Divide by 100 first, so x = 0.90.
  • Using h = x h_g in the wet region. h_g already contains h_f; the correct rule is h = h_f + x h_fg.
  • Mixing specific and total quantities: h is kJ/kg, H = m h is kJ. A 2 kg sample multiplies by 2.
  • Using h_f + x h_fg for superheated steam. Superheated steam needs the steam-table value at its pressure and temperature.

5.4 Steam tables and Mollier chart

Steam tables and the Mollier chart are lookup tools. The tables list measured specific volume v (m³/kg), enthalpy h (kJ/kg) and entropy s (kJ/kg·K) for water and steam. They are arranged by pressure or by temperature; the dryness fraction is applied afterwards through the mixture formulas. The Mollier chart draws the same data on an h–s plane. Boiler designers, turbine testers and exam students use both to find all properties from a known pair. This chapter shows their layout and how to read a state.

You need: 5.1 for the five states of a pure substance, 5.3 for h_f, h_fg and dryness fraction, and Unit 1 for pressure units and gauge versus absolute pressure.

What the tables hold, and which pressure to use

A steam table lists measured properties of water and steam so one known property gives the rest. No single equation covers compressed liquid, wet steam and superheated steam accurately, so values are tabulated. Each row gives specific volume v in m³/kg, enthalpy h in kJ/kg and entropy s in kJ/kg·K.

Tables use absolute pressure. Absolute pressure is gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure; atmospheric pressure is about 1.013 bar. A boiler gauge showing 10 bar means about 11.01 bar absolute, and 11 bar is what you look up.

p_abs = p_gauge + p_atm

p_abs is absolute pressure, p_gauge the gauge reading, p_atm the local atmospheric pressure (1.013 bar at sea level); all three in the same unit, bar or kPa.

The saturated table lists pressure with T_sat or temperature with p_sat. Both carry v_f, v_g, h_f, h_fg, h_g, s_f, s_fg, s_g. Subscript f marks saturated liquid, g saturated vapour, fg the change during boiling. So h_g = h_f + h_fg and s_fg = h_fg / T_sat, with T_sat in kelvin.

Inside the dome, pressure and temperature are not independent; a table row alone cannot fix the state. The dryness fraction x does: x = 0 is saturated liquid, x = 1 saturated vapour.

h = h_f + x·h_fg,   v = v_f + x(v_g − v_f),   s = s_f + x·s_fg

x is the dryness fraction (0 to 1); h_f and h_fg in kJ/kg; v_f and v_g in m³/kg; s_f and s_fg in kJ/kg·K. At low and moderate pressure v_f is far smaller than v_g (about 1/170 of v_g at 10 bar), so v ≈ x·v_g is used, except at very small dryness fractions where v_f must be kept.

Reading the superheated table

Above T_sat the steam is superheated and the saturated table stops helping. The superheated table puts pressure down the rows and temperature across the columns. It has entries only where T is above T_sat at that pressure. Each cell gives v, h and s for that (p, T).

  1. Look up T_sat at the given absolute pressure in the saturated table.
  2. If T > T_sat, the state is superheated; open the superheated table.
  3. If T = T_sat, the steam is saturated; use the saturated row with x.
  4. If T < T_sat, the water is a compressed liquid; use the compressed liquid table, or h ≈ h_f at that temperature when pressure is small.
  5. Find the pressure row, then the temperature column, and read v, h and s.

Printed tables jump in steps, often 50 K, so a given temperature may fall between two columns. Linear interpolation is accurate enough because each property varies smoothly with T at constant pressure.

y = y₁ + [(T − T₁)/(T₂ − T₁)]·(y₂ − y₁)

y is the property wanted (h, v or s); (T₁, y₁) and (T₂, y₂) are the two table entries either side; T is the given temperature in °C.

Never interpolate across T_sat. Below saturation the temperature stays at T_sat while h climbs with x, so a formula built on T gives nonsense there.

Check: at 10 bar, h_g = 2777.1 kJ/kg, so any superheated h at 10 bar must be larger. A smaller answer means a wrong row or a gauge pressure.

The h-s (Mollier) chart

The Mollier chart, also called the h-s diagram, plots enthalpy h in kJ/kg upward and entropy s in kJ/kg·K across. A state is one point and a process is a line, so you read with a ruler instead of a table.

The saturation dome sits in the middle. The left curve is saturated liquid (x = 0), the right curve is saturated vapour (x = 1), and they meet at the critical point near 221 bar and 374 °C.

  • Constant-pressure lines: inside the dome such a line is also a constant-temperature line, and it is straight, because at constant p the slope dh/ds equals T. In superheated steam the line curves upward and steepens as T rises along it.
  • Constant-temperature lines: inside the dome they coincide with the pressure lines. In superheated steam they slope gently upward to the right and become almost horizontal at low pressure, where steam behaves like an ideal gas and h depends on T alone. Near the saturation line at high pressure they rise much more steeply.
  • Constant-dryness lines: curves for x = 0.9, 0.95 and so on, fanning down from the critical point inside the dome; they place a wet state without a table.
  1. Convert the pressure to absolute.
  2. For superheated steam, find the isobar for p and follow it to the isotherm for T.
  3. Read h on the vertical scale and s on the horizontal scale.
  4. For wet steam, cross the isobar inside the dome with the constant-x line.
  5. Reject any point outside the dome that should have been inside it.

Reading a point on the printed chart is accurate to about 1 to 2 per cent. That is fine for a sketch or quick check; give final exam answers from the tables.

Worked example

Question. Steam flows at 10 bar absolute and 250 °C. (a) State whether it is superheated, saturated or compressed liquid. 5.5 Steam calorimeters

A throttling calorimeter measures steam dryness without collecting water. Wet steam at pressure p₁ passes through a throttle to a lower pressure p₂ and leaves superheated. The steady flow energy equation, with no work, little heat loss and small kinetic and potential energy changes, gives h₁ = h₂. Steam tables turn the outlet p₂ and T₂ into h₂, and then into dryness x₁. Power plants use this check for turbine steam. Only a few universities teach steam calorimetry; it belongs to advanced steam laboratory work.

You need dryness fraction, the steam tables, and the steady flow energy equation from Unit 2.

Throttling calorimeter

Wet steam at p₁ enters a throttle, a small orifice or valve. It leaves at lower p₂. The process is throttling: no work, negligible heat transfer, small kinetic and potential energy changes. The steady flow energy equation gives h₁ = h₂. h₁ is inlet specific enthalpy, kJ/kg; h₂ is outlet specific enthalpy, kJ/kg.

x₁ = (h₂ − h_f1)/h_fg1

h_f1 is saturated liquid enthalpy at inlet pressure p₁, kJ/kg; h_fg1 is latent heat at p₁, kJ/kg; x₁ is inlet dryness fraction.

To use this, the outlet must be superheated. Then p₂ and T₂ fix h₂ in the superheated table. At inlet, h₁ = h_f1 + x₁ h_fg1. Substituting h₁ = h₂ gives the formula above.

  1. Read inlet pressure p₁, outlet pressure p₂ and outlet temperature T₂.
  2. Confirm the outlet is superheated at p₂ and T₂.
  3. Find h₂ from the superheated steam table.
  4. Find h_f1 and h_fg1 at p₁ from the saturated steam table.
  5. Compute x₁ = (h₂ − h_f1)/h_fg1.

If the outlet is still wet, h₂ depends on an unknown outlet dryness x₂. The equation h₁ = h₂ then has two unknowns, so the throttling calorimeter is invalid.

Separating calorimeter and limits

A separating calorimeter works mechanically. Steam passes through baffles or a centrifuge. Water droplets hit the walls and drain into a measuring vessel. The remaining dry steam is condensed and weighed. Let m_w be separated water mass and m_s be dry steam mass over the same time. Then x = m_s/(m_s + m_w).

This instrument suits very wet steam. It is less exact than a throttling calorimeter and causes pressure loss. A combined separating and throttling calorimeter first removes most water; the throttling part then measures the small remaining wetness.

  • Throttling calorimeter: outlet must be superheated; needs accurate p₁, p₂ and T₂.
  • Separating calorimeter: direct mass measurement; used when steam is very wet.
  • Combined unit: separating part handles bulk water, throttling part gives fine dryness.
  • Both need steady flow and a representative steam sample.

Worked example

Question. Wet steam at 10 bar is throttled to 1 bar and 130°C. At 10 bar, h_f = 762.6 kJ/kg and h_fg = 2015.3 kJ/kg. At 1 bar and 130°C, h = 2736 kJ/kg. Find the inlet dryness fraction x₁.

  1. Check the outlet state: at 1 bar the saturation temperature is about 99.6°C, so 130°C is superheated.
  2. Read the outlet enthalpy: h₂ = 2736 kJ/kg.
  3. Apply throttling: h₁ = h₂ = 2736 kJ/kg.
  4. At 10 bar: h_f1 = 762.6 kJ/kg and h_fg1 = 2015.3 kJ/kg.
  5. Compute x₁ = (2736 − 762.6)/2015.3 = 1973.4/2015.3 = 0.9792.

Answer. x₁ ≈ 0.979, or 97.9% dry.

Exam traps

  • Using h₁ = h₂ when the outlet is still wet. Then h₂ contains unknown x₂ and the calculation cannot give x₁.
  • Forgetting to check that the outlet is superheated. A temperature alone does not fix h₂ inside the wet region.
  • Taking h_f and h_fg at the outlet pressure. Both must be at the inlet pressure p₁.
  • Swapping h_f and h_fg in x₁ = (h₂ − h_f1)/h_fg1. h_fg1 is the latent heat, not the sensible part.
  • Applying h₁ = h₂ with large kinetic or potential energy changes. The throttle must be well insulated and changes small.

5.6 Ideal and real gases; equations of state

The ideal gas law pv = RT is the simplest equation of state: it links pressure, molar volume and temperature through one constant. It is accurate for air, nitrogen and carbon dioxide at room temperature and near-atmospheric pressure, and it underlies every cycle calculation in Unit 6. It fails near saturation and at high pressure, where molecules crowd together and pull on each other. Real-gas equations of state repair that by adding two corrections: a molecular volume and an attraction term. Compressor design, gas pipelines and cylinder filling all need the corrected form.

You need: state, property and equilibrium (Unit 1); pv = RT and specific heats (Unit 2); and the phase-change picture in 5.1.

The ideal gas model

An equation of state is any relation among pressure, volume and temperature for a fixed mass. The ideal gas is the simplest such model. Its molecules are treated as point masses: they occupy no volume of their own, they exert no force on one another except at the instant of a collision, and every collision is perfectly elastic. A gas behaves this way because at ordinary pressure it is mostly empty space.

pv = R_u T

p is absolute pressure in Pa; v is molar volume V/n in m³/mol; R_u is the universal gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K, never °C.

Engineers usually work per kilogram, not per mole. Dividing R_u by molar mass M gives the specific gas constant R, and the law becomes pV = mRT. For air M = 0.02897 kg/mol, so R = 8.314/0.02897 = 287 J/(kg·K) = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). For carbon dioxide M = 0.044 kg/mol, so R = 8.314/0.044 = 188.9 J/(kg·K).

pV = mRT, R = R_u/M

V is total volume in m³; m is mass in kg; R is the specific gas constant in J/(kg·K); R_u = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the universal gas constant; M is molar mass in kg/mol.

The same law defines a temperature scale. In a constant-volume gas thermometer, p at fixed v rises in proportion to T. Measuring p at the triple point of water (273.16 K, see 5.2) and then at the unknown state gives T = 273.16 × (p/p_tr). This is the ideal gas temperature scale; it agrees with the thermodynamic scale of Unit 3 for a gas at low pressure.

Why real gases deviate

Two facts spoil the model. First, a molecule has a finite size, so the space available to it is less than the container volume. Second, molecules attract each other at short range, so a molecule about to strike the wall is pulled back and hits with less force. The first effect raises pressure; the second lowers it.

Deviations grow as the gas is compressed towards saturation, or pushed to high pressure at a temperature well above the critical temperature. At 1 atm and 300 K, carbon dioxide is within a fraction of a percent of ideal; at 5 MPa it is off by about 30 percent (Z ≈ 0.7). Near the saturated vapour line the ideal law can be wrong by tens of percent, because molecules are close to condensing.

A real gas, also called a non-ideal gas in some syllabi, therefore needs a better equation of state. The measure of the error is the compressibility factor Z = pv/(R_u T), covered in 5.7. For an ideal gas Z = 1 at every state.

The van der Waals equation

Van der Waals repaired the ideal law with two constants. The constant b subtracts the volume the molecules themselves occupy, so the free volume becomes (v − b). The term a/v² restores the pressure lost because attraction pulls the molecules inward, away from the wall. Adding a/v² to the measured p gives the pressure the gas would exert if attraction were absent.

(p + a/v²)(v − b) = R_u T

p is pressure in Pa; v is molar volume in m³/mol; T is in K; R_u = 8.314 J/(mol·K); a is the attraction constant in Pa·m⁶/mol²; b is the excluded volume in m³/mol, roughly four times the molecular volume.

Rearranged for pressure it is easier to use: p = R_u T/(v − b) − a/v². Both constants are found by fitting measured data, usually from the critical point.

  1. Convert the given mass or moles to molar volume: v = V/n.
  2. Subtract b from v to get the free volume, then compute R_u T/(v − b).
  3. Subtract a/v² from that result to get p. Do the arithmetic in SI units, then convert to kPa or bar.

Van der Waals is the simplest real-gas equation, not the most accurate. Redlich–Kwong and Beattie–Bridgeman are better; they carry more constants and are fitted over wider ranges. Only a few universities teach them in detail, and most examinations ask for van der Waals with its constants supplied.

Worked example

Question. One mole of carbon dioxide is held at 300 K in a tank of volume 0.024 m³. Take R_u = 8.314 J/(mol·K), a = 0.3640 Pa·m⁶/mol² and b = 4.267 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol. Find the pressure from the ideal gas law and from the van der Waals equation, in kPa.

  1. Molar volume: v = V/n = 0.024/1 = 0.024 m³/mol.
  2. Ideal gas law: p = R_u T/v = (8.314 × 300)/0.024 = 2494.2/0.024 = 103 925 Pa.
  3. Van der Waals form for pressure: p = R_u T/(v − b) − a/v².
  4. Free volume: v − b = 0.024 − 0.00004267 = 0.02395733 m³/mol.
  5. Repulsive part: R_u T/(v − b) = 2494.2/0.02395733 = 104 110.1 Pa.
  6. Attraction part: a/v² = 0.3640/(0.024)² = 0.3640/0.000576 = 631.9 Pa.
  7. Van der Waals pressure: p = 104 110.1 − 631.9 = 103 478.2 Pa.

Exam traps

  • Using the total volume V in place of v in the van der Waals equation. There v is molar volume V/n, so for 4 mol in 0.024 m³ you must put v = 0.006 m³/mol.
  • Mixing units of a, b and R. If a is in Pa·m⁶/mol² and b in m³/mol, R must be 8.314 J/(mol·K) and p comes out in Pa, not kPa.
  • Putting T in °C. Both ideal and van der Waals equations need absolute temperature, so add 273.15 before substituting.
  • Using R_u where R is wanted. Divide by molar mass for per-kilogram work; 8.314 in pV = mRT with m in kg gives wrong numbers.
  • Claiming the van der Waals equation is exact near the critical point. It is a two-constant model, good for trends, and the compressibility chart of 5.7 is preferred for accurate work.

5.7 Compressibility factor and compressibility chart

Real gases do not obey pv = RT exactly. The compressibility factor Z = pv/RT measures the correction: Z is 1 for an ideal gas and moves away from 1 near the critical point and at high pressure. Engineers read Z from a generalized chart using reduced pressure and temperature, then get v = ZRT/p. This matters for storing nitrogen at 10 MPa, sizing gas pipelines, and checking when the ideal-gas answer is safe.

You need: 5.6 ideal and real gases and 5.2 critical properties.

Z and reduced coordinates

A real gas approaches ideal behaviour only at low pressure and high temperature. The compressibility factor Z measures the gap. At a given pressure and temperature, Z = pv/(RT). At the same p and T, an ideal gas has videal = RT/p, so Z = vactual/videal. For an ideal gas, Z = 1. For steam near saturation, Z can be well below 1. For nitrogen at ordinary conditions, Z is close to 1.

Z = pv/(RT) = v/videal

p is absolute pressure in Pa or kPa; v is specific volume in m³/kg; R is the gas constant in J/(kg·K) or kJ/(kg·K); T is absolute temperature in kelvin; videal = RT/p.

To compare gases, scale by critical properties: pR = p/pc, TR = T/Tc, and vR = v/vc. Here pc and Tc are the critical pressure and temperature from 5.2, and vc is the critical specific volume. These ratios are dimensionless. Some charts use pseudoreduced volume vR′ = v/(RTc/pc) instead. The principle of corresponding states says gases with the same pR and TR have approximately the same Z. That is why one chart works for many gases. Some syllabi call it the generalized compressibility chart.

Reading the chart

The chart plots pR on the horizontal axis and Z on the vertical axis. Curves are lines of constant TR. At very low pR, every curve meets Z = 1: the gas is ideal. At high pR, the curves spread apart. Near TR = 1 the curve dips sharply to low Z because attractive forces are strong close to the critical point. For TR below 1 the dip ends where the saturation line cuts the curve. At high TR, Z stays near 1 or rises above 1 as pressure grows. The chart is approximate; read Z to two or three significant figures.

  1. Find pc and Tc for the gas.
  2. Use absolute pressure and kelvin temperature.
  3. Compute pR = p/pc and TR = T/Tc.
  4. Locate the point on the chart and read Z; interpolate between TR curves if needed.
  5. Compute v = ZRT/p with matching units.

Do not use gauge pressure or Celsius in pR and TR. The chart does not need the gas identity beyond pc and Tc, but it is less accurate for polar gases such as water and ammonia. For those, use tables or a suitable equation of state. Mixtures need pseudocritical properties; 5.8 mentions this.

Using Z and its limits

Once Z is known, v = ZRT/p. Use p in kPa with R in kJ/(kg·K), because 1 kJ = 1 kPa·m³. For total volume, V = m v. The same correction appears as pV = mZRT. If Z > 1, the gas is less compressible than ideal (it occupies more volume, repulsive forces dominate). If Z < 1, it is more compressible than ideal (it occupies less volume, attractive forces dominate). At low pR or high TR, the ideal-gas answer is close. At pR near 1 and TR near 1, the ideal-gas law can be badly wrong.

v = ZRT/p

v is specific volume in m³/kg; Z is the compressibility factor from the chart; R is the gas constant in kJ/(kg·K); T is kelvin; p is absolute pressure in kPa.

The chart is a hand tool for quick estimates. Detailed process design uses cubic equations of state or reference databases, as noted in 5.6. The worked example applies the chart route to nitrogen.

Worked example

Question. Nitrogen at 300 K and 10 MPa. For nitrogen pc = 3.39 MPa, Tc = 126.2 K, R = 0.2968 kJ/(kg·K). Find TR, pR, and estimate specific volume v using Z from the generalized compressibility chart.

  1. Use absolute values: p = 10 MPa and T = 300 K.
  2. TR = T/Tc = 300/126.2 = 2.377, take 2.38.
  3. pR = p/pc = 10/3.39 = 2.950, take 2.95.
  4. On the chart at pR ≈ 2.95 and TR ≈ 2.38, read Z ≈ 1.0.
  5. Convert pressure to kPa: 10 MPa = 10000 kPa.

  • Using Celsius in TR. All reduced temperatures use kelvin.

5.8 Gas mixtures: Dalton's and Amagat's laws

In an ideal gas mixture the molecules ignore each other: no attraction, no reaction, and each gas fills the whole vessel at the mixture temperature. Dalton splits the total pressure into partial pressures pi = yip; Amagat splits the total volume into partial volumes Vi = yiV. Both use the mole fraction yi as the share. Engineers need this for air, combustion products, LPG–air and natural gas lines, where a compressor or a pipe must be sized for the mixture, not for one gas.

You need: the ideal gas law pV = nRuT and the kmol from 5.6 Ideal and real gases, plus pressure and the mole concept from Unit 1.

Mole fraction and mixture molar mass

An ideal gas mixture is a set of gases sharing one vessel at the same temperature T and pressure p, with no chemical reaction and no forces between molecules. The amount of each gas is measured in kilomoles: 1 kmol = 1000 mol, and one kmol of gas i has mass Mi kg, where Mi is the molar mass in kg/kmol. The total amount is n = n1 + n2 + …, with ni the kmol of component i.

The mole fraction of component i is yi = ni/n. It is dimensionless, and Σ yi = 1. The mass fraction wi = mi/m is a different number; for air, yO2 ≈ 0.21 but wO2 ≈ 0.23. The two are linked by wi = yiMi/Mmix.

A mixture behaves as one gas with molar mass Mmix = m/n = Σ yiMi and gas constant Rmix = Ru/Mmix, where Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K) is the universal gas constant.

M<sub>mix</sub> = Σ y<sub>i</sub>M<sub>i</sub>        R<sub>mix</sub> = R<sub>u</sub>/M<sub>mix</sub>

yi = ni/n is the mole fraction, dimensionless; Mi is the molar mass of component i in kg/kmol; Mmix is the mixture molar mass in kg/kmol; Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K); Rmix is the mixture gas constant in kJ/(kg·K).

Dalton's law: partial pressures

Dalton's model takes the mixture apart. Put component i alone in the same volume V at the same temperature T. It behaves as an ideal gas and presses on the walls with the partial pressure pi = niRuT/V. Dalton's law states that the mixture pressure is the sum of these partial pressures: p = Σ pi.

Divide pi by p = nRuT/V and the temperature and volume cancel: pi = yip. The partial pressure is a share of the total, and the share is the mole fraction. Using the mass fraction here is the most common error in this chapter.

  1. Convert every amount to kmol, every temperature to K and every volume to m³.
  2. Add: n = Σ ni, then yi = ni/n. Check that the yi add to 1.00.
  3. Find the mixture pressure: p = nRuT/V, with Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K) and p in kPa.
  4. Multiply for each gas: pi = yip. Check that the pi add to p.
  5. For masses use mi = niMi, Mmix = Σ yiMi and Rmix = Ru/Mmix.

The V and T in step 3 belong to the whole mixture. In the example below, nitrogen and oxygen share one 0.5 m³ tank; nitrogen is not squeezed into a smaller volume of its own. Partial pressure uses the mixture volume and the mixture temperature, always.

Amagat's law: partial volumes

Amagat's model keeps the mixture pressure p and temperature T and asks a different question: what volume Vi would component i occupy alone at p and T? For an ideal gas, Vi = niRuT/p = yiV. Amagat's law states that the partial volumes add to the vessel volume: V = Σ Vi.

The same mole fraction appears in both laws: pi/p = Vi/V = yi. Dalton and Amagat are two ways of bookkeeping the same ideal-gas model, not two different physical pictures. Use Dalton when a pressure gauge is read. Use Amagat when a gas analyser reports percentages by volume, because for ideal gases volume per cent equals mole per cent.

Mixture properties follow the Gibbs–Dalton rule: umix = Σ yiui and hmix = Σ yihi, each component property taken at the mixture temperature. Entropy of mixing is a finer point, left for later.

Some old notes write Amalgam's law for Amagat's law; the correct name is Amagat. Both laws assume ideal gases. At 200 bar, or near condensation, they fail, and a real mixture needs the compressibility factor or an equation of state with mixing rules (see 5.7 Compressibility factor and chart).

Worked example

Question. A rigid tank of volume 0.5 m³ holds an ideal gas mixture of 2 kmol of nitrogen and 3 kmol of oxygen at 300 K. Take Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K), MN2 = 28 kg/kmol and MO2 = 32 kg/kmol. Find the total pressure, the mole fractions, the partial pressure of each gas, the mixture molar mass and the mixture gas constant.

  1. Data: nN2 = 2 kmol, nO2 = 3 kmol, V = 0.5 m³, T = 300 K, Ru = 8.314 kJ/(kmol·K).
  2. Total amount: n = 2 + 3 = 5 kmol.
  3. Mole fractions: yN2 = 2/5 = 0.4 and yO2 = 3/5 = 0.6; check 0.4 + 0.6 = 1.00.
  4. Total pressure: p = nRuT/V = 5 × 8.314 × 300 / 0.5 = 12471 / 0.5 = 24942 kPa = 24.94 MPa.
  5. Partial pressures: pN2 = 0.4 × 24942 = 9976.8 kPa ≈ 9.98 MPa; pO2 = 0.6 × 24942 = 14965.2 kPa ≈ 15.0 MPa.
  6. Dalton check: 9976.8 + 14965.2 = 24942 kPa, equal to the total pressure.
  7. Mixture molar mass: Mmix = 0.4 × 28 + 0.6 × 32 = 11.2 + 19.2 = 30.4 kg/kmol. Mass: m = n Mmix = 5 × 30.4 = 152 kg.
  8. Mixture gas constant: Rmix = Ru/Mmix = 8.314 / 30.4 = 0.2735 kJ/(kg·K). Check: pV/m = 24942 × 0.5 / 152 = 82.05 kJ/kg and RmixT = 0.2735 × 300 = 82.05 kJ/kg.

Answer. n = 5 kmol, yN2 = 0.4, yO2 = 0.6, p = 24942 kPa (24.9 MPa), pN2 = 9976.8 kPa (9.98 MPa), pO2 = 14965.2 kPa (15.0 MPa), Mmix = 30.4 kg/kmol and Rmix = 0.2735 kJ/(kg·K).

Exam traps

  • pi = yip, never pi = wip. Air is 0.21 oxygen by mole but 0.23 by mass, so the mass fraction overstates pO2 by about 10 %.
  • Partial pressure uses the mixture volume V and the mixture temperature T. Putting a component's own volume into piV = niRuT is the classic mistake.
  • Ru = 8.314 works only with kPa, m³, kmol and K. Mixing MPa with m³, or mol with kmol, shifts the answer by a factor of 1000.
  • The mole fractions must add to 1.00. If an analysis sums to 0.95, a component is missing, not a rounding error.
  • Amagat's partial volume is not the volume of the gas before mixing. It is the volume that gas would occupy alone at the final p and T.

5.9 Psychrometry: humidity and psychrometric chart

Moist air is a mixture of dry air and water vapour. Its state decides whether a room feels comfortable, whether clothes dry on a line, and how much water a cooling tower evaporates. Psychrometry gives four linked measures: dry-bulb temperature, humidity ratio, relative humidity and dew point. The psychrometric chart plots them together so heating, cooling and drying paths can be read without solving equations each time.

You need 5.8 Dalton's law for partial pressures and 5.4 steam tables for psat(T).

Dry air, vapour and humidity ratio

Moist air is dry air plus water vapour. At ordinary pressures both behave as ideal gases, so Dalton's law applies: the total pressure p is the sum of the dry-air partial pressure pa and the vapour partial pressure pv. The dry-bulb temperature T is the temperature read by an ordinary thermometer in the air, with no water on its bulb.

φ = pv / psat(T)

φ is relative humidity, from 0 to 1; pv is the water-vapour partial pressure in kPa; psat(T) is the saturation pressure of water at the dry-bulb temperature T, read from steam tables.

Relative humidity is a ratio of pressures, not a ratio of masses. It says how close the vapour is to condensing. φ = 1 means saturated air.

w = 0.622 pv / (p − pv)

w is the humidity ratio in kg water vapour per kg dry air; 0.622 = 18.015/28.97 is the molar mass ratio; p is total pressure in kPa; pv is vapour partial pressure in kPa.

Here p − pv is pa, the dry-air pressure. The factor 0.622 comes from the molar masses: 18.015 g/mol for water and 28.97 g/mol for dry air. Using total p in the denominator instead of pa is a common error.

h = 1.005 T + w (2500 + 1.86 T)

h is the enthalpy of moist air in kJ per kg dry air; T is dry-bulb temperature in °C; 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) is the specific heat of dry air; 2500 kJ/kg is the latent heat of water at 0 °C; 1.86 kJ/(kg·K) is the specific heat of water vapour.

The enthalpy of moist air is quoted per kg of dry air. The first term is the dry-air enthalpy; the bracket is the vapour enthalpy. This approximation is accurate enough for comfort air-conditioning.

Dew point and wet-bulb temperature

The dew point Tdp is the temperature at which the vapour just becomes saturated at the same pv. It satisfies psat(Tdp) = pv. If moist air is cooled at constant pressure and constant w, the first change is a fall in T; the dew point is reached when the relative humidity becomes 100%. Further cooling makes liquid water condense. This is why a cold water bottle sweats in a Chennai room.

The wet-bulb temperature Twb is read by a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in a wet cloth and exposed to moving air. Water evaporates from the cloth and cools the bulb. The drier the air, the faster the evaporation and the lower the wet-bulb reading. For unsaturated air, Tdp < Twb < Tdb. At saturation all three are equal.

Wet-bulb and dew point answer different questions. Dew point is reached by cooling without evaporation. Wet-bulb is reached by evaporating water into the air and cooling it. For air-water vapour the wet-bulb temperature is close to the adiabatic saturation temperature, which is why the chart uses one line family for both.

Reading the psychrometric chart

The chart plots humidity ratio w on the vertical axis against dry-bulb temperature T on the horizontal axis, at one fixed total pressure, usually 101.325 kPa. The curved saturation line is φ = 100%. Above it is fog or condensation; below it is unsaturated moist air.

Constant relative humidity lines curve down from the saturation line; the 60% line lies below the 100% line. Constant enthalpy and constant wet-bulb lines run nearly parallel, sloping down to the right. The state point fixes all properties: read w across to the left axis and T down to the bottom axis.

Sensible heating moves the state point horizontally to the right: w stays fixed, T rises, and φ falls. Cooling moves it horizontally left; when the saturation line is reached, further cooling follows the curve and water condenses out. Adding water vapour moves the point upward: with a fine water spray the air also cools slightly as the water evaporates, while adding steam raises the temperature. The chart turns these paths into straight-line steps, which is why it survives in air-conditioning work.

Worked example

Question. Moist air at 30 °C dry-bulb and 60% relative humidity flows at a total pressure of 101.325 kPa. The saturation pressure of water at 30 °C is 4.246 kPa. Find the vapour partial pressure, the humidity ratio and the dew point.

  1. Vapour partial pressure: pv = φ × psat(30 °C) = 0.60 × 4.246 kPa = 2.548 kPa.
  2. Dry-air partial pressure: pa = p − pv = 101.325 − 2.548 = 98.777 kPa.
  3. Humidity ratio: w = 0.622 × pv / pa = 0.622 × 2.548 / 98.777 = 0.01605 kg/kg dry air, rounded to 0.0160 kg/kg dry air.
  4. Dew point: find the temperature where psat equals 2.548 kPa. Interpolate between 20 °C, where psat = 2.339 kPa, and 25 °C, where psat = 3.169 kPa: Tdp = 20 + 5 × (2.548 − 2.339) / (3.169 − 2.339) = 20 + 5 × 0.209 / 0.830 = 21.26 °C ≈ 21.3 °C.

Answer. pv = 2.548 kPa, w = 0.0160 kg/kg dry air, and Tdp ≈ 21.3 °C.

Exam traps

  • Using total pressure p instead of dry-air pressure p − pv in the humidity-ratio formula. The error is small at low humidity but large near saturation.
  • Treating relative humidity as a mass ratio. φ compares pv with psat at the same dry-bulb temperature.
  • Taking the wet-bulb temperature as the dew point. They coincide only at saturation.
  • Using psat at the wet-bulb or dew-point temperature when the question gives φ at the dry-bulb temperature. Use psat(Tdb).
  • Forgetting that humidity ratio is per kg of dry air, not per kg of moist air.

Takeaways

  1. All equilibrium states of a pure substance lie on its p-v-T surface, and the two-phase region is only a surface or a line, not a volume.
  2. The triple point is a single point for a given substance; the critical point closes the liquid-vapour curve and removes the difference between liquid and vapour.
  3. Inside the wet region pressure and temperature are not independent — fixing one fixes the other, so a second property such as x is needed to fix the state.
  4. Enthalpy of wet steam follows h = h_f + x·h_fg, and the same mixing rule applies to v, s and u with v_f, v_g and s_f, s_g.
  5. Steam tables and the Mollier chart are read, not solved; they replace the missing equation of state for water in exam problems.
  6. A throttling calorimeter relies on the fact that h is constant across a throttle, and it works only when the steam is dry enough to leave superheated.
  7. Real gases depart from pv = RT near saturation and at high pressure; Z = pv/RT, read from the generalised chart with reduced properties, quantifies that departure.
  8. In an ideal gas mixture each component behaves as if alone in the vessel, so the mixture calculations reduce to weighted sums using mole fractions.
  9. Moist air is treated as an ideal gas mixture of dry air and water vapour, which is why the psychrometric chart carries two independent temperatures.

Dalton: p = Σp_i with each gas at the mixture volume and temperature; Amagat: V = ΣV_i with each gas at the mixture pressure and temperature; Ideal gas for each component: p_i V = n_i R̄ T; Divide component equation by mixture equation to get p_i = y_i p; Volume fraction V_i/V equals n_i/n = y_i; Validity only for ideal gas mixtures at moderate pressure.

Apply

Apply Steam at 10 bar has a dryness fraction of 0.90. Using steam tables (h_f = 762.8 kJ/kg, h_fg = 2015.3 kJ/kg, v_f = 0.001127 m³/kg, v_g = 0.1944 m³/kg) find h, v and u of 1 kg. 12 marks

h = h_f + x h_fg = 762.8 + 0.9 × 2015.3 = 2576.6 kJ/kg; v = v_f + x (v_g − v_f) = 0.001127 + 0.9 × 0.19337 = 0.1751 m³/kg; u = h − p·v with p in kPa: 2576.6 − 1000 × 0.1751 = 2401.5 kJ/kg; Note that v_f is small but not zero and u is not h.

Apply Steam at 8 bar passes through a throttling calorimeter and leaves at 1 bar, 120 °C. Using h_f = 721.1 kJ/kg and h_fg = 2046.5 kJ/kg at 8 bar, and h = 2716.4 kJ/kg for steam at 1 bar, 120 °C, find the dryness fraction of the main steam. 12 marks

Throttling is adiabatic with no work, so h₁ = h₂; h₂ = 2716.4 kJ/kg from superheated values at 1 bar, 120 °C; h₁ = h_f + x h_fg = 721.1 + x × 2046.5; Equate and solve: x = (2716.4 − 721.1)/2046.5 = 0.975; State the assumption of steady flow, negligible kinetic and potential energy, and no heat loss.

Apply One kilogram of carbon dioxide is held in a 0.05 m³ vessel at 300 K. Calculate the pressure using (i) the ideal gas equation and (ii) the van der Waals equation (a = 364.3 kPa·m⁶/kmol², b = 0.0427 m³/kmol), and comment on the difference. 10 marks

n = 1/44 kmol, R = 0.1889 kJ/kg·K; Ideal: p = nR̄T/V = 0.02273 × 8.314 × 300/0.05 ≈ 1134 kPa; van der Waals: p = nR̄T/(V − nb) − a n²/V²; nb = 0.00097 m³, so first term ≈ 1156 kPa; Attraction term ≈ 75 kPa, giving p ≈ 1081 kPa; Real pressure is below ideal because attraction dominates at this moderate density.

Apply A gas mixture has the volumetric analysis CO₂ 12%, O₂ 8%, N₂ 80%. For the mixture find the molar mass, the gas constant, the mass fractions and the partial pressure of each gas at 1 bar, 300 K. 10 marks

M = Σ y_i M_i = 0.12×44 + 0.08×32 + 0.80×28 = 30.24 kg/kmol; R = R̄/M = 8.314/30.24 = 0.275 kJ/kg·K; Mass fraction of CO₂ = y M_i/M = 0.12×44/30.24 = 0.1746, similarly for others; p_i = y_i p: CO₂ 0.12 bar, O₂ 0.08 bar, N₂ 0.80 bar; Volume fraction = mole fraction for an ideal gas mixture.

Apply Moist air at 1 atm has a dry-bulb temperature of 30 °C and a wet-bulb temperature of 20 °C. Using the psychrometric chart, find relative humidity, humidity ratio, dew-point temperature, specific enthalpy and specific volume. 10 marks

Locate the state at the intersection of DBT 30 °C and WBT 20 °C; Read relative humidity from the curved lines, about 53%; Read humidity ratio on the right-hand scale, about 0.014 kg/kg dry air; Follow the horizontal line left to saturation to read dew point, about 19.5 °C; Read enthalpy from the oblique scale through the state point; Read specific volume from the constant-v lines.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Explain physically why a real gas deviates from pv = RT. Derive the van der Waals constants a and b in terms of critical properties and show how the equation predicts the critical point. 12 marks

Two assumptions break down: molecules have finite volume, and they attract each other; b corrects the volume, a/v² corrects the pressure; At the critical point the isotherm has an inflection: (∂p/∂v)_T = 0 and (∂²p/∂v²)_T = 0; Solving the three critical-point equations gives v_c = 3b, T_c = 8a/(27Rb), p_c = a/(27b²); Therefore a = 27R²T_c²/(64p_c) and b = RT_c/(8p_c); Predicted Z_c = 3/8 = 0.375 for all substances, against 0.23–0.31 measured — a known weakness.

Analyze A throttling calorimeter reads reliably only when the main steam is fairly dry. Explain why, and describe how a combined separating and throttling calorimeter extends the range. 10 marks

After throttling the steam must be superheated, otherwise its state is still two-phase and its enthalpy cannot be found from temperature alone; Very wet steam cannot be superheated enough by a small pressure drop; Separating calorimeter removes most of the water mechanically and gives an approximate x from the separated masses; The remaining almost-dry steam is then throttled and its x found by the enthalpy balance; Overall x combines the two stages: x = x_sep × x_throttle style relation on the vapour masses.

Analyze With the help of the psychrometric chart, explain sensible heating, sensible cooling, cooling and dehumidification, and evaporative (adiabatic) cooling of moist air. Give one air-conditioning use of each. 12 marks

Sensible heating: horizontal line, humidity ratio constant, DBT and enthalpy rise — winter room heater; Sensible cooling: horizontal line to the left until saturation — coil above dew point; Cooling and dehumidification: line bends down along the saturation curve, both DBT and humidity ratio fall — summer coil below dew point; Evaporative cooling: constant wet-bulb line, DBT falls and humidity ratio rises — desert cooler; Apparatus dew point and bypass factor may be mentioned as extra detail.

Evaluate Compare steam tables, the Mollier chart and a throttling calorimeter as ways of obtaining the enthalpy of wet steam in a plant trial. State the accuracy, speed and range of each and recommend one for a routine boiler test. 12 marks

Tables: most accurate inside the printed range, interpolation needed, slow by hand; Mollier chart: fast for combined processes and expansions, reading error of the order of a few kJ/kg; Calorimeter: measures the actual steam in the pipe, so it captures the true x including carry-over, but needs dry-enough steam and instruments; Recommendation: calorimeter for x, tables or chart for the rest of the properties, chart for sketching the process; Sources of error: heat loss in the throttle, kinetic energy, gauge errors, interpolation.

Evaluate Saturated steam at 150 °C has p_sat = 4.758 bar and v_g = 0.3925 m³/kg. Compute the specific volume from pv = RT, find Z, and judge whether the ideal gas law can be used for steam near saturation. 10 marks

R for steam = 0.4615 kJ/kg·K, T = 423.15 K, p = 475.8 kPa; Ideal v = RT/p = 0.4104 m³/kg; Z = 0.3925/0.4104 ≈ 0.956; Error in v is about 4.6%, in the same direction as condensation tendency; Conclusion: acceptable for rough work at low pressure, not acceptable when h or mass flow must be accurate; use tables or a real-gas equation near the dome.

Glossary

Amagat's law
The volume of an ideal gas mixture equals the sum of the volumes each component would occupy at the mixture pressure and temperature.
Degree of superheat
The difference between the actual temperature of superheated steam and the saturation temperature at the same pressure.
Dew-point temperature
The temperature at which moist air becomes saturated when cooled at constant pressure and humidity ratio.
Dry air
Air with the water vapour removed; it is the reference mass for humidity ratio calculations.
Dry saturated steam
Steam with x = 1, containing no liquid droplets, at the saturation temperature for its pressure.
Dry-bulb temperature
The ordinary temperature of moist air measured by a thermometer with a dry bulb.
Enthalpy of evaporation
h_fg, the heat needed to convert 1 kg of saturated liquid into saturated vapour at constant pressure.
Partial pressure
The pressure a single component of a gas mixture would exert if it alone filled the same volume at the same temperature.
Principle of corresponding states
All substances at the same reduced pressure and temperature have nearly the same compressibility factor Z.
Quality
Another name for dryness fraction; the vapour mass fraction in a saturated liquid-vapour mixture.
Real gas
A gas whose behaviour departs from pv = RT because its molecules have volume and interact.
Reduced pressure
p_r = p/p_c, the pressure divided by the critical pressure of the substance.
Reduced temperature
T_r = T/T_c, the absolute temperature divided by the critical temperature.
Relative humidity
φ = p_v/p_sat at the dry-bulb temperature, the ratio of actual vapour pressure to the saturation value.
Saturation pressure
The pressure at which a pure substance boils at a given temperature.
Saturation temperature
The boiling or condensing temperature that belongs to a given pressure for a pure substance.
Sensible heat
Heat that changes the temperature of a single phase without changing the phase, equal to m·c·ΔT for a constant specific heat.
Subcooling
The amount by which a liquid is cooler than its saturation temperature at the given pressure.
Superheated steam
Steam above its saturation temperature for the given pressure; it behaves roughly as a gas.
Throttling
A steady-flow expansion through a restriction with no work and negligible heat transfer, so enthalpy stays constant.
Wet steam
A mixture of saturated liquid droplets and saturated vapour in equilibrium, with 0 < x < 1.
Wet-bulb temperature
The temperature reached by a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in wet cloth and exposed to moving air.
