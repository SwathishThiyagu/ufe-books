2.1 First law for closed systems: cycle and process

The first law of thermodynamics says energy is conserved. For a closed system, the heat added minus the work done equals the change in the energy stored inside; over a complete cycle, net heat equals net work. This one balance finds an unknown heat, work or energy change in any process, gives the area inside a p-V loop a meaning, and rules out any machine that claims to create energy.

You need: the closed system, state, path and cycle, and the sign convention for work and heat (see Work and heat: definitions and sign convention, and Displacement work and other work modes).

Energy and its forms

A system stores energy in three forms: kinetic energy KE = mV²/2 of the system moving as a whole at velocity V (m/s); potential energy PE = mgz of its height z (m) above a datum, with g = 9.81 m/s²; and internal energy U, the energy of the molecules themselves. The total stored energy is E = U + KE + PE, in kJ.

Energy crosses the boundary of a closed system in only two ways: as heat Q, driven by a temperature difference, and as work W (a moving piston, a turning shaft, an electric current). Heat and work are energy in transit, not stored energy.

Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = E₂ − E₁ = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE

Q₁₂ is the heat added to the system from state 1 to state 2 (kJ); W₁₂ is the work done by the system (kJ); ΔU, ΔKE and ΔPE are the changes in internal, kinetic and potential energy (kJ).

Most exam systems do not move as a whole, so ΔKE = ΔPE = 0 and the law takes its usual form below. Keep the full form for a falling or moving system.

One balance, two forms

Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = U₂ − U₁ = ΔU

the process form for a stationary closed system; all terms in kJ.

Signs: heat added to the system and work done by the system are positive; heat rejected and work done on the system are negative. Chemistry books count W as work done on the system and write Q + W = ΔU; both are correct if used consistently, but this book uses Q − W = ΔU throughout.

δQ − δW = dU

δQ and δW are small heat and work transfers in an infinitesimal step, written with δ because they depend on the path; dU is the small change of the property U.

Over a complete cycle the system returns to state 1, so ΔU = 0 and the balance becomes ∮δQ = ∮δW, where ∮ means the sum around the whole cycle: net heat in equals net work out. A control mass means the same as a closed system.

Heat and work follow the path; U does not

U depends only on the state, so ΔU between two states is fixed; Q and W depend on the route.

Moving boundary work is the area under the process curve

When a piston moves slowly enough for the gas to stay in equilibrium (a quasi-static process), the work done by the gas is the area under its path on a p-V diagram:

W₁₂ = ∫₁² p dV

p is the absolute pressure of the gas (kPa) and dV the small volume change (m³); the integral runs along the actual path. W comes out in kJ when p is in kPa and V in m³. At constant pressure it is simply p(V₂ − V₁).

Expansion gives W > 0, compression W < 0. Two paths between the same end states have different areas under their curves, which is why W depends on the path. For a cycle the net work is the area enclosed by the loop, positive when it runs clockwise, and it equals the net heat.

∫p dV needs a quasi-static path. A gas rushing into a vacuum when a membrane bursts (free expansion) grows in volume but pushes on nothing, so W = 0. Slow, resisted expansion is the ideal of a reversible process; a sudden one is irreversible (see Reversible and irreversible processes).

Worked example

Question. A gas in a piston-cylinder device receives 80 kJ of heat and does 30 kJ of work as it goes from state 1 to state 2. (a) Find the change in internal energy. (b) The gas is then brought back to state 1 along a path in which 20 kJ of work is done on the gas. Find the heat transfer for the return path. (c) Verify the cycle form of the first law.

  1. Path 1 → 2: U₂ − U₁ = Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = 80 − 30 = +50 kJ.
  2. For the return path 2 → 1 the internal-energy change reverses: U₁ − U₂ = −50 kJ.
  3. Work is done on the gas, so W₂₁ = −20 kJ by the sign convention.
  4. Rearrange: Q₂₁ = ΔU + W₂₁ = −50 + (−20) = −70 kJ. The gas rejects 70 kJ of heat.
  5. Net heat over the cycle: Qnet = 80 − 70 = 10 kJ. Net work: Wnet = 30 − 20 = 10 kJ.
  6. Qnet = Wnet = 10 kJ, so ∮δQ = ∮δW is satisfied, and the loop of Figure 2.1.1 encloses 10 kJ.

Answer. ΔU = +50 kJ for 1 → 2; the return path rejects 70 kJ of heat; over the cycle Qnet = Wnet = 10 kJ.

Worked example 2

Question. Gas in a cylinder at a constant 200 kPa expands from 0.05 m³ to 0.15 m³ while 50 kJ of heat is added. Find the boundary work and the change in internal energy.

  1. Constant pressure: W = p(V₂ − V₁) = 200 × (0.15 − 0.05) = 20 kJ (kPa × m³ = kJ), positive, so done by the gas.
  2. First law: ΔU = Q − W = 50 − 20 = 30 kJ.

Answer. W = 20 kJ done by the gas and ΔU = +30 kJ; of the 50 kJ added, 30 kJ stays in the gas and 20 kJ leaves as work.

Exam traps

  • Calling Q or W a property: “the heat of state 2” is wrong; only U (and E) is stored and fixed by the state.
  • Sign slip when work is done on the system: W = −20 kJ, not +20 kJ. Taking +20 in Q = ΔU + W gives −50 + 20 = −30 kJ instead of the correct −70 kJ.
  • Using ∫p dV for a free expansion into a vacuum. The volume changes but the gas pushes on nothing, so W = 0.
  • Using Q − W = ΔU for a turbine, nozzle or any open system. Mass carries energy in and out there; use the steady flow energy equation.

U is fixed by the state, so ΔU between two states is the same along every path. Q is energy in transit and changes with the path, so it belongs to the process, not the state.

Apply A gas absorbs 120 kJ of heat and its internal energy falls by 40 kJ. Find the work done, with its sign. 4 marks

Q − W = ΔU, so W = Q − ΔU = 120 − (−40) = 160 kJ. Positive, so 160 kJ of work is done by the gas.

Apply A 2 kg stone falls 10 m inside a closed, insulated box and comes to rest at the bottom. What happens to its 196 J of potential energy? 3 marks

Q = 0 and W = 0 for the box, so ΔE = 0: ΔU + ΔPE = 0. PE falls by mgz = 2 × 9.81 × 10 = 196.2 J, so U of the box contents rises by 196.2 J; the stone and floor warm slightly.

Analyze A student writes Q + W = ΔU but takes W as positive when done by the system. Show with the cycle form why this mix fails. 5 marks

Around a cycle ΔU = 0, so the student gets Qnet = −Wnet: an engine that delivers work would need negative net heat, which contradicts ∮δQ = ∮δW. Q + W = ΔU is right only when W means work done on the system; with W done by the system the law is Q − W = ΔU.

2.2 Internal energy and enthalpy

Internal energy U is the energy stored inside matter: the kinetic energy of its molecules plus the potential energy of the forces between them. Enthalpy H = U + pV adds the flow work pV to U. The two terms always appear together when mass crosses a boundary, so H gets its own name. You meet u in a rigid tank and h in boilers, turbines and heat exchangers. Both are state properties, in kJ or, per kilogram, in kJ/kg.

You need: the first law for a closed system, Q − W = ΔU, and the ideal-gas equation pV = mRT.

Internal energy is energy stored inside matter

Internal energy U is the sum of the kinetic energy of the molecules (translation, rotation, vibration) and the potential energy of the forces between them. It does not include the kinetic or potential energy of the whole system moving or raised. The unit is kJ, and the specific internal energy is u = U/m in kJ/kg, where m is the mass in kg.

U is a state property. For a simple substance two independent properties fix the state, and so fix u: pressure and temperature in a single-phase gas or liquid, but pressure and dryness fraction in a wet steam mixture, where p and T are tied together. U is extensive: doubling the mass doubles U. Thermodynamics never needs absolute U, only changes ΔU.

For an ideal gas the molecules exert no forces on one another, so u depends on temperature alone and

ΔU = m cv ΔT

m is the mass (kg); cv is the specific heat at constant volume (kJ/kg·K); ΔT is the final minus the initial temperature (K); ΔU is in kJ. Valid for an ideal gas with constant cv.

For steam and refrigerants u also depends on pressure, so it is read from property tables (see Steam tables and Mollier chart).

Enthalpy adds flow work to internal energy

Fluid entering a control volume is pushed in by the fluid behind it. That push-in work per kg is pv, where p is the absolute pressure in kPa and v the specific volume in m³/kg (kPa × m³/kg = kJ/kg). Each entering kilogram carries its internal energy u and this flow work pv. Enthalpy bundles them:

H = U + pV, h = u + pv

H is the enthalpy (kJ), U the internal energy (kJ), p the absolute pressure (kPa), V the volume (m³); h, u and v are the same per kilogram (kJ/kg, kJ/kg, m³/kg).

Take changes: ΔH = ΔU + Δ(pV). For an ideal gas pV = mRT, where R is the gas constant in kJ/kg·K, so Δ(pV) = mRΔT and ΔH = m(cv + R)ΔT = mcpΔT. Why cp − cv = R is shown in Specific heats of gases.

Three places where heat equals an enthalpy change: a closed system at constant pressure with only boundary work (Q = ΔH); a steady-flow heater, boiler or condenser with no shaft work and negligible changes of kinetic and potential energy (q = h₂ − h₁); and a phase change at constant pressure, where the latent heat is an enthalpy change such as hfg for steam.

Enthalpy is not heat. “Heat content” is a loose old name. Heat Q is energy in transit and depends on the path; H is a state property. Q equals ΔH only in the cases above.

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of air (R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K) is heated at constant pressure from 300 K to 400 K. Find ΔU, ΔH and Δ(pV), and the heat supplied.

  1. Data: m = 2 kg, T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K, so ΔT = 100 K.
  2. ΔU = mcvΔT = 2 × 0.718 × 100 = 143.6 kJ.
  3. ΔH = mcpΔT = 2 × 1.005 × 100 = 201.0 kJ.
  4. Δ(pV) = mRΔT = 2 × 0.287 × 100 = 57.4 kJ. At constant pressure this is also the boundary work W = pΔV.
  5. Check: ΔU + Δ(pV) = 143.6 + 57.4 = 201.0 kJ = ΔH.
  6. Constant pressure, closed system: Q = ΔH = 201.0 kJ. The first law agrees: Q = ΔU + W = 143.6 + 57.4 = 201.0 kJ.

Answer. ΔU = 143.6 kJ, ΔH = 201.0 kJ, Δ(pV) = 57.4 kJ, and the heat supplied is 201.0 kJ.

Worked example 2

Question. Steam tables give, for dry saturated steam at 1 MPa (1000 kPa): ug = 2582.8 kJ/kg and vg = 0.1944 m³/kg. Find hg from h = u + pv.

  1. Flow work: pv = 1000 kPa × 0.1944 m³/kg = 194.4 kJ/kg.
  2. h = u + pv = 2582.8 + 194.4 = 2777.2 kJ/kg.
  3. The table lists hg = 2777.1 kJ/kg at 1 MPa; the 0.1 kJ/kg gap is rounding of v.

Answer. hg = 2777.2 kJ/kg, which matches the table: about 7 % of the enthalpy of this steam is flow work.

Exam traps

  • Using ΔH = mcpΔT as the heat for a closed rigid tank. A rigid tank has Q = ΔU = mcvΔT.
  • Adding R = 287 J/kg·K to cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K. Convert R to 0.287 kJ/kg·K first.
  • Writing ΔH = ΔU + pΔV in every process. The general form is ΔH = ΔU + Δ(pV); pΔV is enough only at constant pressure.
  • Writing q = Δh for a nozzle because it has no shaft work. There q = 0 and the enthalpy drop becomes kinetic energy.
  • Using cpΔT for steam or refrigerants. Their h depends on both T and p; read it from tables.

2.3 Specific heats of gases

A specific heat is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of a gas by 1 K. At constant volume the heat goes only into internal energy, so q = cvΔT. At constant pressure the gas also expands and does work, so q = cpΔT and cp is larger. For an ideal gas cp − cv = R. These numbers size compressors, engine cylinders and air-conditioning coils.

You need Internal energy and enthalpy for u, h and h = u + pv.

Two specific heats

cv = (∂u/∂T)v, cp = (∂h/∂T)p

u and h are the specific internal energy and enthalpy (kJ/kg); T is the temperature (K); the subscript outside the bracket is the property held fixed. Both specific heats are in kJ/kg·K.

For an ideal gas u and h depend only on T, so du = cv dT and dh = cp dT along any path, not only at constant volume or pressure. In a rigid vessel no boundary moves, so W = 0 and Q = mcvΔT. In a cylinder at constant pressure the piston rises and the gas does work pΔV, so the same ΔT needs extra heat: Q = mcpΔT.

For 1 kg of ideal gas at constant pressure, the work is pΔv = RΔT. The first law then gives cpΔT = cvΔT + RΔT, so cp − cv = R: at constant volume every kilojoule of heat stays in the gas, at constant pressure part of it leaves as work.

The relation cp − cv = R and the ratio γ

cp − cv = R, γ = cp/cv

R is the gas constant of the gas (kJ/kg·K), equal to the universal gas constant Ru = 8.314 kJ/kmol·K divided by the molar mass M (kg/kmol); γ is the specific-heat ratio, a pure number. Many books call the first equation Mayer’s relation.

cv = R/(γ − 1), cp = γR/(γ − 1)

obtained by solving the two equations above together; useful when a question gives only R and γ.

Typical values: monatomic gases such as helium and argon γ ≈ 1.67; diatomic gases such as air, N₂ and O₂ γ ≈ 1.4; polyatomic gases such as CO₂ and steam γ ≈ 1.3. For air near 300 K: cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K. The specific heats of an ideal gas still rise with temperature, because molecules start to vibrate; over a range of several hundred kelvin use mean values or gas tables. The general form of the relation for any substance is part of Expansivity, compressibility, Mayer relation and Joule-Thomson coefficient.

Worked example

Question. Air has cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K and cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K. Find R and γ. Check R using M = 28.97 kg/kmol and Ru = 8.314 kJ/kmol·K. Then find the heat needed to warm 2 kg of air by 20 K in a rigid tank and in a constant-pressure cylinder.

  1. R = cp − cv = 1.005 − 0.718 = 0.287 kJ/kg·K.
  2. γ = cp/cv = 1.005/0.718 = 1.3997 ≈ 1.40.
  3. Molar check: R = Ru/M = 8.314/28.97 = 0.28699 ≈ 0.287 kJ/kg·K. The two values agree.
  4. Rigid tank: Q = mcvΔT = 2 × 0.718 × 20 = 28.72 kJ.
  5. Constant pressure: Q = mcpΔT = 2 × 1.005 × 20 = 40.20 kJ.
  6. The difference 40.20 − 28.72 = 11.48 kJ is the boundary work mRΔT = 2 × 0.287 × 20 = 11.48 kJ.

Answer. R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K and γ = 1.40; the rigid tank needs 28.72 kJ, the cylinder 40.20 kJ, and the 11.48 kJ gap is the work of expansion.

Exam traps

  • Using cp for a rigid vessel. For air this overestimates Q by the factor γ ≈ 1.4, that is by 40 %.
  • Mixing R = 287 J/kg·K with cp and cv in kJ/kg·K. Convert R to 0.287 kJ/kg·K first.
  • Using air’s values for every gas. R = Ru/M and γ change from gas to gas.
  • Applying cp − cv = R to a liquid or a vapour near saturation. It is an ideal-gas relation; for liquids cp ≈ cv.

2.4 Non-flow processes: isothermal, adiabatic, polytropic

A closed system can follow many paths between two states. One family obeys pVn = C, where p is the pressure, V the volume, n the polytropic index and C a constant. The value of n fixes the process and gives ready formulas for work and heat. Engine cylinders, reciprocating compressors and gas springs follow these paths. This chapter integrates p dV once and then reads the isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric and isochoric processes as special cases.

You need: the first law and moving boundary work, W = ∫p dV, and the specific heats cv, cp and γ.

One family of paths

A polytropic process follows pVn = C, with n a constant pure number. For an ideal gas pV = mRT, so the path also fixes T at every point. The index picks out the familiar processes: n = 0 gives constant pressure (isobaric); n = 1 gives pV constant, which for an ideal gas is constant temperature (isothermal); n = γ = cp/cv gives the reversible adiabatic; n → ∞ gives constant volume (isochoric). W is work done by the gas and Q heat added to it, so compression has V₂ < V₁ and W < 0.

Work from the integral

Put p = C/Vn into W = ∫p dV from V₁ to V₂ and integrate:

W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1) = mR(T₁ − T₂)/(n − 1)

valid for n ≠ 1; p in kPa, V in m³, T in K, m in kg, R in kJ/kg·K, W in kJ, positive when the gas does work.

For n = 1 the denominator is zero, so integrate p = C/V directly:

W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = mRT ln(p₁/p₂)

ln is the natural logarithm. For an ideal gas at constant T, ΔU = 0, so Q = W: in isothermal compression, work goes in and the same amount of heat comes out.

The end states of any polytropic path are linked by p₁V₁n = p₂V₂n and, for an ideal gas,

T₂/T₁ = (V₁/V₂)n−1 = (p₂/p₁)(n−1)/n

put n = γ for the reversible adiabatic. In compression V₁/V₂ > 1, so T₂ > T₁ whenever n > 1.

Adiabatic and polytropic heat

Adiabatic means Q = 0. For a reversible adiabatic of an ideal gas with constant specific heats the path is pVγ = C, and the first law gives W = −ΔU = mcv(T₁ − T₂). In compression W < 0 and T rises; in expansion the work comes out of the internal energy and T falls.

For a general polytropic path, find Q from the first law, Q = ΔU + W, or in one line with the polytropic specific heat:

Q = m cn(T₂ − T₁), cn = cv(n − γ)/(n − 1)

cn (kJ/kg·K) belongs to the path, not the gas; use it for n ≠ 1. Checks: n = γ gives cn = 0 (adiabatic), n = 0 gives cp, n → ∞ gives cv.

When 1 < n < γ, cn is negative: a compression that heats the gas still rejects heat, and an expansion that cools the gas still takes heat in. On a p-V diagram the adiabatic p = C/Vγ is steeper than the isothermal p = C/V because γ > 1, and a polytropic path with 1 < n < γ lies between them (Figure 2.4.1). The same paths in flow machines are handled with the steady flow energy equation.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of air at 300 K and 100 kPa is compressed to one-eighth of its volume. Find the final pressure, final temperature, work and heat if the process is (a) isothermal and (b) reversible adiabatic. Take R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4.

2.5 Perpetual motion machine of the first kind

A perpetual motion machine of the first kind, PMM-1, is a claimed device that creates energy. It either gives net work in a complete cycle with no energy input, or gives more work than the heat supplied to it, an efficiency above 100 percent. The first law forbids both, because energy is conserved. Only a few syllabi list PMM-1 as a separate topic; most teach it in a line beside the first-law statement. Engineers use the idea to reject impossible energy claims with one cycle balance, before any money is spent.

You need the cycle form of the first law, ∮δQ = ∮δW.

The two claims and the cycle balance

∮δQ = ∮δW

for any cycle: the net heat added (kJ) equals the net work done by the system (kJ), because the system returns to its starting state and ΔU = 0.

A PMM-1 claim comes in two forms. Form 1: a machine runs in a cycle, exchanges no heat and still gives net work. Form 2: an engine gives more net work Wnet than the heat Qin supplied to it. The cycle balance rejects both. With Qnet = 0 it gives Wnet = 0, so a no-heat cycle cannot deliver work. And since Wnet = Qnet = Qin − Qout with Qout ≥ 0, the work can never exceed Qin.

The converse statement is often quoted too: there can be no machine that delivers work continuously without some other form of energy disappearing at the same time.

Efficiency, and how to test a claim

η = Wnet/Qin = 1 − Qout/Qin

η is the thermal efficiency (a pure number, often given in percent); Wnet is the net work out per cycle, Qin the heat supplied and Qout the heat rejected, all in the same unit.

Since Qout ≥ 0, η ≤ 1. A claimed efficiency of 125 percent means 125 kJ of work for every 100 kJ of heat supplied; the extra 25 kJ, a quarter of the input, would be created from nothing.

  1. Put every energy flow on one basis: all in kJ per cycle, or all in kW.
  2. Find Qin, Qout and Wnet from the claim.
  3. If Wnet > Qin − Qout, energy is created: the claim is a PMM-1.
  4. If Wnet is less than Qin, that alone is not a violation: the rest is heat rejected, which every real engine has.

A perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM-2, is different. It conserves energy but turns all the heat from a single reservoir into work. The first law cannot reject it; the second law does (see Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements).

Worked example

Question. Test three claimed cyclic engines. (a) Takes in 300 kW of heat, delivers 500 kW of shaft work and exchanges nothing else. (b) Takes in 200 kW of heat and delivers 250 kW of work. (c) Takes in 80 kW of heat, rejects 20 kW to a river and delivers 60 kW of work.

  1. (a) Qnet = 300 kW and Wnet = 500 kW. The cycle balance needs Wnet = Qnet; the excess 500 − 300 = 200 kW would be created. PMM-1, impossible.
  2. (b) η = Wnet/Qin = 250/200 = 1.25, that is 125 percent. Even with Qout = 0 the work can be at most 200 kW; 50 kW would be created. PMM-1, impossible.
  3. (c) Qnet = 80 − 20 = 60 kW = Wnet. The balance closes, so the first law is satisfied.
  4. For (c), η = 60/80 = 0.75, or 75 percent. Whether 75 percent is reachable between the source and river temperatures is a second-law question, not a first-law one.

Answer. Claims (a) and (b) create 200 kW and 50 kW and are PMM-1; claim (c) satisfies the first law with η = 75 percent.

Exam traps

  • Calling a low-efficiency engine a PMM-1. Low efficiency means heat rejected, not energy created.
  • Using Qin where the balance needs Qnet: an engine that takes 80 kW and rejects 20 kW has Qnet = 60 kW.
  • Confusing PMM-1 with PMM-2. PMM-1 breaks the first law; PMM-2 obeys it and breaks the second.
  • Mixing kW with kJ per cycle in one balance. kW is a rate; convert to one basis first.

2.6 Steady flow energy equation and its applications

A turbine, compressor, nozzle and boiler all keep the same account: energy carried in by the fluid plus heat added equals energy carried out plus work delivered. Because mass crosses the boundary, the energy each kilogram carries is its enthalpy plus its kinetic and potential energy, not its internal energy. Written once, with terms struck out for each device, this steady flow energy equation answers exam questions on nozzles, turbines, throttles, condensers and pumps. It is the working tool of power plants, refrigerators and air-conditioners.

You need: enthalpy, h = u + pv, and the first law with its sign convention.

The control volume and mass balance

An open system lets mass cross its boundary. We draw a fixed region in space around the device, the control volume: a turbine casing, a compressor, a radiator, a length of pipe. The fluid crosses it at the inlet 1 and the outlet 2.

ṁ = ρAV = AV/v

ṁ is the mass flow rate (kg/s); ρ the density (kg/m³); A the flow area (m²); V the mean velocity (m/s); v = 1/ρ the specific volume (m³/kg).

The steady flow energy equation (SFEE) rests on four assumptions:

  • Steady flow: nothing inside changes with time, so mass in equals mass out, ṁ₁ = ṁ₂ = ṁ (the continuity equation, A₁V₁/v₁ = A₂V₂/v₂).
  • Uniform properties across each inlet and outlet section.
  • Constant rates of heat and work transfer.
  • No change in the mass or energy stored inside the control volume.

The steady flow energy equation

Per kilogram of fluid, energy in equals energy out:

h₁ + V₁²/2000 + gz₁/1000 + q = h₂ + V₂²/2000 + gz₂/1000 + w

h is the specific enthalpy (kJ/kg); V the velocity (m/s); z the height above a datum (m); g = 9.81 m/s²; q the heat added (kJ/kg, negative if lost); w the shaft work delivered (kJ/kg, negative if work is put in). Subscript 1 is the inlet, 2 the outlet.

The 2000 and 1000 are unit fixes. V²/2 comes out in J/kg, so V²/2000 is in kJ/kg; likewise gz/1000. Adding J/kg to an h in kJ/kg is the classic error.

Why enthalpy? The fluid behind the inlet pushes each kilogram in, doing flow work p₁v₁ on it, and each kilogram leaving pushes the fluid ahead, doing p₂v₂. This flow work travels with the mass, and h = u + pv already contains it, so u never appears alone.

Multiply every term by ṁ for the rate form in kW: Q̇ = ṁq is the heat transfer rate and Ẇ = ṁw the shaft power.

Applications: strike out the terms

Every device is the same equation with some terms removed. Learn the striking out, not eight separate formulas.

DeviceTerms droppedResult
Nozzle (speeds fluid up) or diffuser (slows it)w = 0, q ≈ 0, Δz = 0h₁ + V₁²/2000 = h₂ + V₂²/2000
TurbineΔKE, ΔPE small; q ≈ 0 if insulatedw = h₁ − h₂ (+ q if given)
Compressor or fanΔKE, ΔPE smallwin = h₂ − h₁ − q
Pump (liquid)ΔKE, ΔPE small, q ≈ 0win = h₂ − h₁ ≈ v(p₂ − p₁)
Boiler, condenser, heaterw = 0, ΔKE, ΔPE smallq = h₂ − h₁
Throttle valvew = 0, q ≈ 0, ΔKE smallh₁ = h₂ (isenthalpic)

In a throttle the pressure drops but h stays the same. An ideal gas then keeps its temperature, because its h depends on T alone. Most real gases and refrigerants cool, which is why a refrigerator has an expansion valve; hydrogen and helium at room temperature warm up instead.

A heat exchanger has two streams and no work. If it is insulated, the energy one stream loses the other gains: ṁhot(hin − hout)hot = ṁcold(hout − hin)cold.

Filling or emptying a tank is not steady, because the mass inside changes; that case has its own balance in Unsteady flow processes: filling and emptying of vessels.

Worked example

Question. Steam enters a turbine at 2 kg/s with h₁ = 3200 kJ/kg, V₁ = 60 m/s and z₁ = 5 m. It leaves with h₂ = 2600 kJ/kg, V₂ = 150 m/s and z₂ = 0. The casing loses 20 kJ of heat per kg of steam. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the work output per kg and the power.

  1. Solve the SFEE for w: w = (h₁ − h₂) + (V₁² − V₂²)/2000 + g(z₁ − z₂)/1000 + q.
  2. Enthalpy drop: h₁ − h₂ = 3200 − 2600 = 600 kJ/kg.
  3. Kinetic term: V₁² = 3600 m²/s² and V₂² = 22 500 m²/s², so (3600 − 22 500)/2000 = −18 900/2000 = −9.45 kJ/kg.
  4. Potential term: 9.81 × 5/1000 = 0.049 kJ/kg.
  5. Heat is lost, so q = −20 kJ/kg.
  6. w = 600 − 9.45 + 0.049 − 20 = 570.6 kJ/kg.
  7. Power Ẇ = ṁw = 2 × 570.6 = 1141.2 kW.

Answer. w = 570.6 kJ/kg and the power is 1141.2 kW, about 1.14 MW. The kinetic term changed the answer by under 2 percent and the height term by under 0.01 percent.

2.7 Unsteady flow processes: filling and emptying of vessels

Filling a gas cylinder from a compressor line, pumping up a tyre and letting air out of a receiver are not steady: the mass inside the vessel changes with time. The steady flow energy equation does not apply, but the same bookkeeping does, written for the whole process: energy brought in by the mass that enters, minus energy carried out by the mass that leaves, plus heat minus work, equals the change in the energy stored inside. This chapter gives that balance and uses it to explain a surprise: a tank filled from a line at room temperature ends up hot.

You need: the steady flow energy equation and flow work, and internal energy and enthalpy of an ideal gas.

Mass and energy balances for a changing vessel

Take the vessel as the control volume. Let m₁ and m₂ be the masses inside at the start and the end, mi the mass that enters through the inlet during the process and me the mass that leaves through the outlet, all in kg. Mass is conserved:

mi − me = m₂ − m₁

mass in minus mass out equals the gain of mass inside (kg).

Most textbook problems use the uniform-state, uniform-flow model (some syllabi call it USUF, the steady case being SSSF). The fluid inside is uniform at every instant, and the fluid crossing each opening has fixed properties hi or he. With kinetic and potential energy neglected, the energy balance for the whole process is

Q − W + mihi − mehe = m₂u₂ − m₁u₁

Q is the heat added and W the work done by the contents during the whole process (kJ); hi and he are the specific enthalpies of the entering and leaving fluid (kJ/kg); u₁ and u₂ are the specific internal energies inside at the start and the end (kJ/kg).

The entering and leaving streams carry h, because each kilogram that crosses an opening has flow work pv done on it or by it, as in steady flow. The contents are stored mass, so they carry u. For a rigid tank W = 0 unless a shaft or paddle is fitted.

Filling: why the tank gets hot

Fill an insulated, rigid, evacuated tank from a supply line at fixed pressure pi and temperature Ti. Then Q = 0, W = 0, m₁ = 0 and me = 0, so m₂ = mi and the balance shrinks to

u₂ = hi

the final internal energy per kg inside equals the enthalpy per kg of the line gas.

The gas arrives with its own ui plus the flow work pivi done on it by the line gas pushing it in. Inside the closed tank that push becomes internal energy. For an ideal gas with u = cvT and h = cpT,

T₂ = (cp/cv) Ti = γTi

Ti is the line temperature (K); γ is the specific-heat ratio. The answer does not depend on the tank volume or the final pressure.

Air from a line at 300 K therefore fills an insulated empty tank at 420 K. If the tank already holds gas, or loses heat to the room, T₂ lies between Ti and γTi; find it from the full balance together with p₂V = m₂RT₂. A tank filled slowly enough to stay at Ti must reject heat Q = m₂(u₂ − hi) = −m₂RTi = −p₂V.

Emptying: the gas left behind cools

Now let an insulated tank discharge through a valve. Gas leaves at the state inside the tank, so for a small mass dme leaving, the balance is d(mu) = −h dme = h dm, where m is the mass still inside and dm = −dme is its (negative) change. For an ideal gas:

mcv dT + cvT dm = cpT dm, so dT/T = (γ − 1) dm/m

obtained with cp − cv = R and R/cv = γ − 1.

Integrating and using m = pV/(RT) gives T₂/T₁ = (p₂/p₁)(γ−1)/γ: the gas that stays behind expands exactly as in a reversible adiabatic process. This is why a cylinder valve frosts when gas is let out quickly.

2.8 Limitations of the first law

The first law of thermodynamics is an energy ledger. For a closed system, heat added minus work done equals the change in stored energy, and over a cycle net heat equals net work. The ledger always balances, but it does not say which of two balanced processes will actually happen, or how much of a heat supply can become work. Those gaps matter when you judge a heat engine, a refrigerator or a claim of a machine that turns all its heat into work. This chapter lists what the first law cannot decide and names the law that fills each gap.

You need the first law for a closed system and the perpetual motion machine of the first kind.

A balance sheet, not a compass

A cup of hot water left in a room cools. Heat leaves the water and enters the room. Now write the same number of kilojoules in the other direction: the room gives that heat back to the cup and the cup warms up again. Both versions satisfy Q − W = ΔU exactly. Only the first ever happens on its own. The first law cannot choose between them.

That is the first limitation: no direction. A second follows from a paddle wheel stirring water in an insulated tank. All the shaft work becomes internal energy of the water. The reverse, warm water spinning the paddle and cooling down, also balances, and never happens. Work can always turn completely into heat; heat cannot turn completely into work in a cycle. A kilojoule of work and a kilojoule of heat are equal in amount but not in usefulness, and the first law, which counts only amounts, misses the difference.

Five limits to state clearly

Use these five points as an exam checklist. Each is a job the first law cannot do.

  • No direction. Heat flowing from hot to cold and from cold to hot both balance energy; the first law does not say which is spontaneous.
  • No quality. Work and heat are both counted in kJ; the first law does not rank work above heat, or heat at 800 °C above heat at 40 °C.
  • It allows a perpetual motion machine of the second kind (PMM-2): a cyclic engine that takes heat from one reservoir and turns all of it into work. Q = W, so the first law is satisfied; the second law forbids it.
  • No criterion for equilibrium. The first law cannot say whether a process that conserves energy will occur, or where it will stop.
  • No difference between reversible and irreversible paths. A slow, resisted expansion and a sudden one between the same end states both satisfy Q − W = ΔU, even though their Q and W differ and the sudden one wastes the chance to do work.

Do not mix up the two perpetual motion machines. A PMM-1 creates energy and fails the first law; a PMM-2 conserves energy and fails the second. The first law is still necessary: any process that fails the energy balance is impossible at once. It is a filter, not the full judge.

What fills the gap

The missing rules come from the second law of thermodynamics. Its two classical statements say that heat does not flow on its own from cold to hot, and that no cyclic engine can turn all its heat input into work (see Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements). Entropy turns the direction into a number: the entropy of an isolated system rises in every real process and can never fall (see Principle of increase of entropy).

The second law also measures quality. Work is high-grade energy; heat is worth less the closer its temperature is to the surroundings. Available energy and exergy put a number on how much of an energy stream could become work (see Available and unavailable energy). The worked example below shows both laws applied to the cooling cup.

Worked example

Question. A cup holds 0.2 kg of water at 80 °C in a room at 25 °C. Take c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K for water. (a) Find the heat the water loses as it cools to room temperature. (b) Show that the reverse process also satisfies the first law, and use the entropy change of the cup and the room to show which direction is possible.

  1. Data: m = 0.2 kg, c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K, T₁ = 80 °C = 353.15 K, T₂ = 25 °C = 298.15 K, ΔT = 55 K.
  2. Q = mcΔT = 0.2 × 4.18 × 55 = 0.836 × 55 = 45.98 kJ, about 46.0 kJ, which the water rejects to the room.
  3. Reverse: the room gives 46.0 kJ to the water and it warms from 25 °C back to 80 °C. The energy balance closes with the same 46.0 kJ, so the first law allows both directions.
  4. Entropy change of the water as it cools: ΔScup = mc ln(T₂/T₁) = 0.836 × ln(298.15/353.15) = 0.836 × (−0.1693) = −0.1415 kJ/K.
  5. The room is so large that its temperature stays 298.15 K: ΔSroom = Q/T = 45.98/298.15 = +0.1542 kJ/K.
  6. Total: ΔS = −0.1415 + 0.1542 = +0.0127 kJ/K > 0, so cooling is possible. The reverse would give −0.0127 kJ/K, a fall in the entropy of the isolated cup-and-room system, which the second law forbids.

Answer. The water loses 46.0 kJ. Both directions balance energy, but only cooling raises the total entropy (by 0.0127 kJ/K), so only cooling happens.

Exam traps

  • Saying a cold cup warming on its own would break the first law. Its energy balance is perfect; it breaks the second law.
  • Calling a PMM-2 a first-law violator. A PMM-2 satisfies the first law and fails the second; a PMM-1 fails the first.
  • Writing “heat can never become work”. Heat cannot become work completely in a cycle; a single process such as an isothermal expansion can turn all the heat it takes in into work.
  • Treating a kilojoule of work and a kilojoule of heat as equally useful. The first law counts both; the second law ranks them.

Understand State the first law of thermodynamics for a closed system undergoing (a) a process and (b) a complete cycle. Explain the sign convention you use, and show that the two statements are consistent. 8 marks

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Energy stored and energy in transit. A closed system holds a fixed mass. It stores energy E = U + KE + PE: internal energy U of its molecules, kinetic energy mV²/2 and potential energy mgz of the system as a whole. Energy crosses its boundary only as heat Q (driven by a temperature difference) and work W (a moving boundary, a shaft, an electric current).
  2. (a) Process form. For a change from state 1 to state 2:
    Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = E₂ − E₁ = ΔU + ΔKE + ΔPE
for a stationary system:  Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = U₂ − U₁
differential form:        δQ − δW = dU
  3. Q₁₂ is the heat added and W₁₂ the work done by the system, in kJ. δ marks small amounts of path functions; d marks the change of a property.
  4. Sign convention. Heat added to the system is positive and heat rejected negative; work done by the system is positive and work done on it negative. With this convention one equation covers every case. (Chemistry books count work done on the system and write Q + W = ΔU; the physics is the same.)
  5. (b) Cycle form. In a cycle the system returns to its initial state, so every property, U included, returns to its starting value:
    ∮dU = 0   ⇒   ∮δQ = ∮δW     (net heat in = net work out)
  6. Consistency. Write the process form for each process of a cycle 1 → 2 → 3 → 1 and add:
    (Q₁₂ − W₁₂) + (Q₂₃ − W₂₃) + (Q₃₁ − W₃₁) = (U₂ − U₁) + (U₃ − U₂) + (U₁ − U₃) = 0
⇒ Q₁₂ + Q₂₃ + Q₃₁ = W₁₂ + W₂₃ + W₃₁

Historically the order was the reverse: Joule’s paddle-wheel experiments showed the cycle form, and the process form follows from it by taking two different paths between the same states, which proves that Q − W depends only on the end states and so defines the property U.

    Q − W = ΔU   ⇒   0 − 0 = U₂ − U₁   ⇒   U₂ = U₁
  4. This holds for any gas, ideal or real: it uses only the energy balance.
  5. Step 3: ideal gas. For an ideal gas U = mcvT depends on T alone, so U₂ = U₁ gives
    T₂ = T₁ = 300 K
m  = p₁V₁/(RT₁) = 500 × 0.2/(0.287 × 300) = 100/86.1 = 1.161 kg
p₂ = mRT₂/V₂ = p₁V₁/V₂ = 500 × 0.2/0.5 = 200 kPa
  6. Step 4: real gas. For a real gas U depends on temperature and specific volume. As the molecules move apart, work is done against their attractive forces, raising their potential energy; with U fixed, their kinetic energy, and so T, falls slightly. This Joule effect is very small for air near room temperature, which is why T₂ = T₁ is a good answer here, but it is a property of the gas, not a consequence of the first law.
  7. Summary.
    ResultHolds for
    Q = 0, W = 0, U₂ = U₁any gas (first law)
    T₂ = T₁ = 300 Kideal gas only
    p₂ = p₁V₁/V₂ = 200 kPaideal gas only (needs T₂ = T₁ and pV = mRT)
  8. What the first law does not say. The process is irreversible: the gas never returns into one side by itself. Its entropy rises by mR ln(V₂/V₁) = 1.161 × 0.287 × ln 2.5 = 0.333 × 0.916 = 0.305 kJ/K. A slow, reversible isothermal expansion between the same end states would have delivered W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = 100 × 0.916 = 91.6 kJ of work (with 91.6 kJ of heat taken in). The free expansion wastes that chance, yet both satisfy the first law; only the second law shows the loss (see Limitations of the first law).
  9. Mistakes that cost marks. Computing W = p₂(V₂ − V₁) or ∫p dV for the expansion (the gas pushes on nothing, so W = 0); taking the system as only the 0.2 m³ side, which then has mass leaving and needs an open-system balance; and writing “T₂ = T₁ for any gas”.
  10. Closing check. p₂V₂ = 200 × 0.5 = 100 kJ = p₁V₁, as it must be at constant temperature for an ideal gas.
Evaluate State the limitations of the first law of thermodynamics. Use a paddle-wheel experiment and a hot cup of tea on a table to show what the first law does not tell us. 14 marks

Points to cover. The first law in one line and what it does decide; Limitations: direction, quality, PMM-2, equilibrium, reversibility; Paddle wheel: work → heat completely; the reverse never happens though it conserves energy; Tea: heat flows hot to cold; the reverse conserves energy too; entropy decides; Conclusion: the second law is needed.

Model answer.

  1. What the first law does. It conserves energy: Q − W = ΔU for a process and ∮δQ = ∮δW for a cycle. It rejects any process that creates or destroys energy, such as a PMM-1. It is necessary, but it is a filter, not a full judge.
  2. Limitations.
    LimitationWhat the first law cannot say
    No directionwhether a process that conserves energy will happen on its own, or only its reverse
    No qualitythat work is worth more than heat, or heat at 800 °C more than heat at 40 °C
    Allows a PMM-2that an engine turning all heat from one reservoir into work is impossible
    No equilibrium testwhere a spontaneous process stops
    No reversibility testwhether a process wasted work potential, and how much
  3. Paddle wheel. A paddle stirs 2 kg of water in an insulated tank, doing 50 kJ of work on it. Q = 0, W = −50 kJ:
    ΔU = Q − W = 0 − (−50) = +50 kJ
ΔT = ΔU/(mc) = 50/(2 × 4.18) = 5.98 K
  4. All the work has become internal energy; the water warms by about 6 K. Now imagine the reverse: the water cools by 5.98 K and the paddle turns, delivering 50 kJ of work. Q − W = 0 − 50 = −50 kJ = ΔU, so the energy balance is perfect. It never happens. The first law cannot tell the two directions apart, and it hides the asymmetry behind them: work can turn completely into heat, but heat cannot turn completely into work in a cycle. That is the quality limitation.
  5. Hot tea. A cup holds 0.2 kg of tea (take c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K) at 80 °C in a room at 25 °C:
    Q = mcΔT = 0.2 × 4.18 × 55 = 45.98 ≈ 46.0 kJ  (from the tea to the room)
  6. The reverse, 46.0 kJ flowing from the room back into the tea and re-heating it to 80 °C, balances energy just as well. Only the cooling happens. That is the direction limitation.
  7. What decides. The entropy of the isolated tea-and-room system:
    ΔStea  = mc ln(T₂/T₁) = 0.836 × ln(298.15/353.15) = −0.1415 kJ/K
ΔSroom = Q/Troom = 45.98/298.15        = +0.1542 kJ/K
ΔStotal = +0.0127 kJ/K > 0   (cooling is possible; re-heating would give −0.0127)
  8. Quality in numbers. The best any engine can do with heat at temperature T, rejecting to surroundings at 298 K, is to turn the fraction 1 − 298/T of it into work (the Carnot limit, from the second law):
    heat at 800 °C (1073 K):  1 − 298.15/1073.15 = 0.722  → at most 72 % can become work
heat at  40 °C (313 K):   1 − 298.15/313.15  = 0.048  → at most 5 % can become work
  9. To the first law both are simply kilojoules. This is the quality limitation in numbers, and it is why a power plant burns fuel at high temperature instead of using warm river water.
  10. Evaluation. The two experiments show the same gap from two sides: energy is conserved in both directions, yet nature picks one. The second law fills the gap, in the Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements and in the principle that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. It also forbids the PMM-2, which the first law accepts, and through exergy it puts a number on the quality of energy. Neither law replaces the other: a process must pass both.
  11. Closing check. 0.836 × 55 = 45.98 kJ and −0.1415 + 0.1542 = +0.0127 kJ/K, so the entropy test, not the energy test, separates the two directions of the tea example.
Analyze Air at 0.2 kg/s flows steadily through a compressor, a cooler and a throttle in series. It enters the compressor at 100 kPa and 300 K and leaves at 500 kPa and 500 K with no heat loss; the cooler brings it to 310 K at 500 kPa; the throttle drops it to 100 kPa. Write the SFEE for each device, state which terms drop out and why, find the power and heat rates, and combine the three into one statement about the whole flow path (cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K). 16 marks

Points to cover. Compressor: q = 0 here, ΔKE and ΔPE neglected: w = h₁ − h₂ (negative); Cooler: w = 0: q = h₃ − h₂ (negative); Throttle: q = 0, w = 0: h₄ = h₃; ideal gas so T₄ = T₃; Sum: qtotal − wtotal = h₄ − h₁; Comment on what the energy balance hides.

Model answer.

  1. Assumptions for every device. Steady flow, ṁ = 0.2 kg/s through all three; uniform inlet and outlet properties; changes of kinetic and potential energy negligible (no velocities or heights are given and they are small beside the enthalpy changes); air an ideal gas, so h = cpT up to a constant.
  2. Compressor (1 → 2). No heat loss, so q = 0; work goes in.
    h₁ + q = h₂ + w   ⇒   w = h₁ − h₂ = cp(T₁ − T₂) = 1.005 × (300 − 500) = −201.0 kJ/kg
power input = ṁ × 201.0 = 0.2 × 201.0 = 40.2 kW
  3. Cooler (2 → 3). A heat exchanger: no shaft, so w = 0; pressure stays 500 kPa.
    q = h₃ − h₂ = cp(T₃ − T₂) = 1.005 × (310 − 500) = −190.95 kJ/kg
heat removed = 0.2 × 190.95 = 38.19 kW
  4. Throttle (3 → 4). No shaft and too fast for heat transfer: q = 0, w = 0.
    h₄ = h₃   ⇒   T₄ = T₃ = 310 K   (ideal gas: h depends on T only)
  5. For real air near room temperature the Joule-Thomson effect would cool it by a few kelvin; the ideal-gas answer is the textbook one.
  6. Sketch of the flow path.
  7. Summary of the flow path.
    Statep (kPa)T (K)Device after itq (kJ/kg)w (kJ/kg)
    1100300compressor0−201.0
    2500500cooler−190.950
    3500310throttle00

  8. Combined statement. Add the three SFEEs; the intermediate enthalpies h₂ and h₃ cancel:
    (h₁ − h₂ − w₁₂) + (h₂ − h₃ + q₂₃) + (h₃ − h₄) = 0
⇒ qtotal − wtotal = h₄ − h₁
numbers: −190.95 − (−201.0) = 10.05 kJ/kg = 1.005 × (310 − 300) ✓
  9. In words: for the whole path the shaft work put in minus the heat removed equals the rise in enthalpy of the stream from inlet to final outlet. Per second: 40.2 − 38.19 = 2.01 kW = ṁcp(T₄ − T₁) = 0.2 × 10.05.
  10. Why each term drops out. In the compressor the question says no heat is lost; in a real machine q would be a small negative number and win = h₂ − h₁ − q. In the cooler nothing turns or moves a boundary, so w = 0; its pressure drop is neglected. In the throttle the flow is too fast for heat transfer and nothing turns, so q = w = 0; its exit velocity is assumed close to its inlet velocity, so the kinetic term cancels.
  11. Energy bookkeeping in kW. Into the air: 40.2 kW of shaft power. Out of the air: 38.19 kW of heat to the cooling water. Kept by the air: 2.01 kW, carried away as a 10 K higher outlet temperature. Nothing is created or lost, which is all the first law can confirm.
  12. What the balance hides. The air was compressed to 500 kPa at a cost of 40.2 kW, then throttled straight back to 100 kPa with no work recovered. The energy balance closes perfectly, yet the plant has thrown away the pressure it paid for. Only a second-law (exergy) analysis puts a number on that waste; this is a practical example of the limits of the first law.
  13. Closing check. 201.0 − 190.95 = 10.05 kJ/kg, and 310 − 300 = 10 K × 1.005 = 10.05 kJ/kg: work in exceeds heat out by exactly the enthalpy the air keeps.

Glossary

Adiabatic process
A process with no heat transfer.
Closed system
A system of fixed mass; only heat and work cross its boundary. Also called a control mass.
Control volume
A region fixed in space whose boundary mass may cross; used for open systems.
Cycle
A series of processes that returns the system to its initial state.
Diffuser
A passage that slows a fluid and raises its pressure and enthalpy.
Enthalpy
H = U + pV; per kilogram h = u + pv.
Flow work
The work pv per kilogram done to push fluid across a boundary.
Free expansion
Expansion of a gas into a vacuum; no work is done and, for an ideal gas, the temperature does not change.
Gas constant
R = Ru/M, the constant in pV = mRT; 0.287 kJ/kg·K for air.
Heat
Energy crossing a boundary because of a temperature difference.
Internal energy
The energy of the molecules of a system, excluding the motion and height of the system as a whole.
Isobaric process
A process at constant pressure.
Isochoric process
A process at constant volume.
Isothermal process
A process at constant temperature.
Nozzle
A passage that speeds up a fluid at the expense of its enthalpy.
Path function
A quantity that depends on how a process is carried out; heat and work are path functions.
PMM-1
A machine that would deliver work with no energy input, or more work than the heat supplied; forbidden by the first law.
PMM-2
A cyclic machine that would turn all heat from one reservoir into work; allowed by the first law, forbidden by the second.
Polytropic index
The exponent n in pVn = C.
Property
A quantity fixed by the state alone, such as pressure, temperature, U or H.
Quasi-static process
A process slow enough for the system to pass through equilibrium states, so that W = ∫p dV applies.
Specific heat
The heat needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 K, at constant volume (cv) or constant pressure (cp).
Steady flow
Flow in which nothing inside the control volume changes with time.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system boundary that can interact with the system.
System
The quantity of matter or region of space chosen for study.
Throttling
Flow through a restriction with no heat or work transfer, so the enthalpy after equals the enthalpy before.
Total energy
E = U + KE + PE, the energy stored in a system.
Uniform-state, uniform-flow model
The model for filling and emptying vessels: uniform contents at each instant and fixed properties at each opening.
Work
Energy crossing a boundary by any means other than a temperature difference, such as a moving piston or a turning shaft.
