2.1 First law for closed systems: cycle and process

The first law of thermodynamics says energy is conserved. For a closed system, the heat added minus the work done equals the change in internal energy stored inside. Over a complete cycle the system returns to its starting state, so the net heat equals the net work. This one balance finds unknown heat, work or internal-energy change in any process, it gives the area inside a p-V loop a meaning, and it rules out any machine that claims to create energy. Every engine, refrigerator and pressure vessel in later units is checked against it first.

You need: the closed system, state, path, cycle and the sign convention for work and heat from Unit 1 (Foundations); Chapter 2.2 then names the property U properly.

One balance, two forms

The first law states: when a closed system moves from state 1 to state 2, the heat added to it minus the work done by it equals the change in its internal energy. No mass crosses the boundary of a closed system; energy crosses only as heat and work. Internal energy U is the energy stored in the matter itself, in kJ.

Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = U₂ − U₁ = ΔU

Q₁₂ is the heat added to the system during the process (kJ); W₁₂ is the work done by the system (kJ); U₁ and U₂ are internal energies at the start and end states (kJ); ΔU is their difference (kJ).

The signs are fixed by convention. Heat added to the system is positive, heat rejected is negative. Work done by the system is positive, work done on the system is negative. With that convention one equation covers every case, so you never flip a sign by hand.

δQ − δW = dU

δQ and δW are small heat and work transfers in an infinitesimal step, written with δ because they depend on the path; dU is the small change of the property U.

Over a complete cycle the system returns to state 1, so ΔU = 0 and the balance becomes ∮δQ = ∮δW. The symbol ∮ means sum around the whole cycle: net heat in equals net work out. Some syllabi call the process form 'the first law applied to a process'. A control mass means the same thing as a closed system.

Heat and work follow the path; U does not

U depends only on the state, so ΔU between two states is fixed however you travel between them. Q and W are transfers, not stored quantities, so they depend on the route.

Path B does only 10 kJ of work, so it needs 60 kJ of heat to reach the same state. Both paths give ΔU = 50 kJ. You can say 'the internal energy of state 2'; you cannot say 'the heat of state 2'.

  • An isolated system has Q = 0 and W = 0, so ΔU = 0: its energy stays constant.
  • A cycle that delivers net work must absorb net heat equal to it.
  • A cycle with net work but zero net heat is impossible. That device is the perpetual motion machine of the first kind, covered in Chapter 2.5.

Work is the area under the process curve

For a quasi-static process the displacement work is the area under the curve on a p-V diagram, taken from Unit 1:

W₁₂ = ∫₁² p dV

p is absolute pressure (kPa), dV the small volume change (m³), and the integral runs along the actual path from 1 to 2; W comes out in kJ when p is in kPa and V in m³.

Expansion gives dV > 0 and W > 0, work done by the system. Compression gives W < 0. Two different paths between the same states enclose different areas, which is the geometric reason W is path-dependent.

For a cycle the outbound and return curves enclose a loop. The net work is the enclosed area: positive when the loop runs clockwise on the p-V diagram, negative when anticlockwise. That area equals the net heat, exactly as ∮δQ = ∮δW says.

Worked example

Question. A gas in a piston-cylinder device receives 80 kJ of heat and does 30 kJ of work as it goes from state 1 to state 2. (a) Find the change in internal energy. (b) The gas is then brought back to state 1 along a path in which 20 kJ of work is done on the gas. Find the heat transfer for the return path. (c) Verify the cycle form of the first law.

  1. Apply the process form to path 1 → 2: Q₁₂ − W₁₂ = U₂ − U₁.
  2. Substitute: 80 kJ − 30 kJ = 50 kJ, so U₂ − U₁ = 50 kJ. The internal energy rises by 50 kJ.
  3. For the return path 2 → 1, the internal-energy change reverses: U₁ − U₂ = −50 kJ.
  4. Work is done on the gas, so W = −20 kJ by the sign convention.
  5. Rearrange to Q = ΔU + W = −50 kJ + (−20 kJ) = −70 kJ. The gas rejects 70 kJ of heat.
  6. Net heat over the cycle: Q_net = 80 − 70 = 10 kJ. Net work over the cycle: W_net = 30 − 20 = 10 kJ.
  7. Q_net = W_net = 10 kJ, so the cycle form ∮δQ = ∮δW is satisfied.

Answer. ΔU = +50 kJ for 1 → 2; the return path rejects 70 kJ of heat; over the cycle Q_net = W_net = 10 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Calling Q or W a property: 'the heat of state 2' is wrong, only U is stored and state-fixed.
  • Sign slip when work is done on the system: W = −20 kJ, not +20 kJ. Adding it instead of subtracting turns −70 kJ into −30 kJ.
  • Using Q − W = ΔU for a turbine, nozzle or any open system. Mass carries energy in and out there; use the steady flow energy equation in Chapter 2.6.
  • Mixing kJ with kJ/kg: if the question gives 80 kJ/kg and the mass is 2 kg, multiply before you add or subtract.
  • For a cycle, quoting net work without checking net heat, or the reverse. The examiner checks that both come out equal.

2.2 Internal energy and enthalpy

Internal energy U is the energy stored inside matter: molecular kinetic energy plus potential energy from forces between molecules. Enthalpy H = U + pV adds flow work pV to U. The two terms U and pV always appear together when mass crosses a boundary, so H gets its own name. You meet u in a rigid tank and h in boilers, turbines and heat exchangers. Both are state properties, in kJ or kJ/kg.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for Q − W = ΔU, and Unit 1 for properties, state and closed/open systems.

Internal energy is energy stored inside matter

Internal energy U is the energy stored inside the matter. It is the sum of the kinetic energy of the molecules (translation, rotation, vibration) and the potential energy from the forces between them. It does not include the kinetic energy or potential energy of the whole system moving or falling. The unit is kJ, and the specific internal energy is u = U/m in kJ/kg, where m is mass in kg.

U is a state property: once pressure, temperature and phase are fixed, U is fixed. It is extensive: doubling the mass doubles U. We never know absolute U; thermodynamics uses changes ΔU. For a closed system the first law gives Q − W = ΔU, and in differential form δQ − δW = dU. Here Q is heat added in kJ, W is work done by the system in kJ, and dU is the small change in internal energy.

For an ideal gas the molecules have no intermolecular forces, so u depends on temperature T only. Then ΔU = m cv ΔT, where cv is the specific heat at constant volume in kJ/kg·K and ΔT is the temperature change in K. For steam and other real substances, u also depends on pressure, so use tables (Unit 5).

ΔU = m cv ΔT

m is mass in kg; cv is specific heat at constant volume in kJ/kg·K; ΔT is final minus initial temperature in K; ΔU is in kJ. Valid for an ideal gas.

Enthalpy adds flow work to internal energy

Fluid entering a control volume is pushed in by the fluid behind it. That push-in work per kg is pv, where p is absolute pressure in kPa and v is specific volume in m³/kg. The entering kg carries its internal energy u and this flow work pv. Enthalpy bundles them: H = U + pV, h = u + pv. H is in kJ, h in kJ/kg, V is total volume in m³.

H = U + pV, h = u + pv

H is total enthalpy in kJ; U is total internal energy in kJ; p is absolute pressure in kPa; V is total volume in m³; h and u are specific values in kJ/kg; v is specific volume in m³/kg.

Take changes: ΔH = ΔU + Δ(pV). For an ideal gas pV = mRT, where R is the gas constant in kJ/kg·K and T is absolute temperature in K. Then Δ(pV) = mRΔT. Using ΔU = m cv ΔT gives ΔH = m(cv + R)ΔT = m cp ΔT, where cp is the specific heat at constant pressure. The proof that cp − cv = R is in Chapter 2.3.

At constant pressure in a closed system, with only pV work, the heat added is Qp = ΔH. In a steady-flow heat exchanger with no shaft work, q = Δh per kg. During a phase change at constant pressure, the latent heat is an enthalpy change, such as hfg for steam (Unit 5).

Enthalpy is not heat. 'Heat content' is a loose name. Heat Q is energy in transit and depends on the path; H is a state property fixed by the state. Q equals ΔH only in the constant-pressure case above. Do not write H = Q.

Choosing U or H correctly

Read the process before choosing a formula. A closed rigid tank has no boundary movement, so W = 0 and Q = ΔU = m cv ΔT for an ideal gas. A closed piston–cylinder at constant pressure has Q = ΔH = m cp ΔT. A flow device such as a turbine or nozzle uses h in the steady flow energy equation (Chapter 2.6).

For steam, compressed liquids and refrigerants, cp is not constant and u, h come from property tables. The bookkeeping still holds: Δh = Δu + Δ(pv), but you read h directly from the table instead of integrating a specific heat. This is why the Mollier chart plots h against s (Unit 5).

Worked example

Question. 2 kg of air (R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K) is heated at constant pressure from 300 K to 400 K. Find ΔU, ΔH and Δ(pV).

  1. Data: m = 2 kg, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K, T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K. ΔT = 400 − 300 = 100 K.
  2. ΔU = m cv ΔT = 2 × 0.718 × 100 = 143.6 kJ.
  3. ΔH = m cp ΔT = 2 × 1.005 × 100 = 201.0 kJ.
  4. Δ(pV) = m R ΔT = 2 × 0.287 × 100 = 57.4 kJ.
  5. Check: ΔU + Δ(pV) = 143.6 + 57.4 = 201.0 kJ, matching ΔH.

Answer. ΔU = 143.6 kJ, ΔH = 201.0 kJ and Δ(pV) = 57.4 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Using ΔH = m cp ΔT for a closed rigid tank. A rigid tank has constant volume, no flow work, and Q = ΔU = m cv ΔT for an ideal gas.
  • Adding R to cv when R is in J/kg·K and cv is in kJ/kg·K. Convert R = 287 J/kg·K to 0.287 kJ/kg·K before adding.
  • Calling enthalpy 'heat content'. H is a state property; heat Q is a path function. Q = ΔH only at constant pressure with no other work.
  • Writing ΔH = ΔU + pΔV in every process. The correct form is ΔH = ΔU + Δ(pV); pΔV alone is valid only at constant pressure.
  • Using cp ΔT for steam or refrigerants. For real substances h comes from tables because h depends on both T and p.

2.3 Specific heats of gases

Specific heat is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of a gas by 1 K. At constant volume the heat per kg goes only into internal energy, so q = cv ΔT. At constant pressure the gas also expands and does pΔv work, so q = cp ΔT and cp is larger. For an ideal gas cp − cv = R. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic, but these numbers size compressors, engine cylinders and air-conditioning coils.

You need Chapter 2.2 for u, h and Δh = cpΔT.

Two specific heats

cv is the specific heat at constant volume: cv = (∂u/∂T) at fixed v. It is the heat per kg per kelvin that raises internal energy when volume is fixed. cp is the specific heat at constant pressure: cp = (∂h/∂T) at fixed p. It is the heat per kg per kelvin that raises enthalpy when pressure is fixed. Both have units kJ/kg·K. For an ideal gas u and h depend only on T, so du = cv dT and dh = cp dT.

cv = (∂u/∂T)v, cp = (∂h/∂T)p

u and h are specific internal energy and enthalpy in kJ/kg; T is temperature in K; subscripts v and p mean volume or pressure held fixed.

In a rigid closed vessel no boundary moves, so w = 0 and Q = ΔU = m cv ΔT. In a piston at constant pressure the gas expands and does pΔv work. The same ΔT then needs extra heat: Q = ΔH = m cp ΔT.

For 1 kg of ideal gas, pΔv = RΔT. The first law gives cpΔT = cvΔT + RΔT, so cp − cv = R. At constant volume every kilojoule of heat stays in the gas; at constant pressure part leaves as work.

Mayer's relation and gamma

Mayer's relation is cp − cv = R. R is the gas constant in kJ/kg·K. The specific heat ratio γ = cp/cv is dimensionless. Kinetic theory gives γ = 1 + 2/f, where f is degrees of freedom; use this only as a memory aid.

cp − cv = R, γ = cp/cv, cv = R/(γ − 1), cp = γR/(γ − 1)

cp and cv are in kJ/kg·K; R is in kJ/kg·K; γ is dimensionless.

Standard values: monatomic gases γ ≈ 1.67, diatomic gases such as air γ ≈ 1.4, polyatomic gases γ ≈ 1.3. For air at about 300 K: cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K. At high temperature cp rises, so use gas tables for large temperature ranges.

Use cv for a rigid tank and cp for a constant-pressure heater. For a nozzle or turbine, cpΔT still gives Δh for an ideal gas. Chapter 2.4 uses γ in pV^γ = C.

Worked example

Question. Air has cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K and cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K. Find R and γ. Check R using M = 28.97 kg/kmol and Ru = 8.314 kJ/kmol·K.

  1. For an ideal gas, R = cp − cv = 1.005 − 0.718 = 0.287 kJ/kg·K.
  2. γ = cp/cv = 1.005/0.718 = 1.3997 ≈ 1.40, dimensionless.
  3. The molar check uses R = Ru/M, with M = 28.97 kg/kmol.
  4. R = 8.314/28.97 = 0.28699 ≈ 0.287 kJ/kg·K.
  5. The two R values agree to three decimals; any 0.001 difference is rounding.

Answer. R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.40, and the molar check also gives R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K.

  • Mixing R = 287 J/kg·K with cp and cv in kJ/kg·K. Convert R to 0.287 kJ/kg·K first.
  • Using air's cp and cv for every gas. R and γ depend on the gas.
  • Treating cp and cv as constant over a large temperature range. They rise with T; use tables.
  • Applying cp − cv = R to a real gas near saturation or to a liquid. It is an ideal-gas relation.

2.4 Non-flow processes: isothermal, adiabatic, polytropic

A closed system can follow many paths between two states. One family obeys pV^n = C, where p is pressure in kPa, V is volume in m³, n is the polytropic index, and C is a constant. The value of n fixes the process and gives ready formulas for work and heat. Compressors, engine cylinders and gas pipelines use these paths. This chapter integrates p dV once and then reads isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric and isochoric processes as special cases.

You need: the first law Q − W = ΔU from Chapter 2.1, internal energy depending on T from Chapter 2.2, and γ = c_p/c_v from Chapter 2.3.

One family of paths

A process is a change from state 1 to state 2. A polytropic process follows pV^n = C. Here p is pressure, V is volume, n is the polytropic index (a pure number), and C is a constant. For an ideal gas pV = mRT, so the path also fixes T.

The index selects familiar processes: n = 0 gives constant p (isobaric); n = 1 gives constant T (isothermal, because pV is constant); n = γ gives adiabatic; n → ∞ gives constant V (isochoric). Here γ = c_p/c_v. Sign convention: W is work done by the gas and Q is heat added to the gas. Compression makes V₂ < V₁ and W negative. The area under the p-V path is W.

Work from the integral

Displacement work is W = ∫p dV from V₁ to V₂. Put p = C/V^n and integrate.

W = ∫ from V₁ to V₂ p dV = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1) = mR(T₁ − T₂)/(n − 1),  n ≠ 1

p is pressure in kPa; V is volume in m³; T is temperature in K; m is mass in kg; R is the gas constant in kJ/kg·K; n is the polytropic index; state 1 is start and state 2 is end. W is positive when the gas does work.

For n = 1 the denominator is zero, so use the log form:

W = p₁V₁ ln(V₂/V₁) = mRT ln(V₂/V₁), n = 1

ln is the natural logarithm; for an ideal gas at constant T, ΔU = 0, so Q = W.

In isothermal compression, V₂ < V₁, ln(V₂/V₁) is negative, W is negative, and Q is negative: work in, heat out.

Adiabatic and polytropic

Adiabatic means Q = 0. For an ideal gas with constant specific heats, pV^γ = C. The gas relations are:

T₂/T₁ = (V₁/V₂)^(γ−1) = (p₂/p₁)^((γ−1)/γ)

γ = c_p/c_v is the ratio of specific heats; the volume ratio is inverted because V falls when T rises in compression.

Work is W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(γ − 1) = m c_v (T₁ − T₂). Since Q = 0, ΔU = −W. In compression W < 0, so ΔU > 0 and T rises. For a general polytropic path:

Q = m c_n (T₂ − T₁),   c_n = c_v (n − γ)/(n − 1)

c_n is the polytropic specific heat in kJ/kg·K; use this only for n ≠ 1; c_v is the specific heat at constant volume in kJ/kg·K.

Here c_n is a path-specific heat, not a property. Check limits: n = γ gives c_n = 0 (Q = 0); n = 0 gives c_n = c_p; n → ∞ gives c_n = c_v. For n = 1 use the isothermal formula. On a p-V diagram, p = C/V^γ falls more steeply than p = C/V because γ > 1. Flow versions are in Chapter 2.6.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of air at 300 K and 100 kPa is compressed to one-eighth of its volume. Find the final pressure, final temperature, work and heat transfer if the process is (a) isothermal and (b) adiabatic. Take R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K, c_v = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, γ = 1.4.

  1. Given m = 1 kg, T₁ = 300 K, p₁ = 100 kPa, V₂/V₁ = 1/8, so V₁/V₂ = 8.
  2. (a) Isothermal: T₂ = T₁ = 300 K. W = mRT ln(V₂/V₁) = 1 × 0.287 × 300 × ln(1/8). ln(1/8) = −2.079, so W = 86.1 × (−2.079) = −179.0 kJ.
  3. For an ideal gas at constant T, ΔU = 0. First law Q − W = ΔU gives Q = W = −179.0 kJ. Work is done on the gas and heat is rejected.
  4. (b) Adiabatic: p₂ = p₁(V₁/V₂)^γ = 100 × 8^1.4 = 100 × 18.379 = 1838 kPa.

2.5 Perpetual motion machine of the first kind

A perpetual motion machine of the first kind, PMM-1, is a claimed device that makes energy. It either gives net work in a complete cycle with no heat input, or gives more work than the heat it receives, so its efficiency is above 100 percent. The first law of thermodynamics forbids both forms because energy is conserved. Inventors still propose such machines, and engineers screen them with a cycle energy balance. Most syllabi treat PMM-1 as a named topic, usually beside the first-law statement. The idea is used to reject impossible energy claims before money is spent.

You need Chapter 2.1 for the cycle form ∮δQ = ∮δW.

The two claims and the cycle balance

For a closed system, Q is heat added to the system and W is work done by the system, both in kJ. The symbol ∮ means sum over a complete cycle. The small amounts δQ and δW are heat and work for one part of the cycle. Chapter 2.1 gives the cycle balance:

∮δQ = ∮δW

Q is heat added to the system in kJ; W is work done by the system in kJ; ∮ sums over the complete cycle.

A PMM-1 has two equivalent forms. Form 1: a machine completes a cycle, exchanges no heat, and still gives net work. Form 2: a heat engine in a cycle gives more net work Wnet than the heat Qin supplied to it, so its thermal efficiency η = Wnet/Qin is greater than 1, above 100 percent. Here Qin is heat supplied to the engine in kJ, and Wnet is net work out in the cycle in kJ. Set Qnet = 0 in the cycle balance. The result is Wnet = 0. A no-heat cycle cannot deliver work. If a claim gives Wnet > Qnet, then Qnet − Wnet < 0. Energy would be created, so the first law rejects the claim.

Efficiency and the PMM-2 contrast

Thermal efficiency is η = Wnet/Qin. Here Wnet is net work out in the cycle, in kJ, and Qin is heat supplied to the engine, in kJ. For a cycle, Wnet = Qnet = Qin − Qout, where Qout is heat rejected. For a real engine Qout ≥ 0, so Wnet ≤ Qin and η ≤ 1, that is ≤ 100 percent. A claim of 125 percent means that for every 100 kJ of heat supplied, 125 kJ of work comes out; the extra 25 kJ, 25 percent of the heat input, was never converted from heat.

Exam wording often gives input and output in kW for a closed-cycle claim. Compute Qin, Qout and W. Check that W = Qin − Qout. If W is more than Qin, energy is being created and the claim is a PMM-1. If W is less than Qin, the difference is simply heat rejected, which is allowed. Do not reject it by saying 'efficiency is too low'; that is not the first-law test.

PMM-2 is different. It obeys the first law; it does not create energy. It breaks the second law by converting a single heat reservoir fully into work. First-law tests cannot reject PMM-2; see Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. An inventor claims a closed-cycle machine takes in 300 kW of heat and delivers 500 kW of shaft work, with no other energy exchange. A second claim takes in 200 kW of heat and delivers 250 kW of work. Test both claims.

  1. First claim: Q_net = 300 kW and W_net = 500 kW.
  2. Cycle balance requires W_net = Q_net. The excess is 500 − 300 = 200 kW.
  3. That 200 kW would be created energy. The claim violates the first law, so it is a PMM-1.
  4. Second claim: η = W_net/Q_in = 250/200 = 1.25.
  5. In percent, η = 1.25 × 100 = 125 percent. Efficiency above 100 percent is impossible, so this claim is also PMM-1.

Answer. Both claims are impossible: the first creates 200 kW, and the second claims 125 percent efficiency.

Exam traps

  • Using Q_in in place of Q_net in a cycle; the cycle balance uses net heat.
  • Calling a low-efficiency device a PMM-1; low efficiency is inefficiency, not energy creation.
  • Confusing PMM-1 with PMM-2; PMM-2 obeys the first law and fails the second.
  • Mixing kW and kJ in a cycle claim; kW is a rate, kJ is an amount, so use one basis.
  • Sign convention slip: heat added is positive, work done by the system is positive.

2.6 Steady flow energy equation and its applications

A turbine, compressor, nozzle and boiler all do the same accounting: energy in plus heat added equals energy out plus work delivered. Because mass crosses the boundary, energy per kg is enthalpy, not internal energy. Written once and with terms struck out per device, this equation answers exam questions on nozzles, turbines, throttles, condensers and pumps. It is the working tool of power plants, refrigerators and air-conditioners.

You need: the energy balance of Chapter 2.1, enthalpy and flow work from Chapter 2.2, and the control volume idea from Unit 1, Systems, Properties and Zeroth Law.

The control volume and the four assumptions

An open system lets mass cross its boundary. A fixed region in space is a control volume: a turbine casing, a compressor, a radiator, a pipe.

The mass flow rate ṁ is the mass crossing a section each second, in kg/s.

ṁ = ρAV = AV/v

ρ is density in kg/m³, A is the cross-sectional area in m², V is the velocity in m/s, and v = 1/ρ is the specific volume in m³/kg.

Four assumptions simplify the balance:

  • Steady state. Nothing inside changes with time; mass in equals mass out, ṁ₁ = ṁ₂ = ṁ, and properties at a fixed point stay constant.
  • Uniform properties. At each section every fluid element has the same h, V and z.
  • Negligible storage. Energy held by the control volume does not change, so it never appears in the balance.
  • q and w are per unit mass. They are in kJ/kg and are the only energy crossings besides the flowing mass.

The steady flow energy equation

Energy in equals energy out, per kg of fluid:

h₁ + V₁²/2000 + gz₁/1000 + q = h₂ + V₂²/2000 + gz₂/1000 + w

h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg; V is velocity in m/s; z is height above a datum in m; g = 9.81 m/s²; q is heat added in kJ/kg, negative if lost; w is shaft work delivered in kJ/kg, negative if work is put in. Subscript 1 is inlet, 2 outlet.

The 2000 and 1000 are unit fixes: V²/2 is J/kg, so V²/2000 is kJ/kg; gz/1000 is also kJ/kg. Adding J/kg to an h in kJ/kg is the classic error.

Why enthalpy, not internal energy? Fluid behind the inlet pushes incoming fluid in, and fluid ahead of the outlet is pushed out. That flow work pv travels with the mass; h = u + pv contains it, so u never appears.

Multiply every term by ṁ for the rate form (kW):

ṁh₁ + ṁV₁²/2000 + ṁgz₁/1000 + Q̇ = ṁh₂ + ṁV₂²/2000 + ṁgz₂/1000 + Ẇ

Q̇ = ṁq is the heat transfer rate in kW and Ẇ = ṁw is the shaft power in kW.

Applications: strike out the terms

Every device is this one equation with terms removed. Learn the striking out, not eight separate formulas.

  • Nozzle. A short duct that speeds the fluid up. No shaft, negligible heat, no height change: h₁ + V₁²/2000 = h₂ + V₂²/2000. Velocity rises; enthalpy, pressure and temperature fall.
  • Diffuser. The same duct widening. Velocity falls and enthalpy rises; the equation is identical.
  • Turbine. Work leaves, the casing is insulated so q ≈ 0, and ΔKE and ΔPE are small beside Δh: w = h₁ − h₂.
  • Compressor. Work goes in, so w is negative and work input = h₂ − h₁. Again ΔKE and ΔPE are dropped.
  • Throttle or expansion valve. A plug or part-closed valve. No work, no heat, no time for velocity change: h₁ = h₂, an isenthalpic process. Pressure falls and, for most real gases near room temperature, temperature falls too — the basis of refrigeration; hydrogen and helium are exceptions and warm up.
  • Boiler, condenser, heater. No shaft work: q = h₂ − h₁.
  • Heat exchanger. Two streams, no work. For each stream q = h_out − h_in; if insulated, with Δh = h_out − h_in for each stream, ṁ_hot Δh_hot + ṁ_cold Δh_cold = 0.
  • Pump. A compressor for a liquid, so w_in = h₂ − h₁ ≈ v(p₂ − p₁) because a liquid barely changes volume.

Filling a tank from a supply line is transient: track total mass and total energy. Some syllabi call the steady case SSSF, the tank case USUF (uniform-state uniform-flow).

2.7 Limitations of the first law

The first law of thermodynamics is an energy ledger. For a closed system, heat added minus work done equals the change in internal energy, and over a cycle net heat equals net work. The ledger always balances. It does not say which balanced process will happen. That gap matters when you judge a heat engine, a refrigerator, or a claim of a machine that turns all heat into work. This chapter lists what the first law cannot decide and names the law that fills the gap.

You need the energy balance from Chapter 2.1 and the losses in real devices from Chapter 2.6.

A balance sheet, not a compass

For a closed system the first law is Q − W = ΔU. Q is heat added to the system in kJ, W is work done by the system in kJ, and ΔU is the change in internal energy in kJ. For a complete cycle ΔU = 0, so Qnet = Wnet. Every joule is counted.

Q − W = ΔU

Q is heat added to the system in kJ; W is work done by the system in kJ; ΔU is the change in internal energy in kJ.

A cup of water at 80 °C in a room at 25 °C cools. Heat leaves the water and enters the room. The same number of joules can be written in the reverse direction: the room gives that heat back to the cup, and the cup warms to 80 °C. Both versions balance. Only the first happens on its own. The first law cannot choose between them.

This is the first limitation: no direction. A second limitation follows: no quality. A joule of work and a joule of heat are equal in energy units, but not equal in usefulness. Work can become heat completely through friction. In a cyclic engine, heat cannot become work completely; some heat must be rejected. The first law sees only totals, so it misses this difference.

Five limits to state clearly

Use these five limits as an exam checklist. Each is a job the first law cannot do.

  • No direction. Hot to cold and cold to hot both balance energy. The first law does not say which is spontaneous.
  • No quality. Work and heat are counted in the same unit, kJ. The first law does not mark work as more useful than heat.
  • It accepts a perpetual motion machine of the second kind, PMM-2. This is a device that takes heat from one reservoir and converts all of it into work in a cycle, with no cold reservoir. The first law gives Q = W, so it passes. The second law rejects it.
  • No criterion for equilibrium or spontaneity. It can balance a state that never occurs on its own.
  • No separation of reversible and irreversible processes. A slow reversible expansion and a fast irreversible one can have the same end states and the same energy balance, yet they differ in usefulness.

The first law also forbids a perpetual motion machine of the first kind, PMM-1, which creates energy. Do not mix the two. PMM-1 fails the first law. PMM-2 passes the first law and fails the second. See Chapter 2.5 for PMM-1.

The first law is still necessary. Any proposed process that fails the energy balance is impossible at once. The first law is a filter, not a full judge.

What fills the gap

The missing rules come from the second law of thermodynamics. It states that heat flows on its own from hot to cold, and that a cyclic engine cannot convert all its heat input into work. Entropy gives a number for the direction. In an isolated system, the entropy of an irreversible process increases. The reverse of the cup cooling would decrease entropy, so it is forbidden even though energy balances.

The second law also separates quality. Work is high-grade energy. Heat at a low temperature is low-grade energy. Unit 3 develops the Kelvin-Planck and Clausius statements, the Carnot cycle and entropy. Unit 4 develops available energy, unavailable energy and exergy, which measure how much of an energy stream can become work.

For the cup in the worked example, cooling from 80 °C to 25 °C rejects 46.0 kJ. The reverse needs the room to give back exactly 46.0 kJ. Energy closes both ways. Only the second law rules out the reverse.

Worked example

Question. A cup holds 0.2 kg of water at 80 °C in a room at 25 °C. Take the specific heat of water as 4.18 kJ/kg·K. (a) Find the heat the water loses on cooling to room temperature. (b) Show that the reverse process also balances energy, and state which law rules it out.

  1. List data: m = 0.2 kg, c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K, T1 = 80 °C, T2 = 25 °C. The temperature drop is ΔT = 80 − 25 = 55 K.

  3. 0.2 × 4.18 = 0.836 kJ/K. Then 0.836 × 55 = 45.98 kJ, which rounds to 46.0 kJ.
  4. The water rejects 46.0 kJ to the room.
  5. For the reverse, the room supplies 46.0 kJ to the water. The water warms from 25 °C to 80 °C. The energy balance closes to the same 46.0 kJ.
  6. The first law permits both directions. The second law forbids the reverse because it would reduce entropy.

  • Do not call a PMM-2 a first-law violator. A PMM-2 passes the first law and fails the second. A PMM-1 fails the first law.
  • Do not say heat can never become work. In a cycle, heat cannot become work completely; some heat must be rejected. A single non-cyclic process can convert heat to work.
  • Do not treat kJ of work and kJ of heat as equally useful. The first law counts both; the second law ranks them.

Throttle (expansion valve)A restriction with q = 0 and w = 0; enthalpy stays constant across it (h₁ = h₂) while pressure falls2.6
Limitations of the first lawThe first law only counts energy; it gives no direction of flow and cannot rule out a PMM-2 or explain why heat flows hot to cold2.7

Takeaways

  1. For a closed system Q − W = ΔU, and over a full cycle the net heat equals the net work, because the system comes back to the same U.
  2. Internal energy is stored inside the matter and is fixed by the state; enthalpy H = U + pV adds the flow work and is the natural property for open systems.
  3. For an ideal gas cp − cv = R and γ = cp/cv, so measuring γ and R gives both specific heats without any table.
  4. Any closed process that follows pVⁿ = C can be handled with one work formula, W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1), and n = 0, 1, γ, ∞ reproduce isobaric, isothermal, adiabatic and isochoric processes.
  5. A PMM-1 would create energy from nothing; the first law forbids it, which is the practical use of the law as a check on any proposed device.
  6. The steady flow energy equation balances enthalpy, kinetic energy, potential energy, heat and work per kilogram of flowing fluid.
  7. In a nozzle, diffuser or throttle the work term vanishes, so the equation collapses into a simple exchange between enthalpy and velocity, or into h₁ = h₂.
  8. The first law is a book-keeping rule, not a direction-finder; the second law is needed to say which energy-conserving processes can actually happen.

Remember and understand

Understand State the first law of thermodynamics for a closed system undergoing (a) a process and (b) a complete cycle. Explain the sign convention you use, and show that the two statements are consistent. 8 marks

Write Q − W = U₂ − U₁ with every symbol and unit defined; Write the cycle form ∮Q = ∮W and explain that U returns to its initial value so ΔU = 0; State the sign convention: heat in and work out positive; Show that summing the process form over the steps of a cycle gives the cycle form.

Understand Define internal energy and enthalpy. Derive H = U + pV and explain why enthalpy, and not internal energy, appears in flow devices such as boilers and turbines. 8 marks

Internal energy as the microscopic energy stored in a fixed mass; a state property; Definition H = U + pV with units kJ and specific enthalpy h in kJ/kg; Flow work pV as the work a fluid does pushing its way into and out of a control volume; Hence the energy carried by a flowing stream per kg is h + V²/2 + gz.

Understand Explain why cp is always greater than cv for a gas, and derive cp − cv = R for an ideal gas. 8 marks

At constant volume no expansion work is done, so all heat raises U; At constant pressure the gas expands and also does pΔV work, so more heat is needed; First law at constant pressure: qp = ΔU + pΔV = ΔH; Use pV = RT for 1 kg and ΔU = cvΔT to get cp − cv = R; Mention that for solids and liquids cp ≈ cv because expansion is tiny.

Remember Define a perpetual motion machine of the first kind and state why the first law forbids it. Give two devices that people have claimed to be PMM-1 and explain the flaw. 8 marks

Definition: delivers work with no energy input, or more work than heat supplied; First law for a cycle: W_net cannot exceed Q_net; Efficiency greater than 100 percent means W > Q, which would need ΔU to keep falling forever; Take any claimed machine and show its energy balance fails; note friction and losses make real efficiency lower still.

Apply

Apply One kilogram of air at 500 kPa and 0.2 m³ expands to 100 kPa along pV^1.3 = C. Take R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K) and γ = 1.4. Find the final volume, the work done, the heat transferred, and the change in internal energy. 12 marks

Use p₁V₁ⁿ = p₂V₂ⁿ to get V₂ = V₁(p₁/p₂)^(1/n); Work W = (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(n − 1), with pressures in kPa and volumes in m³ to get kJ; Temperatures from pV = mRT, then ΔU = m cv (T₂ − T₁) with cv = R/(γ − 1); First law: Q = ΔU + W, and state whether heat is added or rejected.

Apply A gas is compressed isothermally from 100 kPa, 0.5 m³ to 500 kPa. Find the work and heat transfer, and compare with the work needed if the same compression were adiabatic between the same two pressures. 12 marks

Isothermal: W = p₁V₁ ln(p₁/p₂), negative for compression; ΔU = 0 for an ideal gas at constant T, so Q = W; Adiabatic work from (p₁V₁ − p₂V₂)/(γ − 1) with V₂ = V₁(p₁/p₂)^(1/γ); Compare magnitudes and explain that the adiabatic path ends hotter, so more work is spent.

Apply Steam enters a turbine at 3 MPa and 350 °C with a velocity of 60 m/s and leaves at 20 kPa with a velocity of 150 m/s and a dryness fraction of 0.92. The heat loss from the casing is 12 kJ per kg of steam. Find the work output per kg. 13 marks

Choose the turbine as a control volume with steady flow; Write the SFEE with q = −12 kJ/kg and no elevation change; Look up h₁ from superheated steam tables at 3 MPa, 350 °C; h₂ from hf + x·hfg at 20 kPa; Solve w = (h₁ − h₂) + (V₁² − V₂²)/2000 + q, taking care with the 1000 in the kinetic term to get kJ/kg.

Apply Air enters a nozzle at 400 K and 30 m/s and leaves at 300 K. Treat air as an ideal gas with cp = 1.005 kJ/(kg·K) and assume the nozzle is adiabatic with no work transfer. Find the exit velocity. 10 marks

SFEE with q = 0, w = 0 and negligible height change; h₁ + V₁²/2 = h₂ + V₂²/2; Replace h₁ − h₂ by cp(T₁ − T₂); watch units: divide V² by 2000 to convert to kJ/kg; Solve V₂ = √(V₁² + 2cp(T₁ − T₂)·1000).

Apply A refrigerant passes through an expansion valve from saturated liquid at 800 kPa to 200 kPa. Show that the process is isenthalpic and find the exit quality. State every assumption. 10 marks

Control volume around the valve: q = 0, w = 0, and changes in kinetic and potential energy are neglected; SFEE then gives h₁ = h₂; h₁ = hf at 800 kPa; h₂ = hf + x·hfg at 200 kPa; Solve for x and comment that throttling is irreversible even though enthalpy is constant.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A closed system is proposed to follow a cycle in which the net work output is 200 kJ while the net heat supplied is 150 kJ. Using the first law, examine the claim and state what you must check before rejecting it. 10 marks

For a cycle ΔU = 0, hence ∮Q = ∮W; The proposal has ∮W > ∮Q, so it violates the first law; Check whether the system returns exactly to its initial state and whether all heat and work terms were counted, including surroundings; Conclude that the device is a PMM-1 and no design of bearings or materials can make it work.

Evaluate Compare the work produced by isothermal and adiabatic expansion of the same ideal gas from the same initial state to the same final pressure. Explain which path gives more work and where the missing energy goes. 12 marks

Both arrive at the same pressure; plot both on a p–V diagram and compare areas; Isothermal expansion at constant T gives W = p₁V₁ ln(p₁/p₂); Adiabatic expansion ends at a lower temperature, ΔU is negative, so part of the work comes from internal energy and the area is smaller; State that the isothermal path requires heat input from a reservoir, which is why it delivers more work.

Analyze A rigid insulated tank is divided into two parts by a thin membrane, one side holding high-pressure air and the other a vacuum. The membrane bursts. Using the first law, determine the final temperature, and explain why this result is the same whatever the gas. 12 marks

Control volume is the whole tank: q = 0, w = 0 (rigid, insulated), no mass crosses; First law gives ΔU = 0, so U₂ = U₁; For an ideal gas U depends on T only, so T₂ = T₁; Note that pressure and volume change, entropy rises, and the process is irreversible, but the first law alone tells us nothing about that.

Evaluate State the limitations of the first law of thermodynamics. Use a paddle-wheel experiment and a hot cup of tea on a table to show what the first law does not tell us. 14 marks

List: no direction of process, no quality of energy, no test for feasibility other than energy balance, cannot rule out PMM-2; Work turning fully into heat (paddle wheel) and heat never turning fully into work in a cycle, though both conserve energy; Heat flows from hot tea to the room and never the reverse, with no energy balance violation either way; Conclude that a second law is required, and mention that entropy and the Kelvin-Planck statement supply it.

Apply Air flows steadily through a compressor, is cooled in a heat exchanger, then passes through a throttle. Write the SFEE for each device, state which terms drop out and why, and combine the three into one statement about the whole flow path. 16 marks

Compressor: q small or given, w negative (work input), velocity and height neglected, so w = h₂ − h₁ + q; Heat exchanger: w = 0, no work, no change in kinetic or potential energy, so q = h₃ − h₂; Throttle: q = 0, w = 0, so h₄ = h₃; Combine to relate shaft work input to the change in enthalpy of the stream and the heat removed; Note each device is steady flow with uniform properties at inlet and outlet.

Glossary

Adiabatic
A process or boundary with zero heat transfer.
Closed system
A system with a fixed mass across whose boundary only heat and work can pass, not matter.
Control volume
A region fixed in space, with mass allowed to cross its boundary, used for open systems.
Cycle
A sequence of processes that returns the system to its initial state.
Diffuser
A passage of increasing area that converts kinetic energy into pressure.
Enthalpy
The combination H = U + pV, useful whenever mass flows across a boundary.
Gas constant R
The constant in pV = mRT, equal to the universal gas constant divided by the molar mass.
Heat Q
Energy transferred across a boundary because of a temperature difference.
Internal energy
The microscopic energy stored within the matter of a system, denoted U.
Isobaric
A process carried out at constant pressure.
Isochoric
A process carried out at constant volume.
Isothermal
A process carried out at constant temperature.
Nozzle
A passage of reducing area that increases fluid velocity at the expense of enthalpy.
Path function
A quantity whose value depends on how a process is carried out; heat and work are path functions.
PMM-1
A hypothetical device that produces work with no energy input, forbidden by the first law.
Polytropic index
The exponent n in pVⁿ = C, which fixes the shape of the process path.
Process
A change of state from one equilibrium state to another.
Property
A measurable quantity that depends only on the state, such as pressure, temperature or volume.
Specific heat
The heat required to raise the temperature of one kilogram of a substance by one kelvin.
State
The complete set of properties of a system at one instant.
State function
A quantity fixed by the state alone, independent of history; internal energy and enthalpy are state functions.
Steady flow
Flow in which mass and energy within the control volume do not change with time.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system boundary that can interact with the system.
System
The fixed quantity of matter or the region in space chosen for study.
Throttling
Flow through a restriction with no heat or work transfer, so enthalpy is constant.
Work W
Energy transferred across a boundary as a force acting through a displacement, or by any non-heat means.
