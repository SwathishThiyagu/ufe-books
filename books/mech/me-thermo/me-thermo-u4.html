4.1 Available and unavailable energy

Heat is not all equally useful. Of the heat Q drawn from a body at temperature T, only a part can ever become work, because any engine must reject heat to the surroundings at T₀. That part is the available energy; the rest is the unavailable energy. This chapter fixes the surroundings (the dead state), finds both parts for a source at constant temperature and for a body that cools, and shows that heat transfer across a temperature difference always loses available energy.

You need: the Carnot efficiency 1 − T₀/T, the entropy change ΔS = ∫δQ/T of a reversible process, and T–s diagrams, all from the second law and entropy.

The dead state and the surroundings

The surroundings (the environment) are a very large body at temperature T₀ and pressure p₀, usually 25 °C (298 K) or 27 °C (300 K) and 1 atm. Their temperature does not change when they take or give heat.

A system is at the dead state when it is in full equilibrium with these surroundings: the same temperature T₀ and pressure p₀, at rest (no kinetic energy), at the reference height (no potential energy), and with no chemical difference from the surroundings. At the dead state no work can be drawn from the system, so every quantity in this unit is measured from that state.

Some syllabi call available energy "availability" and unavailable energy "anergy".

Heat from a source at constant temperature

Let a reservoir at absolute temperature T give heat Q. The most work we can get from Q comes from a reversible (Carnot) engine working between T and T₀. Its efficiency is 1 − T₀/T, so

AE = Q(1 − T₀/T) = Q − T₀ΔS

AE is the available energy in kJ; Q is the heat given by the source in kJ; T and T₀ are absolute temperatures in K; ΔS = Q/T is the entropy the source gives up, in kJ/K.

UE = Q − AE = T₀ΔS = Q·T₀/T

UE is the unavailable energy in kJ: the heat that even the reversible engine must reject to the surroundings.

On a T–s diagram the heat Q is the area under the source line. The horizontal line T = T₀ cuts that area in two: the part above the line is AE, and the rectangle below it, of height T₀ and width ΔS, is UE. With T₀ = 300 K, half of the heat from a 600 K source is available; from a 1200 K source, three quarters.

Heat from a body that cools

A real hot body, such as exhaust gas or hot water, cools while it gives heat, so its temperature is not fixed. Split the heat into small parts δQ, each given at the body's present temperature T, and add the Carnot work of every part. The result keeps the same form, AE = Q − T₀ΔS, where ΔS is the entropy the body gives up:

AE = Q − T₀·m·c·ln(T₁/T₂)

m is the mass in kg; c is the specific heat in kJ/(kg·K); the body cools from T₁ to T₂, both in K; the heat given is Q = m·c·(T₁ − T₂) in kJ.

The most work is obtained when the body cools all the way to T₀; below T₀ it would have to take heat from the surroundings instead. Figure 4.1.1 draws this for the first worked example: 10 kg of water cooling from 360 K to 300 K. The sliver above the T₀ line is small, because warm water gives its heat close to T₀.

Loss of available energy in heat transfer

Let heat Q flow from a hot reservoir at T₁ to a colder body at T₂ (T₁ > T₂) across a finite temperature difference. The energy is the same Q on both sides, but its available part is not. The loss is

Loss of AE = T₀·Q·(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) = T₀Sgen

Sgen = Q/T₂ − Q/T₁ is the entropy generated by the transfer, in kJ/K. Both temperatures are absolute.

The loss is always positive, because 1/T₂ is larger than 1/T₁. Energy is conserved, but its quality falls: heat that has dropped in temperature can give less work. This is why a boiler, where hot combustion gas heats water that boils at a far lower temperature, is the biggest loss of available energy in a steam plant.

Worked example

Question. 10 kg of water at 360 K is used as a heat source. The surroundings are at 300 K. Take c = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K). Find the heat the water can give as it cools to the temperature of the surroundings, and how much of it is available and how much unavailable.

  1. Given: m = 10 kg, c = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K), T₁ = 360 K, T₂ = T₀ = 300 K.
  2. Heat given: Q = m·c·(T₁ − T₀) = 10 × 4.18 × (360 − 300) = 2508 kJ.
  3. Entropy given up by the water: ΔS = m·c·ln(T₁/T₀) = 41.8 × ln 1.2 = 41.8 × 0.18232 = 7.621 kJ/K.
  4. Unavailable energy: UE = T₀ΔS = 300 × 7.621 = 2286.3 kJ.
  5. Available energy: AE = Q − UE = 2508 − 2286.3 = 221.7 kJ.
  6. Share available: 221.7/2508 = 0.088, about 8.8 %.
  7. Check: the water gives its heat at a mean temperature Q/ΔS = 2508/7.621 = 329.1 K, and a Carnot engine at that temperature gives 2508 × (1 − 300/329.1) = 221.7 kJ, the same answer.

Answer. Q = 2508 kJ, of which 221.7 kJ (about 8.8 %) is available and 2286.3 kJ is unavailable. Warm water stores a lot of energy but little work.

Worked example 2

Question. A reservoir at 1000 K gives 1000 kJ of heat to a system held at 500 K. The surroundings are at 300 K. Find the available energy of the heat before and after the transfer, the loss of available energy, and the entropy generated.

  1. Before the transfer, at 1000 K: AE₁ = 1000 × (1 − 300/1000) = 1000 × 0.7 = 700 kJ.
  2. After the transfer, at 500 K: AE₂ = 1000 × (1 − 300/500) = 1000 × 0.4 = 400 kJ.
  3. Loss of available energy: AE₁ − AE₂ = 700 − 400 = 300 kJ.
  4. Entropy generated: Sgen = Q/T₂ − Q/T₁ = 1000/500 − 1000/1000 = 2 − 1 = 1 kJ/K.
  5. Check: T₀Sgen = 300 × 1 = 300 kJ, the same loss.

Answer. The available energy falls from 700 kJ to 400 kJ, so 300 kJ is lost, equal to T₀Sgen = 300 K × 1 kJ/K.

Exam traps

  • Putting °C into 1 − T₀/T or into ln(T₁/T₂). Convert to kelvin first: 87 °C is about 360 K.
  • Using Q(1 − T₀/T) for a body that cools. Its temperature changes, so use AE = Q − T₀·m·c·ln(T₁/T₂).
  • Taking T₀ as the temperature of an engine's sink or of its cooling water. T₀ is the temperature of the surroundings given in the question, often 25 °C or 27 °C.
  • Writing the loss in a heat transfer as T₀Q(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). That is negative; the colder body's term 1/T₂ comes first.
  • Calling unavailable energy "destroyed energy". It is not destroyed; it is energy that must leave as heat at T₀.

4.2 Exergy analysis and second law efficiency

Exergy is the largest useful work a system or a stream can give as it comes to the dead state, exchanging heat only with the surroundings. Energy is always conserved, but exergy is not: every real process destroys some, and the amount destroyed is T₀ times the entropy generated. An exergy balance shows where a plant wastes its ability to do work, and the second law efficiency tells how close a device comes to the best possible device. Some syllabi call exergy "availability" and exergy destruction "irreversibility".

You need: Available and unavailable energy for the dead state, and the steady-flow energy equation and entropy generation from the first and second laws.

Exergy of a closed system

Take a closed system at a state (T, p) in surroundings at T₀ and p₀. As it comes to the dead state it can give work, but part of any expansion only pushes back the atmosphere. That part, p₀(V₂ − V₁), is not useful, so the useful work of a process is Wu = W − p₀(V₂ − V₁). The largest useful work, on a fully reversible path that exchanges heat only with the surroundings, is the exergy:

φ = (u − u₀) + p₀(v − v₀) − T₀(s − s₀)

φ is the specific exergy of the closed system in kJ/kg (add V²/2 and gz if it moves or is raised); u, v and s are the specific internal energy (kJ/kg), volume (m³/kg) and entropy (kJ/(kg·K)) at the state; subscript 0 marks the dead state; T₀ is in K and p₀ in kPa. The total exergy is Φ = mφ in kJ.

u − u₀ is the energy stored above the dead state; p₀(v − v₀) is the work exchanged with the atmosphere as the volume changes to v₀ (negative for a compressed gas); −T₀(s − s₀) is the heat that must pass to or from the surroundings to bring the entropy to s₀. The exergy is never negative and is zero only at the dead state.

Between two states the reversible useful work is the fall in exergy, Wrev = Φ₁ − Φ₂, and the exergy destroyed is I = Wrev − Wu.

Flow exergy and the exergy balance

A stream crossing the boundary of a control volume also carries flow work pv, so its exergy uses enthalpy:

ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀) + V²/2 + gz

ψ is the flow exergy in kJ/kg; h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg; V is velocity in m/s and z height in m (divide V²/2 and gz by 1000 to get kJ/kg). For most turbines and heat exchangers the last two terms are small and dropped.

For a steady device, per kg of flow, the exergy that comes in with heat and with the stream equals the work out, the exergy that leaves with the stream, and the exergy destroyed:

Σ(1 − T₀/Tk)qk + ψ₁ = w + ψ₂ + i

qk is heat received through a part of the boundary at temperature Tk; w is the work out in kJ/kg; i is the exergy destroyed per kg.

i = T₀ sgen

This is the Gouy-Stodola theorem. sgen is the entropy generated per kg in kJ/(kg·K), found from an entropy balance; for an adiabatic steady device sgen = s₂ − s₁. In rate form, İ = T₀Ṡgen in kW.

Exergy is destroyed by the same causes that generate entropy: friction, throttling, mixing, and heat transfer across a finite temperature difference. For an adiabatic turbine the balance becomes w = (ψ₁ − ψ₂) − T₀(s₂ − s₁): the work is the exergy drop less the exergy destroyed. Figure 4.2.1 draws this split for the first worked example.

Second law efficiency

First-law efficiency compares energy out with energy in. Second law efficiency compares the exergy recovered with the exergy supplied, so it measures how close a device is to the reversible one. A reversible device has ηII = 1.

ηII = exergy recovered ÷ exergy supplied

Both exergies are in the same units and measured from the same dead state.

Do not mix ηII with the isentropic efficiency ηis = (h₁ − h₂)/(h₁ − h₂s). The isentropic efficiency compares with an ideal turbine that ends at the same pressure; the second law efficiency compares with the whole exergy drop. In an adiabatic turbine whose expansion stays above T₀, the friction heat is still above T₀ and could do some work further down, so T₀sgen is smaller than the isentropic loss and ηII comes out higher than ηis.

An electric heater turns 1 kW of electricity into 1 kW of heat, a first-law efficiency of 100 %. Heat for a room at 295 K with the outside at T₀ = 278 K carries only 1 × (1 − 278/295) = 0.058 kW of exergy, so ηII is about 5.8 %.

Worked example

Question. Steam enters an adiabatic turbine with h₁ = 3200 kJ/kg and s₁ = 6.80 kJ/(kg·K) and leaves with h₂ = 2600 kJ/kg and s₂ = 7.00 kJ/(kg·K). The surroundings are at T₀ = 300 K. Neglect kinetic and potential energy. Find the work per kg, the exergy destroyed and the second law efficiency.

  1. Energy balance for the adiabatic turbine: w = h₁ − h₂ = 3200 − 2600 = 600 kJ/kg.
  2. Entropy generated (adiabatic, steady): sgen = s₂ − s₁ = 7.00 − 6.80 = 0.20 kJ/(kg·K).
  3. Exergy destroyed: i = T₀sgen = 300 × 0.20 = 60 kJ/kg.
  4. Exergy drop of the stream: ψ₁ − ψ₂ = (h₁ − h₂) − T₀(s₁ − s₂) = 600 − 300 × (6.80 − 7.00) = 600 + 60 = 660 kJ/kg. Check with the balance: w + i = 600 + 60 = 660 kJ/kg.
  5. Second law efficiency: ηII = w/(ψ₁ − ψ₂) = 600/660 = 0.909.

Answer. w = 600 kJ/kg, exergy destroyed = 60 kJ/kg, ηII = 0.909 (90.9 %).

Worked example 2

Question. 2 kg of air is held in a rigid tank at 500 kPa and 400 K. The surroundings are at 100 kPa and 300 K. Treat air as an ideal gas with cp = 1.005, cv = 0.718 and R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). Find the exergy of the air.

  1. Stored energy: u − u₀ = cv(T − T₀) = 0.718 × (400 − 300) = 71.8 kJ/kg.
  2. Volumes: v = RT/p = 0.287 × 400/500 = 0.2296 m³/kg; v₀ = RT₀/p₀ = 0.287 × 300/100 = 0.861 m³/kg.
  3. Atmosphere term: p₀(v − v₀) = 100 × (0.2296 − 0.861) = −63.14 kJ/kg.
  4. Entropy: s − s₀ = cpln(T/T₀) − R·ln(p/p₀) = 1.005 × ln(4/3) − 0.287 × ln 5 = 0.2891 − 0.4619 = −0.1728 kJ/(kg·K).
  5. Entropy term: −T₀(s − s₀) = −300 × (−0.1728) = +51.84 kJ/kg.
  6. Exergy: φ = 71.8 − 63.14 + 51.84 = 60.50 kJ/kg, so Φ = 2 × 60.50 = 121.0 kJ.

Answer. The air holds Φ = 121.0 kJ of exergy (60.50 kJ/kg). The atmosphere term and the entropy term almost cancel here, so the exergy is not simply the stored internal energy of 143.6 kJ.

Exam traps

  • Using ψ (with h) for a closed tank, or φ (with u) for a stream. A closed system uses u and p₀(v − v₀); a stream uses h.
  • Dropping the p₀(v − v₀) term. In the tank example it is −63.14 kJ/kg, larger than the final answer of 60.50 kJ/kg.
  • Taking the isentropic efficiency as the second law efficiency. They use different ideal references.
  • Writing sgen = s₁ − s₂ for an adiabatic device. Entropy generated is s₂ − s₁ ≥ 0, so exergy destroyed is never negative.

4.3 Exact differentials, reciprocity and cyclic relations

A pure substance in a single phase has its state fixed by any two independent properties (the state postulate). Every other property is then a function of two variables, z = z(x, y), and the calculus of such functions gives the three tools of property relations: the exact differential and its test, the reciprocity relation and the cyclic relation. With them a slope that cannot be measured is written in terms of slopes that can, which is the whole purpose of thermodynamic relations. This chapter sets out the three tools and tries each one on an ideal gas.

You need: partial derivatives from calculus, and the ideal-gas equation pv = RT.

Two-variable functions and the total differential

For a simple compressible substance any property z can be written as z = z(x, y), where x and y are two independent properties; for example p = p(T, v). A small change in both gives the total differential

dz = (∂z/∂x)ydx + (∂z/∂y)xdy = M dx + N dy

The subscript names the property held fixed. M = (∂z/∂x)y and N = (∂z/∂y)x are the two slopes. For p = RT/v, M = (∂p/∂T)v = R/v and N = (∂p/∂v)T = −RT/v².

A partial derivative is a slope along a line on the property surface: (∂p/∂T)v is the slope of a constant-volume line on a p–T diagram, and (∂p/∂v)T is the slope of an isotherm on a p–v diagram.

The exact differential and its test

M dx + N dy is exact when it is the differential of some function z. Then the two mixed second derivatives of z are equal, ∂²z/∂y∂x = ∂²z/∂x∂y, which gives the test:

(∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y

If the test holds, the integral of dz between two states is the same on every path and the integral round any cycle is zero: z is a property (a point function). If it fails, the quantity depends on the path.

Properties such as p, v, T, u, h and s have exact differentials. Heat and work do not: they are path functions, which is why many books write δq and δw. For an ideal gas on a reversible path, δq = cvdT + p dv = cvdT + (RT/v)dv. Here ∂(cv)/∂v = 0 but ∂(RT/v)/∂T = R/v, so δq is not exact. Divide by T: ds = (cv/T)dT + (R/v)dv, and now both cross-derivatives are zero, so ds is exact. 1/T is the integrating factor that turns heat into the property entropy. Figure 4.3.1 shows this with two paths between the same two states.

Reciprocity and cyclic relations

Take three properties x, y and z linked by one equation of state, so each is a function of the other two. The two relations follow in five steps:

  1. Write x = x(y, z): dx = (∂x/∂y)zdy + (∂x/∂z)ydz. Write y = y(x, z): dy = (∂y/∂x)zdx + (∂y/∂z)xdz.
  2. Put the second into the first: dx = (∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂x)zdx + [(∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂z)x + (∂x/∂z)y]dz.
  3. x and z are independent, so the coefficient of dx must be 1 and the coefficient of dz must be 0.
  4. Coefficient of dx: (∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂x)z = 1, the reciprocity relation.
  5. Coefficient of dz: (∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂z)x = −(∂x/∂z)y; with reciprocity on the right this is the cyclic relation.

(∂x/∂y)z = 1/(∂y/∂x)z

Reciprocity: a slope and its inverse, taken with the same property held fixed.

(∂x/∂y)z(∂y/∂z)x(∂z/∂x)y = −1

Cyclic relation: x, y, z go round in the numerators and denominators, and each subscript is the third property. The product is −1, not +1, because the three slopes are taken along three different lines on the surface, so the ∂ symbols do not cancel like fractions.

With p, v and T the cyclic relation gives the most used result of this kind: (∂p/∂T)v = −(∂v/∂T)p/(∂v/∂p)T. The pressure rise of a liquid heated in a sealed vessel, which is hard to measure, follows from two easy measurements, one at constant pressure and one at constant temperature (see Expansivity, compressibility, Mayer relation and Joule-Thomson coefficient).

Worked example

Question. Check the cyclic relation (∂p/∂v)T(∂v/∂T)p(∂T/∂p)v = −1 for air as an ideal gas at 300 K and 100 kPa, with R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). Also check reciprocity for (∂T/∂p)v.

  1. Specific volume: v = RT/p = 0.287 × 300/100 = 0.861 m³/kg.
  2. From p = RT/v: (∂p/∂v)T = −RT/v² = −0.287 × 300/0.861² = −86.1/0.7413 = −116.1 kPa per m³/kg.
  3. From v = RT/p: (∂v/∂T)p = R/p = 0.287/100 = 0.00287 m³/(kg·K).
  4. From T = pv/R: (∂T/∂p)v = v/R = 0.861/0.287 = 3.000 K/kPa.
  5. Product: (−116.1) × 0.00287 × 3.000 = −1.000.
  6. In symbols: (−RT/v²)(R/p)(v/R) = −RT/(pv) = −1, because pv = RT.
  7. Reciprocity: (∂p/∂T)v = R/v = 0.287/0.861 = 0.3333 kPa/K, and 0.3333 × 3.000 = 1.000.

Answer. The product of the three slopes is −1.000, as the cyclic relation says, and (∂T/∂p)v = 3.000 K/kPa is the reciprocal of (∂p/∂T)v = 0.3333 kPa/K.

  5. Test of δq = cvdT + (RT/v)dv: ∂(cv)/∂v = 0 but ∂(RT/v)/∂T = R/v, so δq is not exact. Test of ds = (cv/T)dT + (R/v)dv: both cross-derivatives are 0, so ds is exact.

4.4 Gibbs and Helmholtz functions

A system kept at constant temperature and volume, or at constant temperature and pressure, is judged not by U or H alone but by two combinations: the Helmholtz function A = U − TS and the Gibbs function G = H − TS. Their fall tells which way a process can go on its own and how much work can be taken out. Chemists use G to decide whether a reaction runs; engineers use it for phase change, equilibrium and fuel cells. This chapter defines both functions, finds their differentials, and reads the two tests for spontaneous change from them.

You need: Exact differentials, reciprocity and cyclic relations, the relation T dS = dU + p dV, and the entropy principle.

Two combinations of U

The natural variables of U are entropy and volume, and no laboratory controls entropy. Two combinations replace entropy by temperature.

Helmholtz function: A = U − TS, also written F and called the Helmholtz free energy. Gibbs function: G = H − TS, the Gibbs free energy, with H = U + pV. U, H, TS, A and G are all in kJ when T is in K and S in kJ/K. Per kg, a = u − Ts and g = h − Ts are in kJ/kg.

A = U − TS G = H − TS

U is internal energy, H enthalpy (kJ); T is absolute temperature (K); S is entropy (kJ/K). Both A and G are built only from properties, so they are properties too, and their changes depend only on the end states.

Differentials and natural variables

Start from dU = T dS − p dV for a closed system that does only pV work.

  1. Differentiate A = U − TS: dA = dU − T dS − S dT. The T dS inside dU cancels the −T dS, leaving dA = −S dT − p dV, so A is naturally a function of T and V.
  2. Differentiate G = H − TS with dH = T dS + V dp: dG = dH − T dS − S dT = −S dT + V dp, so G is naturally a function of T and p.
FunctionDefinitionNatural variablesDifferential
UUS, VdU = T dS − p dV
HU + pVS, pdH = T dS + V dp
AU − TST, VdA = −S dT − p dV
GH − TST, pdG = −S dT + V dp

In both new differentials the coefficient of dT is −S: (∂G/∂T)p = −S, so G falls as T rises, faster for the phase with more entropy. Figure 4.4.1 uses this slope for ice and water. These four differentials are exact, and their cross-derivatives give the Maxwell relations.

What the fall of A and G means

  1. A closed system at temperature T exchanges heat Q with surroundings at the same T. First law: Q = ΔU + W, where W is the work done by the system.
  2. Entropy principle for system and surroundings: ΔS − Q/T ≥ 0, so Q ≤ TΔS.
  3. Put this in the first law: W ≤ TΔS − ΔU = −ΔA. The work from a process at constant T cannot exceed the fall of A.
  4. At constant T and V with no other work, W = 0, so ΔA ≤ 0: A falls until it reaches a minimum, which is equilibrium.
  5. At constant T and p split W into pV work pΔV and useful work Wu: then Wu ≤ −ΔG. With no useful work, ΔG ≤ 0, and equilibrium is the minimum of G.

So −ΔA is the largest total work at constant T, and −ΔG is the largest non-pV (electrical or chemical) work at constant T and p. A battery or a fuel cell delivers at most −ΔG of electrical work. A turbine is a different case: its shaft work is limited by the fall in flow exergy, not by −ΔG.

For a pure substance the Gibbs function per mole is the chemical potential. Two phases of a pure substance are in equilibrium when their specific Gibbs functions are equal, gliquid = gsolid: this is the starting point of the Clausius-Clapeyron equation.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of air (ideal gas, R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K)) is compressed slowly and isothermally at 300 K from 100 kPa to 500 kPa. Find ΔU, ΔH, ΔS, ΔA and ΔG, and the least work needed.

  1. Ideal gas at constant T: ΔU = 0 and ΔH = 0, because u and h depend on T only.

Answer. ΔU = ΔH = 0; ΔS = −0.4619 kJ/K; ΔA = ΔG = +138.6 kJ; the least work input is 138.6 kJ.

4.5 Maxwell relations

A Maxwell relation turns an entropy slope, which cannot be measured, into a pressure-volume-temperature slope, which can. It exists because each of the four potentials U, H, A and G has an exact differential, and exactness forces its two cross-derivatives to be equal. Four potentials give four relations. With the TdS equations they give entropy, internal energy and enthalpy changes of real gases and liquids from p–v–T data alone, which is how steam and refrigerant tables are built.

You need: the exactness test of Exact differentials, reciprocity and cyclic relations, and the four differentials of Gibbs and Helmholtz functions.

From an exact differential to a Maxwell relation

Start with dU = T dS − p dV. It has the form M dx + N dy with x = S, M = T, y = V and N = −p. The exactness test (∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y gives at once

(∂T/∂V)S = −(∂p/∂S)V

The left side is the rate of change of temperature with volume at constant entropy; the right side is minus the rate of change of pressure with entropy at constant volume.

That is the first Maxwell relation. It is not a new law, only the exactness of dU written out. The same steps work for every potential:

  1. Pick the potential whose natural variables match the slope you want: U(S, V), H(S, p), A(T, V) or G(T, p).
  2. Write its differential and read the coefficients M and N, with their signs.
  3. Apply the exactness test, keeping every minus sign in front of a coefficient.
  4. Check the subscripts: they must be the natural variables of that potential.

The four relations and entropy from p–v–T data

PotentialDifferentialMaxwell relation
UdU = T dS − p dV(∂T/∂V)S = −(∂p/∂S)V
HdH = T dS + V dp(∂T/∂p)S = (∂V/∂S)p
AdA = −S dT − p dV(∂S/∂V)T = (∂p/∂T)V
GdG = −S dT + V dp(∂S/∂p)T = −(∂V/∂T)p

The last two hold T fixed and put entropy on the left, so they are the working relations: (∂s/∂v)T = (∂p/∂T)v and (∂s/∂p)T = −(∂v/∂T)p, per unit mass. Their right-hand sides are slopes of an equation of state or of a table. For an ideal gas, v = RT/p gives (∂v/∂T)p = R/p, so (∂s/∂p)T = −R/p and an isothermal change gives Δs = −R·ln(p₂/p₁).

TdS equations and energy relations

Write s = s(T, v) and s = s(T, p), use cv = T(∂s/∂T)v and cp = T(∂s/∂T)p, and replace the entropy slopes by Maxwell relations. This gives the two TdS equations:

T ds = cvdT + T(∂p/∂T)vdv

First TdS equation: cv in kJ/(kg·K); the slope (∂p/∂T)v comes from the equation of state.

T ds = cpdT − T(∂v/∂T)pdp

Second TdS equation: cp in kJ/(kg·K); the slope (∂v/∂T)p is measured at constant pressure.

Put the first into du = T ds − p dv and the second into dh = T ds + v dp:

du = cvdT + [T(∂p/∂T)v − p]dv

The bracket is how internal energy changes with volume at constant temperature.

dh = cpdT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p]dp

The bracket is how enthalpy changes with pressure at constant temperature.

For an ideal gas both brackets are zero (T·R/v − p = 0 and v − T·R/p = 0), so u and h depend on temperature alone. For a real gas they are not zero, which is why steam tables need pressure as well as temperature. The enthalpy bracket also gives the Joule-Thomson coefficient, and the two TdS equations together give cp − cv (see Expansivity, compressibility, Mayer relation and Joule-Thomson coefficient).

4.6 Clausius-Clapeyron equation

Two phases of a pure substance coexist in equilibrium only along one curve of the p–T plane, the saturation line. Along it their Gibbs functions are equal. Differentiating that equality gives the slope dp/dT in terms of the latent heat and the volume change: the Clapeyron equation. Its liquid-vapour form with an ideal-gas vapour, the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, turns vapour-pressure readings into latent heats, predicts boiling points, and explains why pressure lowers the melting point of ice.

You need: dg = v dp − s dT and the equality of Gibbs functions at phase equilibrium from Gibbs and Helmholtz functions, and saturation properties from the steam tables.

Equal Gibbs functions fix the slope of the saturation line

Take a pure substance with two phases, 1 and 2, in equilibrium at p and T. Then g₁ = g₂. Move a small step along the saturation line: T rises by dT and p by dp, and the phases stay in equilibrium, so dg₁ = dg₂. Each phase changes by dg = v dp − s dT:

v₁dp − s₁dT = v₂dp − s₂dT

v₁, s₁ are the specific volume (m³/kg) and entropy (kJ/(kg·K)) of phase 1; v₂, s₂ of phase 2.

Collect terms and use s₁ − s₂ = h₁₂/T, because the phase change happens at constant T and p with reversible heat h₁₂ per kg:

dp/dT = (s₁ − s₂)/(v₁ − v₂) = h₁₂/(T·v₁₂)

dp/dT is the slope of the saturation line in Pa/K when h₁₂ is in J/kg; h₁₂ is the latent heat; v₁₂ = v₁ − v₂ is the change of specific volume (m³/kg); T is in K.

This is the Clapeyron equation. It makes no ideal-gas assumption and holds for any pair of phases: solid-liquid, liquid-vapour, solid-vapour. Some syllabi call it the Clapeyron equation and keep the name Clausius-Clapeyron for the vapour form below.

The vapour form: ideal gas and small liquid volume

For liquid and vapour, dp/dT = hfg/(T·vfg) with vfg = vg − vf. Two approximations make it usable with vapour-pressure readings alone. Far below the critical point vf ≪ vg, so vfg ≈ vg; and the vapour is close to an ideal gas, vg = RT/p, with R the gas constant of the vapour (0.4615 kJ/(kg·K) for steam). Then

d(ln p)/dT = hfg/(RT²)

This is the Clausius-Clapeyron equation. A plot of ln p against 1/T is nearly straight over a short range, with slope −hfg/R in kelvin.

With hfg held constant between two states, integration gives the form used in problems:

ln(p₂/p₁) = −(hfg/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

p₁, T₁ is a known saturation pair and p₂, T₂ the other; both temperatures in K; hfg/R is in K, so the right side is a pure number.

Reading the sign: melting lines and boiling lines

For melting, dp/dT = hsf/(T·vsf), with hsf the latent heat of fusion and vsf = vliquid − vsolid. Most solids are denser than their liquids, so vsf > 0 and pressure raises the melting point.

Water is the standard exception: ice is less dense than water, so vsf < 0 and the melting line slopes backwards, about −1.35 × 10⁷ Pa/K. Turned round, dT/dp is about −0.0075 K per atmosphere, so pressure lowers the melting point of ice slightly. This explains regelation: a loaded wire sinks through a block of ice as the ice melts under it, and the water refreezes above it.

On the liquid-vapour line dp/dT is always positive and grows with temperature, so the curve bends upward. A pressure cooker at about 2 bar boils water at a higher temperature and cooks faster; water boils below 100 °C on a mountain. Answer. The melting line has dp/dT = −1.35 × 10⁷ Pa/K; at 10 MPa ice melts at about 272.4 K, 0.73 K below the normal melting point.

4.7 Expansivity, compressibility, Mayer relation and Joule-Thomson coefficient

Three measurable coefficients describe how the volume of a substance responds to heat and pressure: the volume expansivity β, the isothermal compressibility κT and the isentropic compressibility κs. With the TdS equations they give the Mayer relation, cp − cv = Tvβ²/κT, which yields cv of a liquid, where it cannot be measured directly. The same slopes give the Joule-Thomson coefficient, which says whether a gas cools or warms in a throttle.

You need: the cyclic relation from Exact differentials, reciprocity and cyclic relations, and the TdS equations and the enthalpy relation from Maxwell relations.

Expansivity and compressibility

β = (1/v)(∂v/∂T)p

β is the volume expansivity (coefficient of volume expansion), in 1/K: the fractional increase of volume per kelvin at constant pressure.

κT = −(1/v)(∂v/∂p)T

κT is the isothermal compressibility, in 1/Pa: the fractional decrease of volume per pascal at constant temperature. The minus sign makes it positive. Its inverse is the isothermal bulk modulus.

κs = −(1/v)(∂v/∂p)s

κs is the isentropic (adiabatic) compressibility; it sets the speed of sound, c = √(v/κs).

For an ideal gas, v = RT/p gives β = 1/T, κT = 1/p and κs = 1/(γp), where γ = cp/cv. At 300 K and 100 kPa, β = 0.00333 K⁻¹ and κT = 0.01 kPa⁻¹. Liquids are far stiffer: for water at 25 °C, β ≈ 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ and κT ≈ 4.52 × 10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹.

The cyclic relation links the two coefficients to the pressure slope: (∂p/∂T)v = −(∂v/∂T)p/(∂v/∂p)T = β/κT. For water this is about 5.7 × 10⁵ Pa/K, nearly 6 bar for each kelvin, which is why a completely full, sealed vessel of liquid must never be heated.

The Mayer relation for cp − cv

  1. Take the two TdS equations: T ds = cvdT + T(∂p/∂T)vdv and T ds = cpdT − T(∂v/∂T)pdp.
  2. Subtract them: (cp − cv)dT = T(∂p/∂T)vdv + T(∂v/∂T)pdp.
  3. Hold v fixed (dv = 0): cp − cv = T(∂v/∂T)p(∂p/∂T)v.
  4. Replace (∂p/∂T)v by the cyclic relation: cp − cv = −T(∂v/∂T)p²(∂p/∂v)T.
  5. Write the slopes with β and κT: (∂v/∂T)p = vβ and (∂p/∂v)T = −1/(vκT).

cp − cv = Tvβ²/κT

With T in K, v in m³/kg, β in 1/K and κT in 1/Pa, the result is in J/(kg·K).

  • κT is positive for every stable substance and β² cannot be negative, so cp ≥ cv always.
  • cp = cv where β = 0, as for water at about 4 °C, its density maximum.
  • For an ideal gas Tvβ²/κT = T(RT/p)(1/T²)(p) = R, so cp − cv = R. Many textbooks call this ideal-gas form Mayer's relation.
  • Dividing the two TdS equations at constant s gives the ratio γ = cp/cv = κT/κs.

The Joule-Thomson coefficient

A throttle (a valve or porous plug) is adiabatic, does no work and leaves the kinetic energy nearly unchanged, so h₁ = h₂: throttling is a constant-enthalpy process. The Joule-Thomson coefficient (some syllabi say Joule-Kelvin) is the temperature change per unit pressure change at constant enthalpy:

μJT = (∂T/∂p)h

μJT is in K/Pa, or K/bar in practice. Since pressure falls in a throttle, μJT > 0 means the gas cools.

Set dh = 0 in dh = cpdT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p]dp and solve for dT/dp:

μJT = [T(∂v/∂T)p − v]/cp = v(Tβ − 1)/cp

cp is the specific heat at constant pressure; v is the specific volume.

For an ideal gas Tβ = 1, so μJT = 0: throttling changes its pressure but not its temperature. A real gas cools when Tβ > 1 and warms when Tβ < 1. The states with μJT = 0 form the inversion curve, and its highest temperature is the maximum inversion temperature. Nitrogen and air have it far above room temperature, so they cool in a throttle; hydrogen and helium have it far below, so they must be precooled before throttling can cool them.

At low pressure a van der Waals gas gives μJT ≈ (2a/RT − b)/cp,m per mole, which is zero at the inversion temperature Ti = 2a/(Rb). It shows the mechanism, attraction (a) against molecular size (b), but overshoots measured values. Figure 4.7.1 plots it for nitrogen.

Worked example

Question. For liquid water at 25 °C (298.15 K), v = 0.001003 m³/kg, β = 2.57 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹, κT = 4.52 × 10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹ and cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K). Find cp − cv, cv and γ, and the pressure rise per kelvin if the water is heated in a full, rigid, sealed vessel.

Question. Nitrogen at 300 K is throttled from 20 bar to 1 bar. Takeaways

  1. Of heat Q from a source at T, only Q(1 − T₀/T) can become work; the rest, T₀ΔS, must go to the surroundings.
  2. Heat that falls in temperature loses available energy equal to T₀Sgen, even though no energy is lost.
  3. Exergy is measured from the dead state and is destroyed at the rate T₀Sgen; second law efficiency compares a device with the reversible one, not with the energy input.
  4. A property has an exact differential; heat and work do not. The cyclic relation carries a minus sign: the product of the three slopes is −1.
  5. At constant T and V a process can go on its own only if A falls; at constant T and p only if G falls.
  6. Maxwell relations turn entropy slopes into p–v–T slopes, and the TdS equations turn them into entropy, energy and enthalpy changes of real substances.
  7. Along a phase boundary the Gibbs functions of the two phases are equal, which gives the Clapeyron slope h₁₂/(T·v₁₂).
  8. cp − cv = Tvβ²/κT is never negative and equals R for an ideal gas.
  9. A gas cools in a throttle only below its inversion temperature, where μJT > 0; an ideal gas does not change temperature at all.

Model answer.

  1. The idea. The only sink is the surroundings at T₀. The most work any heat can give comes from a reversible engine that takes the heat at the temperature it is supplied and rejects heat at T₀. Understand What is an exact differential? State and prove the test for exactness. Derive the reciprocity and cyclic relations among p, v and T, and verify the cyclic relation for air as an ideal gas at 300 K and 100 kPa. 13 marks

Points to cover. Total differential dz = M dx + N dy; Exact when it is the differential of a function; test (∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y from equal mixed derivatives; Properties are exact; δq and δw are not; 1/T as integrating factor; Derive reciprocity and the cyclic relation from x(y, z) and y(x, z); Numerical check with v = 0.861 m³/kg.

Model answer.

  1. Total differential. For a simple compressible substance any property z depends on two independent properties x and y. A small change gives dz = (∂z/∂x)ydx + (∂z/∂y)xdy = M dx + N dy.
  2. Exact differential. An expression M dx + N dy is exact when it is the differential of some function z(x, y). Its integral between two states then does not depend on the path, and its integral round a cycle is zero.

Apply Define the Joule-Thomson coefficient and derive μJT = [T(∂v/∂T)p − v]/cp. Show that it is zero for an ideal gas, explain the inversion temperature, and estimate the temperature change of nitrogen throttled from 20 bar to 1 bar at 300 K using μJT ≈ (2a/RT − b)/cp,m (a = 0.137 Pa·m⁶/mol², b = 3.87 × 10⁻⁵ m³/mol, cp,m = 29.1 J/(mol·K)). 14 marks

Points to cover. Throttling: adiabatic, no work, so h₁ = h₂; μJT = (∂T/∂p)h; dh = cpdT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p]dp with dh = 0; Ideal gas T(∂v/∂T)p = v, so μJT = 0; Inversion curve and Ti = 2a/(Rb) for a van der Waals gas; Nitrogen: 0.245 K/bar, about −4.6 K, Ti ≈ 852 K.

Model answer.

  1. Throttling. Flow through a valve or porous plug is adiabatic, does no work and changes the kinetic energy very little, so the steady-flow energy equation gives h₁ = h₂. The Joule-Thomson coefficient is μJT = (∂T/∂p)h, the temperature change per unit pressure change at constant enthalpy.
  2. Derivation. Write h = h(T, p) and use the enthalpy relation from the second TdS equation:
    dh = cpdT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p]dp
at constant h:  0 = cpdT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p]dp
μJT = (∂T/∂p)h = [T(∂v/∂T)p − v]/cp = v(Tβ − 1)/cp
  3. Ideal gas. v = RT/p gives T(∂v/∂T)p = T·R/p = v, so μJT = 0. Throttling an ideal gas lowers its pressure but not its temperature, because its enthalpy depends on temperature only.
  4. Real gas and inversion. Pressure always falls in a throttle, so μJT > 0 means cooling and μJT < 0 means warming. The states where μJT = 0 form the inversion curve on a T–p diagram, and its highest temperature is the maximum inversion temperature. A gas must be below it for throttling to cool it.
  5. van der Waals estimate. At low pressure μJT ≈ (2a/RT − b)/cp,m: attraction (a) makes the gas cool, molecular size (b) makes it warm. Setting it to zero gives Ti = 2a/(Rb).

The van der Waals inversion temperature is an overestimate; the measured maximum for nitrogen is lower, near 620 K, but still far above room temperature.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A room must be kept at 22 °C (295 K) when the outside air is at 5 °C (278 K); it needs 10 kW of heat. Compare an electric heater, a heat pump with a COP of 3.5, and a reversible heat pump by their work input, exergy destroyed and second law efficiency. Explain why the heater's first-law efficiency of 100 % hides its waste. 14 marks

Points to cover. Exergy of the heat delivered: Q(1 − T₀/Troom); Heater: W = Q, ηII about 5.8 %; Heat pump COP 3.5: W = 2.857 kW, ηII about 20 %; Reversible: COP = T/(T − T₀) = 17.35, ηII = 1; Exergy destroyed = W − exergy delivered; First-law efficiency counts kJ, not their quality.

Model answer.

  1. Reference. Take the outside air as the surroundings, T₀ = 278 K. The 10 kW of heat is delivered at T = 295 K.
  2. Exergy of the heat delivered. ẊQ = Q̇(1 − T₀/T) = 10 × (1 − 278/295) = 10 × 0.05763 = 0.576 kW. This is the least work any device could use to keep the room warm.
  3. Electric heater. All 10 kW of heat comes from 10 kW of electricity, which is pure exergy. Exergy destroyed = 10 − 0.576 = 9.42 kW; ηII = 0.576/10 = 5.8 %.
  4. Heat pump, COP 3.5. Work W = Q/COP = 10/3.5 = 2.857 kW. The heat pump also takes 10 − 2.857 = 7.143 kW from the outside air, which carries no exergy because it is at T₀. Exergy destroyed = 2.857 − 0.576 = 2.28 kW; ηII = 0.576/2.857 = 20.2 %.
  5. Reversible heat pump. COPrev = T/(T − T₀) = 295/17 = 17.35, so W = 10/17.35 = 0.576 kW, equal to the exergy delivered. Exergy destroyed = 0; ηII = 1.
  6. Summary.

  7. Why the first law misleads. The heater's energy balance is perfect: 10 kW in, 10 kW out. But it turns high-quality energy (electricity) into low-quality energy (heat only 17 K above the surroundings), destroying 9.42 kW of exergy. The second law efficiency exposes this; it also shows that even a good heat pump (ηII = 0.20) has room to improve.
  8. Closing check. ηII of the heat pump also equals COP/COPrev = 3.5/17.35 = 0.202, the same as the exergy route.
Evaluate Use the Clapeyron equation to find the slope of the ice-water melting line at 273.15 K (hsf = 333.5 kJ/kg, vice = 0.0010908, vwater = 0.0010002 m³/kg) and of the water-steam line at 100 °C (hfg = 2257 kJ/kg, vg = 1.672, vf = 0.001043 m³/kg). Explain the signs, estimate the melting point at 10 MPa, and check the boiling-line slope against saturation pressures of 84.61 kPa at 95 °C and 120.90 kPa at 105 °C. 13 marks

Points to cover. Clapeyron: dp/dT = h₁₂/(T v₁₂) from equal Gibbs functions; Melting: vsf < 0 so dp/dT ≈ −1.35 × 10⁷ Pa/K; Melting point at 10 MPa about −0.73 °C; Boiling: dp/dT ≈ 3.62 kPa/K; Table secant 3.63 kPa/K agrees; Sign follows the sign of the volume change.

Model answer.

  1. The equation. Equal Gibbs functions along a phase boundary give dp/dT = h₁₂/(T·v₁₂) (the Clapeyron equation), with h₁₂ the latent heat and v₁₂ the volume change in the same direction (for example liquid minus solid with the heat of melting).
  2. Melting line.
    vsf = 0.0010002 − 0.0010908 = −9.06 × 10⁻⁵ m³/kg
dp/dT = 333 500/(273.15 × (−9.06 × 10⁻⁵)) = −1.348 × 10⁷ Pa/K
dT/dp = −7.42 × 10⁻⁸ K/Pa = −0.0075 K per atmosphere
  3. Melting point at 10 MPa. Δp = 10 × 10⁶ − 101 325 = 9.90 × 10⁶ Pa, so ΔT = −7.42 × 10⁻⁸ × 9.90 × 10⁶ = −0.73 K: ice melts at about 272.4 K (−0.73 °C). The line is so steep that pressure has only a small effect.
  4. Boiling line at 100 °C.
    vfg = 1.672 − 0.001043 = 1.671 m³/kg
dp/dT = 2 257 000/(373.15 × 1.671) = 3620 Pa/K = 3.62 kPa/K
  5. Check with the tables. The secant slope from 95 °C to 105 °C is (120.90 − 84.61)/10 = 3.63 kPa/K, within 0.3 % of the Clapeyron value. The exact equation needs no ideal-gas assumption, so it agrees closely when table values of v are used.
  6. Why the signs differ. The latent heat is positive in both cases (heat is taken in on melting and on boiling), so the sign of dp/dT is the sign of the volume change. Steam takes far more room than water, so the boiling line slopes forward. Ice takes more room than water, so vsf is negative and the melting line slopes backward: raising the pressure favours the denser phase, liquid water, and lowers the melting point. Most other substances shrink on freezing, and their melting lines slope forward.
  7. Judgement. The two slopes differ by a factor of about 3700 (1.35 × 10⁷ against 3.62 × 10³ Pa/K). Pressure moves the boiling point strongly (a pressure cooker at 2 bar boils near 120 °C) but the melting point hardly at all, so claims that skating works only by pressure melting should be treated with care: 10 MPa lowers the melting point by under one kelvin.
  8. Closing check. 1 atm × dT/dp = 101 325 × (−7.42 × 10⁻⁸) = −0.0075 K, the figure usually quoted for ice.

Glossary

Anergy
Another name for unavailable energy: the part of energy that can never become work in given surroundings.
Availability
Another name for exergy, used in many syllabi.
Bulk modulus
The inverse of a compressibility: the pressure rise per fractional decrease of volume.
Chemical potential
The Gibbs function per mole of a substance; equal in two phases in equilibrium.
Entropy generation
Entropy created inside a real process; never negative and zero only for a reversible process.
Integrating factor
A factor that turns an inexact differential into an exact one; 1/T turns δqrev into ds.
Inversion curve
The states where μJT = 0; its highest temperature is the maximum inversion temperature.
Irreversibility
Another name for exergy destroyed, I = T₀Sgen.
Isentropic compressibility
κs = −(1/v)(∂v/∂p)s; fixes the speed of sound.
Isentropic efficiency
Actual work of a turbine divided by the work of an isentropic turbine between the same inlet state and exit pressure.
Natural variables
The two properties that each potential depends on most simply: S, V for U; S, p for H; T, V for A; T, p for G.
Path function
A quantity that depends on the path between states, such as heat and work.
Point function
A quantity that depends only on the state, such as p, T, u or s; its differential is exact.
Regelation
Melting of ice under pressure and refreezing when the pressure is removed.
Reversible work
The largest useful work between two given states, obtained on a fully reversible path.
Saturation line
The curve on a p–T diagram along which two phases coexist in equilibrium.
State postulate
The state of a simple compressible substance is fixed by two independent properties.
Surroundings
The very large body around the system, at T₀ and p₀, that can take or give heat without changing temperature.
Thermodynamic potential
Any of U, H, A and G, whose fall at fixed natural variables bounds the work of a process.
Throttling
Flow through a valve or porous plug with no heat, no work and no change of kinetic energy, so h₁ = h₂.
Useful work
Work done by a closed system minus the work spent pushing back the atmosphere, W − p₀(V₂ − V₁).
van der Waals equation
p = RT/(v − b) − a/v²: a gas model with molecular attraction a and molecular volume b.
