4.1 Available and unavailable energy

Every heat transfer carries a hidden tax. A reservoir at T delivers heat Q, but the surroundings sit at T₀. Only part of Q can become work; the rest must be dumped as heat to the surroundings, however good the engine. This chapter defines the dead state, splits Q into available and unavailable energy, and extends the split to a closed system. Power plants, data-centre cooling and solar thermal design use this split to judge how much work is really on offer.

You need entropy and reversible processes from Unit 3, Entropy, and the closed-system first law from Unit 2, First Law.

Dead state first

The dead state is the state of the surroundings: temperature T₀ and pressure p₀. At this state a system has no unused potential to produce work. Choose T₀ and p₀ once for a problem and keep them fixed. All availability in this chapter is measured relative to that state.

A source at absolute temperature T gives heat Q. The best engine between T and T₀ is reversible. Its Carnot efficiency is 1 − T₀/T, so the largest work is:

A = Q(1 − T₀/T) = Q − T₀ΔS

A is available energy in kJ; Q is heat supplied in kJ; T is source absolute temperature in K; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K; ΔS = Q/T is entropy delivered by the source in kJ/K.

The remaining heat cannot become work. It must go to the surroundings. The source loses entropy ΔS = Q/T. At T₀ that entropy carries heat T₀ΔS. Thus unavailable energy is T₀ΔS. Some syllabi call A availability and T₀ΔS anergy.

Closed-system availability

For a closed system, use internal energy U, volume V and entropy S. The dead state has U₀, V₀ and S₀ at T₀ and p₀. The non-flow availability is:

Φ = (U − U₀) + p₀(V − V₀) − T₀(S − S₀)

Φ is the maximum useful work as the system reaches the dead state, in kJ; U, V, S are system state values; U₀, V₀, S₀ are dead-state values; p₀ is dead-state pressure in kPa; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K.

The term U − U₀ is energy stored above the dead state. p₀(V − V₀) is work the surroundings do or absorb as volume changes. T₀(S − S₀) subtracts the part locked by entropy. Kinetic and potential terms are added separately when present. Flowing-stream exergy is in 4.2.

Availability is not a property

A property depends only on the state of the system. Φ depends also on T₀ and p₀, so it is not a property. Change the environment and the same system state has a different Φ. For the heat example, at T₀ = 300 K and T = 500 K the available fraction is 0.40. Raise T to 1000 K and it becomes 0.70.

  • At T = T₀ the available fraction is zero: no work from heat at environment temperature.
  • As T becomes large, 1 − T₀/T approaches one: hot heat is nearly all available.
  • Keep T and T₀ in kelvin in every formula.

Worked example

Question. Heat of 1000 kJ is supplied from a reservoir at 500 K. The environment is at 300 K. Find the available part, the unavailable part, and the entropy change of the reservoir. Check that the two parts add to Q.

  1. Given: Q = 1000 kJ, T = 500 K, T₀ = 300 K.
  2. Entropy delivered by the reservoir: ΔS = Q/T = 1000/500 = 2 kJ/K.
  3. Available energy: A = Q(1 − T₀/T) = 1000 × (1 − 300/500) = 1000 × 0.6 = 400 kJ.
  4. Unavailable energy: T₀ΔS = 300 × 2 = 600 kJ.
  5. Check: 400 kJ + 600 kJ = 1000 kJ = Q.

Answer. Available energy is 400 kJ, unavailable energy is 600 kJ, and the reservoir entropy change is 2 kJ/K.

Exam traps

  • Putting °C into 1 − T₀/T. Convert both temperatures to kelvin first.
  • Using the sink or working-fluid temperature instead of T₀. T₀ is fixed by the environment.
  • Treating T₀ΔS as the heat an actual engine rejects. An irreversible engine rejects more; T₀ΔS is the reversible-limit unavailable part.
  • Calling availability a property. It changes when the dead state changes.
  • Using flow exergy in a closed-system question. Flow exergy belongs to 4.2.

4.2 Exergy analysis and second law efficiency

Exergy is the useful work a system or stream can still give as it comes to equilibrium with the surroundings. The surroundings are at T₀ and p₀, called the dead state. At the dead state a system has zero exergy. Exergy analysis shows where useful work is lost: every irreversibility destroys exergy equal to T₀ times the entropy generated. Power plants, turbines, heat exchangers and refrigeration units use this to compare real performance with the best possible performance, not merely with the first law energy balance.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the dead state and T₀ΔS, and Unit 3 for entropy generation in an adiabatic device.

Dead state and exergy of a system or stream

The dead state is the state of the environment: temperature T₀, pressure p₀. A system at the dead state cannot produce work by interacting with the environment. Exergy is the maximum useful work a system can deliver as it moves reversibly from its present state to the dead state, exchanging heat only with the environment at T₀. Some syllabi call exergy availability.

Φ = (u − u₀) + p₀(v − v₀) − T₀(s − s₀)

Φ is specific exergy in kJ/kg; u is specific internal energy; v is specific volume; s is specific entropy; subscript 0 is the dead state; T₀ and p₀ are dead-state temperature and pressure.

For a closed system of mass m kg, the total exergy is mΦ kJ, and Φ is the specific exergy in kJ/kg. Here u is specific internal energy in kJ/kg, v is specific volume in m³/kg, s is specific entropy in kJ/kg·K. The term p₀(v − v₀) is work done against the environment; the last term subtracts the heat that must be dumped to the environment.

ψ = (h − h₀) − T₀(s − s₀)

ψ is flow exergy in kJ/kg; h is specific enthalpy; s is specific entropy; subscript 0 is the dead state; T₀ is the dead-state temperature in kelvin.

For a flowing stream, the exergy per kg is ψ. Flow exergy does not contain a p₀v term because the environment already pushes the stream in and out. A stream at the dead state has ψ = 0. Absolute values of h and s are not needed; only changes from the dead state appear. For a steady adiabatic device, the exergy supplied by the stream is ψ₁ − ψ₂. This drop is the maximum work the device could give. It is larger than the actual work when the process is irreversible.

Exergy balance and irreversibility

Write an exergy balance for any steady device: exergy in − exergy out − I = 0. I is the exergy destroyed, also called irreversibility in some syllabi. For a turbine with adiabatic flow, ψ₁ − ψ₂ − w − I = 0, so w = ψ₁ − ψ₂ − I. The work is always less than the exergy drop.

Exergy in − Exergy out − I = 0

I is exergy destroyed, in kJ for a mass m, in kJ/kg for unit mass, in kW for a steady stream; also called irreversibility in some syllabi.

I = T₀ S_gen

I is exergy destroyed; T₀ is dead-state temperature in K; S_gen is entropy generated: S_gen in kJ/kg·K gives I in kJ/kg, S_gen in kW/K gives I in kW.

The Gouy-Stodola theorem gives I = T₀ S_gen. T₀ is the environment temperature in kelvin, S_gen is the entropy generated in kJ/kg·K for a unit mass, so I is in kJ/kg. For an adiabatic device, S_gen = s₂ − s₁. For a device with heat transfer, include the entropy change of the reservoirs that exchange heat. Exergy is not conserved; it is destroyed by friction, mixing, finite-temperature heat transfer, and throttling.

Why T₀? Entropy generation represents energy that has become unavailable. Multiplying by T₀ converts that entropy to the equivalent work that can no longer be obtained. Chapter 4.1 available and unavailable energy uses the same product T₀ΔS.

The figure shows the bookkeeping. Exergy enters with the stream. Part leaves as useful work, and the rest is destroyed. The destroyed part is not a stream; it has left the exergy account.

Second law efficiency, not isentropic efficiency

Second law efficiency compares what we get with the best possible. It is η_II = exergy recovered ÷ exergy supplied. A reversible device has η_II = 1. A real device has η_II < 1.

η_II = Exergy recovered ÷ Exergy supplied

η_II is second law efficiency; both exergy terms use the same units and the same dead state.

η_II,turbine = w / (ψ₁ − ψ₂)

w is actual work out in kJ/kg; ψ₁ and ψ₂ are flow exergies at inlet and outlet in kJ/kg.

Do not confuse η_II with isentropic efficiency. Isentropic efficiency uses only the enthalpy drop to the same exit pressure; second law efficiency uses the exergy drop, which carries T₀(s₂ − s₁). For an adiabatic turbine that expands entirely above T₀, the entropy is generated at temperatures above T₀, so T₀·S_gen is less than the isentropic lost work and η_II comes out higher than η_is. The two numbers are different measures; always check which one the question asks for.

η_II,HE = Δψ_cold / Δψ_hot

Δψ_cold is exergy gained by the cold stream; Δψ_hot is exergy lost by the hot stream, both in kW.

For a heat exchanger, exergy is supplied by the hot stream as it cools: Δψ_hot = ψ_h,in − ψ_h,out. Exergy is recovered by the cold stream: Δψ_cold = ψ_c,out − ψ_c,in. If the hot stream gives 200 kW of exergy and the cold stream receives 150 kW, η_II = 150/200 = 0.75, and 50 kW is destroyed. A reversible heat exchanger with no temperature difference would have equal exergy changes, but real heat transfer across a finite temperature difference destroys exergy.

Worked example

Question. Steam enters an adiabatic turbine at h₁ = 3200 kJ/kg, s₁ = 6.80 kJ/kg·K and leaves at h₂ = 2600 kJ/kg, s₂ = 7.00 kJ/kg·K. The dead state is at T₀ = 300 K. Find the work per kg, the exergy destroyed and the second law efficiency. Take no heat loss.

  1. Energy balance for the adiabatic turbine gives w = h₁ − h₂ = 3200 − 2600 = 600 kJ/kg.
  2. Entropy generation for the adiabatic device is S_gen = s₂ − s₁ = 7.00 − 6.80 = 0.20 kJ/kg·K.
  3. Exergy destroyed is I = T₀ S_gen = 300 K × 0.20 kJ/kg·K = 60 kJ/kg.
  4. Stream exergy drop is ψ₁ − ψ₂ = (h₁ − h₂) − T₀(s₁ − s₂) = 600 − 300 × (6.80 − 7.00) = 600 + 60 = 660 kJ/kg. The same value is w + I = 600 + 60 = 660 kJ/kg.
  5. Second law efficiency is η_II = w/(ψ₁ − ψ₂) = 600/660 = 0.9091, about 0.909 or 90.9%.

Answer. The work is 600 kJ/kg, the exergy destroyed is 60 kJ/kg, and the second law efficiency is 0.909, about 90.9%.

Exam traps

  • Using isentropic efficiency as second law efficiency. Isentropic efficiency uses h₁ − h₂s; second law efficiency uses ψ₁ − ψ₂.
  • Dropping the T₀Δs term and calling the enthalpy drop the exergy drop. For a turbine, ψ₁ − ψ₂ = (h₁ − h₂) − T₀(s₁ − s₂).
  • Using 298 K when the question gives T₀ = 300 K. Use the stated dead-state temperature.
  • Writing S_gen = s₁ − s₂ for an adiabatic device. Entropy generation is positive, s₂ − s₁.
  • Treating destroyed exergy as a stream leaving the device. It is a loss in the balance, not a flow.

4.3 Gibbs and Helmholtz functions

A system at constant T and V, or at constant T and p, is judged not by U or H alone but by two combinations: A = U − TS and G = H − TS. Their fall tells which way a process can go on its own, and how much work can be taken out. Chemists use G to decide if a reaction runs; engineers use it for phase change, equilibrium and shaft work. This chapter defines both functions, obtains their differentials, and reads the two spontaneity tests from them.

You need H = U + pV and the non-flow processes from Unit 2, entropy as a property and the T-s diagram from Unit 3, and 4.1 for why a sink temperature appears in work limits.

Two combinations of U

Internal energy U(S, V) holds everything, but its natural variables are entropy and volume. A laboratory fixes temperature and pressure, not entropy. Two combinations of U replace entropy by temperature and answer the questions a lab asks.

Helmholtz function: A = U − TS. Many books write F instead of A and call it the Helmholtz free energy. U is internal energy in kJ, T is absolute temperature in K, S is entropy in kJ/K. The product TS is in kJ, so A is in kJ.

Gibbs function: G = H − TS, the Gibbs free energy. H = U + pV is enthalpy in kJ, with p in kPa and V in m³; the term pV is in kJ, so G is in kJ.

Each is built only from state properties, so A and G are themselves properties. Their changes depend on the end states alone, not on the path.

A = U − TS G = H − TS

U is internal energy, H is enthalpy and S is entropy, all in kJ (S in kJ/K); T is absolute temperature in K; A and G are in kJ.

Differentials and natural variables

Begin with dU = T dS − p dV for a closed system of fixed mass doing only pV work.

Differentiate A: dA = dU − T dS − S dT. The T dS inside dU cancels the −T dS, leaving dA = −S dT − p dV. So A is a function of T and V.

Differentiate G: dG = dH − T dS − S dT, and dH = T dS + V dp. Again T dS cancels, giving dG = −S dT + V dp. So G is a function of T and p.

In both, the coefficient of dT is −S. The second term changes with the variable that is held: −p dV for A, +V dp for G. These slopes are the starting point of the Maxwell relations.

FunctionDefinitionNatural variablesDifferential
UUS, VdU = T dS − p dV
HU + pVS, pdH = T dS + V dp
AU − TST, VdA = −S dT − p dV
GH − TST, pdG = −S dT + V dp

What the fall means

Fix T and the second law decides the direction.

  1. A closed system at temperature T exchanges heat Q with a reservoir at the same T. First law: Q = ΔU + W, where W is work done by the system.
  2. Second law for system plus reservoir: ΔS − Q/T ≥ 0, so Q ≤ TΔS.
  3. Substitute in the first law: W ≤ TΔS − ΔU = −(ΔU − TΔS) = −ΔA at constant T. The work delivered cannot exceed −ΔA.
  4. At constant T and V no pV work is possible. With no other work, W = 0 and ΔA ≤ 0. A falls to a minimum, and that minimum is equilibrium.
  5. At constant T and p, split W into pV work and useful work: W = W_u + pΔV. Then W_u ≤ −ΔG. With no useful work, ΔG ≤ 0, and equilibrium is the minimum of G.

So −ΔA is the maximum total work from a process at constant T, and −ΔG is the maximum non-pV work at constant T and p. A battery, a fuel cell, an electrolyser: the non-pV work they deliver or absorb at constant T and p is bounded by −ΔG. A steady-flow device such as a turbine at constant T and p has the same bound on shaft work.

For a pure substance G = μn, where n is the amount in mol and μ is the chemical potential in kJ/mol, equal to the molar Gibbs function. Two phases in equilibrium share the same μ, which is the fact behind the Clausius-Clapeyron equation.

Worked example

Question. 1 kg of water freezes at 273 K and 1 atm. The latent heat of fusion is 334 kJ/kg and the volume change is negligible. Find ΔG and ΔA for the freezing, and state what the answers mean.

  1. Temperature and pressure are both fixed, so Gibbs decides the direction: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.
  2. Freezing releases the latent heat: ΔH = −334 kJ/kg × 1 kg = −334 kJ.
  3. ΔS = ΔH/T = −334/273 = −1.2234 kJ/K.
  4. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS = −334 − 273 × (−1.2234) = −334 + 334.0 = 0 kJ.
  5. For ΔA, use dA = −S dT − p dV. Here ΔT = 0 and ΔV ≈ 0, so ΔA = 0 kJ. Check: ΔU = ΔH − pΔV ≈ ΔH, so ΔA = ΔU − TΔS ≈ 0 too.
  6. ΔG is exactly zero for a phase change at its equilibrium temperature and pressure. ΔA is zero here only because the volume change is negligible; in general ΔA = −pΔV, which is small but not zero.

Answer. ΔG = 0 kJ and ΔA ≈ 0 kJ; at equilibrium ΔG is exactly zero, and ΔA is zero here only because the volume change is negligible.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: dA = −S dT − p dV but dG = −S dT + V dp. The second term flips sign and changes variable; write both together until they stick.
  • Using ΔG ≤ 0 as the test at constant T and V. There the criterion is ΔA ≤ 0, minimum of A.
  • Quoting A or G in kJ/kg K. They are totals in kJ. The specific functions are a = u − Ts and g = h − Ts, in kJ/kg, with u and h in kJ/kg and s in kJ/(kg·K).
  • Reading ΔG = 0 as 'nothing happens'. It means the states are in equilibrium, like ice and water at 273 K, not that melting has stopped.
  • Using ΔG as a rate. A large negative ΔG says the change is favoured, not that it is fast.

4.4 Maxwell relations

A Maxwell relation turns an entropy derivative you cannot measure into a pressure–volume–temperature slope you can. It exists because every thermodynamic potential — internal energy, enthalpy, Helmholtz function, Gibbs function — has an exact differential, and exactness forces the two mixed second derivatives to be equal. That single fact yields four equations. Engineers use them to compute entropy changes from p-v-T data, to build the enthalpy departure term in real-gas charts, and to predict whether a gas cools or warms when it is throttled through a valve.

You need: 4.3 for the exact differentials of A and G, and Unit 3 for entropy as a state property.

Exact differentials make mixed derivatives equal

Internal energy U is a state property: the change between two states is the same whatever path the gas took. Its differential is therefore exact. For a simple compressible system, with two independent properties:

dU = T dS − p dV

U is internal energy in kJ, T is absolute temperature in K, S is entropy in kJ/K, p is pressure in kPa and V is volume in m³.

Euler's criterion says: if dz = M dx + N dy is exact, then (∂M/∂y)x = (∂N/∂x)y. The subscript names the variable held constant. The criterion is only the statement that the two mixed second derivatives of z are equal, since both are ∂²z/∂x∂y.

Match terms. The coefficient of dS is T, so M = T and x = S. The coefficient of dV is −p, so N = −p and y = V. Applying the criterion:

(∂T/∂V)S = −(∂p/∂S)V

the left side is the rate of change of temperature with volume at constant entropy; the right side is minus the rate of change of pressure with entropy at constant volume.

That is the first Maxwell relation. It is not a new law, only the exactness of dU written out. Its value is that it swaps one partial derivative for another, so a quantity you cannot measure is replaced by one you can.

  1. Pick the potential whose natural variables match the derivative you want: U(S,V), H(S,p), A(T,V) or G(T,p).
  2. Write its exact differential from the definition of the potential.
  3. Read the coefficient of each differential as a partial derivative; this fixes M, N, x and y.
  4. Apply Euler's criterion, keeping every minus sign that sits in front of a coefficient.
  5. Check the subscripts: they must be the two natural variables of that potential.

The four relations and entropy from p-v-T data

H = U + pV, A = U − TS and G = H − TS each give an exact differential (see 4.3). The same steps on each produce the remaining three Maxwell relations.

PotentialExact differentialMaxwell relation
UdU = T dS − p dV(∂T/∂V)S = −(∂p/∂S)V
HdH = T dS + V dp(∂T/∂p)S = (∂V/∂S)p
AdA = −S dT − p dV(∂S/∂V)T = (∂p/∂T)V
GdG = −S dT + V dp(∂S/∂p)T = −(∂V/∂T)p

Two of the four put entropy on the left with T held fixed. Those are the working relations, because the right-hand sides are ordinary p-v-T slopes:

(∂s/∂v)<sub>T</sub> = (∂p/∂T)<sub>v</sub> , (∂s/∂p)<sub>T</sub> = −(∂v/∂T)<sub>p</sub>

lowercase s and v are per unit mass, in kJ/(kg·K) and m³/kg; the first gives entropy change from a volume change, the second from a pressure change, both at constant temperature.

The second one is the standard route to isothermal entropy change. For an ideal gas v = RT/p, so (∂v/∂T)p = R/p and (∂s/∂p)T = −R/p. Integrating from p₁ to p₂ at constant T gives Δs = −R ln(p₂/p₁).

The same substitution gives the enthalpy relation. Start from dh = T ds + v dp and hold T constant:

(∂h/∂p)T = v − T(∂v/∂T)p

h is specific enthalpy in kJ/kg and v is specific volume in m³/kg; the term v − T(∂v/∂T)p is the enthalpy departure from ideal-gas behaviour.

For an ideal gas T(∂v/∂T)p = v, so the departure vanishes and enthalpy depends on temperature alone. For a real gas it does not, which is why steam tables need pressure as well as temperature.

The Joule-Kelvin coefficient

Push a gas through a throttle — a porous plug or a slightly open valve. It is adiabatic, does no shaft work and its kinetic energy is unchanged, so h₁ = h₂: throttling is a constant-enthalpy process. The Joule-Kelvin coefficient asks whether the temperature falls.

μ<sub>JK</sub> = (∂T/∂p)<sub>h</sub> = [T(∂v/∂T)<sub>p</sub> − v] / c<sub>p</sub>

μJK is in K/kPa, cp is the specific heat at constant pressure in kJ/(kg·K), and the bracket is the enthalpy departure term above.

Derivation: write dh = cp dT + [v − T(∂v/∂T)p] dp. Set dh = 0 and solve for (∂T/∂p)h.

For an ideal gas v = RT/p, so T(∂v/∂T)p = v and μJK = 0 exactly. Throttling an ideal gas drops its pressure and leaves its temperature alone. A real gas has μJK > 0 below its inversion temperature (it cools, which is what a refrigerator or an air liquefier wants) and μJK < 0 above it (it warms).

Only a few universities teach the van der Waals result. For one mole, μJK ≈ (2a/RT − b)/cp, where a is the attraction constant in Pa·m⁶/mol², b is the co-volume in m³/mol and cp is in J/(mol·K). Setting μJK = 0 gives the inversion temperature Ti = 2a/(Rb).

Worked example

Question. Air is treated as an ideal gas with R = 0.287 kJ/(kg·K). It is compressed isothermally at 300 K from 1 bar to 5 bar. Using a Maxwell relation, find the entropy change per kg.

  1. Pick the relation linking s, T and p. From dG = −s dT + v dp, the Maxwell relation is (∂s/∂p)T = −(∂v/∂T)p.
  2. For an ideal gas v = RT/p, so (∂v/∂T)p = R/p.
  3. Hence (∂s/∂p)T = −R/p, and at constant T, ds = −R dp/p.
  4. Integrate from p₁ = 1 bar to p₂ = 5 bar: Δs = −R ln(p₂/p₁) = −0.287 × ln 5 kJ/(kg·K).
  5. ln 5 = 1.6094, so Δs = −0.287 × 1.6094 = −0.4619 kJ/(kg·K).
  6. Round to −0.462 kJ/(kg·K). The sign is negative because squeezing the gas into a smaller volume at constant temperature lowers its entropy.

Answer. Δs = −0.462 kJ/(kg·K); the entropy falls because the gas is compressed into a smaller volume at constant temperature.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the Joule-Kelvin coefficient of an ideal gas and say what it means for a throttle.

  1. For an ideal gas, v = RT/p.
  2. Differentiate at constant p: (∂v/∂T)p = R/p = v/T.
  3. Then T(∂v/∂T)p = T × (v/T) = v.
  4. Substitute in μJK = [T(∂v/∂T)p − v]/cp = (v − v)/cp = 0.
  5. A gas at the hot end of the bracket gets no temperature change on throttling, though its pressure still drops.

Answer. μ_JK = 0 exactly for an ideal gas; throttling changes pressure but not temperature.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: (∂s/∂v)T = (∂p/∂T)v and (∂T/∂p)S = (∂V/∂S)p carry no minus, the other two do. Writing (∂s/∂p)T = +(∂v/∂T)p is the commonest error; re-derive from the potential instead of memorising signs.
  • Wrong subscript: the subscript names the variable held constant and must match the potential you started from. (∂s/∂p)T comes from G; (∂s/∂p)h is a different quantity used in throttling.
  • Mixing V and v: the relations hold for total volume in m³ or specific volume in m³/kg, never both in one problem — the mismatch costs a factor of mass.
  • Reading μJK = 0 as 'no throttling': for an ideal gas the pressure falls and enthalpy stays constant; only the temperature is unchanged.
  • Using the relations outside their assumption: they belong to a simple compressible system with two independent properties, so a system with magnetic, electrical or surface work does not obey them as written.

4.5 Clausius-Clapeyron equation

Two phases of a pure substance coexist in equilibrium along one curve in the p-T plane, the saturation line. There their Gibbs functions are equal. Differentiating that equality along the line gives dp/dT in terms of latent heat and volume change: the Clapeyron equation. Its liquid-vapour form, the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, converts vapour-pressure data into latent heats, predicts boiling points, and explains regelation.

You need 4.3 for equal Gibbs functions at phase equilibrium and dg = v dp − s dT, and 4.4 for turning differentials into measurable slopes; saturation data come from Unit 5, Pure Substances.

Equal Gibbs functions fix the slope of the saturation line

Take a pure substance with two phases, say liquid and vapour, at pressure p and temperature T. Equilibrium means no reason to leave one phase for the other. From 4.3, this requires equal specific Gibbs functions: g₁ = g₂, with g in kJ/kg.

Move a small step along the saturation line: T rises by dT, p by dp, both phases stay in equilibrium. Each phase changes by dg = v dp − s dT, where v is specific volume (m³/kg) and s is specific entropy (kJ/kg·K). Equality must persist, so dg₁ = dg₂:

v₁ dp − s₁ dT = v₂ dp − s₂ dT

v₁, s₁ are for phase 1 at the point; v₂, s₂ are for phase 2.

Collect terms and divide by dT:

dp/dT = (s₁ − s₂)/(v₁ − v₂) = h₁₂/(T v₁₂)

dp/dT is the saturation-line slope (Pa/K); s₁ − s₂ is entropy change of the phase change; h₁₂ = T(s₁ − s₂) is latent heat (J/kg); v₁₂ is specific-volume change (m³/kg); T is in kelvin.

This is the Clapeyron equation. It assumes no ideal gas and holds for any phase pair: solid-liquid, liquid-vapour, solid-vapour, even two solid forms. Some syllabi call it the Clapeyron equation and reserve Clausius-Clapeyron for the vapour form.

The vapour form: ideal gas, small liquid volume

Apply it to liquid and vapour. Take phase 1 as vapour, phase 2 as liquid:

dp/dT = h_fg/(T v_fg), with v_fg = v_g − v_f

h_fg is latent heat of vaporisation (kJ/kg); v_g and v_f are specific volumes of saturated vapour and liquid (m³/kg).

Two approximations make it usable from vapour-pressure data. Far below the critical point v_f ≪ v_g, so v_fg ≈ v_g. Treat the vapour as ideal gas: v_g = RT/p, with R the vapour gas constant (kJ/kg·K). Substituting:

d(ln p)/dT = h_fg/(R T²),  or  d(ln p)/d(1/T) = −h_fg/R

ln p is natural log of saturation pressure; the slope of ln p against 1/T is −h_fg/R, in kelvin.

This is the Clausius-Clapeyron equation. A plot of ln p against 1/T is nearly straight over a short range, with slope −h_fg/R. That slope gives average h_fg. With h_fg constant, integrate between two states:

ln(p₂/p₁) = −(h_fg/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁)

p₁, T₁ are a known saturation pair; p₂, T₂ the unknown pair; both T in kelvin. h_fg/R has units K, so the right side is a plain number.

Reading the sign: melting lines and boiling lines

For melting, the same equation gives dp/dT = h_fus/(T v_fus), where h_fus is latent heat of fusion and v_fus = v_liquid − v_solid. Most solids are denser than their liquids, so v_fus is positive and the melting line leans forward: pressure raises the melting point.

Water reverses this because ice is less dense than liquid water. v_fus is negative, so dp/dT is negative, about −1.3 × 10⁷ Pa/K; dT/dp ≈ −0.0074 K per atmosphere. The melting point of ice falls as pressure rises. This is regelation: a weighted wire melts ice below it, water flows up and refreezes above, and the block ends whole. Pressure melting is only one contributor to skating; friction and the liquid-like surface layer do most of the work.

On the liquid-vapour line dp/dT is always positive and grows with T, so the curve bends upward and steepens. A pressure cooker at about 2 bar boils water hotter and cooks faster; boiling point falls with altitude. Run backwards, the curve gives h_fg from two pressure readings, or saturation pressure at an unmeasured temperature.

Worked example

Question. Water boils at T₁ = 373 K where p₁ = 101.3 kPa. Take h_fg = 2257 kJ/kg and R = 0.4615 kJ/kg·K for steam. Using the Clausius-Clapeyron equation with h_fg held constant, estimate the saturation pressure at T₂ = 323 K, and say why the answer differs from the steam-table value of about 12.3 kPa.

Python program.

import math
hfg = 2257.0
R = 0.4615
T1, T2 = 373.0, 323.0
p1 = 101.3
d = 1/T2 - 1/T1
ratio = hfg/R
lnp = -ratio*d
p2 = p1*math.exp(lnp)
print(round(ratio, 2))
print(round(d, 8))
print(round(lnp, 4))
print(round(p2, 2))
  1. Both temperatures are already in kelvin.
  2. 1/T₂ = 1/323 = 3.0960 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹ and 1/T₁ = 1/373 = 2.6810 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹.
  3. 1/T₂ − 1/T₁ = 4.150 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
  4. h_fg/R = 2257/0.4615 = 4890.6 K.
  5. Exponent = −(4890.6)(4.150 × 10⁻⁴) = −2.0296.
  6. p₂/p₁ = e^(−2.0296) = 0.13138.
  7. p₂ = 101.3 × 0.13138 = 13.31 kPa.
  8. Comment: the estimate is about 8 per cent high. The real h_fg rises from 2257 to about 2382 kJ/kg between 373 K and 323 K, and the vapour is not perfectly ideal; both approximations matter more over a wide gap.

Exam traps

  • Using Celsius in ln p or the exponent. The integrated form needs kelvin; 50 instead of 323 is nonsense.
  • Mixing units so h_fg and R are not both per kg. 2257 kJ/kg with 461.5 J/kg·K makes h_fg/R a thousand times too small.
  • Using v_fg ≈ v_g and ideal vapour near the critical point, where v_f and v_g are both large and nearly equal. Use full Clapeyron with table values there.

Takeaways

  1. Of any heat Q from a source at T, only Q(1 − T₀/T) is available as work; the rest, T₀ΔS, is thrown to the surroundings.
  2. Available energy grows with source temperature, so the same 100 kJ of heat is worth more at 800 K than at 400 K.
  3. Exergy is measured from the dead state; it is destroyed by irreversibility at the rate T₀ S_gen, and it is never created.
  4. Second-law efficiency tells you how close a device is to the best possible device, while first-law efficiency only compares output to input.
  5. At constant T and V the Helmholtz function must fall for a process to be spontaneous; at constant T and p the Gibbs function must fall.
  6. Maxwell relations turn entropy, which is hard to measure, into pressure, volume and temperature slopes, which are easy to measure.
  7. TdS equations plus Maxwell relations give entropy changes of real gases and liquids from p–v–T data alone.
  8. At phase equilibrium the Gibbs functions of the two phases are equal, and differentiating that equality gives the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Evaluate Derive the TdS equations and explain how, together with the Maxwell relations, they let you compute entropy changes of liquids and real gases from property data. 14 marks

First law for a reversible path: dQ_rev = dU + p dV and dQ_rev = T dS; Hence T dS = dU + p dV; use H = U + pV for T dS = dH − V dp; Divide by T and expand in the chosen pair of variables; Replace (∂S/∂p)_T and (∂S/∂V)_T using Maxwell relations; For a liquid use v ≈ constant and s₂ − s₁ = c ln(T₂/T₁); For a gas use the ideal-gas forms or integrate (∂v/∂T)_p from tables.

Analyze Explain why a plant's exergy analysis points to different improvement targets than a first-law energy balance. Illustrate with a boiler, a turbine and a condenser. 14 marks

Energy balance conserves kJ; exergy balance destroys some; Boiler: large irreversibility from burning fuel at flame temperature against steam at lower temperature; Turbine: irreversibility from friction and throttling, given by T₀ S_gen; Condenser: heat rejected near T₀ carries little exergy but wastes a lot of energy; Exergy destruction as a percentage of fuel exergy ranks the components; Recommendation follows the ranking, not the energy losses.

Evaluate From Gibbs phase equilibrium, derive the slope of the fusion line for water and explain why it is negative while the vaporisation line is positive. 13 marks

dp/dT = h_fg/(T v_fg) for any phase change; For melting, h_sl > 0 and v_sl < 0 because ice is less dense than water; Product gives dp/dT < 0, so pressure lowers the melting point; For vaporisation h_fg > 0 and v_fg > 0, so dp/dT > 0; Compare with the general Clausius–Clapeyron form and the triple point; Mention use in refrigeration and in skating-type pressure effects cautiously.

Glossary

Absolute entropy
Entropy measured from the zero fixed by the third law, so tables list s at 0 K as zero.
Availability function
The combination (U + p₀V − T₀S) for a closed system, or (H − T₀S) for a stream, whose fall gives useful work.
Clapeyron equation
dp/dT = s_fg/v_fg, the exact slope of any phase boundary, valid for melting and sublimation too.
Control volume
A fixed region in space with mass flowing in and out, used for steady-flow exergy balances.
Critical point
The end of the saturation line, where liquid and vapour become identical so that v_fg → 0 and h_fg → 0.
Enthalpy
H = U + pV, the property that accounts for both internal energy and flow work, in kJ.
Entropy generation S_gen
The entropy created inside a real process, always ≥ 0 and zero only for a reversible process.
Exergy balance
Exergy in = exergy out + exergy destroyed + change in stored exergy, for a control volume over a time interval.
Ideal gas Joule–Thomson coefficient
Zero, because h depends on T alone; a real gas cools or warms on throttling depending on its inversion temperature.
Isentropic efficiency
Ratio of actual work of a turbine (or compressor work input) to the work of an isentropic device between the same inlet state and outlet pressure.
Isothermal compressibility
κ = −(1/v)(∂v/∂p)_T, the fractional volume drop per unit pressure rise, in 1/Pa.
Kelvin–Planck statement
No device working in a cycle can convert all the heat from a single reservoir into work.
Natural variables
The pair of properties held constant for a potential: S and V for U, S and p for H, T and V for A, T and p for G.
Phase equilibrium
The state where two phases coexist without net transfer of mass, requiring equal temperature, equal pressure and equal Gibbs function per mole.
p–v–T data
Measured pressure, specific volume and temperature of a substance, from which entropy and exergy can be computed through Maxwell relations.
Reversible work
The largest work a system can give between two given end states, obtained on a fully reversible path.
Saturation line
The curve on a p–T diagram along which a liquid and its vapour coexist in equilibrium.
Sublimation
Direct change from solid to vapour, described by the same Clausius–Clapeyron form with h_sg and v_sg.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system boundary; its temperature T₀ and pressure p₀ define the reference for exergy.
Thermodynamic potential
Any property such as U, H, A or G whose decrease at fixed natural variables gives the maximum work of a process.
Third law of thermodynamics
The entropy of a perfect crystal tends to zero as the absolute temperature tends to zero, giving absolute entropy values.
Universe
System plus surroundings together; its entropy never decreases, and the rise equals S_gen.
Useful work
Work delivered to a device other than the surroundings, that is, shaft work; work spent pushing the atmosphere is excluded.
Volume expansivity
β = (1/v)(∂v/∂T)_p, the fractional volume change per kelvin, in 1/K.
