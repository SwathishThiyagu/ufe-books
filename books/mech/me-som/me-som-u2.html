2.1 States of stress: uniaxial, biaxial, triaxial

A stress state at a point is not one number. It is a small table of normal and shear stresses on three faces, and six independent values fix it. Engineers label it uniaxial, biaxial or triaxial to choose the right formula for strength, strain and failure. The geometry and restraints decide whether a problem is plane stress or plane strain; once that choice is made it fixes σz (zero for plane stress, ν(σx + σy) for elastic plane strain) and every later result.

You need Unit 1: normal and shear stress, Hooke's law and Poisson's ratio.

Stress at a point: six numbers

Take a tiny cube at a point. On each face there is a normal stress σ and two shear stresses τ. For equilibrium, τxy = τyx, so nine components reduce to six: σx, σy, σz, τxy, τyz, τzx. Tension is positive. The first index of τxy is the face normal; the second is the direction. Some books swap this order.

σ = ( σ<sub>x</sub>  τ<sub>xy</sub>  τ<sub>zx</sub> ; τ<sub>xy</sub>  σ<sub>y</sub>  τ<sub>yz</sub> ; τ<sub>zx</sub>  τ<sub>yz</sub>  σ<sub>z</sub> )

σ is the stress tensor; σx, σy, σz are normal stresses; τxy, τyz, τzx are shear stresses, with τxz = τzx; all in MPa.

The tensor describes the point, not the whole body. A tensile bar has one nonzero normal stress on its cross-section. A shaft in torsion has shear. A beam in bending has normal stress varying across depth (see Unit 4). The six numbers are enough to find stresses on any plane, which is the job of 2.2 and transformation in 2.3.

Uniaxial, biaxial and triaxial states

A uniaxial state has only one nonzero normal stress, often σx; all other normals and shears are zero. A tensile test coupon away from the grips is close to this. A biaxial state has two nonzero normal stresses. A thin cylinder under internal pressure has hoop and longitudinal stresses (Unit 7), a common biaxial field. If the third normal stress is zero and shear on that face is zero, the state is plane stress. Biaxial loading is therefore often treated as a plane-stress problem. Principal axes are in 2.4.

A triaxial state has three nonzero normal stresses. A point deep in a thick cylinder or under a bearing contact is triaxial. Hydrostatic pressure has all three normals equal and compressive; it is triaxial but has no shear. The labels count nonzero principal normal stresses. They do not count the number of lines in a drawing.

Plane stress, plane strain and the spherical split

Plane stress means σz = 0 and τxz = τyz = 0, but εz is not zero. A thin plate loaded in its plane is close to plane stress. Plane strain means εz = 0, not σz = 0. A long dam or a long retaining wall changes little along its length. For an elastic isotropic material, setting εz = 0 gives σz = ν(σx + σy). With σx = 100 MPa, σy = 40 MPa and ν = 0.3, σz = 42 MPa, not zero.

σ<sub>m</sub> = (σ<sub>x</sub> + σ<sub>y</sub> + σ<sub>z</sub>) / 3

σm is the mean normal stress in MPa; σx, σy, σz are the three normal stresses in MPa.

The advanced split writes the tensor as a spherical part plus a deviatoric part. The spherical part has σm on all three diagonals and zero shear. The deviatoric part is the original tensor minus the spherical part. The spherical part tends to change volume; the deviatoric part tends to change shape and drives yielding in ductile metals. Only a few universities teach this split in Strength of Materials; it is standard in advanced solid mechanics.

Worked example

Question. At a point in a machine part, the normal stresses are σx = 120 MPa, σy = 60 MPa and σz = 0. All shear stresses are zero. (a) Name the stress state. (b) Split the stress tensor into spherical and deviatoric parts.

  1. Write the tensor. All shear stresses are zero, so only the three diagonal terms remain: σx = 120 MPa, σy = 60 MPa, σz = 0.
  2. Two normal stresses are nonzero and σz = 0 with zero shear on the z-face. The state is biaxial plane stress.
  3. Mean normal stress: σm = (120 + 60 + 0) / 3 = 60 MPa.
  4. Spherical part: put 60 MPa on all three diagonals. The spherical tensor is diag(60, 60, 60) MPa.
  5. Deviatoric part: subtract the spherical part from the original tensor. The deviatoric tensor is diag(120 − 60, 60 − 60, 0 − 60) = diag(60, 0, −60) MPa.

Answer. The state is biaxial plane stress. The mean stress is 60 MPa; the spherical part is diag(60, 60, 60) MPa; the deviatoric part is diag(60, 0, −60) MPa.

Exam traps

  • Calling any two-dimensional drawing plane stress when the problem states plane strain. In plane strain, εz = 0, and σz = ν(σx + σy), not zero.
  • Mixing biaxial with plane stress. A biaxial state has two nonzero principal normal stresses; a plane-stress state is fixed by σz = 0 and zero shear on the z-face.
  • Forgetting the mean-stress denominator. The mean uses all three normal stresses, so σm = (σx + σy + σz) / 3, not the average of two.
  • Sign slips in the deviatoric part. Use deviatoric = original − spherical. A compressive normal stress is negative, so its deviatoric value can be positive after subtraction.
  • Thinking a hydrostatic stress changes shape. Equal normal stresses on all faces give no shear and no distortion; the deviatoric part is the shape-changing part.

2.2 Stresses on inclined planes

An inclined plane is any cut through a stressed body that is not one of the original x or y faces. The same material carries different normal and shear stresses on that cut. Engineers need these values because a crack, a weld or a shaft keyway may fail on a plane that is not horizontal or vertical. Force equilibrium on a small wedge gives σn and τnt as functions of 2θ. The result explains why a tensile bar yields on a 45° plane and why a thin cylinder under pressure is checked in two directions.

You need: 2.1 States of stress and Unit 1 equilibrium of forces.

The plane, its normal and the angle θ

Take a point in a body under plane stress. The x face is the face whose outward normal is +x. On it the normal stress is σx (MPa) and the shear stress is τxy (MPa). On the y face the normal stress is σy and the shear stress is the same τxy. Draw a cut through the point. Its outward normal n makes an angle θ with the +x axis, measured counterclockwise. The plane itself is perpendicular to n. At θ=0 the plane is the x face, so σn=σx and τnt=τxy. Some syllabi say θ is measured from the x face; this means the normal turns from +x towards +y.

The stress on this cut has two parts. The normal stress σn acts along n; it is positive when tensile. The shear stress τnt acts along t, which is n rotated 90° counterclockwise; it is positive in the +t direction. On the x face at θ=0, +t is +y, which is the usual positive shear.

σn = (σx + σy)/2 + (σx − σy)/2 · cos2θ + τxy · sin2θ ;  τnt = −(σx − σy)/2 · sin2θ + τxy · cos2θ

σx and σy are normal stresses on the x and y faces in MPa; τxy is the shear stress on those faces in MPa; θ is the angle from the +x axis to the outward normal n, positive counterclockwise; σn is the normal stress on the inclined plane in MPa, positive tensile; τnt is the shear stress on that plane in MPa along t, positive along t. Some syllabi call τnt the shear stress on the inclined plane.

Force equilibrium on a wedge

Cut a small triangular wedge at the point. The inclined face has area A and normal n. The x face has area A cosθ and the y face has area A sinθ. Stresses are constant because the wedge is small.

The x-face force is σx A cosθ along x plus τxy A cosθ along y. The y-face force is τxy A sinθ along x plus σy A sinθ along y. The inclined face carries σn A along n and τnt A along t.

Resolve along n = (cosθ, sinθ). The x-face contribution is σx A cos²θ + τxy A sinθ cosθ. The y-face contribution is τxy A sinθ cosθ + σy A sin²θ. Divide by A: σn = σx cos²θ + σy sin²θ + 2τxy sinθ cosθ. Use cos²θ = (1 + cos2θ)/2, sin²θ = (1 − cos2θ)/2 and 2 sinθ cosθ = sin2θ. This gives the first equation above.

Resolve along t = (−sinθ, cosθ). The result is τnt = −(σx − σy) sinθ cosθ + τxy(cos²θ − sin²θ), which is the second equation above. The 2θ terms repeat every 180°. The opposite face has opposite traction, but because t also reverses, τnt has the same value; τnt changes sign when θ increases by 90°.

Uniaxial and biaxial fields

For a uniaxial field, set σx=σ, σy=0 and τxy=0. Then σn=σ cos²θ and τnt=−σ sinθ cosθ=−(σ/2) sin2θ. At θ=0: σn=σ, τnt=0. At θ=45°: σn=σ/2, τnt=−σ/2. At θ=90°: both are zero. The largest shear magnitude is σ/2 on the 45° planes. A mild steel tensile specimen shows slip lines near 45° for this reason.

For a biaxial field with τxy=0, σn=σx cos²θ + σy sin²θ and τnt=−(σx−σy) sinθ cosθ. If σx=σy=σ, then σn=σ on every plane and τnt=0. Equal tension in two directions creates no in-plane shear. A thin spherical pressure vessel is close to this state; see Unit 7, Thin and Thick Cylinders.

When τxy is not zero, the term τxy sin2θ adds to σn. A shear stress on the x face therefore changes the normal stress on a tilted plane. Students often remember the τxy effect in τnt but omit the τxy sin2θ term from σn. Keep the sign of τnt. A negative value means the shear acts opposite to t; its magnitude is positive. Do not change the formula to make the answer positive.

Worked example

Question. A plane stress state has σx=80 MPa, σy=40 MPa and τxy=30 MPa. Find the normal stress σn and shear stress τnt on a plane at θ=30° from the x face.

  1. Data: σx=80 MPa, σy=40 MPa, τxy=30 MPa, θ=30°, so 2θ=60°.
  2. Mean stress = (σx+σy)/2 = (80+40)/2 = 60 MPa. Half difference = (σx−σy)/2 = (80−40)/2 = 20 MPa.
  3. cos60°=0.5 and sin60°=0.8660.
  4. σn = 60 + 20(0.5) + 30(0.8660) = 60 + 10 + 25.98 = 95.98 MPa.
  5. τnt = −20(0.8660) + 30(0.5) = −17.32 + 15.00 = −2.32 MPa.
  6. The negative sign means the shear acts opposite to t. Its magnitude is 2.32 MPa.

Answer. The normal stress is 95.98 MPa (tension) and the shear stress is −2.32 MPa, a magnitude of 2.32 MPa acting opposite to t.

Exam traps

  • Putting θ, not 2θ, inside cos and sin. The double angle is 2θ.
  • Measuring θ from the y face. Then θ=0 would give σy, not σx, and the formulas no longer match.
  • Dropping τxy sin2θ from σn. A shear on the x face produces normal stress on a tilted plane.
  • Changing the sign of τnt because it came out negative. Negative only gives direction opposite to t.
  • Using degrees in a calculator set to radians. 2θ=60° is 1.047 rad, not 60.

2.3 Plane stress transformation

Plane stress transformation answers one question: if you know the stresses on a small element aligned with x and y, what are the stresses on the same element after you rotate the axes? The answer is a rotation of the stress tensor. Normal and shear components mix through cos 2θ and sin 2θ. The sum σx + σy does not change. Designers use this to find the worst orientation of a shaft, a pressure vessel or a bracket before choosing a material.

You need: 2.1 and 2.2, plus basic trigonometry (sin 2θ, cos 2θ).

The stress tensor and a rotation

Plane stress means σz = 0, τxz = 0 and τyz = 0. The element still carries three independent components: σx, σy and τxy. Here σx is the normal stress on the x-face, σy is the normal stress on the y-face, and τxy is the shear stress on the x-face in the y-direction. The tensor is symmetric because τxy = τyx.

Write these components as a 2 × 2 matrix:

S = [ [σx, τxy], [τxy, σy] ]

S is the plane stress tensor in MPa; σx and σy are normal stresses in MPa; τxy is shear stress in MPa.

Rotate the axes anticlockwise by angle θ to new axes x′ and y′. Use the rotation matrix R = [ [cos θ, sin θ], [−sin θ, cos θ] ]. The new tensor is σ′ = R S Rᵀ, where Rᵀ is the transpose of R. This is the matrix statement of plane stress transformation. It is the same physical element, described in a new coordinate system.

The three transformation equations

Multiplying the matrices gives three equations. They are the ones used in exams and in design checks.

σx′ = (σx + σy)/2 + (σx − σy)/2 cos 2θ + τxy sin 2θ

σx′ is the normal stress on the new x′-face in MPa; θ is the anticlockwise angle from x to x′ in degrees or radians; other symbols are as above.

σy′ = (σx + σy)/2 − (σx − σy)/2 cos 2θ − τxy sin 2θ

σy′ is the normal stress on the new y′-face in MPa; the same θ and original stresses apply.

τx′y′ = −(σx − σy)/2 sin 2θ + τxy cos 2θ

τx′y′ is the shear stress on the x′-face in the y′-direction in MPa; positive shear follows the same face-and-direction rule as τxy.

Check the equations at θ = 0: cos 0 = 1, sin 0 = 0, so σx′ = σx, σy′ = σy and τx′y′ = τxy. At θ = 90°, the normal stresses swap and the shear changes sign. The angle 2θ appears because a stress component repeats after a 180° rotation of the element, not after 360°. The force-equilibrium method of 2.2 gives the same result on one inclined plane.

The invariant and eigenvalues

Add the equations for σx′ and σy′. The cos 2θ and sin 2θ terms cancel:

σx + σy = σx′ + σy′

The sum of the two normal stresses is the first invariant of plane stress, in MPa. It is the same in every coordinate system.

This invariant is the trace of the matrix S. The determinant, σxσy − τxy², is a second invariant. The invariant is a check on any rotation calculation: if the sum changes, you made an arithmetic or sign error.

Principal stresses are the eigenvalues of S. They are the two values of λ that satisfy det(S − λI) = 0, where I is the 2 × 2 identity matrix. In equation form:

λ² − (σx + σy)λ + (σxσy − τxy²) = 0

λ is a principal stress in MPa; the two roots are σ1 and σ2. 2.4 solves for the roots and the principal angles.

Plane strain transformation uses the same rotation idea, but the shear term is γ/2, not γ. See 2.6.

Worked example

Question. A plane stress state has σx = 100 MPa, σy = 20 MPa and τxy = 40 MPa. Rotate the axes anticlockwise by θ = 45°. Find σx′, σy′ and τx′y′. Check the invariant.

  1. 2θ = 90°. cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1.
  2. Mean normal stress = (100 + 20)/2 = 60 MPa.
  3. Half difference = (100 − 20)/2 = 40 MPa.
  4. σx′ = 60 + 40 × 0 + 40 × 1 = 100 MPa.
  5. σy′ = 60 − 40 × 0 − 40 × 1 = 20 MPa.
  6. τx′y′ = −40 × 1 + 40 × 0 = −40 MPa.
  7. Invariant: σx + σy = 100 + 20 = 120 MPa. Also σx′ + σy′ = 100 + 20 = 120 MPa. The check passes.

Answer. σx′ = 100 MPa, σy′ = 20 MPa, τx′y′ = −40 MPa; the invariant sum is 120 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using θ inside sin 2θ and cos 2θ instead of 2θ. Compute 2θ first, then take sine and cosine.
  • Assuming a positive τxy gives a positive τx′y′. At θ = 45° in the worked example, τxy = +40 MPa becomes τx′y′ = −40 MPa.
  • Thinking the invariant fixes each normal stress. Only the sum σx + σy is fixed; σx′ and σy′ can swap or change.
  • Using the stress equations for strain. For strain, the shear term is γ/2; see 2.6.
  • Mixing degrees and radians. If θ = 45°, use 90° in the trig functions, not 90 rad.

2.4 Principal stresses, principal planes and maximum shear stress

Cut a stressed point on a plane tilted by angle θ and the normal stress changes while a shear appears. At two particular tilts the shear vanishes and the normal stress reaches its largest and smallest values for that point. Those are the principal planes, and the two normal stresses on them are the principal stresses. They tell a designer where a brittle part will crack and which plane a twisted shaft will fail on. This chapter finds them from σx, σy and τxy alone.

You need: 2.2 for the normal and shear stress on a tilted plane, 2.3 for the 2θ transformation, and calculus for locating a maximum.

Where the shear disappears

Take a small element in plane stress. It carries normal stresses σx and σy in MPa, tension positive, and a shear τxy in MPa on the x and y faces. Three numbers describe the whole state at that point.

σn = (σx+σy)/2 + ((σx−σy)/2)cos2θ + τxy sin2θ

σn is the normal stress on a plane whose normal is at angle θ from the x axis, in MPa; σx, σy are the given normal stresses in MPa; τxy is the given shear in MPa; θ is measured anticlockwise from the x face.

The first term is a constant, the average of the given normal stresses. The rest is a cosine wave, so σn swings between a high and a low twice in every full turn of the plane. The shear on the same plane is also a wave in 2θ.

Ask where σn is extreme. There dσn/dθ = 0, and the shear then comes out zero as well. Those planes are the principal planes; the normal stresses on them are the principal stresses σ1 and σ2. Read them as two pure pulls or pushes at right angles, with no shear — the simplest description of the same stress state.

The formulas and the 90° rule

Differentiate σn with respect to θ and set the result to zero.

−(σx−σy) sin2θ + 2τxy cos2θ = 0  →  tan2θp = 2τxy/(σx−σy)

θp is the angle from the x face to a principal plane, in degrees or radians; the other symbols are as before.

This is the same condition as τ = 0, so principal planes and shear-free planes are one and the same. The tangent repeats every 180° in its argument, so 2θp has two solutions 180° apart. In θ that gives two principal planes 90° apart, for every stress state.

Put θp back into σn and simplify. Draw a right triangle with opposite side 2τxy and adjacent side σx − σy; sin2θp and cos2θp drop out and σn collapses to a centre plus or minus a radius.

C = (σx+σy)/2,  R = √(((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²),  σ1 = C + R,  σ2 = C − R

C is the centre, the average normal stress, in MPa; R is the radius in MPa; σ1 is the algebraically larger principal stress and σ2 the smaller, both in MPa.

σ1 is the more tensile of the two, not the larger in size. Two checks catch most slips: σ1 + σ2 = σx + σy = 2C must hold, and the plane at θp carries σ1 while the plane at θp + 90° carries σ2.

Maximum shear and the third dimension

Shear on a tilted plane is τ = −((σx−σy)/2)sin2θ + τxy cos2θ. Its extreme value is the radius itself.

τmax = R = √(((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²)  at  tan2θs = −(σx−σy)/(2τxy)

τmax is the largest in-plane shear stress in MPa; θs is the angle of the plane carrying it, measured from the x face.

The two answers sit 45° apart: θs = θp ± 45°. On the maximum-shear plane the normal stress is C, not zero. Pure torsion is the sharpest case: σ1 = +τ, σ2 = −τ, and the worst shear is τ on planes at 45° to the shaft axis.

In three dimensions a point has three principal stresses, ordered σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3, along three mutually perpendicular directions. In plane stress, on a free surface, one of the three is zero. Call that one the third stress; its rank comes from the ordering. If both in-plane stresses are tensile it ranks as σ3; if both are compressive it ranks as σ1. Each pair has its own maximum shear, and the largest of the three is the absolute maximum shear.

τabs = max(|σ1−σ2|, |σ2−σ3|, |σ1−σ3|) / 2

σ1, σ2, σ3 are the three principal stresses in MPa, ordered algebraically; τabs is the absolute maximum shear stress at the point, in MPa.

If σ1 and σ2 have opposite signs, the zero stress lies between them and τabs = R. If they have the same sign, τabs = max(|σ1|,|σ2|)/2, which is larger than R, so quoting R is wrong. Solving the full 3D cubic is asked in only a few universities; 2.5 gets the plane case faster by drawing.

Worked example

Question. A point in plane stress has σx = 80 MPa, σy = −40 MPa and τxy = 50 MPa. Find the principal stresses, the principal plane angles, and the maximum in-plane shear stress. State whether that shear is also the absolute maximum shear.

  1. Centre: C = (σx + σy)/2 = (80 + (−40))/2 = 20 MPa.
  2. Half-difference: (σx − σy)/2 = (80 − (−40))/2 = 60 MPa.
  3. Radius: R = √(60² + 50²) = √(3600 + 2500) = √6100 = 78.10 MPa.
  4. σ1 = C + R = 20 + 78.10 = 98.10 MPa.
  5. σ2 = C − R = 20 − 78.10 = −58.10 MPa.
  6. Check: σ1 + σ2 = 98.10 − 58.10 = 40 MPa, and σx + σy = 80 − 40 = 40 MPa; they match.
  7. Principal angle: tan2θp = 2τxy/(σx − σy) = 2(50)/(80 + 40) = 100/120 = 0.8333, so 2θp = 39.81° and θp = 19.90°.
  8. Second principal plane: θp + 90° = 109.90°, carrying σ2.
  9. Maximum in-plane shear: τmax = R = 78.10 MPa, on a plane at θs = 19.90° − 45° = −25.10° from the x face.
  10. Absolute maximum shear: the three principal stresses in order are 98.10, 0 and −58.10 MPa. The zero stress lies between the other two, so τabs = (98.10 − (−58.10))/2 = 78.10 MPa, equal to R.

Answer. σ1 = 98.10 MPa (tension), σ2 = −58.10 MPa (compression), θp = 19.90° and 109.90°, and τmax = 78.10 MPa in plane, which also equals the absolute maximum shear because the zero principal stress lies between the other two.

Exam traps

  • Quoting τmax = R when σ1 and σ2 have the same sign. The zero stress then sits outside the pair and τabs = max(|σ1|,|σ2|)/2, which is bigger than R.
  • Giving 2θp instead of θp, or giving only one principal plane. The two planes are always 90° apart, and only substituting back tells you which carries σ1.
  • Sorting by size instead of by sign. With σx = −40 MPa and σy = −100 MPa both compressive, σ1 = −40 MPa is the algebraically larger even though it looks 'smaller'.
  • Using a wrong sign for τxy. Shear is positive on the +x face in the +y direction and positive on the +y face in the +x direction; a slip flips θp and nothing else.
  • Believing the principal plane has zero normal stress. Only the shear is zero; the normal stress there is C + R or C − R, usually not small.

2.5 Mohr's circle of stress

Mohr's circle is a graph of every (σ, τ) pair that a plane through a stressed point can carry. For plane stress with normal stresses σx and σy and shear τxy, all such pairs lie on one circle. The circle turns the transformation equations into a picture: its centre gives the average normal stress, its radius gives the maximum shear, and its left and right edges give the principal stresses. Designers use it to read combined bending and torsion in shafts, pressure in vessels and stresses in beams without solving long trigonometric equations.

You need stresses on inclined planes, plane stress transformation, principal stresses and maximum shear, and the coordinate geometry of a circle.

Drawing the circle

Start with a plane stress state. The symbol σx is the normal stress on the x face, σy is the normal stress on the y face, and τxy is the shear stress on the x face. Take tensile normal stress as positive and plot positive τ upward. τxy is positive when it acts on the +x face in the +y direction. The angle θ is measured counter-clockwise from the x face. The centre C of Mohr's circle lies on the σ axis at the average normal stress.

C = ((σx + σy)/2, 0),   R = √[((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²]

C is the circle centre in MPa; R is the radius in MPa; σx and σy are the normal stresses on the x and y faces in MPa; τxy is the shear stress on the x face in MPa.

Plot point A at (σx, τxy) and point B at (σy, −τxy). The minus sign on B is the step most often missed. The two points are opposite ends of a diameter. A circle drawn on AB has centre C and radius R, so it contains every (σ, τ) pair for every plane through the point.

  1. Compute C and R from σx, σy and τxy.
  2. Plot A(σx, τxy) and B(σy, −τxy).
  3. Draw the circle centred at C through A and B.
  4. Mark the right edge σ1 = C + R, the left edge σ2 = C − R, and the top and bottom τmax = ±R.
  5. To read the plane at angle θ measured counter-clockwise from the x face, rotate CA by 2θ clockwise on the plot and read the coordinates.

Some books plot positive shear downward. The circle then mirrors about the σ axis; magnitudes such as σ1, σ2 and τmax do not change, but the sense of the turn you make inside the circle is reversed.

Reading the answers

The circle gives principal stresses directly. The rightmost point has τ = 0, so it is the major principal stress σ1 = C + R. The leftmost point is σ2 = C − R. The top and bottom points have the largest in-plane shear, τmax = R. The planes carrying τmax are 45° from the principal planes because the circle angle between them is 90°.

The angle needs care. The vector from C to A is ((σx − σy)/2, τxy). Its angle α from the +σ direction satisfies tan α = τxy / ((σx − σy)/2). The turn from CA to the right edge is 2θp on the circle; the physical rotation of the plane is θp = α/2 in the opposite sense. If A is above the axis and right of C, the turn on the plot to σ1 is clockwise, so the physical rotation is counter-clockwise. The other principal plane is 90° away.

σ1 = C + R,   σ2 = C − R,   τmax = R,   θp = ½ tan⁻¹[τxy / ((σx − σy)/2)]

θp is the angle from the x face to the major principal plane; the inverse tangent gives a magnitude, and the turn from CA to the right edge is 2θp on the circle while the physical rotation is θp in the opposite sense.

All points on the circle are possible planes. As the plane rotates by θ, the point moves by 2θ on the circle. The centre and radius stay fixed, which is why the average normal stress and the maximum shear do not change when the axes rotate.

Using it in design

A shaft under bending and torsion is a common use of this circle. Bending gives σx, torsion gives τxy, and σy is zero. The circle then gives the principal stresses without a long substitution. A thin pressure vessel has hoop and longitudinal normal stresses; the circle shows how a small shear changes the principal values. A beam with an inclined crack or a weld has a local stress state that can be checked the same way.

Use the circle as a check on calculation. The distance from C to any plotted point must equal R. The sum σ1 + σ2 must equal σx + σy. The diameter AB must pass through C. If one of these fails, the plotted points or the sign of τxy is wrong.

After reading σ1 and σ2, compare them with yield strength using Tresca or von Mises. The circle supplies the numbers; the failure theory supplies the acceptance rule.

Worked example

Question. A plane stress element has σx = 80 MPa, σy = −40 MPa and τxy = 50 MPa. Draw Mohr's circle and determine σ1, σ2, τmax, and the angle from the x face to the major principal plane.

  1. Identify the data: σx = 80 MPa, σy = −40 MPa, τxy = 50 MPa. Tensile normal stress is positive.

  4. Radius: R = √(60² + 50²) = √(3600 + 2500) = √6100 = 78.10 MPa.
  5. Principal stresses: σ1 = C + R = 20 + 78.10 = 98.10 MPa; σ2 = C − R = 20 − 78.10 = −58.10 MPa.
  6. Maximum in-plane shear: τmax = R = 78.10 MPa. It acts on planes at 45° to the principal planes.
  7. Plot A(80, 50) and B(−40, −50). The vector CA = (60, 50). Its angle from the +σ axis is α = tan⁻¹(50/60) = 39.81°.

Exam traps

  • Plotting B as (σy, +τxy). The diameter must join (σx, τxy) to (σy, −τxy); the same sign puts the centre off the σ axis.
  • Using AB as the radius. AB is the diameter; R is half of AB.
  • Using θp in place of 2θp on the circle. The circle turns through twice the physical angle.
  • Mixing shear sign conventions. If your book plots positive shear downward, the circle mirrors about the σ axis; magnitudes stay the same and the physical direction of θp is unchanged, but the sense of the turn you make inside the circle is reversed.
  • Forgetting that τmax = R is the in-plane maximum. A three-dimensional stress state can have a larger absolute shear on an out-of-plane plane.

2.6 Principal strains and strain transformation

Strain at a point is a tensor, just like stress. Rotate the axes and the normal strains and shear strain change. The equations look like stress transformation, with one difference: the shear term is γxy/2, not γxy. This matters in strain-gauge work, where three readings give the strain state, and in finite-element output, where principal strains show where a part stretches most. The same formulas locate the principal strains and the maximum shear strain.

You need: plane stress transformation, principal stresses, and Hooke's law from Unit 1, Simple Stresses, Strains and Elastic Constants.

Strain tensor and engineering shear strain

At a point, strain has three normal components εx, εy, εz and three shear components. The strain tensor stores shear as εxy = γxy/2. Here γxy is the engineering shear strain: the change in angle between two lines originally along x and y, in radians. The factor 1/2 makes the tensor symmetric, εxy = εyx, so it rotates like the stress tensor.

εx′ = (εx+εy)/2 + ((εx−εy)/2)cos2θ + (γxy/2)sin2θ

εx, εy are normal strains along x and y; γxy is engineering shear strain; θ is the angle from x to the new normal; εx′ is the normal strain on the rotated plane.

γx′y′/2 = −((εx−εy)/2)sin2θ + (γxy/2)cos2θ

γx′y′ is the engineering shear strain on the rotated axes; the other symbols are defined above. All strains are dimensionless, often written in microstrain, where 1 με = 10⁻⁶.

The figure plots these expressions for εx = 400×10⁻⁶, εy = −200×10⁻⁶ and γxy = 300×10⁻⁶. The normal strain peaks at θ = 13.28°, where the shear curve crosses zero.

Principal strains and maximum shear strain

Principal strains are the largest and smallest normal strains at the point. On those planes the shear strain is zero. Set the shear expression to zero, or complete the square in the normal expression.

ε1,2 = (εx+εy)/2 ± √(((εx−εy)/2)² + (γxy/2)²)

ε1 is the algebraically largest principal strain; ε2 is the smallest; the first term is the centre; the square root is the radius R.

tan 2θp = γxy/(εx−εy)

θp is the angle from x to a principal plane normal. Use atan2(γxy, εx−εy) to keep the correct quadrant.

The maximum in-plane engineering shear strain is twice the radius: γmax = ε1 − ε2 = 2R. It acts on planes at θp ± 45°. The normal strain there is the average (εx+εy)/2.

  1. Compute the centre C = (εx+εy)/2.
  2. Compute the radius R = √(((εx−εy)/2)² + (γxy/2)²).
  3. Get principal strains ε1 = C + R and ε2 = C − R.
  4. Find θp from tan 2θp = γxy/(εx−εy), then check which root gives ε1.
  5. Get γmax = ε1 − ε2 on planes at θp ± 45°.

Plane strain and Hooke's law

Plane strain means εz = 0, not σz = 0. A long dam or a thick plate restrained in the z direction is close to plane strain. Plane stress, common in thin sheets, has σz = 0 but εz = −ν(σx+σy)/E. Do not mix the two.

ε1 = [σ1 − ν(σ2+σ3)]/E, ε2 = [σ2 − ν(σ3+σ1)]/E, ε3 = [σ3 − ν(σ1+σ2)]/E

E is Young's modulus; ν is Poisson's ratio; σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses; ε1, ε2, ε3 are the corresponding principal strains.

For plane strain, ε3 = 0, so σ3 = ν(σ1+σ2). For plane stress, σ3 = 0 and ε3 = −ν(σ1+σ2)/E. Strain gauges measure normal strain along their grid; a rosette reduces three readings to εx, εy and engineering γxy. See strain gauges and strain rosettes.

Worked example

Question. A plane strain state has εx = 400×10⁻⁶, εy = −200×10⁻⁶ and γxy = 300×10⁻⁶. Find the principal strains, the maximum engineering shear strain, and the principal strain direction. Take the direction of ε1 from the x-axis.

  1. Write the data in microstrain: εx = 400 με, εy = −200 με, γxy = 300 με.
  2. Average strain = (400 + (−200))/2 = 100 με.
  3. Half difference = (400 − (−200))/2 = 300 με. Half shear = 300/2 = 150 με.
  4. Radius R = √(300² + 150²) = √(90000 + 22500) = √112500 = 335.41 με.
  5. Principal strains: ε1 = 100 + 335.41 = 435.41 με; ε2 = 100 − 335.41 = −235.41 με.
  6. Maximum engineering shear strain: γmax = ε1 − ε2 = 435.41 − (−235.41) = 670.82 με.
  7. Direction: tan 2θp = γxy/(εx−εy) = 300/600 = 0.5, so 2θp = 26.565° and θp = 13.28°. At this angle εn is maximum, so it is the direction of ε1.

Exam traps

  • Using γxy in place of γxy/2 in the radius. That doubles the shear contribution and gives a wrong radius.
  • Mixing microstrain and strain. 400 με is 400×10⁻⁶, not 400.
  • Treating plane strain as plane stress. Plane strain has εz = 0; plane stress has σz = 0.
  • Taking θp from tan 2θp without checking which root gives ε1. The two roots differ by 90°.
  • Forgetting that principal shear planes carry the average normal strain, not zero normal strain.

2.7 Strain gauges and strain rosettes

A strain gauge is a small resistor glued to a surface. When the surface stretches, the gauge stretches and its resistance changes. The change is tiny, so a bridge circuit converts it into a voltage. One gauge measures strain along one line only. Three gauges fixed at known angles form a strain rosette, and three readings give the complete plane strain state: εx, εy and γxy. From these, principal strains and their directions follow. Engineers use rosettes on pressure vessels, bridge girders, machine frames and aircraft panels where a single strain reading cannot describe the surface.

You need 2.6 for strain transformation and basic electrical resistance (R = ρL/A).

The electrical resistance strain gauge

A bonded foil gauge is a thin zigzag metal grid on a plastic backing. It is glued so its long grid direction lies along the strain to be measured. The resistance of a conductor is R = ρL/A, where R is resistance in ohms (Ω), ρ is resistivity in Ω·m, L is conductor length in metres and A is cross-sectional area in square metres. Axial stretch increases L and reduces A, so R rises. The gauge factor packs these changes into one number.

GF = (ΔR/R)/ε

GF is the gauge factor (dimensionless); ΔR is the change in resistance in Ω; R is the initial resistance in Ω; ε = ΔL/L is the normal strain along the gauge, dimensionless. For metal foil, GF is about 2.

A 500×10⁻⁶ strain on a 120 Ω gauge changes R by only 0.12 Ω, so a Wheatstone bridge is used. A quarter bridge has one active gauge and three fixed resistors. Its output voltage is proportional to ΔR/R and hence to strain. Temperature also changes resistance, so a dummy gauge is bonded to an unstrained piece of the same material and wired in the adjacent bridge arm. Both gauges see the same temperature, and the bridge cancels the common change. The dummy gauge does not cancel real strain in the active gauge; it cancels apparent thermal strain.

Rectangular and delta rosettes

A single gauge measures εθ, the normal strain along its own axis. A rosette holds three gauges at known angles θ. For plane strain, the reading at angle θ from the x-axis is given by:

ε<sub>θ</sub> = ε<sub>x</sub> cos²θ + ε<sub>y</sub> sin²θ + γ<sub>xy</sub> sinθ cosθ

εθ is the strain along the gauge axis; εx, εy are normal strains along x and y; γxy is the engineering shear strain; θ is measured from the x-axis to the gauge axis, positive anticlockwise.

In a rectangular rosette the gauges sit at 0°, 45° and 90°. Put θ = 0°, 45°, 90° into the equation. The 0° and 90° readings give εx and εy directly; the 45° reading then gives the shear strain:

ε<sub>x</sub> = ε<sub>0</sub>,  ε<sub>y</sub> = ε<sub>90</sub>,  γ<sub>xy</sub> = 2ε<sub>45</sub> − ε<sub>x</sub> − ε<sub>y</sub>

ε0, ε45, ε90 are the three gauge readings in microstrain or dimensionless strain. The factor 2 is part of the reduction.

In a delta rosette the gauges are at 0°, 60° and 120°. With εx = ε0, the reduction is:

γ<sub>xy</sub> = 2(ε<sub>60</sub> − ε<sub>120</sub>)/√3,  ε<sub>y</sub> = [2(ε<sub>60</sub> + ε<sub>120</sub>) − ε<sub>x</sub>]/3

ε60 and ε120 are the readings at 60° and 120°. The factor 2 comes from sin60° cos60° = √3/4, which makes ε60 − ε120 = (√3/2)γxy. The same sign convention applies: positive θ is anticlockwise from the x-axis.

Once εx, εy and γxy are known, principal strains follow from:

ε<sub>1,2</sub> = (ε<sub>x</sub> + ε<sub>y</sub>)/2 ± (1/2)√[(ε<sub>x</sub> − ε<sub>y</sub>)² + γ<sub>xy</sub>²]

ε1 is the larger principal strain and ε2 is the smaller; the square-root term is the diameter of the strain circle.

tan 2θ<sub>p</sub> = γ<sub>xy</sub>/(ε<sub>x</sub> − ε<sub>y</sub>)

θp is the angle from the x-axis to a principal strain direction. For an isotropic linear elastic material, principal strain directions equal principal stress directions.

The equation for θp gives two directions 90° apart. Both satisfy tan 2θp. Substitute each candidate back into the εθ equation; the one that returns ε1 is the principal direction of ε1, and the other is the direction of ε2. Report the one the question asks for.

Reducing a rosette reading

  1. Record the gauge angles from the rosette diagram. Mark which gauge is 0°, which is 45° or 60°, and so on.
  2. Convert every reading to absolute strain. A reading of 500 με is 500×10⁻⁶.
  3. Use the rectangular or delta equations to find εx, εy and γxy. Keep the sign of γxy.
  4. Compute the mean strain (εx + εy)/2 and the radius ½√[(εx − εy)² + γxy²].
  5. Add and subtract the radius to get ε1 and ε2. Check that εx + εy = ε1 + ε2.
  6. Find θp from tan 2θp = γxy/(εx − εy). Convert the angle to degrees if the question asks for degrees.

A gauge length averages strain over its grid. Use a short gauge where the strain changes quickly, such as near a hole or a weld toe. Transverse sensitivity makes a foil gauge respond slightly to strain across its grid; manufacturers supply a correction. Lead resistance and poor bonding add errors. For an isotropic material, principal stresses can be obtained from the principal strains using Hooke's law from Unit 1.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular rosette on a steel plate reads ε0 = 500×10⁻⁶, ε45 = 350×10⁻⁶ and ε90 = 100×10⁻⁶. Find εx, εy, γxy, the principal strains and the principal direction relative to the 0° gauge.

  1. The 0° gauge lies along the x-axis, so εx = ε0 = 500×10⁻⁶.
  2. The 90° gauge lies along the y-axis, so εy = ε90 = 100×10⁻⁶.
  3. At 45°, ε45 = (εx + εy)/2 + γxy/2. Rearrange: γxy = 2ε45 − εx − εy = 2(350×10⁻⁶) − 500×10⁻⁶ − 100×10⁻⁶ = 100×10⁻⁶.
  4. Mean strain = (500×10⁻⁶ + 100×10⁻⁶)/2 = 300×10⁻⁶. Difference = 400×10⁻⁶. Radius = ½√[(400×10⁻⁶)² + (100×10⁻⁶)²] = 206.2×10⁻⁶.
  5. ε1 = 300×10⁻⁶ + 206.2×10⁻⁶ = 506.2×10⁻⁶. ε2 = 300×10⁻⁶ − 206.2×10⁻⁶ = 93.8×10⁻⁶.
  6. tan 2θp = γxy/(εx − εy) = 100×10⁻⁶ / 400×10⁻⁶ = 0.25. So 2θp = 14.04° and θp = 7.02° ≈ 7.0° from the 0° gauge toward the 90° gauge.

Answer. εx = 500×10⁻⁶, εy = 100×10⁻⁶, γxy = 100×10⁻⁶, ε1 = 506.2×10⁻⁶, ε2 = 93.8×10⁻⁶, and θp = 7.0°.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the factor 2 in the rectangular rosette: γxy = 2ε45 − εx − εy, not ε45 − εx − εy.
  • Using microstrain as a plain number in GF = (ΔR/R)/ε. A strain of 500 με must be entered as 500×10⁻⁶.
  • Treating ε45 as a principal strain. It is only the normal strain along the 45° gauge axis.
  • Mixing degrees and radians in the general strain equation or in tan 2θp. Use one unit consistently and convert the final angle if needed.
  • On a strain Mohr circle, plotting γxy instead of γxy/2. The circle uses half the engineering shear strain.

2.8 Theories of failure: Rankine, Tresca, von Mises

A failure theory turns a complex stress state into one number, called an equivalent stress, and compares it with the yield strength of the material. Without it, a shaft under bending and torsion would need a separate test for every combination of loads. The theory gives a factor of safety for design. Brittle parts such as cast iron brackets use Rankine; ductile parts such as mild steel shafts use Tresca or von Mises. The calculation starts from the principal stresses.

You need: principal stresses from 2.4, stress state labels from 2.1, and factor of safety from Unit 1.

Yield strength, factor of safety and equivalent stress

Yield strength σY is the stress at which a standard uniaxial tensile specimen begins to deform permanently. It carries units of MPa. For a design, the working stress must stay below σY by a margin. The factor of safety FS is the ratio FS = σY / σworking. A value FS = 2 means the part carries twice the load it is expected to see before yielding. For brittle materials the limiting stress is the ultimate strength: σut in tension and σuc in compression. In the formulas below, use that limiting strength in place of σY when the material is brittle.

At a point inside a machine part, the stress state is not uniaxial. It has three principal stresses, ordered σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3, with tension positive. A failure theory defines an equivalent stress σeq from these three numbers. Yielding is predicted when σeq = σY. For design, the condition is σeq ≤ σY / FS.

σeq ≤ σY / FS

σeq is the equivalent stress from the chosen theory (MPa); σY is yield strength (MPa); FS is factor of safety (dimensionless).

The theory is a model, not a new material property. Each theory is a statement about which stress measure causes failure: a large normal stress, a large shear stress, or a large change of shape.

Rankine and Tresca

Rankine theory, also called the maximum principal stress theory, says failure occurs when the largest principal stress reaches the limiting strength. For brittle materials that limiting strength is the ultimate strength: σut when the largest principal stress is tensile, σuc when it is compressive. For ductile materials in this theory the limiting strength is σY. For a general stress state, use the largest absolute value. The condition is max(|σ1|, |σ2|, |σ3|) = σL / FS, where σL is σut, σuc or σY as above. Use Rankine for brittle materials: cast iron, ceramics, concrete. A cast iron bracket cracks on the plane of maximum tension; it does not yield first. Rankine ignores shear, so it is unsafe for ductile metals. Under pure shear, σ1 = τ, σ3 = −τ, σ2 = 0. Rankine predicts failure at τ = σY, but mild steel yields near 0.5σY.

Tresca theory, also called the maximum shear stress theory, says failure occurs when the maximum shear stress reaches the shear yield strength, σY/2. The maximum shear is τmax = (σ1 − σ3) / 2. The yield condition is σ1 − σ3 = σY. The factor of safety is FS = σY / (σ1 − σ3). Use Tresca for ductile materials. It is conservative: it predicts yield earlier than tests in most combined stress states. It is simple for design checks.

In plane stress one principal stress is zero. Sort it into the set before taking σ1 − σ3. If both in-plane principal stresses are positive, then σ3 = 0, and σ1 − σ3 = σ1, not σ1 − σ2.

von Mises and the yield surfaces

The von Mises equivalent stress is σvm = √(0.5[(σ1 − σ2)2 + (σ2 − σ3)2 + (σ3 − σ1)2]). Yielding is predicted when σvm = σY, and FS = σY / σvm. This is the maximum distortion energy theory. Hydrostatic stress σ1 = σ2 = σ3 = p gives σvm = 0, so deep sea pressure alone does not yield a ductile metal. Only the change of shape, called distortion, stores the energy that causes yield.

In plane stress with σ3 = 0, the formula becomes σvm = √(σ12 − σ1σ2 + σ22). In terms of the given components, σvm = √(σx2 − σxσy + σy2 + 3τxy2).

Plot σ1 against σ2 for plane stress with σ3 = 0. Tresca gives a hexagon: six straight lines where the largest stress difference equals σY. von Mises gives an ellipse through the hexagon's corners. The ellipse is outside the hexagon, so von Mises allows a slightly higher load before yield. Under pure shear, Tresca predicts τ = 0.5σY; von Mises predicts τ = σY/√3 ≈ 0.577σY. Tests on ductile steels lie closer to von Mises. Use Rankine for brittle, Tresca for a safe ductile design, and von Mises for an accurate ductile check.

Worked example

Question. A plane stress point has σx = 100 MPa, σy = −50 MPa and τxy = 40 MPa. Yield strength is σY = 250 MPa. Find the principal stresses and the factor of safety by Rankine, Tresca and von Mises.

  1. Average normal stress: σavg = (σx + σy) / 2 = (100 − 50) / 2 = 25 MPa.
  2. Radius of the principal stress circle: R = √[((σx − σy) / 2)2 + τxy2] = √[(150 / 2)2 + 402] = √(752 + 402) = √(5625 + 1600) = √7225 = 85 MPa.
  3. In-plane principal stresses: σa = σavg + R = 25 + 85 = 110 MPa and σb = σavg − R = 25 − 85 = −60 MPa.
  4. Add the zero out-of-plane stress and sort: σ1 = 110 MPa, σ2 = 0 MPa, σ3 = −60 MPa.
  5. Rankine: largest absolute principal stress = 110 MPa. FS = σY / 110 = 250 / 110 = 2.27.
  6. Tresca: σ1 − σ3 = 110 − (−60) = 170 MPa. FS = σY / 170 = 250 / 170 = 1.47.
  7. von Mises: σvm = √(0.5[(110 − 0)2 + (0 − (−60))2 + (−60 − 110)2]) = √(0.5[12100 + 3600 + 28900]) = √22300 = 149.33 MPa. FS = 250 / 149.33 = 1.67.

Answer. The principal stresses are 110, 0 and −60 MPa. The factors of safety are 2.27 by Rankine, 1.47 by Tresca and 1.67 by von Mises; Tresca is the most conservative.

Exam traps

  • Using σx and σy in Tresca or von Mises without care. Tresca needs the principal stresses, with σ3 = 0 included in plane stress, because it uses σ1 − σ3. von Mises may be found from the components only if τxy is kept: σvm = √(σx2 − σxσy + σy2 + 3τxy2). Dropping τxy or using σx − σy where σ1 − σ3 is required gives a wrong answer.
  • Forgetting to sort the principal stresses. Tresca always uses σ1 − σ3, the largest minus the smallest.
  • Using Rankine for ductile steel or Tresca for cast iron. Rankine is for brittle materials; Tresca and von Mises are for ductile materials.
  • Inverting the factor of safety. FS = σY / σeq, not σeq / σY.
  • Dropping the negative sign on a compressive principal stress. It changes σ1 − σ3 and the squared differences in von Mises.

2.9 Equilibrium equations and strain-displacement relations

A small cube of material does not fly apart. The stresses on its six faces must balance, which forces the stress gradients to satisfy three equilibrium equations. Separately, every strain comes from how the displacement changes from point to point. These two sets are the starting point for solving a loaded body: equilibrium links stresses, and strain-displacement links strains to displacements. Together with Hooke's law they define boundary value problems in plane stress, plane strain and three dimensions. Design software uses them in finite element analysis; hand calculations use simplified forms.

You need: 2.3 and 2.6; partial derivatives.

Equilibrium of a small block

Cut a tiny rectangular block of sides Δx, Δy, Δz from a loaded body. The face at x carries normal stress σx. The opposite face at x + Δx carries σx + (∂σx/∂x)Δx. The difference is a gradient times length. Summing forces in x and dividing by volume ΔxΔyΔz leaves the gradient terms. No body force first.

∂σx/∂x + ∂τxy/∂y + ∂τxz/∂z = 0

σx is normal stress on the x face; τxy is shear stress on the x face in the y direction; τxz is shear stress on the x face in the z direction; all stresses in N/mm² and lengths in mm.

∂τxy/∂x + ∂σy/∂y + ∂τyz/∂z = 0; ∂τxz/∂x + ∂τyz/∂y + ∂σz/∂z = 0

These are the cyclic y and z forms. σy and σz are normal stresses; τyz is shear on the y face in the z direction; τxz is shear on the x face in the z direction.

If body force per unit volume has components X, Y, Z (N/mm³), add X to the x equation, Y to y, Z to z. Gravity on a long bar is the common case. The equations say a stress field cannot be chosen freely; it must vary in a balanced way. A uniform stress field with no body force automatically satisfies them, so the interesting cases have gradients. See 2.1 for the stress components on a face.

Strain from displacement

Let a point move by u in x, v in y, w in z, all in mm. For small strains, normal strain is the change in length per original length. A line element dx stretches by (∂u/∂x)dx, so εx = ∂u/∂x. Similarly εy = ∂v/∂y and εz = ∂w/∂z. These derivatives are dimensionless when u and x use the same unit.

εx = ∂u/∂x, εy = ∂v/∂y, εz = ∂w/∂z

u, v, w are displacements in x, y, z in mm; εx, εy, εz are normal strains, dimensionless.

Shear strain measures angle change. Two originally perpendicular lines along x and y rotate toward each other. The x line tilts by ∂v/∂x; the y line tilts by ∂u/∂y. The total decrease in angle is γxy = ∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x. Both terms are needed. γxy is in radians. The cyclic forms are γyz = ∂v/∂z + ∂w/∂y and γzx = ∂w/∂x + ∂u/∂z.

γxy = ∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x

u is displacement in x; v is displacement in y; γxy is engineering shear strain in radians.

The figure follows the worked example: differentiate the field, then read the strains at the point. Once εx, εy and γxy are known, principal strains follow from 2.6. Strain gauges and rosettes use this same idea in reverse, as shown in 2.7.

Plane stress, plane strain and the full problem

Plane stress keeps σz = τxz = τyz = 0 but not εz. Thin plates loaded in their plane are the usual case. Plane strain keeps εz = γxz = γyz = 0 but not σz. A long dam or tunnel section away from the ends is the usual case. Both are two-dimensional boundary value problems: equilibrium gives stress gradients, strain-displacement gives strains, and Hooke's law connects stress to strain. Boundary conditions fix the displacements or tractions on the edges.

Compatibility is the extra set of conditions that makes a strain field come from a single-valued displacement field. Only a few universities derive it; most syllabi stop at the equilibrium equations and strain-displacement relations. The full three-dimensional problem has three equilibrium equations, six strain-displacement relations and six stress-strain relations. Solving them by hand is rare; finite element software assembles and solves them numerically. Your exam usually asks you to write the equations, substitute a given displacement field, or check whether a stress field satisfies equilibrium.

Worked example

Question. A displacement field in a thin plate is u = (3x + 1.5y) × 10⁻³ mm and v = (1.5x + 2y) × 10⁻³ mm, with x and y in mm. Find εx, εy and γxy at the point (2,1) mm.

  1. Write the displacement components: u = (3x + 1.5y) × 10⁻³ mm, v = (1.5x + 2y) × 10⁻³ mm.
  2. Differentiate u with respect to x: ∂u/∂x = 3 × 10⁻³. Hence εx = 3 × 10⁻³ = 0.003.
  3. Differentiate v with respect to y: ∂v/∂y = 2 × 10⁻³. Hence εy = 2 × 10⁻³ = 0.002.
  4. Differentiate u with respect to y: ∂u/∂y = 1.5 × 10⁻³. Differentiate v with respect to x: ∂v/∂x = 1.5 × 10⁻³.
  5. Add the two shear terms: γxy = ∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x = 1.5 × 10⁻³ + 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 3 × 10⁻³ = 0.003 rad.
  6. At (2,1) mm the derivatives are constant, so the same values apply at that point. The normal strains are dimensionless; γxy is 0.003 rad.

Answer. εx = 0.003, εy = 0.002, and γxy = 0.003 rad, all at (2,1) mm.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the second term in γxy: γxy is ∂u/∂y plus ∂v/∂x, not either one alone.
  • Mixing units of displacement and length. If u is in mm and x is in m, convert before differentiating, or the strain is wrong by 1000.
  • Treating X, Y, Z as stresses. They are body force per unit volume, in N/mm³, while stress is N/mm².
  • Assuming plane stress means εz = 0. Plane stress means σz = 0; εz is generally nonzero through Poisson's ratio.
  • Writing equilibrium with σx itself instead of ∂σx/∂x. A uniform stress field balances without body force; gradients create the net force.

Takeaways

  1. A stress state at a point is fully fixed by the normal and shear components on three mutually perpendicular faces, and its simplest labels are uniaxial, biaxial and triaxial.
  2. Stresses on a plane tilted by θ vary with 2θ, so the complete picture repeats every 180° of rotation.
  3. When axes rotate, the normal stresses change but their sum does not; this invariant equals σ1 + σ2.
  4. Principal planes carry zero shear and carry the largest and smallest normal stresses at the point.
  5. The maximum in-plane shear stress equals half the difference of the principal stresses and sits 45° from the principal planes.
  6. Mohr's circle converts the transformation equations into one drawing whose centre gives the mean stress and whose radius gives the maximum shear.
  7. Strains transform exactly like stresses, except that the shear strain appears as γ/2 in every equation.
  8. Three strain gauges at known angles supply the three unknowns εx, εy and γxy, enough for principal strains and then principal stresses through Hooke's law.
  9. A failure theory reduces a complex stress state to one number that is compared with the yield strength to give a factor of safety.
  10. Tresca and von Mises suit ductile metals, Rankine suits brittle materials; the choice of theory can change the safe size of a part.
  11. Equilibrium equations and strain-displacement relations together give the field description of a deforming body, with compatibility closing the set.

Apply At a point in a machine part, σx = 120 N/mm², σy = 40 N/mm² and τxy = 60 N/mm². Find the principal stresses, the principal planes and the maximum in-plane shear stress. 12 marks

Use σ1,2 = (σx+σy)/2 ± √(((σx−σy)/2)² + τxy²); Mean = 80 N/mm², radius = 72.1 N/mm²; σ1 = 152.1 N/mm², σ2 = 7.9 N/mm²; tan2θp = 2τxy/(σx−σy) = 1.5, so 2θp = 56.3°; θp = 28.15° and 118.15°; τmax = 72.1 N/mm² at 45° to the principal planes.

Apply Solve the previous stress state by drawing Mohr's circle to scale: locate the centre and radius, and read off the stresses on a plane at 30° to the x-face. 10 marks

Centre at (80, 0) N/mm², radius 72.1 N/mm²; Point A plotted at (120, 60); Rotate diameter by 2θ = 60° in the correct sense; Read normal stress from the horizontal coordinate; Read shear stress from the vertical coordinate; Compare with values from the transformation equations.

Apply A 45° strain rosette reads ε0 = 400×10⁻⁶, ε45 = 200×10⁻⁶ and ε90 = −100×10⁻⁶. Find εx, εy, γxy, the principal strains and their directions. Take E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.3. 13 marks

εx = ε0, εy = ε90; ε45 = (εx+εy)/2 + γxy/2 gives γxy; γxy = 2ε45 − εx − εy = 100×10⁻⁶; Principal strains from ε1,2 = (εx+εy)/2 ± √(((εx−εy)/2)² + (γxy/2)²); tan2θp = γxy/(εx−εy); Convert to stresses using plane stress Hooke's law.

Apply A solid shaft of diameter d carries a bending moment M and a torque T. Obtain the principal stresses at the surface and find the diameter using the maximum shear stress theory with yield strength σy and factor of safety n. 14 marks

Bending stress σ = 32M/(πd³), torsional shear τ = 16T/(πd³); Principal stresses = σ/2 ± √((σ/2)² + τ²); τmax = √((σ/2)² + τ²); Tresca: τmax ≤ σy/(2n); Substitute and solve for d; Note the equivalent moment form √(M² + T²).

Apply A thin cylindrical pressure vessel of diameter D and thickness t has internal pressure p. Taking the surface element as biaxial, find the principal stresses and check the wall thickness by the von Mises theory with factor of safety n. 12 marks

Hoop stress σ1 = pD/(2t), axial σ2 = pD/(4t), shear zero; State that these are already principal stresses; von Mises σ′ = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²); Substitute to get σ′ = pD/(2t) × 0.866 approximately; Set σ′ ≤ σy/n and solve for t; Compare with the Tresca result.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the maximum principal stress, maximum shear stress and maximum distortion energy theories. State the stress measure used, the safe-condition inequality and the material each suits. 13 marks

Rankine: σ1 compared with σy, brittle materials; Tresca: σ1 − σ2 compared with σy, ductile materials; von Mises: √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²) compared with σy, ductile materials; Behaviour under pure shear: Rankine and Tresca disagree, von Mises lies between; Tresca is conservative and simple; von Mises fits test data for metals; Hydrostatic stress causes no yielding in Tresca and von Mises.

Evaluate For a state of pure shear τ, evaluate the stress measure predicted by each of the three theories and comment on which gives the safest design and at what cost. 12 marks

Principal stresses are +τ and −τ; Rankine measure = τ; Tresca measure = 2τ; von Mises measure = √3 τ ≈ 1.732τ; Rankine is unsafe here because it ignores the compressive principal stress; Tresca demands the largest section; von Mises is intermediate and best supported by tests.

Evaluate A component is designed by one theory and fails in service under a combined bending and torsion load. Analyse the possible reasons and recommend a procedure for revising the design. 14 marks

Check whether the material is ductile or brittle and whether the theory matches; Recompute principal stresses including stress concentration; Check whether fatigue, not static yield, governs; Confirm the factor of safety definition used; Compare Tresca and von Mises diameters; Recommend combined-load equivalent stress with a fatigue factor.

Understand Write the two-dimensional equilibrium equations and the strain-displacement relations. Explain why compatibility conditions are needed along with equilibrium to solve a plane stress problem. 12 marks

∂σx/∂x + ∂τxy/∂y + X = 0 and ∂τxy/∂x + ∂σy/∂y + Y = 0; εx = ∂u/∂x, εy = ∂v/∂y, γxy = ∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x; Three strains from two displacements means one compatibility link; ∂²εx/∂y² + ∂²εy/∂x² = ∂²γxy/∂x∂y; Equilibrium alone gives stresses that may not be compatible with a continuous displacement field; Compatibility plus Hooke's law gives the governing equation in terms of stress or displacement.

Analyze Derive the strain transformation equations for plane strain and show why γ/2 appears where τ appeared in the stress equations. 12 marks

Start from displacement of a line element rotated by θ; Express εx′ and γx′y′ in terms of εx, εy, γxy and 2θ; Compare term by term with the stress equations; Identify ε ↔ σ and γ/2 ↔ τ; Explain that γ is an engineering shear strain equal to twice the tensor component; Note the invariant εx + εy = ε1 + ε2.

Glossary

Body force
A force per unit volume acting throughout the material, such as self weight.
Brittle material
A material that fractures with little plastic deformation before failure, such as cast iron.
Compatibility
The requirement that strains derived from a displacement field fit together without gaps or overlaps.
Deviatoric stress
The stress remaining after the mean stress is subtracted from each normal component.
Ductile material
A material that yields and stretches a large amount before fracture, such as mild steel.
Equivalent stress
A single uniaxial stress that a chosen failure theory says is equally severe as the real combined stress state.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure or yield strength to the working stress actually allowed in the part.
Hooke's law
The linear relation between stress and strain below the proportional limit, with constants E, G and ν.
Invariant
A combination of stress or strain components that stays the same when the axes are rotated.
Mean stress
One third of the sum of the three normal stresses; also called hydrostatic or spherical stress.
Mohr's circle
A circle on the σ–τ plane whose points give the normal and shear stress on every plane through a point.
Plane strain
A state in which the strain in one direction is zero, leaving εx, εy and γxy.
Plane stress
A state in which the stresses on one pair of faces are zero, leaving σx, σy and τxy.
Pole of Mohr's circle
A point on the circle from which a line drawn parallel to a plane meets the circle at that plane's stress point.
Principal direction
The normal to a principal plane, along which the shear stress vanishes.
Rosette
A group of three strain gauges set at fixed angles so the full plane strain state can be found.
Strain gauge
A thin resistive foil bonded to a surface whose change in resistance measures the normal strain along its axis.
Stress concentration
A local rise in stress near a hole, notch or sudden change of section.
Stress tensor
A set of nine components, six independent in the symmetric case, that gives the stress on any plane through a point.
Traction
The force per unit area acting on a chosen plane through a point.
Yield strength
The stress at which a material begins to deform permanently, usually taken at 0.2% offset for metals.
Yield surface
The boundary in principal-stress space that separates elastic from plastic behaviour.
