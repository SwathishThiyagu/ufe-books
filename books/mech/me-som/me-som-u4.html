4.1 Centroid and second moment of area

Every beam section has a balance point called the centroid. The area spread about that point is measured by the second moment of area, I. Together they fix the neutral axis and the bending stiffness of the section. A deep I-section carries more bending moment than a flat strip of the same area because its material sits far from the neutral axis. This chapter shows how to find the centroid of a composite area, how to compute I about centroidal axes, and how to use the parallel-axis theorem. These numbers feed directly into the bending formula in 4.2.

You need: basic statics from Unit 3, Shear Force and Bending Moment.

Centroid and first moment of area

The centroid of an area is the point where a thin uniform sheet of that shape would balance. For a section in the x-y plane, write the centroid as (x̄, ȳ). The first moment of area about the x-axis is Q_x = Σ A_i y_i, and about the y-axis is Q_y = Σ A_i x_i. Here A_i is the area of part i in mm², and (x_i, y_i) is its centroid in mm. First moments carry units of mm³.

x̄ = Σ A_i x_i / Σ A_i,    ȳ = Σ A_i y_i / Σ A_i

x̄, ȳ are centroid coordinates in mm; A_i is each part area in mm²; x_i, y_i are each part centroid in mm; Σ A_i is total area in mm².

For a composite section, split it into simple rectangles, circles or triangles. Choose an origin and positive directions first. Find each part area and its own centroid from the base. Multiply area by centroid distance, add, and divide by total area. A hole is a negative area: subtract both its area and its first moment. For the second moment, a hole is also subtracted in full: subtract (I_c of the hole + A d² for the hole) about the required axis. Always state where ȳ is measured from, because the same section has different ȳ values from the top and from the bottom.

  1. Draw the section and choose x and y axes.
  2. Split into simple parts; label holes as negative.
  3. Compute each area A_i and its centroid (x_i, y_i).
  4. Form Σ A_i x_i and Σ A_i y_i.
  5. Divide each sum by total area Σ A_i to get x̄ and ȳ.

Second moment of area

The second moment of area about an axis measures how far the area is spread from that axis. About the x-axis it is I_x = ∫ y² dA. Here y is the perpendicular distance from the axis in mm, dA is an area element in mm², and I_x is in mm⁴. A larger I means a stiffer section in bending. Some syllabi call this quantity moment of inertia; it is not mass moment of inertia because no mass appears.

For an axis through the centroid, write I_c. The parallel-axis theorem moves I from a centroidal axis to any parallel axis: I = I_c + A d². Here A is the area in mm², d is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes in mm, and d² makes the shift positive either way. The theorem fails if the reference axis is not parallel or if I_c is not about the centroid.

I = I_c + A d²

I is the second moment about the required axis in mm⁴; I_c is about the centroidal axis parallel to it in mm⁴; A is area in mm²; d is the perpendicular distance between axes in mm.

For perpendicular axes x and y in the plane of the area, and z normal to the plane through the same point, the perpendicular-axis theorem gives I_z = I_x + I_y. I_z is the polar second moment about that point. Use this for circular sections or for any area where two perpendicular I values are known.

Standard shapes and composite sections

Learn the standard results. For a rectangle of width b and depth h, about the centroidal axis parallel to b, I_c = b h³/12. For a circle of diameter d, about a diameter, I_c = π d⁴/64. For a triangle of base b and height h, about the centroidal axis parallel to the base, I_c = b h³/36. About the base, the rectangle value becomes b h³/3 by the parallel-axis theorem.

For an I-section or T-section, split into rectangles and follow the same order. Find the overall centroid first. Then take each rectangle's own I_c about its centroidal axis parallel to the neutral axis, add A d² to shift it to the overall centroid, and sum over all rectangles. Do not add A d² to a rectangle without its own I_c. In bending of a homogeneous beam, the neutral axis passes through the centroid, so this I is the I used in M/I = σ/y, where M is bending moment, σ is bending stress, and y is distance from the neutral axis; see 4.2. The same I leads to section modulus in 4.3.

Worked example

Question. A T-section has a flange 120 mm wide and 20 mm deep and a web 20 mm thick and 80 mm deep, so the total depth is 100 mm. Find the centroid from the bottom and the second moment of area about the horizontal centroidal axis.

  1. Set y upward from the bottom. Split the T into a top flange rectangle and a bottom web rectangle.
  2. Flange area A_f = 120 × 20 = 2400 mm². Web area A_w = 20 × 80 = 1600 mm². Total area A = 2400 + 1600 = 4000 mm².
  3. Flange centroid height y_f = 100 - 20/2 = 90 mm. Web centroid height y_w = 80/2 = 40 mm.
  4. First moment sum: A_f y_f + A_w y_w = 2400 × 90 + 1600 × 40 = 216000 + 64000 = 280000 mm³.
  5. Centroid: ȳ = 280000 / 4000 = 70.0 mm from the bottom.
  6. Flange own I about its centroid: I_fc = 120 × 20³ / 12 = 80000 mm⁴. Shift d_f = 90 - 70 = 20 mm. A_f d_f² = 2400 × 20² = 960000 mm⁴. Flange contribution = 80000 + 960000 = 1040000 mm⁴.
  7. Web own I about its centroid: I_wc = 20 × 80³ / 12 = 853333.3 mm⁴. Shift d_w = 40 - 70 = -30 mm. A_w d_w² = 1600 × 30² = 1440000 mm⁴. Web contribution = 853333.3 + 1440000 = 2293333.3 mm⁴.
  8. Total I about the horizontal centroidal axis = 1040000 + 2293333.3 = 3333333.3 mm⁴.

Answer. The centroid is 70.0 mm from the bottom and the second moment of area about the horizontal centroidal axis is 3,333,333.3 mm⁴, about 3.333 × 10⁶ mm⁴.

Exam traps

  • Measuring ȳ from the top when dimensions are given from the bottom, or the reverse. The centroid stays in the same place, but the number changes (70 mm from the bottom is 30 mm from the top). Always say which edge ȳ is measured from.
  • Using d to the wrong axis in I = I_c + A d². The d must be between the part centroid and the required axis, perpendicular to both.
  • Forgetting each rectangle's own I_c and adding only A d². A rectangle has its own b h³/12 even when shifted.
  • Mixing first moment (mm³) with second moment (mm⁴), or using area in m² with lengths in mm.
  • Taking a T-section centroid at mid-depth. For the usual T with a wider flange, ȳ lies closer to the flange, not at 50 mm.

4.2 Theory of simple bending and bending equation

Pure bending is the simple case where a beam carries a constant bending moment and no shear force. The cross-section rotates but stays flat, so strain grows in a straight line from a neutral axis. Hooke's law turns that strain into a linear stress field. The result is the bending equation M/I = σ/y = E/R, the working formula for beam stress and curvature. Designers use it for floor beams, crane girders, railway sleepers and machine shafts: pick a section stiff enough that stress stays below the allowable value.

You need: 4.1 for centroid and second moment I, and Unit 3 (shear force and bending moment) for M.

Pure bending and its assumptions

Pure bending means the bending moment is constant along a length of beam and the shear force is zero there. A simply supported beam with two equal loads placed symmetrically has such a zone between the loads. In that zone every cross-section bends the same way. The top fibres shorten, the bottom fibres lengthen, and one layer neither shortens nor lengthens. That layer is the neutral surface; its line on a cross-section is the neutral axis.

  • Plane sections remain plane: a cross-section may rotate but stays flat.
  • Material is homogeneous (same everywhere) and isotropic (same E in every direction).
  • Hooke's law holds: stress is proportional to strain.
  • Deflections are small, so the radius of curvature R is large compared with the depth.
  • Young's modulus E is constant along the beam.
  • No shear force acts on the section; bending is the only action.

The assumptions let us relate geometry to stress without solving a full three-dimensional problem. Where shear is large, near supports or concentrated loads, the plane-section picture is only approximate; see 4.5.

From strain to the bending equation

  1. Bend a short length of beam to radius R. The angle between its end sections is θ. The neutral layer keeps length R θ.
  2. A fibre at distance y from the neutral axis has length (R + y) θ on the tension side. Its change in length is y θ, so strain ε = y θ / (R θ) = y/R. Strain is proportional to y.
  3. Hooke's law gives stress σ = E ε = E y/R. Stress also varies linearly with y.
  4. The axial force on a small area dA is σ dA. A pure bending moment has no resultant axial force, so ∫ σ dA = 0. With σ = E y/R, this gives (E/R) ∫ y dA = 0, so the neutral axis passes through the centroid. Section 4.1 shows how to find that centroid.
  5. Take moments about the neutral axis. The moment of σ dA is σ y dA, so M = ∫ σ y dA = (E/R) ∫ y² dA = (E/R) I. Here I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis.
  6. Divide by I: M/I = E/R. Combine with σ = E y/R to get the bending equation.

M / I = σ / y = E / R

M is the bending moment (N·mm); I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis (mm⁴); σ is the bending stress at distance y (N/mm² or MPa); y is the distance from the neutral axis (mm); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); R is the radius of curvature (mm).

For a sagging moment, top fibres are in compression and bottom fibres in tension. Keep one sign convention throughout; many texts take y positive towards the tension side, so σ = M y/I gives tension there and compression on the other side.

Units, limits and where shear enters

Use one consistent set of units. In millimetres, M must be in N·mm, I in mm⁴, y in mm, E in N/mm² (= MPa) and σ in N/mm². A moment in kN·m becomes N·mm after multiplying by 10⁶. Do not put kN·m into a formula that expects N·mm.

Measure y from the neutral axis, not from the top or bottom. For a rectangle of depth d, the farthest fibre is at y = d/2 from the neutral axis. For an unsymmetric section, find the centroid first, then take the larger of the two distances to the extreme fibres.

Simple bending theory is exact only for pure bending. With shear present, cross-sections warp, and shear stress follows a parabolic distribution (see 4.5). The bending formula still gives good stresses away from supports and concentrated loads, but for deep or short beams shear deformation adds deflection. The section modulus form M = σ Z, with Z = I/y_max, is in 4.3; design checks are in 4.7.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam 50 mm wide and 100 mm deep carries a bending moment of 5 kN·m. Take E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum bending stress and the radius of curvature.

  1. Convert M: 5 kN·m = 5 × 10⁶ N·mm. Convert E: 200 GPa = 200,000 N/mm².
  2. Second moment about the neutral axis: I = b d³/12 = 50 × 100³ / 12 = 4,166,666.67 mm⁴.
  3. The neutral axis is at mid-depth, so y_max = 100/2 = 50 mm.
  4. Maximum stress: σ_max = M y_max / I = (5 × 10⁶ × 50) / 4,166,666.67 = 60 N/mm² = 60 MPa.
  5. Radius of curvature: R = E I / M = (200,000 × 4,166,666.67) / (5 × 10⁶) = 166,666.67 mm = 166.67 m.
  6. Check with R = E y / σ = 200,000 × 50 / 60 = 166,666.67 mm.

Answer. The maximum bending stress is 60 MPa and the radius of curvature is 166.67 m.

Exam traps

  • Putting kN·m straight into M/I with mm and N/mm². Convert M to N·mm: multiply by 10⁶.
  • Using the full depth as y. For a rectangle, y_max is half the depth, measured from the neutral axis.
  • Taking I about the top or base instead of the neutral axis. I must be about the centroidal axis.
  • Forgetting that the bending formula assumes no shear. Near a support or a point load, shear stress and local effects need 4.5.
  • Mixing the sign of sagging and hogging. Fix one convention and state which side is tension.

4.3 Bending stress distribution and section modulus

The bending equation gives the stress at every fibre, but design needs only the worst fibre. Section modulus packs the geometry into one number: Z = I/y_max, in mm³. The moment a section can carry is then M = σ Z, where σ is the allowable bending stress. A designer compares that against the bending moment diagram from Unit 3. The depth of a floor joist or the rolled steel beam over a platform is fixed by making Z large enough.

You need: 4.1 for centroid and I, 4.2 for M/I = σ/y = E/R, and Unit 3 for the bending moment diagram.

Stress varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis

Chapter 4.2 gave M/I = σ/y. Rearranged, σ = M y / I. Here σ is the bending stress at a fibre in N/mm² (the same as MPa), M is the bending moment at the section in N·mm, y is the distance of the fibre from the neutral axis in mm, and I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis in mm⁴.

The equation is linear in y. On the neutral axis y = 0, so σ = 0. Stress grows with distance, and the largest value sits on the extreme fibre at distance y_max. One fibre decides the strength of the whole section.

σ = M y / I and σ_max = M y_max / I

σ is the bending stress at distance y from the neutral axis (N/mm²); M is the bending moment at the section (N·mm); y is the distance of the fibre from the neutral axis (mm); I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis (mm⁴); y_max is the distance to the extreme fibre (mm); σ_max is the stress at that extreme fibre (N/mm²).

Draw it as a stress diagram: plot σ horizontally against y vertically. Two triangles meet at zero on the neutral axis, one in compression, one in tension, both peaking at the extreme fibres. The figure shows this for a 200 mm deep beam at 10 MPa extreme fibre stress.

Once M is known, the stress does not depend on the material. Two beams of the same section under the same moment carry the same bending stress whether steel or aluminium; only strain and curvature differ, through E.

Section modulus puts the geometry into one number

The designer needs only the worst fibre. Write σ_max = M y_max / I and group the geometry: Z = I / y_max, the section modulus in mm³. Then σ_max = M / Z, or M = σ Z. In this form M is the moment of resistance: the largest moment the section can take before the extreme fibre reaches the allowable stress σ.

Z = I / y_max so M = σ Z

A 100 mm wide, 200 mm deep rectangle has Z = 100 × 200² / 6 = 666,666.67 mm³. Laid flat, 200 mm wide and 100 mm deep, Z = 200 × 100² / 6 = 333,333.33 mm³, exactly half. Depth enters as the square, width only linearly: doubling the depth quadruples Z and the moment capacity.

Unsymmetric sections have two section moduli

In a T-section, a channel, or an I-section with unequal flanges, the centroid is not at mid-depth, so y_top and y_bottom differ. Compute I about the centroidal axis, then Z_top = I / y_top and Z_bottom = I / y_bottom. Two section moduli, not one.

  1. Find the centroid and put the neutral axis there (4.1).
  2. Compute I about that centroidal axis, using the parallel axis theorem for each rectangle.
  3. Measure y_top and y_bottom from the neutral axis, not from mid-depth. The larger is y_max.
  4. If one allowable stress serves both tension and compression (mild steel), the smaller Z governs: M = σ I / y_max.
  5. If the allowable stresses differ (cast iron is weak in tension), check each face: M_top = σ_top I / y_top and M_bottom = σ_bottom I / y_bottom. The smaller M is the moment of resistance.

Proportioning runs backwards: given M and σ, the required section modulus is Z = M / σ. Pick b and d so b d² / 6 reaches that value, then round to a standard size. A rectangular beam is usually two to three times deeper than it is wide, because depth buys capacity cheaply.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 3 m carries a central point load W. Its cross-section is 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep. The allowable bending stress is 10 MPa. Find the safe load W. Neglect self weight.

  1. Z = b d² / 6 = 100 × 200² / 6 = 666,666.67 mm³.
  2. Moment of resistance: M = σ Z = 10 × 666,666.67 = 6,666,666.7 N·mm = 6.67 kN·m.
  3. For a central point load on a simply supported span, the maximum moment is at midspan: M = W L / 4, with L = 3 m.
  4. W = 4 M / L = 4 × 6.6667 / 3 = 8.89 kN.
  5. Check: σ = M / Z = 6,666,666.7 / 666,666.67 = 10 MPa, the allowable value.

Answer. The safe central load is W ≈ 8.89 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using d/2 as y_max for an unsymmetric section. y_max is measured from the neutral axis, not mid-depth: a T-section can put the far fibre 120 mm from the neutral axis on a 160 mm deep shape.
  • Using b d² / 6 for bending about the weak axis without swapping b and d. The squared dimension must be the one in the plane of bending.
  • Mixing units. M in N·mm needs σ in N/mm² and Z in mm³. Feeding a moment in kN·m into M = σ Z is wrong by a factor of 10⁶.
  • Treating Z as a material property. It is pure geometry: same Z, same moment at the same stress, whether the steel is Fe 415 or Fe 500.
  • Taking the larger Z of an unsymmetric section when only one allowable stress is given. The farthest fibre reaches its limit first, so the smaller Z governs.

4.4 Flitched beams

A flitched beam is a timber beam with steel plates bolted or glued to it so both materials bend as one. Timber is cheap and light; steel is stiff. They share moment in proportion to stiffness. Only a few universities teach this. The tool is the modular ratio: replace steel by m times timber width, find one neutral axis, multiply steel stress by m. It appears in old timber bridges, temporary props and repair work.

You need: 4.2 for M/I = σ/y, 4.1 for centroid and I, and Unit 1 for modular ratio.

Full composite action and modular ratio

Full composite action means equal curvature 1/R in steel and timber. At distance y from the neutral axis, strain is ε = y/R and stress is σ = Eε, so σ_s = m σ_t, where m=E_s/E_t is the modular ratio, E_s is steel Young's modulus and E_t is timber Young's modulus; m≈20. Moment share follows EI, so stiffness decides share. The transformed section replaces each steel area by m times the timber area; then the ordinary bending formula applies. The neutral axis is the real one. The usual textbook picture is a steel plate on each side of the joist, or one plate clamped between two joists; top-and-bottom plates use the same method, only steel geometry changes. Bolts, nails or glue must prevent slip. Without connectors, steel carries less and timber more than full-composite values: steel stress falls, timber stress rises, deflection increases.

m = Es / Et

m is the modular ratio; E_s is steel Young's modulus; E_t is timber Young's modulus. m is dimensionless.

  2. Replace steel width b_s by m b_s; keep thickness.

4.5 Shear stress distribution in beam sections

A beam carries shear force as well as bending moment. The shear force does not spread evenly over the section: it produces a shear stress τ that changes with height, found from τ = VQ/(Ib). The same idea gives shear flow q = VQ/I along a joint. Engineers use it to check webs of steel beams, to space bolts and welds in built-up sections, and to avoid shear failure near supports where V is largest.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for centroid and second moment of area, and Unit 3 for shear force diagrams.

The shear stress formula

A shear force V on a section is not spread evenly as V/A. It creates a shear stress τ that changes with height. The formula is τ = VQ/(Ib).

τ = VQ/(I b), q = VQ/I

τ is shear stress at a chosen level, in N/mm² (same as MPa); V is shear force at the section, in N; Q is first moment about the neutral axis of the area beyond that level, in mm³; I is second moment of area of the whole section about the neutral axis, in mm⁴; b is width of the section at that level, in mm; q is shear flow, in N/mm.

Q = A1 × y1. A1 is the area beyond the level, and y1 is the distance from its centroid to the neutral axis. Take the area above or below the level; both give the same Q. At a free top or bottom surface the area beyond is zero, so τ = 0. At the neutral axis Q is largest for a rectangle, so τ is largest there.

The vertical shear stress has an equal horizontal shear stress on a small element. That is why the same formula gives the shear between flange and web, or between a beam and a bolted plate. Some syllabi call it horizontal shear stress. Use the level where you want the stress, not a fixed level.

Rectangle, circle and triangle

For a rectangle of width b and depth d, I = bd³/12 and A = bd. At height y from the neutral axis, τ = V/(2I)(d²/4 − y²). At y = 0, τ_max = 1.5 V/A. At y = ±d/2, τ = 0. For a solid circle of diameter d, τ_max = 4/3 V/A at the centre. For a triangle, the maximum is 1.5 V/A at mid-height; its neutral axis is at h/3 from the base.

Rectangle b×d1.5 V/ANeutral axis
Solid circle, diameter d4/3 V/ACentre
Triangle, base b, height h1.5 V/AMid-height, h/2 from base

These factors are not the average. V/A is the average shear stress. For a rectangle the peak is half as large again. A common exam error is to answer V/A as the maximum.

For a symmetric hollow circle or box, the peak is at the centre. Use b as the total material width at that level: 2(R − r) for a hollow circle, where R is the outer radius and r is the inner radius, and twice the wall thickness at a web level for a box. For unsymmetrical sections, compute Q and b at each level to locate the peak.

I- and T-sections: the web carries most shear

In an I- or T-section, almost all shear force is carried by the web. The flanges are thin and far from the neutral axis; the web is the continuous vertical plate. At a level in the web, b is the web thickness t_w. At a level in a flange, b is the full flange width. So τ drops sharply when the level crosses from web into flange.

To find τ at a level, use Q of the area above that level. In the web, that area includes the top flange plus the part of the web above the level. At the neutral axis, Q is largest and b = t_w, so τ is usually largest there.

  1. Locate the neutral axis and find I for the whole section.
  2. Choose the level y where τ is needed.
  3. Find the area beyond y and its centroid distance y1 from the neutral axis.
  4. Compute Q = A1 × y1.
  5. Take b as the width at that level.
  6. Use τ = VQ/(Ib). For shear flow use q = VQ/I.

Shear flow q is useful for bolts and welds. A weld joining flange to web must supply q N per mm of beam length. If the weld strength is s N per mm of weld length and a length L mm of weld is laid in each pitch p, then sL/p ≥ q, so p ≤ sL/q. Equivalently, the required weld length per unit beam length is q/s.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep carries a shear force of 60 kN. Find the maximum shear stress and the shear stress at 50 mm from the neutral axis.

  1. I = bd³/12 = 100 × 200³ / 12 = 66,666,666.7 mm⁴.
  2. A = bd = 100 × 200 = 20,000 mm².
  3. τ_max = 1.5 V/A = 1.5 × 60,000 / 20,000 = 4.5 N/mm² = 4.5 MPa.
  4. At y = 50 mm, area above the level = 100 × 50 = 5,000 mm². Its centroid is 75 mm from the neutral axis, so Q = 5,000 × 75 = 375,000 mm³.
  5. τ = VQ/(Ib) = 60,000 × 375,000 / (66,666,666.7 × 100) = 3.375 N/mm² = 3.375 MPa.

Answer. I = 66,666,666.7 mm⁴, A = 20,000 mm², maximum shear stress = 4.5 MPa at the neutral axis, and shear stress at 50 mm from the neutral axis = 3.375 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using V/A as the maximum shear stress. For a rectangle the peak is 1.5 V/A, for a solid circle 4/3 V/A.
  • In an I-section, taking b as flange width at the neutral axis. At the neutral axis b is the web thickness.
  • Using the area itself as Q. Q is area times the distance from its centroid to the neutral axis, so Q has units of mm³.
  • Mixing metres and millimetres. Use N and mm throughout to get N/mm², which is MPa.
  • Forgetting that b changes at a flange-web junction. The shear stress jumps there; the web value is the one that matters for shear design.

4.6 Shear centre

A beam bends cleanly only when the load line passes through one special point of its cross-section. That point is the shear centre, also called the flexural centre. Load there and the section bends without twisting. Load anywhere else, even through the centroid, and the section twists as it bends. Thin-walled channels, angles and Z-sections are common in purlins and crane rails, and their shear centre is usually not the centroid. This chapter finds the shear centre of a channel using shear flow.

You need: 4.1 for centroid and second moment of area, and 4.5 for shear flow q = VQ/I.

The point that gives pure bending

A transverse load on a beam produces a shear force V and a bending moment. The bending part is covered by the bending equation. The shear part is carried by shear stresses spread across the section. The resultant of those shear stresses acts along one line. Where that line cuts the cross-section is the shear centre.

If a load acts through the shear centre, the beam bends without twisting. If the load line misses it, the internal shear forces form a torque about the longitudinal axis, and the beam twists as well as bends. Twisting adds warping and extra shear stress, and can buckle a thin flange.

For a section with one axis of symmetry, the shear centre lies on that axis. For a doubly symmetric section such as a rectangle, a circle or an I-section, it lies at the centroid. For a channel, an angle or a Z-section, it lies somewhere else. A channel is the standard example: its centroid lies between the web and the flange tips, on the flange side, while its shear centre lies outside the section, on the other side of the web.

Shear flow in the flanges

Take a thin-walled channel with the web vertical and both flanges pointing to the right. A vertical shear force V acts on the section. In any wall, the shear flow q is the shear force per unit length along the wall, in N/m. It is found from q = VQ/I. Here Q is the first moment of area of the part of the section beyond the cut, about the neutral axis, in m³; I is the second moment of area of the whole section about the same axis, in m⁴.

In each flange, the shear flow runs horizontally. The flow in the top flange and the flow in the bottom flange run in opposite directions. These two horizontal forces are separated by the web depth h, so together they form a couple about the longitudinal axis. That couple is a torque. The vertical shear V acting through the web centreline does not cancel it. The section therefore twists.

Move the load line sideways by a distance e. The vertical force V now produces a balancing torque V e. No twist occurs when V e equals the flange couple. Solving that balance gives the shear centre offset for a thin-walled channel with equal flanges and uniform wall thickness t:

e = b² / (2b + h/3)

e is the distance from the web centreline to the shear centre, measured on the side away from the flanges; b is the flange width from the web centreline to the free edge; h is the web depth between flange centrelines. Use the same length unit for b, h and e. The wall thickness t cancels out.

Use the formula only for a thin-walled channel with equal flanges. An I-section needs no offset because its flanges produce equal and opposite torques that cancel. A box section behaves in the same way.

Where the shear centre matters

A roof purlin of channel section is often loaded through its top flange, where the roof sheet sits. That load line is far from the shear centre, so the purlin tends to twist. Over time the twist can crack the sheet or fail the purlin. Designers either load through the shear centre, restrain the flanges, or choose a box or I-section.

The shear centre is a property of the section geometry. It does not depend on the size of the load or on the material. Find it once and you know where to apply transverse loads to avoid torsion.

Only a few syllabi go beyond the channel formula. Full unsymmetrical bending needs the shear flow around the whole wall and the principal axes; that is advanced study. For beam design against bending and shear stress, see 4.7.

Worked example

Question. A thin-walled steel channel has web depth h = 200 mm measured between flange centrelines, equal flange width b = 100 mm measured from the web centreline to the free edge, and uniform thickness t = 3 mm. Locate the shear centre. State on which side of the web it lies.

  1. List the data: b = 100 mm, h = 200 mm, t = 3 mm. The section is a thin-walled channel with equal flanges, so the formula applies.
  2. Compute the denominator: 2b + h/3 = 2(100) + 200/3 = 200 + 66.67 = 266.67 mm.
  3. Compute the numerator: b² = (100 mm)² = 10 000 mm².
  4. Divide: e = 10 000 mm² / 266.67 mm = 37.5 mm.
  5. The shear centre lies on the horizontal axis of symmetry. The flanges point one way, so e is measured on the opposite side of the web centreline, outside the web.
  6. Check the thickness: t appears in Q and in I, so it cancels. Changing t does not move the shear centre.

Answer. The shear centre is 37.5 mm from the web centreline, on the side away from the flanges (outside the web).

Exam traps

  • The centroid is not the shear centre for a channel. The centroid lies between the web and the flange tips (about 25 mm from the web centreline in the example), while the shear centre lies outside the section on the other side of the web, 37.5 mm from the web centreline. A load through the centroid still twists the channel.
  • Measure b from the web centreline to the flange tip. Using the full flange width twice, or measuring from the flange edge, gives a wrong e.
  • Measure h between flange centrelines. Using the overall depth including flange thickness shifts e slightly; the formula assumes thin walls.
  • The offset is on the side away from the flanges. Drawing e inside the channel reverses the balancing torque.
  • The formula is for equal flanges and uniform thickness. Do not apply it to a lipped channel, an unequal-flange channel or a thick-walled section without a proper shear flow analysis.

4.7 Beam design for flexure and shear

A beam fails in bending when the stress at its farthest fibre passes the allowable stress, and in shear when the stress at the neutral axis does. Design runs analysis backwards: you know the bending moment and shear force from the BMD and SFD, you know the allowable stresses, and you must pick a cross-section that satisfies both at once. One number fixes the bending size — the required section modulus Z = M/σ_allow. A second check fixes the shear size. Every rolled joist in a steel table and every timber size in a roof was chosen this way.

You need: 4.2 for M/I = σ/y, 4.3 for Z = I/y_max, 4.5 for τ = VQ/(Ib), and Unit 3 for the BMD and SFD.

Step one: size the section for bending

The bending moment diagram gives the largest moment in the beam, M_max. A section is safe in bending when the stress at its farthest fibre stays at or below the allowable stress. That turns into a demand on the section modulus.

Z_req = M_max / σ_allow

Z_req is the smallest acceptable section modulus (mm³); M_max is the largest bending moment from the BMD (N·mm); σ_allow is the allowable bending stress (N/mm²).

Units do the work for you. Keep M in N·mm and σ in N/mm² and Z comes out in mm³. A moment of 40 kN·m is 40 × 10⁶ N·mm, not 40. Getting this wrong is the single most common mistake in the exam.

  1. Draw the SFD and BMD (Unit 3). Record M_max and V_max and where each occurs.
  2. Compute Z_req = M_max / σ_allow.
  3. Pick a trial section whose Z about the actual bending axis is at least Z_req.
  4. Check that section for shear using the correct τ_max formula.
  5. If shear fails, increase the web or the area and repeat. If both pass, accept the size.

For a rolled steel section, pick the lightest one in the table whose Z about the bending axis meets Z_req. For a rectangle Z = bd²/6, so depth buys far more strength than width.

Step two: check shear

A section that passes bending can still fail in shear. Short deep beams and beams with a heavy load just inside a support are the usual cases. Shear stress is largest at the neutral axis, where bending stress is zero.

τ_max = 1.5 V_max / A ≤ τ_allow

V_max is the largest shear force from the SFD (N); A = b × d is the whole rectangular area (mm²); τ_allow is the allowable shear stress (N/mm²). The factor 1.5 comes from the parabolic shear distribution of 4.5.

For other shapes use τ_max = VQ/(Ib) at the neutral axis: a solid circle gives 4V/(3A), a thin tube gives about 2V/A. Using plain V/A for a rectangle understates the peak stress by one third and passes sections that would actually crack.

In a long, lightly loaded beam the shear check usually passes with a huge margin. In a short span carrying a big load it decides the section. Shear is bought with area A = bd, from width or from depth; extra depth helps only in direct proportion to the area it adds, without the squared advantage it gives in bending.

Proportioning a rectangle and beams of uniform strength

For width b and depth d, Z = bd²/6 while area A = bd. Doubling d multiplies Z by four but area only by two; doubling b doubles both. Depth is the cheaper way to buy bending strength, which is why beams are deep and narrow.

A frequent exam proportion is d = 2b. Then Z = b(2b)²/6 = 2b³/3, so b = (3 Z_req/2)^(1/3) and d = 2b. Round both up to practical sizes, then recheck bending and shear on the rounded section — rounding down is never allowed.

A section is balanced when both limits are nearly used up. If τ_max sits far below τ_allow the beam is over-designed in shear, usually shallow, and will deflect too much — deflection is a separate check, see Unit 5.

A beam of uniform strength changes its section along the span so that Z(x) = M(x)/σ_allow at every point. Which profile you get depends on what is held constant. Keep the width constant and let the depth follow the moment: a cantilever under a UDL has M ∝ x², so d ∝ x and the side view is a triangle; a cantilever with an end point load has M ∝ x, so d ∝ √x and the side view is a parabola. Keep the depth constant instead and let the width follow the moment: a cantilever with an end point load is then a triangle in plan, and a simply supported beam under a UDL is parabolic in plan, widest at midspan. It saves material but costs fabrication, so standard rolled sections are never made this way.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 20 kN/m over its whole length. The allowable bending stress is 160 MPa and the allowable shear stress is 100 MPa. Design a rectangular section with depth twice the width, round up to the next 25 mm, and check the section for bending and shear.

  1. Reactions: R = wL/2 = 20 × 4 / 2 = 40 kN. So V_max = 40 kN at each support.
  2. Maximum moment at midspan: M_max = wL²/8 = 20 × 4² / 8 = 40 kN·m = 40 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  3. Required section modulus: Z_req = M_max / σ_allow = 40 × 10⁶ / 160 = 250,000 mm³.
  4. With d = 2b: Z = b(2b)²/6 = 2b³/3, so b³ = 3 × 250,000 / 2 = 375,000 and b = 72.1 mm, d = 144.2 mm.
  5. Round up: b = 75 mm, d = 150 mm. Provided Z = 75 × 150² / 6 = 281,250 mm³, which exceeds 250,000 mm³.
  6. Bending check: σ_max = M_max / Z = 40 × 10⁶ / 281,250 = 142.2 MPa ≤ 160 MPa. Safe.
  7. Shear check: A = 75 × 150 = 11,250 mm²; τ_max = 1.5 V_max / A = 1.5 × 40,000 / 11,250 = 5.33 MPa ≤ 100 MPa. Safe.

Answer. Use a 75 mm × 150 mm rectangle: σ_max = 142.2 MPa against 160 MPa allowed, and τ_max = 5.33 MPa against 100 MPa allowed.

Exam traps

  • Skipping the shear check after the bending design. Bending picks the trial size; shear can still reject it.
  • Using τ = V/A for a rectangle instead of 1.5V/A. That understates the peak stress by a third.
  • Feeding kN·m straight into Z = M/σ. Convert: 40 kN·m = 40 × 10⁶ N·mm, or your Z is a thousand times too small.
  • Using Z about the wrong axis. A rectangle bent about its other axis has Z = db²/6, not bd²/6.
  • Rounding the chosen b and d downwards, or rounding up without rechecking σ and τ on the new size.

Increase the area (widen the section or add a web) — costs material and self-weight but directly cuts τ = 1.5V/A. Reduce the span or move the load away from the support by adding a support — cuts V_max itself, but changes the structural layout. Deepening helps only in proportion to the area it adds, since τ = 1.5V/(bd), while the same depth in bending raises Z with d². Widening helps shear and bending only linearly.

Analyze Explain a beam of uniform strength and say why standard rolled sections are not made that way. 6 marks

The section varies along the span so that Z(x) = M(x)/σ_allow everywhere, giving a triangular or parabolic profile. The extreme fibre is at the allowable stress at every section, so no material is carried where the moment cannot use it. Rolling mills produce only straight constant-section shapes, fabrication of a tapered beam costs far more than the material saved, and deflection and stability still need checking.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real design codes replace the single allowable stress with partial safety factors on load and material: IS 800 for steel and IS 456 for concrete both work this way, so the working stress method here is the historical base, not the current rule. Plastic design goes further and uses the full yield capacity of the section, giving a shape factor of 1.5 for a rectangle — the same 1.5 that appears in the shear formula, for an unrelated reason. Software now searches thousands of rolled sections and optimises a whole frame, not one beam. Useful search terms: limit state design, plastic section modulus, shape factor, section classification.

Takeaways

  1. The centroid fixes where the neutral axis sits, and the second moment of area about that axis fixes how stiff the section is in bending.
  2. The bending equation M/I = σ/y = E/R packs three ideas into one line: moment capacity, stress at any depth, and how sharply the beam curves.
  3. Bending stress is linear across the depth and zero at the neutral axis, so material near the centre of a section does very little work.
  4. Section modulus Z = I/y_max is the practical design number: capacity M = σZ, and bigger Z for the same area means a more efficient shape.
  5. In a flitched beam the two materials share the moment in proportion to their E values, so a transformed section with a modular ratio lets one formula handle both.
  6. Shear stress does not stay uniform: it is zero at the top and bottom fibres, is maximum at the neutral axis for a rectangle, and is carried mostly by the web in an I-section.
  7. Shear flow q = VQ/I tells you how much force must be carried by bolts or welds per millimetre of length.
  8. A load applied away from the shear centre adds twisting to bending, so bolts and welds feel extra forces that plain bending formulas miss.
  9. Design is a double check: Z must be large enough for the bending moment and the shear area large enough for the shear force.

Exam question bank

Long answers in this unit almost always combine a derivation (bending equation or the shear stress formula) with a clearly labelled stress distribution diagram and a worked numerical check, so show assumptions, units and sketches, not just algebra.

Remember and understand

Understand State the assumptions of the theory of simple bending and derive the equation M/I = σ/y = E/R. Explain what each symbol means and its unit. 13 marks

List assumptions: plane sections remain plane, material is homogeneous and elastic, E is the same in tension and compression, stresses stay within the proportional limit, the beam is initially straight; Draw two sections a distance dx apart rotated relative to each other; strain at distance y is ε = y/R; Apply Hooke's law: σ = E y/R; Equate the internal moment of the stress force about the neutral axis to M and get M = EI/R; Combine to write M/I = σ/y = E/R and define every symbol and unit.

Understand Define centroid and second moment of area. State the parallel axis theorem and explain why these two quantities decide where the neutral axis lies and how stiff a section is. 10 marks

Definition of centroid with the area formula; axis passes through the centroid; Definition of I = Σa y², unit mm⁴; Parallel axis theorem I = I_c + A d², with the meaning of each term; The neutral axis coincides with the centroidal axis because the net axial force in pure bending is zero; I about that axis gives flexural rigidity EI; a larger I means smaller curvature and smaller stress for the same moment.

Understand Explain section modulus. Derive Z for a rectangle and for a solid circle, and show why an I-section is more efficient than a rectangle of the same area. 10 marks

Z = I/y_max, unit mm³, capacity M = σZ; Rectangle: I = bd³/12, y_max = d/2, Z = bd²/6; Circle: I = πd⁴/64, y_max = d/2, Z = πd³/32; I-section: most area is placed far from the neutral axis, so I and Z are large for the same area; Rectangle of the same area puts a lot of material near the neutral axis where stress is low.

Understand What is a flitched beam? Explain the modular ratio and the transformed section method, and write the equations used to find the stresses in the two materials. 12 marks

Definition: two materials, typically timber and steel plates, bolted to act as one beam; Assumptions: same curvature, no slip between the materials, strain is continuous at the interface; Modular ratio m = E_steel/E_timber; Transform steel into equivalent timber by multiplying its width by m; the section is then one material with I_eq; σ_timber = M y/I_eq and σ_steel = m M y/I_eq.

Apply

Apply A T-section has flange 100 mm × 20 mm and web 20 mm × 120 mm. Find the centroid, the second moment of area about the centroidal axis, the section modulus, and the maximum bending stress for a bending moment of 15 kN·m. 13 marks

Split into two rectangles, take a reference edge, compute ȳ = Σa y/Σa correctly in mm; Find I about the centroid with the parallel axis theorem for both rectangles; state the unit mm⁴; Take y_max as the larger of the two distances to the top and bottom fibres; Z = I/y_max in mm³; σ_max = M/Z with M = 15 × 10⁶ N·mm, answer in N/mm².

Apply A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 20 kN/m over its whole length. The section is rectangular, 150 mm wide, and the permissible bending stress is 10 N/mm². Find the required depth and then check the maximum shear stress. 14 marks

Maximum bending moment wL²/8 = 20 × 4²/8 = 40 kN·m; Set M = σZ with σ = 10 N/mm² to get the required Z; For a rectangle Z = bd²/6, solve for d in mm and round up to a practical size; Maximum shear force V = wL/2 = 40 kN at the supports; τ_max = 1.5 V/(bd) and compare with the permissible shear stress.

Apply A rectangular beam 100 mm wide and 200 mm deep carries a shear force of 60 kN. Plot the shear stress distribution across the depth and find the shear stress at the neutral axis and at 50 mm from it. 12 marks

State τ = VQ/(Ib) and identify b = 100 mm constant for a rectangle; At the neutral axis Q = b(d/2)(d/4) giving τ_max = 1.5 V/A; Compute A = 100 × 200 = 20 000 mm² and τ_max = 1.5 × 60 000/20 000 = 4.5 N/mm²; At a general y, τ = 6V/(bd³) × (d²/4 − y²); substitute y = 50 mm; Draw the parabola: zero at top and bottom fibres, maximum at the neutral axis.

Apply A timber beam 150 mm wide and 250 mm deep is strengthened by a steel plate 150 mm × 10 mm fixed to its top. Take E_steel = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm² and E_timber = 1 × 10⁴ N/mm². Find the moment the compound section can carry if the timber stress is limited to 8 N/mm². 14 marks

Modular ratio m = 2 × 10⁵/1 × 10⁴ = 20; Transform steel into equivalent timber: width 150 × 20 = 3000 mm; Locate the centroid of the transformed section, measuring from the bottom; Compute I_eq about that centroid using parallel axis theorem for both parts; σ_timber = M y_timber/I_eq gives M from the timber limit; check the steel stress as m times the timber stress at the same distance.

Apply Design a simply supported beam of span 6 m carrying a total factored load of 50 kN/m using an I-section. The permissible bending stress is 165 N/mm² and the permissible shear stress is 100 N/mm². Show the two checks. 14 marks

Maximum BM = wL²/8; maximum SF = wL/2, both with consistent units; Required Z = M/σ_allow in mm³, then choose a standard I-section with Z at least this value; Required shear area = V/τ_allow, then check that the web area (depth × web thickness) is adequate; Note the maximum shear occurs at the supports where the bending moment is zero, and vice versa; Comment on deflection serviceability as a third check in practice.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare a rectangular section and an I-section of the same cross-sectional area for bending. Explain, using the stress distribution, why the I-section carries far more moment and where its weak point lies. 13 marks

Both have the same area, so the same weight per metre; Bending stress grows linearly with y, so material near the neutral axis is lightly stressed and wasted; Move that material to the flanges and I and Z rise sharply for the same area; Sketch the linear stress diagram on both sections and mark the extreme fibres; Weak point: the thin web carries most of the shear, so shear stress at the neutral axis becomes the limiting check.

Analyze A load on a channel section is applied along the vertical centre line of the web. Explain why the section still twists, define the shear centre, and describe how you would locate it for a thin-walled channel. 13 marks

The shear flow in the flanges produces a horizontal force couple that is not balanced on the web centre line; Twisting occurs unless the load passes through the shear centre; Definition of shear centre: the point where the resultant of the internal shear forces acts, so a load there causes no twist; For a singly symmetric section the shear centre lies on the axis of symmetry, but not at the centroid; Method: take moments of the flange shear forces about the web, e ≈ (b²h²t)/(4I) type thin-walled result; state that bolts and welds must carry the added torsion.

Evaluate A beam has been checked in bending and found safe. Explain why it may still fail, using the shear stress distribution in an I-section, and state the design conditions for a beam that is safe in both flexure and shear. 13 marks

Bending stress is maximum at the extreme fibre but zero at the neutral axis; Shear stress is maximum at the neutral axis where the bending stress is zero — the two maxima are at different depths; In an I-section the web is thin, so τ = VQ/(Ib) can be high even when bending stress is low; Design conditions: σ_max = M/Z ≤ σ_allow and τ_max = VQ/(Ib) ≤ τ_allow; Also check shear flow at the flange-to-web junction, q = VQ/I per mm, for the weld or bolt size.

Evaluate Discuss when a flitched beam is preferred over a plain timber beam, and evaluate the effect of bolt slip and of a different coefficient of thermal expansion between the two materials. 12 marks

Timber alone needs a very deep section for large spans and moments; steel plates add stiffness cheaply; The transformed section assumes no slip, so the bolts must be designed for the shear flow q = VQ/I at the interface; Efficiency drops if the connection slips because plane sections no longer stay plane; Different thermal expansion creates locked-in stresses even with no external load; Verdict: efficient for strengthening and repair work when the connection is properly designed.

Glossary

Beam of uniform strength
A beam whose cross-section changes along the span so that the extreme fibre stress remains constant.
Centroid
The geometric centre of an area, the point through which the neutral axis passes in pure bending.
Complementary shear stress
The equal shear stress that acts on the longitudinal face at the same point as the transverse shear stress on the cross-section.
Curvature
The reciprocal of the radius of curvature, 1/R, in mm⁻¹; it equals M/(EI).
Extreme fibre
The topmost or bottommost point of a section, that is, the point farthest from the neutral axis.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure stress of a material to the working stress chosen for design.
First moment of area
The product of an area and the perpendicular distance of its centroid from an axis, measured in mm³.
Flexural rigidity
The product EI of a beam, in N·mm², which measures its resistance to bending.
Flitched beam
A beam made of two materials, commonly timber with steel plates, bolted or glued together to act as one.
Load carrying capacity
The maximum load a beam can support without exceeding the permissible bending or shear stress.
Modular ratio
The ratio of the Young's modulus of the stiffer material to that of the weaker material in a compound beam.
Moment of resistance
The bending moment a section can safely resist, equal to the permissible stress times the section modulus.
Neutral axis
The line in a bent cross-section along which the longitudinal strain and stress are zero.
Parallel axis theorem
The second moment of area about any axis equals that about a parallel centroidal axis plus the area times the square of the distance between them.
Permissible stress
The working stress a material is allowed to carry, obtained by dividing the yield or ultimate stress by a factor of safety.
Perpendicular axis theorem
For a thin lamina, the polar second moment about an axis normal to the plane equals the sum of the two second moments about any two perpendicular axes in the plane.
Plane sections
A cross-section that is flat before bending remains flat after bending, only rotating about the neutral axis.
Proportioning of sections
Choosing the depth-to-width ratio and the shape of a beam section so that material is used efficiently in bending.
Pure bending
Bending in which the shear force is zero throughout the length considered, so the bending moment is constant.
Second moment of area
The sum of area elements each multiplied by the square of its distance from an axis, measured in mm⁴; also called moment of inertia of the section.
Section modulus
The second moment of area divided by the extreme fibre distance, I/y_max, in mm³.
Shear centre
The point in a section through which a transverse load must act if the beam is to bend without twisting.
Shear flow
The shear force transmitted per unit length along a longitudinal plane, equal to VQ/I and measured in N/mm.
Transformed section
A fictitious one-material section, made by scaling one width by the modular ratio, that behaves the same way as the real compound section.
