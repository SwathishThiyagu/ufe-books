1.1 Normal and shear stresses: tension, compression, shear

A bar under load does not feel force at a point; it spreads that force over an area. Stress is the spread: internal force divided by the area of the face it acts on. When the force is perpendicular to the face you get normal stress, in tension or compression. When the force slides along the face you get shear stress, the kind that slices a rivet or punches a hole. Every strength check — a tie rod, a bolt, a hoarding's anchor in a Chennai cyclone — begins with these two numbers.

Cut the bar and look at the internal force

A rigid body is a bookkeeping idea: it never bends, so forces only balance. Real members bend and break, so we must look inside. Take a 20 mm steel rod carrying 50 kN in tension. Imagine a saw cut straight across it. The two halves still pull on each other with 50 kN, because the rod was in balance before the cut. That pull, spread over the cut face, is what the material feels.

Normal stress is the internal force perpendicular to the cut, per unit area.

σ = P / A

σ is the normal stress in N/m² (Pa); P is the axial force normal to the cut in N; A is the cross-section area in m².

The pascal is small: 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Steel works at millions of pascals, so engineers use 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa. Also 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², so a value in N/mm² equals the same value in MPa.

The sign is a convention, not a size. Plus means tension: the force pulls away from the cut face and the bar gets longer. Minus means compression: the force pushes into the face and the bar gets shorter. Always write it. A compressive 159 MPa is a different design case from a tensile 159 MPa.

Stress is not force. Halve the area and the same 50 kN doubles the stress. A thin wire and a thick rod can carry the same load, but the wire sits closer to breaking.

Shear stress acts along the face

Shear stress acts along the cut face, not across it. Two plates joined by a 20 mm rivet and pulled apart slice the rivet at the joint plane. The force on that plane is V, the shear force.

τ = V / A_s

τ is the average shear stress in N/m² (Pa); V is the shear force along the face in N; A_s is the area carrying the shear in m².

For a rivet in single shear, A_s is the rivet cross-section, πd²/4. If the rivet has two cut planes it is in double shear: A_s = 2 × πd²/4, and each plane carries V/2. Miss this and the answer is out by a factor of two.

Punching works the same way. A 20 mm punch through a 10 mm plate shears out a plug; the sheared surface is a cylinder of area πdt = π × 20 mm × 10 mm = 628.3 mm². With 150 kN of punch force, τ = 150 000 N / 628.3 mm² = 238.7 N/mm². Shops check punch capacity exactly this way.

V/A_s gives an average. The real shear stress on a solid circular rivet section is zero at the top and bottom of the section and reaches 4V/(3A) at the centre — one-third above the average. Simple design uses the average V/A_s. Some syllabi call normal stress direct stress and shear stress tangential stress. Same quantities.

  3. Take the matching area: cross-section for tension or compression, shear plane for shear, in m² or mm².
  4. Divide, keep the units consistent, and attach the sign for normal stress.

Judge the size. Mild steel yields near 250 N/mm², so 159 MPa is about two-thirds of yield — working, but not generous. Aluminium is not automatically weaker: many wrought alloys have yield strengths of 200 MPa or more, while some cast grades are far weaker. Never assume a strength; take the value for the actual alloy from a table. A stress means little until you compare it with the material's strength (see 1.2). The rod also stretches, and that uses the same P and A (see 1.6).

Worked example

Question. A 20 mm diameter steel rod carries an axial tensile load of 50 kN. Find the normal stress in the rod.

  1. Given: diameter d = 20 mm, tensile load P = 50 kN = 50 000 N.
  2. Area A = πd²/4 = π(20 mm)²/4 = π × 400/4 = 314.16 mm².
  3. Normal stress σ = P/A = 50 000 N / 314.16 mm² = 159.16 N/mm².
  4. Convert: 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa, so σ = 159.2 MPa, positive because the load is tensile.

Answer. The rod carries a tensile normal stress of 159.2 MPa (159.2 N/mm²).

Exam traps

  • Using the diameter as the radius: the area is πd²/4, never πd².
  • Mixing mm² and m²: 1 m² = 10⁶ mm², and 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa = 10⁶ N/m².
  • Taking the full cross-section as the shear area: shear acts on the cut plane, πdt for a punched hole, twice the section for double shear.
  • Dropping the sign: + for tension, − for compression; an unsigned compression answer loses marks.
  • Stress is force per area; an answer in kN is a force, not a stress, and scores zero however right the number looks.

1.2 Stress-strain diagram for ductile and brittle materials

A tension test pulls a specimen until it breaks and records load against extension. Dividing load by the original area gives stress; dividing extension by the original gauge length gives strain. The plot of one against the other is the stress-strain diagram, the material's signature. Its shape separates ductile materials, which stretch a lot before breaking, from brittle materials, which do not. Designers read yield strength, ultimate strength, stiffness and ductility from this one curve. It sets safe working stresses for beams, shafts and bolts.

You need Chapter 1.1 for the definitions of normal stress σ = P/A and normal strain ε = ΔL/L.

The test and the two axes

A tension test pulls a standard specimen until it breaks. The specimen is a round bar of known diameter d, or a flat strip of known width and thickness. A marked length in the middle is the gauge length L₀. The machine records the load P and the extension ΔL at each instant.

  1. Measure the original diameter d and mark the gauge length L₀.
  2. Mount the specimen in the testing machine and apply load slowly.
  3. Record load P against extension ΔL until the specimen breaks.
  4. Convert the record to stress and strain and plot the curve.

Engineering stress divides the load by the original area. Engineering strain divides the extension by the original gauge length. Both use original dimensions, so any laboratory gets the same curve for the same material.

σ = P / A₀

σ is the engineering stress in N/mm², which is the same as MPa; P is the tensile load in N; A₀ is the original cross-section area in mm².

ε = ΔL / L₀

ε is the engineering strain, a pure number; ΔL is the change in gauge length in mm; L₀ is the original gauge length in mm. Percentage strain is 100ε.

The plot of σ against ε is the material's signature. The first straight part means stress is proportional to strain; its slope is Young's modulus E, taken up in Chapter 1.4. The test assumes the material is homogeneous (the same everywhere) and isotropic (the same in every direction).

Ductile material: mild steel

Mild steel is the reference ductile material. Its curve has five named parts.

  • Proportional limit: the straight part from the origin. Up to this point σ/ε is constant.
  • Elastic limit: the highest stress the specimen can take and still return to its original length. For metals it sits just above the proportional limit.
  • Yield: the curve drops suddenly. The peak is the upper yield point; the dip that follows is the lower yield point; the flat stretch after the dip is the yield plateau. Along the plateau, strain grows with almost no extra load. Designers take the lower yield stress as the yield strength.
  • Strain hardening: after the plateau, stress rises again as the crystal structure tangles. The peak is the ultimate tensile strength (UTS).
  • Necking and fracture: past the peak the bar thins into a neck, the load falls, and the specimen breaks.

The stress at fracture, still computed with A₀, is the breaking stress. For mild steel it is lower than the UTS because the neck carries the load on a smaller area. The metal itself has not softened. In the figure, the vertical drop to zero at the end marks fracture; the breaking stress is the last non-zero stress before that drop.

The figure uses values close to a real test: yield near 229 MPa at about 0.11% strain, so the initial slope is about 200 GPa; UTS near 446 MPa; fracture after about 24% strain.

Brittle material and the offset proof stress

Cast iron, concrete, glass and many ceramics are brittle. Their curve is almost straight and then stops. There is no yield plateau, so fracture and maximum stress are almost the same point. Failure comes without visible warning.

Without a yield point, we use an offset proof stress. Draw a line parallel to the initial straight part, starting at 0.2% strain on the horizontal axis. Where it cuts the curve is the 0.2% proof stress. It is the stress that leaves a permanent strain of 0.2% after unloading. Some alloys use 0.1% instead.

  • Yield strength: the stress at first permanent set; for brittle materials, the proof stress.
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS): the highest stress on the engineering curve.
  • Breaking strength: the stress at fracture, using A₀.
  • Percentage elongation: (L_f − L₀)/L₀ × 100, where L_f is the fractured gauge length. It measures ductility.

Ductile steels commonly show 20–30% elongation; brittle cast iron shows under 1%. Thin sections, sharp corners and low temperature make brittle fracture more likely.

Worked example

Question. A mild steel specimen has gauge length 50 mm and diameter 10 mm. It yields at a load of 18 kN, reaches an ultimate load of 35 kN, and the fractured gauge length is 62 mm. Find the yield stress, the ultimate tensile strength and the percentage elongation.

  1. Original area: A₀ = πd²/4 = π(10 mm)²/4 = 78.54 mm².
  2. Yield stress: σ_y = P_y/A₀ = 18 000 N / 78.54 mm² = 229.2 N/mm² = 229.2 MPa.
  3. Ultimate stress: σ_u = P_u/A₀ = 35 000 N / 78.54 mm² = 445.6 MPa.
  4. Percentage elongation: (L_f − L₀)/L₀ × 100 = (62 − 50)/50 × 100 = 24%.

Answer. Yield stress is 229.2 MPa, ultimate tensile strength is 445.6 MPa, and percentage elongation is 24%.

Exam traps

  • Engineering stress uses the original area A₀. Dividing by the neck area gives true stress, which is a different quantity.
  • The ultimate load is the maximum load, not the breaking load. For mild steel the breaking load is lower than the ultimate load.
  • Brittle materials have no yield plateau. A question asking for the yield strength of cast iron expects the 0.2% proof stress.
  • The 0.2% offset starts at strain 0.002. On a percentage-strain axis, start at 0.2 on that axis; on a plain strain axis, start at 0.002. Check which axis the question uses.
  • Percentage elongation uses the change over the original gauge length. MPa and N/mm² are the same unit.

1.3 Hooke's law and factor of safety

Hooke's law says that inside the elastic limit, stress grows in direct proportion to strain. The constant is Young's modulus E for tension or shear modulus G for shear. Real designs do not run at the failure stress. Engineers divide a chosen failure stress by a factor of safety n to get an allowable stress, also called working stress. The safe load follows from this stress and the cross-section area. Tie rods, bolts, shafts and pressure parts use this pair of ideas every day.

You need Chapter 1.1 and Chapter 1.2.

Hooke's law for tension and shear

Hooke's law is the linear part of the stress-strain curve from Chapter 1.2. For a bar in axial tension, σ = E ε. σ is normal stress in Pa, equal to P/A; ε is axial strain, equal to ΔL/L and dimensionless; E is Young's modulus in Pa, often quoted in GPa. The law holds only within the elastic limit. Below that limit, unloading returns the bar to its original length. Above the limit, permanent strain remains and the straight line no longer applies.

σ = E ε

σ is normal stress (Pa, or N/mm² = MPa); E is Young's modulus (Pa); ε is axial strain (dimensionless).

In shear, the same linear idea uses τ = G γ. τ is shear stress in Pa; γ is shear strain in radians; G is the shear modulus, also called modulus of rigidity, in Pa. G is smaller than E for the same metal. Typical steel has E near 200 GPa and G near 80 GPa. Chapter 1.4 relates E, G and Poisson's ratio.

τ = G γ

τ is shear stress (Pa); G is shear modulus or modulus of rigidity (Pa); γ is shear strain (rad).

Factor of safety and working stress

No useful design sets σ equal to the stress that breaks or yields the material. The factor of safety n compares a failure stress σf with the stress allowed in service σw. σf is a material strength: yield strength σy for a ductile metal, ultimate strength σu for a brittle material. σw is the allowable, working or permissible stress. n is dimensionless and greater than 1.

n = σ<sub>f</sub> / σ<sub>w</sub>   so   σ<sub>w</sub> = σ<sub>f</sub> / n

n is factor of safety (dimensionless); σf is failure stress (Pa); σw is working, allowable or permissible stress (Pa).

For an axial member of area A, the safe load is P = σw A. P is in N when σw is in N/mm² and A in mm². In shear, replace σ by τ and use the shear strength or shear yield strength. The same factor may be applied to shear stress: τw = τf / n.

P = σw A = (σf / n) A

P is safe axial load (N); σw is working stress (Pa); A is cross-section area (m² or mm²); σf is failure stress (Pa); n is factor of safety.

Choosing the failure stress and the factor

The choice of σf follows the material and the design rule. Ductile materials such as mild steel show a clear yield point. A design based on yield prevents permanent stretch. Brittle materials such as cast iron break with little warning. Their design often uses ultimate strength. Some design rules use a proof stress for materials without a clear yield point. The factor n is then chosen for the job, not copied as a universal number.

  • Loads may be larger than the estimate: overload, vibration, wind, or a Chennai power cut that starts a pump against a closed valve.
  • Material strength varies from batch to batch and with temperature, work hardening and corrosion.
  • Analysis is simplified: joints are not perfectly pinned, loads are not exactly axial, and stress concentrations are ignored in a first check.
  • Failure has different costs: a toy bracket and an IRCTC bridge do not carry the same consequence.
  • Inspection and maintenance are imperfect, so a margin covers wear and unnoticed damage.

No single universal FOS value exists. Codes, company rules and the consequence of failure set it. A mild steel tie rod with σy = 250 MPa and n = 2.5 has σw = 100 MPa. The figure follows that chain from failure stress to safe load. The worked example uses a 12 mm rod.

Worked example

Question. A mild steel tie rod has yield strength 250 MPa. The design uses a factor of safety 2.5. The rod diameter is 12 mm. Find the allowable stress and the maximum axial load for the rod.

  1. Design failure stress: σf = σy = 250 MPa = 250 N/mm².
  2. Working stress: σw = σf / n = 250 / 2.5 = 100 MPa = 100 N/mm².
  3. Cross-section area: A = π/4 × d² = π/4 × (12 mm)² = 0.7854 × 144 mm² = 113.1 mm².
  4. Safe load: P = σw A = 100 N/mm² × 113.1 mm² = 11310 N.
  5. Unit change: 11310 N = 11.31 kN.

Answer. The allowable stress is 100 MPa and the maximum safe axial load is 11.31 kN.

Exam traps

  • Do not mix MPa and Pa. 1 MPa = 1 N/mm² = 10⁶ Pa. A stress of 250 MPa is 250 N/mm².
  • For a ductile rod, design usually uses yield strength, not ultimate strength. Ultimate is higher and hides yielding.
  • The factor of safety is dimensionless and greater than 1. A value like 2.5 is not 2.5 MPa.
  • Apply the factor to the failure stress to get working stress. When checking a given load, compare σ = P/A with σw. Do not replace σ by P/(nA) and call it safe.
  • Shear uses G and τ = Gγ in the elastic range, not E and ε. The two moduli are different.

1.4 Elastic constants, Poisson's ratio and their relations

A steel tie rod stretches and gets thinner under load. Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν capture both effects; shear modulus G and bulk modulus K describe resistance to twisting and squeezing. For an isotropic elastic material only two of the four are independent, so one tension test fixes the set. Designers use these relations for shafts, pressure vessels and springs.

You need: normal and shear stresses and Hooke's law from earlier in this unit.

Longitudinal strain, lateral strain and Poisson's ratio

A bar has original length L and lateral dimension d, say its diameter. Under axial load, length grows by ΔL and diameter shrinks by Δd. Both changes are small.

ε = ΔL / L

ε is longitudinal strain (no unit); ΔL is change in length (mm); L is original length (mm).

ε_lat = −Δd / d

ε_lat is lateral strain (no unit); Δd is decrease in lateral dimension (mm); d is original lateral dimension (mm). The minus sign appears because the diameter shrinks while the length grows.

Poisson's ratio links them: lateral strain divided by longitudinal strain, with a minus sign so tension gives positive ν.

ν = −ε_lat / ε = (Δd / d) / (ΔL / L)

ν is Poisson's ratio (no unit); ε_lat is lateral strain; ε is longitudinal strain.

ν is a ratio of strains: no unit, no dimension. It belongs to the material, not the bar size.

  • Steel: ν is about 0.28 to 0.30. Aluminium: about 0.33. Copper: about 0.34.
  • Rubber: about 0.49.
  • Cork: close to 0, so when a cork is pushed into a bottle neck it is compressed radially but does not swell along its length, and it seats without jamming.
  • For ordinary engineering materials 0 ≤ ν ≤ 0.5. A negative ν is possible only in special auxetic materials, and ν above 0.5 is not possible for an isotropic linear elastic solid.

The bar also changes volume; see volumetric strain.

The four elastic constants

Here a constant means a material property independent of test-piece size. Four constants describe a linear elastic material.

E = σ / ε

E is Young's modulus (Pa or GPa); σ is normal stress (Pa); ε is longitudinal strain in the same direction in a uniaxial tension test. E is the slope of the straight stress-strain part.

G = τ / γ

G is shear modulus or modulus of rigidity (Pa); τ is shear stress (Pa); γ is shear strain (rad), the change in a right angle.

K = −p / (ΔV / V)

K is bulk modulus (Pa); p is hydrostatic pressure on all faces (Pa); ΔV/V is volumetric strain (no unit). Some syllabi call K the modulus of volumetric elasticity.

Isotropic means same in every direction; homogeneous means same at every point. Rolled steel sheet is nearly isotropic; wood is not, since it is much stiffer along the grain. For isotropic, homogeneous, linear elastic materials only two constants are independent. Measure any two, usually E and ν, and the rest follow.

Relations between the constants

Learn these links in both directions; exam questions give different pairs.

E = 2G(1 + ν)

E is Young's modulus; G is shear modulus; ν is Poisson's ratio. E and G must be in the same unit; ν has no unit.

E = 3K(1 − 2ν)

E is Young's modulus; K is bulk modulus; ν is Poisson's ratio.

E = 9KG / (3K + G)

E is Young's modulus; K is bulk modulus; G is shear modulus. Use this when G and K are known, for example from a torsion test and a pressure test.

Rearranged, the same relations give G = E / [2(1 + ν)] and K = E / [3(1 − 2ν)].

Origin: axial stress σ gives strain σ/E along the axis and −νσ/E across it. On a plane at 45°, the same stress appears as shear, with shear strain set by G. Matching the two descriptions gives E = 2G(1 + ν). Three equal compressive stresses give the K relation.

Check limits. ν = 0 gives G = E/2 and K = E/3. As ν → 0.5, 1 − 2ν → 0, so K → ∞ and the material becomes incompressible. Rubber is close: it squashes but hardly changes volume.

  4. Substitute: 1 − 2ν = 0.40, so K = 200 / (3 × 0.40) = 166.67 GPa.

  6. Check 9KG = 9 × 166.67 × 76.92 = 115 383 GPa².
  7. Divide: 115 383 / 576.93 = 200.0 GPa, the given E.

Output

G = 76.9 GPa
K = 166.7 GPa
check E = 200.0 GPa

Answer. G = 76.9 GPa and K = 166.7 GPa; back-substitution returns E = 200 GPa.

Exam traps

  • The minus sign in ν = −ε_lat/ε is part of the definition. In tension ε_lat is negative, so ν is positive. Dropping it gives ν = −0.30 for steel and a wrong G.
  • The bulk modulus relation uses 1 − 2ν, not 1 + 2ν. With ν = 0.30 the wrong sign gives 200/(3 × 1.60) = 41.7 GPa instead of 166.7 GPa. Separately, if the correct form K = E/[3(1 − 2ν)] returns a negative K, your ν is above 0.5 and is an error.
  • All moduli must be in the same unit before you substitute. 200 GPa and 200 000 MPa are the same stiffness; 200 and 200 000 are not. Convert first, then divide.
  • ν has no unit. Writing ν = 0.30 GPa loses a mark. E, G and K carry Pa.
  • ν cannot exceed 0.5 for an isotropic elastic solid. If working gives 0.6, look for an arithmetic slip.

1.5 Volumetric strain

A bar pulled in tension gets longer and also thinner. Two changes happen at once. Volumetric strain measures both together: it is the fractional change in volume of the material, ΔV/V₀. Designers need it because a bush pressed into a housing, a rubber pad in a bridge bearing, or a part inside a deep-sea housing must not change size too much. This chapter shows how the three normal strains add up to a volume change, and how Poisson's ratio decides whether the volume grows, stays the same, or shrinks.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for E, K and Poisson's ratio, and Chapter 1.1 for normal stress.

Three normal strains add up

Take a small rectangular block with sides a, b and c along the x, y and z axes. Its original volume is V₀ = a × b × c. Under load each side changes: a becomes a + Δa, and so on. When the strains are small, the new volume works out to V₀(1 + ε_x + ε_y + ε_z). Terms such as ε_x ε_y are products of two small numbers and are dropped.

ε_v = ΔV / V₀ = ε_x + ε_y + ε_z

ε_v is the volumetric strain (no units); ΔV is the change in volume, in mm³ or m³; V₀ is the original volume, in the same unit; ε_x, ε_y, ε_z are the normal strains along the three axes, each no units.

This sum is also called dilation. It is dimensionless. A value of 0.0002 means the volume grew by 0.02 per cent. Positive ε_v means expansion, negative means contraction. Because it is only a sum of normal strains, shear strains do not enter: a pure shear distorts the shape of the block but does not change its volume in small-strain theory.

  1. Find the normal stress in each of the three directions, using loads and areas.
  2. Convert each stress to a strain with Hooke's law, allowing for the sideways pull of the other two stresses.
  3. Add the three strains. The total is ε_v.
  4. Multiply by the original volume to get ΔV.

The small-strain step matters. Once a rubber part doubles in length, or a metal part passes yield, products of strains are no longer negligible and the simple sum fails. Everything here assumes strains well below one per cent, which covers most machine parts in service.

Volumetric strain under axial load

Pull a bar with axial stress σ along x, with the other faces free. Hooke's law gives ε_x = σ/E. The two lateral directions are unloaded, so the bar contracts there by Poisson's ratio: ε_y = ε_z = −νσ/E. Adding the three:

ε_v = σ/E (1 − 2ν)

σ is the axial stress in N/mm² (MPa); E is Young's modulus in N/mm²; ν is Poisson's ratio, no units; ε_v is the volumetric strain.

The bracket (1 − 2ν) decides everything. Steel with ν = 0.30 gives a bracket of 0.40: at σ = 200 MPa and E = 200 GPa, ε_v = 0.001 × 0.40 = 0.0004, so the bar gains 0.04 per cent in volume while it stretches. Cork has ν close to zero, so the bracket is close to 1 and it changes volume almost as much as it stretches — that is why a cork stopper stays gripped in a bottle neck. Rubber has ν close to 0.5, so the bracket is close to zero: stretch it a lot and its volume barely moves.

At ν = 0.5 exactly, ε_v is zero and the material is called incompressible. No ordinary elastic material goes above 0.5. The volume change of a whole bar is ΔV = ε_v × V₀, valid while the stress is uniform along the bar. Compression is the same formula with σ negative: ε_v comes out negative and the volume falls. If the stress varies along the length, the bar must be cut into slices and the changes added — see Chapter 1.6.

A useful number: for steel, ε_v is only about 40 per cent of ε_x. The length grows faster than the volume, which is exactly why a wire stretches without swelling.

Hydrostatic pressure and bulk modulus

Now press the block equally on all six faces with pressure p. Put σ_x = σ_y = σ_z = −p into the generalised Hooke's law, ε_x = (1/E)[σ_x − ν(σ_y + σ_z)]. Each strain becomes −p(1 − 2ν)/E, and all three are equal. Adding them:

ε_v = − p / K with K = E / [3(1 − 2ν)]

p is the hydrostatic pressure in N/mm² (MPa), positive when it pushes inward; K is the bulk modulus in N/mm²; E is Young's modulus in N/mm²; ν is Poisson's ratio; ε_v is the volumetric strain.

K measures resistance to volume change, not to shape change. A fluid with large K needs a big pressure to be squeezed. The relation K = E/[3(1 − 2ν)] is one of the standard links between the elastic constants given in Chapter 1.4. As ν approaches 0.5, K approaches infinity and the material refuses to change volume.

Watch the sign. Pressure p is positive inward, so ε_v comes out negative: the volume shrinks. Water has K about 2.2 GPa, so a pressure of 2.2 MPa squeezes it by about 0.1 per cent. Steel has K about 160 GPa, some seventy times stiffer in volume than water. Oil is preferred in hydraulic systems because it lubricates the moving parts and prevents corrosion.

Worked example

Question. A steel bar of square cross-section 20 mm × 20 mm and length 1.2 m carries an axial tensile load of 48 kN. Take E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.30. Find the axial stress, the three normal strains, the volumetric strain and the change in volume.

  1. Area A = 20 mm × 20 mm = 400 mm².
  2. Axial stress σ = P/A = 48000 N ÷ 400 mm² = 120 N/mm² = 120 MPa.
  3. Axial strain ε_x = σ/E = 120 ÷ 200000 = 6×10⁻⁴.
  4. Lateral strains ε_y = ε_z = −ν ε_x = −0.30 × 6×10⁻⁴ = −1.8×10⁻⁴.
  5. Volumetric strain ε_v = ε_x + ε_y + ε_z = 6×10⁻⁴ − 1.8×10⁻⁴ − 1.8×10⁻⁴ = 2.4×10⁻⁴. Check with the formula: ε_v = (σ/E)(1 − 2ν) = 6×10⁻⁴ × (1 − 0.60) = 2.4×10⁻⁴.
  6. Original volume V₀ = A × L = 400 mm² × 1200 mm = 480000 mm³ = 4.8×10⁻⁴ m³.
  7. Change in volume ΔV = ε_v × V₀ = 2.4×10⁻⁴ × 480000 mm³ = 115.2 mm³, that is 1.152×10⁻⁷ m³.

Answer. The volumetric strain is 2.4×10⁻⁴ (0.024 per cent) and the bar gains 115.2 mm³ of volume.

Exam traps

  • Adding three equal axial strains σ/E and forgetting that two of them are lateral and negative — the answer comes out several times too large.
  • A sign slip on the lateral strains: using +νσ/E gives ε_v = (σ/E)(1 + 2ν) instead of (1 − 2ν).
  • Using ν = 0.5 for a normal metal and getting zero volume change.
  • For ν close to 0.5, K comes out very large — that is correct, not an error; report it as nearly incompressible.
  • Mixing units: σ in MPa with E in GPa. Divide 120 by 200000, not by 200.
  • Writing ΔV = ε_v × V where V is the final volume. Use the original volume V₀; for the small strains here the two differ by a negligible amount, but the definition uses V₀.

1.6 Deformation of simple and compound bars

Axial load stretches or shortens a bar. The extension δ depends on load P, length L, area A and modulus E: δ = P L/(A E). This one relation lets you size a tie rod, check a sag rod in a roof, or predict how much a bolt tightens. A stepped bar adds the extensions of its segments. A compound bar, with two materials bonded side by side, forces both materials to share the same strain and splits the load by stiffness. This chapter builds those two ideas.

You need: normal stress, Hooke's law and elastic constants.

One uniform bar under axial load

Take a straight bar of length L and constant cross-section A. An axial force P acts along its axis. The bar extends by δ. Strain is ε = δ/L, the fractional change in length. Normal stress is σ = P/A.

  1. Hooke's law: σ = E ε, where E is Young's modulus.
  2. Substitute σ and ε: P/A = E δ/L.
  3. Multiply both sides by L and divide by E: δ = P L/(A E).
  4. Check units: N × m /(m² × N/m²) = m.

δ = P L / (A E)

P is axial force in N; L is original length in m; A is cross-sectional area in m²; E is Young's modulus in N/m² (Pa); δ is extension in m. Positive δ is extension, negative is shortening.

The product A E is axial stiffness in N. A stiffer bar extends less. Use consistent units: 300 mm² = 300 × 10⁻⁶ m². The formula holds while stress is below the proportional limit and the section is uniform.

Stepped bars, varying sections and self weight

A bar made of segments in series carries the same axial force P in every segment. Segment i has length L_i, area A_i and modulus E_i. Its extension is δ_i = P L_i/(A_i E_i). The total extension is the sum of segment extensions. A bolt with a reduced shank and a stepped tie rod are common examples.

δ = Σ P L_i / (A_i E_i)

i numbers the segments; L_i is length of segment i in m; A_i is its area in m²; E_i is its modulus in N/m²; P is the common axial force in N.

If area changes continuously, replace the sum by an integral. Let x run along the axis from 0 to L, and let A(x) be the area at x. For constant P and E, δ = ∫₀ᴸ P dx/(A(x) E). A tapered round bar needs A(x) = π r(x)² inside the integral.

Self weight: a uniform vertical bar of length L, area A and density ρ hangs from its top. Its total weight is W = ρ g A L, where g is acceleration due to gravity. Some syllabi include the extension δ = ρ g L²/(2E) = W L/(2 A E). The factor 2 appears because the internal force falls from W at the top to zero at the bottom.

Compound bars in parallel

Two materials bonded along their length form a compound bar in parallel. Both parts have the same extension δ and the same length L, so both have the same strain ε = δ/L. The load splits: P = P1 + P2. Each material obeys its own Hooke's law: σ1 = E1 ε and σ2 = E2 ε. The forces are P1 = σ1 A1 and P2 = σ2 A2.

ε = P / (A1 E1 + A2 E2)

P is total axial load in N; A1 and A2 are areas in m²; E1 and E2 are moduli in N/m²; ε is the common strain. Then σ1 = E1 ε and σ2 = E2 ε.

The term A E is axial stiffness. The stiffer material takes a larger share of load, not a larger strain. For more than two materials, add every A_i E_i in the denominator. A steel rod bonded inside an aluminium tube is a standard example. The bond must transfer shear; if it slips, equal strain fails. For several axial loads in the linear elastic range, superposition applies: add the extensions from each load separately. Do not add strains of materials that are in parallel.

Worked example

Question. A steel bar has two parts in series. Part 1 has length 0.4 m and area 300 mm². Part 2 has length 0.6 m and area 200 mm². E = 200 GPa and the axial load is 40 kN. Find the total elongation.

  1. Convert: P = 40 kN = 40 000 N; L1 = 0.4 m; A1 = 300 mm² = 300 × 10⁻⁶ m²; L2 = 0.6 m; A2 = 200 mm² = 200 × 10⁻⁶ m²; E = 200 GPa = 200 × 10⁹ N/m².
  2. Segment 1 extension: δ1 = P L1/(A1 E) = 40 000 × 0.4 / (300 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹).
  3. Denominator for segment 1: 300 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹ = 60 × 10⁶ N. So δ1 = 16 000 / 60 × 10⁶ = 0.0002667 m = 0.2667 mm.
  4. Segment 2 extension: δ2 = P L2/(A2 E) = 40 000 × 0.6 / (200 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹).
  5. Denominator for segment 2: 200 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹ = 40 × 10⁶ N. So δ2 = 24 000 / 40 × 10⁶ = 0.0006000 m = 0.6000 mm.
  6. Both parts are in series, so add extensions: δ = δ1 + δ2 = 0.2667 + 0.6000 = 0.8667 mm.

Answer. Total elongation = 0.8667 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the total load for every segment is correct, but using the total length or one area for all segments is wrong: each segment has its own L_i and A_i.
  • In a compound bar in parallel, adding strains is wrong. Both materials share the same strain; the loads add.
  • Mixing mm² and m² in δ = P L/(A E) gives answers off by 10⁶. Convert area to m² first.
  • Putting E in GPa directly into the formula gives a result in the wrong units. Use N/m²: 200 GPa = 200 × 10⁹ N/m².
  • In a hanging bar under self weight, using δ = W L/(A E) misses the factor 1/2; the axial force varies along the length.

1.7 Statically indeterminate axial members

Most axial members have just enough supports for equilibrium to find every reaction. A statically indeterminate axial member has more unknown reactions than equilibrium equations. The extra equation comes from compatibility: the fixed ends force the total elongation to a known value, often zero. Engineers meet this in continuous pipes, fixed-end rods, preloaded bolts and composite ties. The method combines equilibrium, compatibility and δ=FL/(AE) to find the real reactions, then checks them against the applied load.

You need: axial deformation δ=FL/(AE) and compound-bar strain sharing from Chapter 1.6, plus free-body equilibrium.

When equilibrium is not enough

An axial member is determinate when equilibrium alone finds every reaction. One unknown, one equation ΣF=0, done. It is statically indeterminate when the number of independent unknown reactions is greater than the number of independent equilibrium equations. For a straight axial member there is one equilibrium equation along the axis. A bar fixed at both ends has two axial reactions, so its degree of indeterminacy is 2 − 1 = 1. Degree of indeterminacy = unknowns − equilibrium equations. Continuous rails, pipes anchored at two walls and rods with end plates often fall in this class.

Equilibrium still holds. For a fixed-fixed bar it gives R_A + R_B = P, one equation for two reactions. The missing equation is geometric: both ends are fixed, so the total change in length is set, often zero. That is compatibility. In the linear elastic range, superposition is allowed: add the displacement caused by the load and the displacement caused by the redundant reaction.

Compatibility and consistent deformations

Use a sign convention before writing equations. Take elongation positive and the axis positive to the right. Define the redundant as the extra unknown reaction (or internal force) removed to make the member determinate; compatibility then finds it. Remove one support, say the right one, and let the bar stretch freely under P. Compute the displacement at the cut by adding δ=FL/(AE) over the segments from the fixed end to the cut. Use each segment's own length, area, E and internal force. Here δ is elongation in m, F is internal axial force in N, L is segment length in m, A is area in m² and E is Young's modulus in Pa. Segments beyond the cut do not contribute when they carry no load. For the worked example below, after freeing B, segment CB carries no force, so δ_P=P a/(AE). Put back the redundant reaction R and compute the signed displacement δ_R it alone causes at the same point. Compatibility says the real cut point does not move: δ_P + δ_R = 0, with both terms signed in the same positive direction. If the redundant opposes δ_P, δ_R is negative. If you work with magnitudes only, write δ_P = δ_R. Solve for R, then use equilibrium for the other reaction. This is the method of consistent deformations.

  1. Draw the free body and count unknown reactions.
  2. Write equilibrium along the axis.
  3. Remove one support and find the free displacement δ_P at the cut as ΣFL/(AE) over the segments from the fixed end to the cut, using each segment's internal force.
  4. Apply the redundant reaction R and find the signed displacement δ_R at the same cut.
  5. Set δ_P + δ_R = 0 and solve for R; with magnitudes only, set δ_P = δ_R.
  6. Back-substitute in equilibrium for the other reaction.

For a uniform bar of area A and length L, fixed at both ends, load P at distance a from the left end and b=L−a from the right end, the reaction magnitudes are R_L = P b/L and R_R = P a/L. R_L and R_R act opposite to P. Derivation: segment AC elongates by R_L a/(AE); segment CB shortens by R_R b/(AE); compatibility gives R_L a/(AE) − R_R b/(AE)=0, so R_L a = R_R b. Combine with R_L+R_R=P. E and A cancel because the bar is uniform; they do not cancel for a stepped bar.

Parallel rods and checks

Parallel rods are another indeterminate case. A rigid block hangs from two vertical rods of the same length but different areas. Assume the block is guided to remain horizontal, or the load is applied so that it does not rotate; then both rods have the same elongation. Equilibrium gives F1+F2=P, where F1 and F2 are rod forces and P is the hanging load. Compatibility gives the same elongation: F1 L/(A1 E1)=F2 L/(A2 E2), where A1 and A2 are areas and E1 and E2 are Young's moduli. If E is equal, F1/F2=A1/A2: the stiffer rod takes more load. A thick tie in a parallel set can carry more than a thin one of the same length.

Run three checks. First, reactions must balance the applied load. Second, each segment's elongation must use that segment's length and internal force, not the full bar. Third, signs must match the chosen convention: tension positive, compression negative. A negative answer means the reaction acts opposite to your assumed arrow. Thermal loading, where a temperature change adds a free strain, is handled in Chapter 1.8. Indeterminate beams use the same compatibility idea; see Unit 5, Deflection of Beams and Energy Methods.

Worked example

Question. A uniform prismatic bar AB is fixed at both ends. Its length is L=1.5 m, area A=300 mm² and E=200 GPa. An axial load P=30 kN acts to the right at point C, 0.5 m from the left end A. Find the reactions at A and B.

  1. Take elongation positive and the axis positive to the right. Let R_A and R_B be reaction magnitudes at A and B, both assumed to act to the left, opposite P.
  2. Equilibrium along the axis: R_A + R_B = P = 30 kN.
  3. Segment AC has length a=0.5 m and internal force N_AC = R_A (tension). Segment CB has length b=1.5−0.5=1.0 m and internal force N_CB = −R_B (compression).
  4. AE = (300×10⁻⁶ m²)(200×10⁹ Pa) = 6.0×10⁷ N. It cancels. Elongations: δ_AC = R_A(0.5)/(AE) and δ_CB = −R_B(1.0)/(AE), with lengths in m and forces in N after unit conversion.
  5. Compatibility of the fixed ends: δ_AC + δ_CB = 0 → 0.5 R_A − 1.0 R_B = 0 → R_A = 2 R_B.
  6. Substitute in equilibrium: 2R_B + R_B = 30 kN → R_B = 10 kN; R_A = 20 kN.

Answer. R_A = 20 kN and R_B = 10 kN, both acting to the left; their sum is 30 kN.

Exam traps

  • Compatibility is about total elongation between fixed points, not about equal reactions. In a uniform fixed-fixed bar the reaction split depends on load position, not on E or A.
  • A sign slip is fatal: choose tension positive, keep it through every segment, and write δ_AC+δ_CB=0 for two fixed ends.
  • Use the segment length and the internal force in that segment. Using L=1.5 m for both segments in the example gives wrong answers.
  • Check R_L+R_R=P. If the reactions do not add to the applied axial load, equilibrium or arithmetic is wrong.
  • E and A cancel only when the whole bar has the same E and A. For a stepped bar, use ΣFL/(AE) and do not cancel.

1.8 Thermal stresses

A temperature change makes a bar want to expand or contract. If nothing stops it, the bar changes length and carries no thermal stress. If supports, neighbouring parts or a composite section stop that free movement, the material must strain mechanically to keep geometry compatible. That mechanical strain is the thermal stress. Rails, bridges, pipelines, boiler tubes and electronic packages all meet this problem. This chapter turns the free expansion αLΔT, where α is the coefficient of thermal expansion, into stress using compatibility and equilibrium.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for δ=PL/(AE) and compound bars, and Chapter 1.7 for compatibility equations.

Thermal strain and free expansion

The coefficient of linear thermal expansion α measures how much a unit length changes for a 1°C change in temperature. Its unit is 1/°C or 1/K. For an isotropic material, the same α applies in every direction. A temperature change ΔT is positive for heating and negative for cooling. A change of 1°C equals a change of 1 K, so the number used for ΔT is the same in both scales.

δ_T = α L ΔT, ε_T = α ΔT

δ_T is free change in length in m; α is coefficient of linear thermal expansion in 1/°C or 1/K; L is original length in m; ΔT is temperature change in °C (same number in K); ε_T is thermal strain, dimensionless.

If the bar is free to expand, the thermal strain is ε_T=αΔT and the stress is zero. The bar has changed length, not stress. For a 2 m steel bar with α=12×10⁻⁶ /°C and ΔT=50°C, δ_T=12×10⁻⁶×2×50=0.0012 m=1.2 mm. This free expansion is the starting point for every restrained problem.

Fully restrained bar

Fix both ends to rigid supports. The supports do not move, so the total length change is zero. Compatibility says the mechanical deformation must cancel the free thermal expansion: δ_mech + δ_T = 0. Mechanical deformation is δ_mech=σL/E for a uniform bar. Substitute δ_T=αLΔT and cancel L. The thermal stress is σ=−EαΔT. The minus sign with tension positive means compression when ΔT is positive. Heating produces compression; cooling produces tension.

δ_mech + δ_T = 0 → σ = −E α ΔT

δ_mech = σL/E is mechanical length change in m; E is Young's modulus in Pa; σ is normal stress in Pa, tension positive; negative σ means compression for a positive ΔT.

For a uniform bar that does not buckle, the result does not contain L or area A. A long bar and a short bar of the same material reach the same thermal stress when fully restrained. The force does depend on area: P=σA. The stress can be large. For steel with E=200 GPa, α=12×10⁻⁶ /°C and ΔT=50°C, σ=200×10⁹×12×10⁻⁶×50=120×10⁶ Pa=120 MPa compression. If one end is free, the bar expands freely and the thermal stress is zero.

Partly restrained and composite bars

Real supports yield, and different materials expand by different amounts. Use one compatibility equation and one equilibrium equation. Let Δ_support be the allowed movement in the expansion direction. Then δ_mech+δ_T=Δ_support. For an axial force P, δ_mech=PL/(AE). Solve for P or stress. For a composite bar with bonded parts, all parts have the same final change in length. For material i, total strain is ε_i=σ_i/E_i+α_iΔT. Set the total strains equal between materials. Equilibrium gives Σσ_iA_i=P_ext, where P_ext is the external axial load, zero if none.

If the bonded composite bar is held between two rigid supports, each part has zero total strain: ε_i=0. Then σ_i=−E_iα_iΔT for every material. The sum Σσ_iA_i is the support reaction R; it is not set to zero. The equal-total-strain pair with Σσ_iA_i=P_ext applies only when the composite bar is restrained at one end and free at the other.

δ_mech + δ_T = Δ_support,  ε_i = σ_i/E_i + α_i ΔT

Δ_support is allowed support movement in m, positive in the expansion direction; ε_i is total strain in material i; σ_i is stress in material i in Pa, tension positive; E_i is Young's modulus of material i in Pa; α_i is its expansion coefficient in 1/°C.

  1. Choose tension positive and write every expansion with its sign.
  2. Write free thermal deformation α_iL_iΔT for each part.
  3. Write mechanical deformation σ_iL_i/E_i or P_iL_i/(A_iE_i).
  4. Apply compatibility: mechanical plus thermal equals the allowed support movement, or equal final lengths for bonded parts.
  5. Apply equilibrium: sum of axial forces equals the external load. Solve the equations together.

A common mistake is to add thermal expansion to mechanical elongation without signs. A positive thermal expansion and a positive mechanical elongation both lengthen the bar. Restraint usually requires a negative mechanical term to cancel the thermal term. Keep the sign of ΔT and the sign of stress in the same convention.

Worked example

Question. A steel bar 2 m long is fixed between two rigid supports. E=200 GPa and α=12×10⁻⁶ /°C. The temperature rises by 50°C. Find the thermal stress. State its nature.

  1. Data: L=2 m; E=200 GPa=200×10⁹ Pa; α=12×10⁻⁶ /°C; ΔT=+50°C.
  2. Free expansion if the bar were free: δ_T=αLΔT=12×10⁻⁶×2×50=0.0012 m=1.2 mm.
  3. The supports are rigid, so total length change is zero. Mechanical deformation must be −1.2 mm, a shortening.
  4. Mechanical strain: ε_mech=δ_mech/L=−0.0012/2=−0.0006=−6×10⁻⁴.
  5. Hooke's law: σ=Eε_mech=200×10⁹×(−6×10⁻⁴)=−120×10⁶ Pa=−120 MPa.
  6. Negative sign with tension positive means compression. Direct check: σ=−EαΔT=−200×10⁹×12×10⁻⁶×50=−120×10⁶ Pa.

Answer. The thermal stress is 120 MPa compressive.

Exam traps

  • Using αLΔT as a stress. It is a length change; stress needs E and restraint.
  • Dropping the sign convention. Heating a fully restrained bar gives compression; cooling gives tension.
  • Expecting thermal stress when the bar has free ends. Free expansion gives δ_T but zero stress.
  • Adding 273 to ΔT. A temperature difference in °C equals the same difference in K.
  • In composite bars, adding forces without areas or ignoring equal final strains. Use both equilibrium and compatibility.

1.9 Strain energy under gradual, sudden and impact loads

A stretched bar stores energy, just like a lifted weight. The work done by the load while the bar extends is locked inside the material as strain energy. This matters when loads arrive fast: a suddenly applied weight or a falling hammer can push stress far above the slow-load value. Designers of crane hooks, tie rods and bumpers use these formulas to keep peak stress below the elastic limit. This chapter computes strain energy for axial members and compares gradual, sudden and impact loading.

You need: Hooke's law and factor of safety (Chapter 1.3) and axial deformation δ = PL/(AE) (Chapter 1.6).

Strain energy and gradual loading

Strain energy U is the elastic energy stored in a member when it deforms. For an axial load P that rises slowly from zero to its final value, the load-extension graph is a straight line. The work done by P is the area under that line.

U = ½ P δ

U is strain energy in joules (J); P is the final axial load in newtons (N); δ is the final extension in metres (m).

Use δ = PL/(AE) from Chapter 1.6.

U = P²L/(2AE)

L is the original length in m; A is the cross-section area in m²; E is Young's modulus in N/m² (Pa).

Divide by volume V = AL to get strain energy per unit volume, u = U/V = σ²/(2E), where σ = P/A is the normal stress in Pa. The unit of u is J/m³. For shear stress τ, the one-line result is U = τ²/(2G) × V, where G is the shear modulus in Pa.

A gradual load means P increases slowly, so no inertia is set up. At the end, σ = P/A. Some syllabi call strain energy resilience. Proof resilience is U at the elastic limit: the largest energy the member can store without permanent set.

Sudden loading

A sudden load is a weight W placed on the member without a slow build-up. The load stays constant at W while the end moves down by δ_max. Work done by the weight is Wδ_max. At maximum extension the internal force is P_max, so stored energy is ½P_maxδ_max.

W δ_max = ½ P_max δ_max → P_max = 2W

W is the suddenly applied weight in N; δ_max is the maximum extension in m; P_max is the internal axial force at maximum extension in N.

σ_max = 2W/A, δ_max = 2δ_st

σ_max is the maximum normal stress in Pa; δ_st = WL/(AE) is the static extension under the same weight in m.

The first peak stress is twice the static stress of the same weight. The member overshoots its static position and vibrates; the formula gives the first maximum, valid within the elastic limit.

  1. Find the static extension δ_st = WL/(AE).
  2. Double it: δ_max = 2δ_st.
  3. Double the static stress: σ_max = 2W/A.

Impact loading

A weight W falls through height h and strikes a collar at the lower end of a vertical rod. At maximum extension δ_max, the weight has lost potential energy W(h + δ_max). This becomes strain energy ½P_maxδ_max.

W(h + δ_max) = ½ P_max δ_max

h is the drop height in m; the other symbols are as above.

For the rod, P_max = (AE/L)δ_max = Wδ_max/δ_st, because δ_st = WL/(AE). Substitution gives the impact extension.

δ_max = δ_st[1 + √(1 + 2h/δ_st)]

δ_st is the static extension under W in m; h is the drop height in m.

σ_max = W/A[1 + √(1 + 2h/δ_st)]

σ_max is the maximum impact stress in Pa.

If h = 0, the result is δ_max = 2δ_st: a suddenly applied load. The square root carries the drop height, and δ_st already appears inside it. Do not add δ_st again after using the formula. The formula ignores the mass of the rod and assumes elastic behaviour.

Worked example

Question. A vertical steel rod is 2 m long and has cross-section area 300 mm². E = 200 GPa. A weight of 10 kN is suddenly applied to its lower end. Find the static stress, maximum stress, static extension and maximum extension.

  1. Convert units: A = 300 mm² = 300 × 10⁻⁶ m²; W = 10 kN = 10,000 N; L = 2 m; E = 200 GPa = 200 × 10⁹ N/m².
  2. Static stress: σ_st = W/A = 10,000/(300 × 10⁻⁶) = 33.33 × 10⁶ Pa = 33.33 MPa.
  3. Sudden maximum stress: σ_max = 2σ_st = 2 × 33.33 = 66.67 MPa.
  4. Static extension: δ_st = WL/(AE) = (10,000 × 2)/(300 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹) = 3.333 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.333 mm.
  5. Sudden maximum extension: δ_max = 2δ_st = 2 × 0.333 = 0.667 mm.

Answer. Static stress is 33.33 MPa; sudden maximum stress is 66.67 MPa; static extension is 0.333 mm; sudden maximum extension is 0.667 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using σ_max = 2W/A for a gradually applied load. A gradual load gives σ = W/A.
  • Adding δ_st to the impact answer after using δ_max = δ_st[1+√(1+2h/δ_st)]. The bracket already contains it.
  • Using the applied load W as the internal force P_max in sudden or impact loading. At the peak, P_max is larger than W.
  • Mixing mm² with GPa without converting. Use A in m² and E in N/m² before calculating.
  • Applying sudden or impact formulas beyond the elastic limit. They assume the material still obeys Hooke's law.

Takeaways

  1. Stress is internal force per unit area; normal stress acts perpendicular to a cut and shear stress acts along it.
  2. A tension-test stress-strain curve reveals elastic limits, yield, ultimate strength and fracture, and separates ductile from brittle behaviour.
  3. Hooke's law holds only within the elastic limit, and factor of safety converts a failure stress into a safe working stress.
  4. For an isotropic elastic material, E, G, K and ν are connected, and only two of them are independent.
  5. Volumetric strain is the sum of the three normal strains; for uniaxial stress ε_v = (σ/E)(1 - 2ν).
  6. Axial deformation is δ = PL/(AE); compound bars share load while bonded parts share the same strain.
  7. Statically indeterminate axial problems need compatibility of deformation in addition to equilibrium.
  8. Thermal stress appears only when free thermal expansion or contraction is restrained.
  9. Gradual axial loading stores U = ½Pδ, while sudden and impact loads produce larger maximum stress and strain energy.
