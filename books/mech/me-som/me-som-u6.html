6.1 Torsion of solid and hollow circular shafts

A circular shaft is a long round bar used to carry twisting. When you turn one end while the other is held, every cross-section rotates a little relative to its neighbour. The material resists by shear stress, not by the normal stress of bending. That shear stress is zero on the axis and largest at the outside surface. The torsion formula connects torque, shear stress, radius and a section property called the polar second moment of area, so one equation sizes solid and hollow shafts for pumps, axles, drill pipes and machine tools.

You need: shear stress and shear modulus from Unit 1, and second moment of area from Unit 4.

Twist becomes shear

Hold one end of a round rubber eraser and twist the other. A straight line drawn along the surface becomes a helix, and a small square on the surface becomes a rhombus. The square has not changed area much; its corners have slid sideways. That sliding is shear strain. A circular shaft works the same way. The twisting moment is torque T, measured in N·m.

Four assumptions make the theory simple. The cross-section is circular and stays circular. A plane cross-section remains plane after twisting and only rotates about the axis. The material is elastic, so Hooke's law applies. Shear stress is proportional to the distance from the centre. The last two assumptions fail after yielding; then the formula gives only an elastic estimate.

Look at a radius in the cross-section. After twist, that radius is still straight, but rotated by angle θ relative to the far end. The shear strain at radius r is γ = rθ/L, where θ is the angle of twist in radians and L is the length in metres. Hooke's law gives τ = Gγ = G r θ/L, with τ the shear stress in Pa and G the shear modulus in Pa. Therefore τ/r = Gθ/L: stress rises in direct proportion to r. At the centre r = 0, so τ = 0. At the outer surface r = R, the stress is largest.

The torsion formula

Equilibrium converts the stress pattern into torque. On a small element of area dA at radius r, the force is τ dA and its moment about the axis is τ r dA. Sum over the section: T = ∫ τ r dA. Substitute τ = (Gθ/L) r: T = (Gθ/L) ∫ r² dA. The integral is the polar second moment of area J, in m⁴. So T = GθJ/L.

τ/r = T/J = Gθ/L

τ is shear stress at radius r (Pa); r is radial distance from the centre (m); T is torque (N·m); J is polar second moment of area (m⁴); G is shear modulus (Pa); θ is angle of twist over length L (rad); L is length (m).

The same relation gives τ = T r/J. The maximum stress is at r = R: τmax = T R/J. The quantity J/R is the polar section modulus, in m³.

  • Solid shaft of diameter d: J = πd⁴/32.
  • Hollow shaft, outer diameter D and inner diameter d: J = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/32.
  • Use radius, not diameter, in τ = T r/J.

Some syllabi call J the polar moment of inertia and call the result the torsion equation; the symbol and meaning are the same.

Solid and hollow shafts compared

A hollow shaft removes material near the axis, where stress is small, and leaves it near the surface, where stress is large. For the same outer diameter and the same torque, a hollow shaft has a smaller J, so its maximum stress is slightly higher. But it also has much less area and mass. That is why drive shafts and axles are often tubes.

Take D = 60 mm outer for both. A solid shaft has J = 1.272 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴. A hollow shaft with d = 30 mm has J = (15/16) × 1.272 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.193 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴, while its area is 75% of the solid area. At T = 2.5 kN·m, τmax rises from 59.0 MPa to 62.9 MPa, a 6.7% penalty for a 25% mass saving. The trade is usually worth it.

Use the torsion formula for a first size check on a shaft. Then check angle of twist in 6.2. A shaft that is strong enough may still be too flexible, and a lathe spindle or a camshaft can fail a stiffness limit before it fails in stress.

Worked example

Question. A solid steel shaft of diameter 60 mm carries a torque of 2.5 kN·m. Find the maximum shear stress. Take π = 3.1416.

  1. Convert the data: T = 2.5 kN·m = 2500 N·m; d = 60 mm = 0.060 m; R = d/2 = 0.030 m.
  2. Polar second moment: J = πd⁴/32 = π(0.060)⁴/32 = 1.272 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴.
  3. Maximum shear stress: τmax = T R/J = 2500 × 0.030 / (1.272 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.90 × 10⁷ Pa.
  4. Convert to megapascals: 5.90 × 10⁷ Pa = 59.0 MPa.

Answer. The maximum shear stress is 59.0 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using diameter d in place of radius r in τ = T r/J. This doubles the stress.
  • For a hollow shaft, writing D⁴ − d⁴ but subtracting diameters first, as (D − d)⁴. The fourth powers are separate.
  • Mixing N·m with mm⁴. Use metres throughout, or millimetres throughout, and remember 1 N/mm² = 1 MPa.
  • Thinking maximum shear stress is at the centre of the shaft. It is zero there and maximum at the outer surface.
  • Confusing polar second moment J = πd⁴/32 with the second moment about a diameter I = πd⁴/64.

6.2 Angle of twist, strength and stiffness of shafts

A shaft can fail in two ways: the shear stress can exceed the material limit, or the twist can become too large for bearings and gears. The torsion formula gives stress, and θ = T L / (G J) gives angle of twist. A safe shaft passes both a strength check and a stiffness check. Drive shafts, pump shafts and machine-tool spindles use these checks. This chapter defines torsional rigidity and stiffness, compares solid and hollow shafts, and gives torsional strain energy.

You need: the torsion formula τ = T r / J and the polar second moment J from Chapter 6.1.

Angle of twist and torsional rigidity

A torque twists one end of a shaft relative to the other. For a circular shaft in the elastic range, the twist is uniform along the length.

θ = T L / (G J)

θ is angle of twist in radians; T is torque in N·m; L is length in m; G is shear modulus in Pa; J is polar second moment of area in m⁴.

Use SI units. Convert radians to degrees by multiplying by 180/π. The product G J is the torsional rigidity, in N·m². A larger G J gives a smaller θ. The torsional stiffness is k = G J / L, in N·m per radian; it is the torque for one radian of twist. For a solid shaft J = π d⁴ / 32, with d in m.

  1. Convert T, L, G and d to N·m, m, Pa and m.
  2. Compute J from the section.
  3. Substitute in θ = T L / (G J). The result is in radians.
  4. Multiply by 180/π for degrees.

Strength check and stiffness check

The maximum shear stress is at the outer surface: τmax = T r / J, where r is outer radius in m. For a solid shaft r = d/2. The strength condition is τmax ≤ τallow, where τallow is allowable shear stress in Pa. This prevents yielding or fracture.

Many shafts are limited by twist. Define q = θ/L, the twist per unit length in rad/m. The stiffness condition is q ≤ qallow, where qallow is allowable twist per unit length in rad/m. Stress and twist per unit length depend on T, G and J, not on length L. A shaft with small J, such as a thin or small-diameter section, can fail the stiffness check even when its stress is safe: q = T/(G J) can exceed qallow while τmax = T r / J stays below τallow. Both checks are required.

q = θ / L = T / (G J)

q is twist per unit length in rad/m; θ is angle of twist in rad; L is length in m; T is torque in N·m; G is shear modulus in Pa; J is polar second moment of area in m⁴.

The two checks use different limits. τallow comes from the material and factor of safety. qallow comes from the application: bearing clearance, gear alignment or seal life. A thick shaft with large J usually passes both. A thin shaft with small J often needs the stiffness check.

Hollow shafts, strain energy and design

For the same mass and length, a hollow shaft has material farther from the axis. Its J is larger than that of a solid shaft of equal area. Then G J is larger and the twist is smaller. Hollow shafts give more torsional stiffness for the same weight. Very thin walls can buckle, so practical designs keep a reasonable wall thickness.

For a hollow shaft, J = π(D⁴ − d⁴) / 32, where D is outer diameter and d is inner diameter, both in m. The ratio J to cross-sectional area rises as material moves outward.

U = T² L / (2 G J)

U is torsional strain energy in J; T is torque in N·m; L is length in m; G is shear modulus in Pa; J is polar second moment of area in m⁴.

U is the energy stored in the shaft as it twists, and it equals ½ T θ. Design uses both limits. From strength, J ≥ T r / τallow. From stiffness, q = T/(G J) ≤ qallow, so J ≥ T/(G qallow). Choose the larger J, then select the diameter.

Worked example

Question. A solid steel shaft of diameter 50 mm and length 2.0 m carries a torque of 1.2 kN·m. Take G = 80 GPa. Calculate the maximum shear stress, the angle of twist in radians and degrees, and the twist per metre.

  1. Convert: d = 0.050 m, r = 0.025 m, T = 1.2 kN·m = 1200 N·m, L = 2.0 m, G = 80 GPa = 80 × 10⁹ Pa.
  2. J = π d⁴ / 32 = π(0.050)⁴ / 32 = 6.136 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  3. τmax = T r / J = 1200 × 0.025 / (6.136 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.89 × 10⁷ Pa = 48.9 MPa.
  4. θ = T L / (G J) = 1200 × 2.0 / (80 × 10⁹ × 6.136 × 10⁻⁷) = 0.0489 rad.
  5. θ in degrees = 0.0489 × 180/π = 2.80°.
  6. q = θ / L = 0.0489 / 2.0 = 0.02445 rad/m.

Answer. τmax = 48.9 MPa, θ = 0.0489 rad = 2.80°, and q = 0.02445 rad/m.

Exam traps

  • Using θ in degrees inside θ = T L / (G J). The formula needs radians; convert only after the calculation.
  • Mixing G in GPa with T in N·m. Convert G to Pa (1 GPa = 10⁹ Pa) before substituting.
  • Using diameter in place of radius in τmax = T r / J. For a solid shaft r = d/2.
  • Using the strength check alone. q = T/(G J) can exceed qallow while τmax = T r / J is below τallow. Length L does not enter q or τmax.
  • Using J for a hollow shaft without subtracting the hole: J = π(D⁴ − d⁴) / 32.

6.3 Power transmitted by shafts

A shaft spinning at N revolutions per minute and carrying torque T delivers power. The same power can come from a high speed with small torque or a low speed with large torque. Motor shafts, car drive shafts and turbine shafts all use this relation. This chapter gives the power–torque–speed formula, then the design route: find the torque from power and speed, size the shaft for shear strength, and check that it does not twist too much.

You need: 6.1 for τ = 16T/(πd³) and 6.2 for the twist check.

Power, torque and angular speed

Power is the rate of doing work. A rotating shaft does work by turning against a torque. If the shaft turns faster, it delivers the same torque more often, so it delivers more power. The link is the angular speed ω, the angle turned per second in radians per second (rad/s). One full turn is 2π rad. A speed of N revolutions per minute is N/60 turns per second, so ω = 2πN/60 rad/s.

P = Tω = 2πNT/60

P is power in watts (W); T is torque in newton metres (N·m); ω is angular speed in rad/s; N is speed in revolutions per minute (rpm).

If power is given in kilowatts, convert to watts first. In kW form the same equation is P = 2πNT/60000, with P in kW, T in N·m and N in rpm. The factor 60000 comes from 60 seconds per minute and 1000 W per kW.

For a fixed power, torque and speed pull in opposite directions. A 10 kW shaft at 100 rpm carries about 955 N·m. The same 10 kW at 1000 rpm carries only about 95 N·m. Figure 1 shows this curve. Slow shafts are fat because they need high torque; fast shafts can be slender for the same power.

The design route for a shaft

A designer is usually given the power P and the speed N. The job is to find a safe diameter d. Start from the power equation and work towards the stress equation from 6.1.

  1. Convert P to watts and N to rad/s: ω = 2πN/60.
  2. Find the torque: T = P/ω.
  3. For a solid shaft, use τmax = 16T/(πd³). Set τmax equal to the allowable shear stress τallow and solve: d³ = 16T/(πτallow).
  4. Take the cube root to get d, then round up to the next standard shaft size.
  5. Check the twist with 6.2: θ/L = T/(GJ) must be within the allowable value. Use J = πd⁴/32 for a solid shaft.

A hollow shaft is used when saving weight matters, as in aircraft or long line shafts. Its polar second moment of area is J = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/32, with D the outer diameter and d the inner diameter. Use τ = Tr/J with r = D/2 at the outer surface. The strength design may give a thin wall, but the stiffness check often decides the final wall thickness. Some syllabi call this power transmission by circular shafts.

Speed, torque and the whole machine

A motor nameplate gives power and speed together: for example, 15 kW at 1440 rpm. The torque at the motor shaft follows from these two numbers. A gearbox or belt drive then changes speed and torque. In an ideal drive the product Tω stays constant, so reducing speed raises torque in the same ratio. Real drives lose a few percent to friction.

The same power can be carried at different speeds, but the shaft size changes. If strength governs, d grows as T^(1/3), and T grows as 1/N. A shaft at one third the speed needs about 1.44 times the diameter for the same power. If stiffness governs, a long shaft may need to be larger still, because twist grows with length.

When two shafts are joined in series, the same torque passes through both and the twists add. When they are joined in parallel, the torques add and both twist by the same angle. That is covered in 6.4. Belt and gear drives themselves are machine-design topics; here we only need the power, torque and speed at the shaft.

Worked example

Question. An electric motor delivers 15 kW at 1440 rpm to a solid steel shaft. The allowable shear stress is 40 MPa. Find the minimum diameter. Choose a standard size from 20, 25, 30 and 35 mm.

  1. Angular speed: ω = 2πN/60 = 2π × 1440/60 = 150.8 rad/s.
  2. Torque: T = P/ω = 15000/150.8 = 99.5 N·m.
  3. Strength limit for a solid shaft: τmax = 16T/(πd³). Rearrange: d³ = 16T/(πτallow).
  4. Substitute: d³ = 16 × 99.5/(π × 40 × 10⁶) = 1.27 × 10⁻⁵ m³.
  5. Cube root: d = (1.27 × 10⁻⁵)^(1/3) = 0.0233 m = 23.3 mm.
  6. The next standard size above 23.3 mm is 25 mm, so choose a 25 mm shaft.

Answer. The minimum diameter is 23.3 mm, so use a standard 25 mm shaft.

Exam traps

  • Feeding rpm straight into P = Tω. Convert speed to rad/s first: ω = 2πN/60, with N in rpm.
  • Using kilowatts in P = Tω. Convert 15 kW to 15000 W before dividing by ω.
  • Mixing diameter and radius. The solid-shaft form τ = 16T/(πd³) uses the diameter throughout; J = πd⁴/32 also uses the diameter.
  • Rounding 23.3 mm down to 20 mm. A smaller shaft has higher shear stress, so always round up to the next standard size.
  • Passing the strength check and stopping. A long shaft can be strong enough yet twist too much; check θ/L = T/(GJ) from 6.2.

6.4 Shafts in series and parallel; tapered shafts

A shaft rarely works alone. A gearbox output may drive a long line shaft, two motors may share one load, and a propeller shaft may taper from one end to the other. Each case asks a different question. In series, the torque is common and the twists add. In parallel, the twist is common and the torques share in proportion to stiffness. A tapered shaft keeps the torque but changes stress along its length. These ideas size shafts for machines, vehicles and pumps without guessing.

You need: 6.1 for J and τ, and 6.2 for θ = TL/(GJ).

Series: one torque, twists add

Torsional stiffness k = GJ/L measures how much torque a shaft needs for one radian of twist. G is the shear modulus in Pa, J is the polar second moment of area in m⁴, and L is the length in m. A series arrangement has two or more shafts joined end to end, like a gearbox output shaft bolted to a propeller shaft. If no torque is added or removed at a joint, every segment carries the same torque T. Each segment twists by its own θᵢ = T Lᵢ/(Gᵢ Jᵢ). The total twist is the sum: θ_total = Σ T Lᵢ/(Gᵢ Jᵢ). In stiffness form, 1/k_eq = Σ 1/kᵢ. A long thin segment dominates because it has low k.

θ_total = Σ T Lᵢ/(Gᵢ Jᵢ), 1/k_eq = Σ 1/kᵢ

T is the common torque in N·m; Lᵢ, Gᵢ and Jᵢ are length, shear modulus and polar second moment of segment i; kᵢ = GᵢJᵢ/Lᵢ is its torsional stiffness in N·m/rad; θ_total is in radians.

Parallel: one twist, torques share

Parallel shafts are connected to the same rigid plate or gear at both ends. The plate forces both shafts to twist by the same angle θ. Each shaft then carries Tᵢ = Gᵢ Jᵢ θ / Lᵢ = kᵢ θ. The total torque is the sum: T_total = Σ Tᵢ = θ Σ kᵢ, so k_eq = Σ kᵢ. The stiffer shaft takes the larger share: Tᵢ/Tⱼ = kᵢ/kⱼ. A solid shaft and a hollow shaft of the same outside diameter share torque in proportion to their J values; the hollow one is softer and takes less. This arrangement is statically indeterminate: equilibrium alone cannot find the individual torques. The compatibility condition is equal twist. Some syllabi call this a composite shaft. Use parallel thinking for two motors driving one load through a common gear, or a shaft inside a sleeve keyed at both ends.

Tᵢ = kᵢ θ, T_total = Σ Tᵢ, k_eq = Σ kᵢ

θ is the common angle of twist in radians; kᵢ = GᵢJᵢ/Lᵢ is the torsional stiffness of shaft i in N·m/rad; Tᵢ is the torque carried by shaft i in N·m; T_total is the sum of all torques in N·m.

Tapered shaft: constant torque, changing stress

A tapered shaft has a diameter that changes along its length. Torque T stays constant if nothing is attached along the span, but the polar second moment changes: J(x) = π[d(x)]⁴/32. The twist is the sum of small twists: θ = ∫₀ᴸ T dx/(G J(x)). For a linear taper from d₁ at one end to d₂ at the other, the integral gives θ = (32TL)/(3πG) × (d₁² + d₁d₂ + d₂²)/(d₁³ d₂³). Here d₁ and d₂ are in metres, L in metres, T in N·m, G in Pa, and θ in radians. Shear stress is τ(x) = 16T/(π[d(x)]³), so it is largest at the smaller diameter: τmax = 16T/(π dmin³). The smaller end is the critical section for strength. A tapered shaft is not solved by using an average diameter; the d⁴ term makes that wrong. It is used where weight must fall towards an end, such as a propeller shaft with a tapered coupling or a machine spindle that is thick at the bearing and thin at the nose.

θ = (32 T L)/(3 π G) × (d₁² + d₁ d₂ + d₂²)/(d₁³ d₂³)

θ is the total twist in radians; T is torque in N·m; L is length in m; G is shear modulus in Pa; d₁ and d₂ are the end diameters in m.

τmax = 16 T/(π dmin³)

τmax is the maximum shear stress in Pa; T is torque in N·m; dmin is the smaller end diameter in m.

Worked example

Question. Two solid circular shafts are joined end to end in series and carry a torque T = 800 N·m. Shaft A has diameter 50 mm and length 1.0 m. Shaft B has diameter 40 mm and length 0.8 m. Both are steel with G = 80 GPa. Find the angle of twist of the free end relative to the fixed end, in radians and degrees.

  1. Convert: dA = 0.050 m, dB = 0.040 m, LA = 1.0 m, LB = 0.8 m, G = 80 × 10⁹ Pa. The torque is common: T = 800 N·m.
  2. JA = π(0.050)⁴/32 = 6.136 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  3. JB = π(0.040)⁴/32 = 2.513 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  4. θA = T LA/(G JA) = 800 × 1.0/(80 × 10⁹ × 6.136 × 10⁻⁷) = 0.0163 rad.
  5. θB = T LB/(G JB) = 800 × 0.8/(80 × 10⁹ × 2.513 × 10⁻⁷) = 0.0318 rad.
  6. θtotal = θA + θB = 0.0163 + 0.0318 = 0.0481 rad. In degrees: 0.0481 × 180/π = 2.76°.

Answer. The total angle of twist is 0.0481 rad, or 2.76°.

Exam traps

  • In series, the torque is common and the twists add. In parallel, the twist is common and the torques add. Swapping these is the most common mistake.
  • Do not use one J for two shafts of different diameter. J depends on d⁴, so a 20% smaller diameter cuts J by about 59%.
  • Convert mm to m and GPa to Pa before using θ = TL/(GJ). Putting d in mm makes J 10¹² times too large (θ 10¹² times too small); putting G in GPa makes the denominator 10⁹ times too small (θ 10⁹ times too large). Doing both at once still leaves the answer 1000 times wrong.
  • For a tapered shaft, use the integral or the linear-taper formula for twist. For stress use the smallest diameter, not the average.
  • Torsional stiffness is k = GJ/L, not GJ. In series add 1/k; in parallel add k.

6.5 Combined bending and torsion

A shaft often carries bending and torsion at the same time. A pulley puts a sideways load and a gear puts torque; the shaft surface then has normal stress σ from bending and shear stress τ from torsion. Neither stress alone tells you if the shaft is safe. Combine them into a principal stress, or into equivalent bending moment and equivalent torque, and design the diameter. This is the standard check for line shafts, axle shafts and machine spindles.

You need: torsion shear stress from 6.1, strength and stiffness from 6.2, bending stress from Unit 4, and principal stresses from Unit 2.

Bending stress and torsional shear on the same fibre

A line shaft between a motor and a machine carries torque T because it transmits power. The same shaft bends under pulley weights, belt pulls and gear tooth loads, so it also carries bending moment M. At a section where both are largest, the outer fibre is the dangerous point: bending gives a normal stress σ along the shaft axis, torsion gives a shear stress τ on the cross-section.

σ = 32M/(πd³)
τ = 16T/(πd³)

M is bending moment in N·m; T is torque in N·m; d is solid shaft diameter in m; σ and τ are in Pa. The factors 32 and 16 are not interchangeable.

The bending result comes from σ = My/I with I = πd⁴/64 and y = d/2. The torsion result comes from τ = Tr/J with J = πd⁴/32 and r = d/2. Both stresses are maximum at the outer surface and zero at the centre for torsion.

For a hollow shaft, let D be the outside diameter, d the inside diameter and k = d/D. Replace d³ by D³(1 − k⁴). Then σ = 32M/[πD³(1 − k⁴)] and τ = 16T/[πD³(1 − k⁴)]. Use metres throughout; a diameter given in millimetres must be divided by 1000.

On the tension side σ is tensile; on the compression side it is compressive. The shear stress direction reverses around the section. For magnitude, take σ as positive on the tensile side. If the shaft also carries axial force, add that normal stress separately to σ before combining.

Principal stress and equivalent moments

The surface element is in plane stress with normal stress σ and shear stress τ. Unit 2 gives the principal stresses and the in-plane maximum shear stress.

σ₁,₂ = σ/2 ± ½√(σ² + 4τ²)
τmax = ½√(σ² + 4τ²)

σ₁ is the algebraically larger principal stress; σ₂ is the smaller; τmax is the maximum shear stress; σ and τ are the bending and torsion stresses in Pa. The factor 4 belongs to τ².

Substituting σ and τ for a solid shaft gives two equivalent quantities. The equivalent bending moment Me is the bending moment that alone would produce the same σ₁. The equivalent torque Te is the torque that alone would produce the same τmax.

Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)]
Te = √(M² + T²)

Me is equivalent bending moment in N·m; Te is equivalent torque in N·m; M is bending moment in N·m; T is torque in N·m. For a solid shaft, σ₁ = 32Me/(πd³) and τmax = 16Te/(πd³).

Design the shaft with d³ ≥ 32Me/(πσallow) if normal stress controls, or d³ ≥ 16Te/(πτallow) if shear stress controls. Here σallow is the allowable bending stress in Pa and τallow is the allowable shear stress in Pa. Use the larger diameter. For a hollow shaft, put D³(1 − k⁴) in place of d³. Some syllabi call this combined bending and torsion of shafts; the failure theories themselves belong to Unit 2.

  1. Find the critical section and the M and T acting there. Convert M and T to N·m.
  2. Compute Me and Te, or compute σ and τ first.
  3. Apply the allowable stress and solve for d, or for D with a chosen k.
  4. Check the angle of twist separately using 6.2; a shaft can pass stress but fail stiffness.

6.6 Close-coiled and open-coiled helical springs

A helical spring is a wire wound into a helix. Under an axial load W, a close-coiled spring mainly twists its wire, so it is compact and stiff. An open-coiled spring has a larger helix angle; the load also bends the wire, so it is softer. You size springs for deflection and shear stress in vehicle suspensions, engine valves, pens, door closers and weighing scales.

You need: torsion and twist from 6.1 and 6.2; shear modulus and strain energy from Unit 1.

Close-coiled spring: torsion only

Let D be the mean coil diameter (m), d the wire diameter (m), n the number of active coils, G the shear modulus (Pa), and W the axial load (N). A close-coiled spring has a small helix angle and D much larger than d. Each coil then behaves like a short circular shaft in torsion. The load acts at radius R = D/2, so the torque on the wire is T = W R = W D/2.

T = W D ÷ 2

T is torque in the wire (N·m); W is axial load (N); D is mean coil diameter (m).

Use the twist result from 6.2. The wire length is L = π D n and its polar second moment is J = π d⁴/32. The total twist over all active coils is θ = T L/(G J), with L = π D n. The axial deflection is then δ = R θ. Substitution gives the spring deflection.

δ = 8 W D³ n ÷ (G d⁴)

δ is axial deflection (m); n is number of active coils; G is shear modulus (Pa); d is wire diameter (m).

k = W ÷ δ = G d⁴ ÷ (8 D³ n)

k is spring stiffness (N/m).

The shear stress at the wire surface from torsion alone is τ = T r/J. With r = d/2 this becomes τ = 8 W D/(π d³). This value ignores direct shear. A simple correction is τmax = (8 W D/(π d³))(1 + d/(2D)). Where your syllabus asks, use the Wahl factor K_w = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C, with C = D/d, and τmax = K_w 8 W D/(π d³).

Open-coiled spring: torsion plus bending

An open-coiled spring has a helix angle α measured from the plane perpendicular to the spring axis. This α is large, so the axial load is not carried by torsion alone. Resolve W at the wire into two parts. The component perpendicular to the wire axis produces torsion T = W R cos α. The component along the wire axis produces bending M = W R sin α. At α = 0 the load is carried by torsion only.

T = W R cos α

T is torque (N·m); R = D/2 is mean coil radius (m); α is helix angle measured from the plane perpendicular to the axis.

M = W R sin α

M is bending moment in the wire (N·m).

Bending adds deflection, so an open-coiled spring is softer than a close-coiled spring with the same D, d and n. The axial deflection is:

δ = (8 W D³ n ÷ (G d⁴)) [cos² α + (2G/E) sin² α] sec α

E is Young's modulus (Pa); sec α = 1/cos α. At α = 0 this reduces to the close-coiled formula.

Do not use the close-coiled formula when α is large. The wire now has both shear stress from torsion and normal stress from bending. The bending stress is σ = 32 M/(π d³) = 32 W R sin α/(π d³). A full check combines these stresses at the critical point; see Unit 2 for principal stresses.

Stiffness, energy and springs in series or parallel

The spring stiffness k = W/δ measures load per unit deflection (N/m). For a close-coiled spring k = G d⁴/(8 D³ n). A spring with more active coils is softer: k falls as 1/n. Use the active coils that actually deform. End coils that only hold the spring do not count.

U = ½ W δ = ½ k δ²

U is strain energy stored (J); δ is axial deflection (m).

Two springs in series carry the same load and their deflections add. Their combined stiffness satisfies 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂. Two springs in parallel have the same deflection and share the load. Their combined stiffness is k = k₁ + k₂. This is the same logic as shafts in series and parallel in 6.4.

  • Series: same W, add δ; 1/k = Σ 1/k_i.
  • Parallel: same δ, add W; k = Σ k_i.

6.7 Leaf springs

A leaf spring is a stack of curved steel strips, clamped at the centre and joined at the eyes. The full-length master leaf and shorter graduated leaves act as cantilevers, so a central load bends the stack. Heavy trucks and railway wagons use semi-elliptic leaf springs because one compact member carries a large load while keeping the bending stress low. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need Unit 4 for cantilever bending and 6.6 for spring stiffness ideas.

The semi-elliptic leaf spring

A semi-elliptic leaf spring, also called a laminated spring, has n steel leaves of width b and thickness t. The master leaf is longest and ends in eyes. Every other leaf is shorter, forming a graduated stack. A central clamp holds the leaves, and load W acts at the centre. L is the distance between eye centres.

Treat the spring as two cantilevers of length L/2. Each carries W/2 at the eye and is fixed at the clamp. Bending moment is largest at the clamp and zero at the eyes. Shorter leaves sit near the eyes, where moment is small, so less section modulus is needed. The graduated shape keeps stress closer to uniform and saves steel.

The leaves are often curved differently before assembly. Clamping them together, called nipping, leaves a pre-stress that keeps them together under light load.

Stress and deflection

At the clamp, each cantilever of length L/2 carries W/2, so M = (W/2)(L/2) = WL/4. For n leaves, I = nbt³/12 and y = t/2, so Z = I/y = nbt²/6.

σ = M/Z = 3WL/(2nbt²)

σ is maximum bending stress in Pa; W is central load in N; L is eye-to-eye length in m; n is number of leaves; b is width in m; t is thickness in m.

δ = 3WL³/(8nEbt³)

δ is central deflection in m; E is Young's modulus in Pa; other symbols are as above. This is for graduated leaves; it equals the deflection of 2n/3 full-length leaves.

The graduated formula assumes all leaves have the same b and t, no friction between leaves, equal load sharing, and an initially straight spring. It is equivalent to 2n/3 full-length leaves. If all n leaves were full length, the deflection would be 2/3 of this value, δ = WL³/(4nEbt³); graduation makes the spring 1.5 times more flexible.

Spring stiffness is k = W/δ = 8nEbt³/(3L³) ≈ 2.67nEbt³/L³, with k in N/m.

Worked example

Question. A semi-elliptic leaf spring has total central load W = 6.0 kN, length L = 1.0 m between eyes, n = 8 leaves, width b = 60 mm, thickness t = 8 mm, and E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum bending stress and the central deflection.

  1. Convert: W = 6000 N, L = 1.0 m, b = 0.060 m, t = 0.008 m, E = 200 × 10⁹ Pa.
  2. Z = nbt²/6 = 8 × 0.060 × 0.008² / 6 = 5.12 × 10⁻⁶ m³. M = WL/4 = 6000 × 1.0 / 4 = 1500 N m. σ = M/Z = 1500 / 5.12 × 10⁻⁶ = 292 968 750 Pa = 293 MPa.
  3. δ = 3WL³/(8nEbt³) = 3 × 6000 × 1.0³ / (8 × 8 × 200 × 10⁹ × 0.060 × 0.008³) = 18000 / 393216 = 0.0458 m.

Answer. Maximum bending stress is 293 MPa and central deflection is 45.8 mm.

  • Forgetting that n leaves share the load: n appears in both stress and deflection denominators.
  • Mixing t² in stress with t³ in deflection, or forgetting to convert mm to m and GPa to Pa.
  • Using a load per side when the question gives total central load W. Define symbols before substituting.

Principal stresses in a shaftσ1,2 = σ/2 ± √((σ/2)² + τ²), with σ = 32M/(πd³) and τ = 16T/(πd³).6.5
Close-coiled helical springHelix angle small (under about 10°); axial load mainly twists the wire, and δ = 8WD³n/(Gd⁴).6.6
Open-coiled helical springHelix angle large; the load both twists and bends the wire, so the spring is softer than a close-coiled one of the same size.6.6
Spring indexC = D/d, the ratio of mean coil diameter to wire diameter; a small C means high curvature stress.6.6
Wahl factorK = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C, which corrects the simple shear stress for wire curvature and direct shear: τmax = K·8WD/(πd³).6.6
Leaf springA stack of graduated cantilever strips clamped at the centre; load is shared, giving σ = 6WL/(nbt²) and δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³).6.7
Graduated leavesProgressively shorter plates in a leaf spring that keep bending stress nearly uniform along the length.6.7
Springs in series and parallelSeries: same load, 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂. Parallel: same deflection, k = k₁ + k₂.6.6

Takeaways

  1. A circular shaft under torque has shear stress that grows linearly from zero at the centre to a maximum at the surface, so material near the axis does little work.
  2. The torsion equation T/J = τ/r = Gθ/L is one equation with three faces: stress, stiffness and twist; the same problem can be asked from any of them.
  3. Design is a two-gate check: the shaft must pass the stress limit (strength) and the twist-per-metre limit (stiffness), and either gate can decide the diameter.
  4. Power depends on torque times speed, so a shaft at high rpm carries the same power with much less torque and a smaller diameter.
  5. Shafts in series share torque and add twist; shafts in parallel share twist and divide torque in proportion to GJ/L.
  6. A tapered shaft keeps the torque constant but pushes the maximum stress to the small end, and its twist needs a logarithmic-type formula, not θ = TL/(GJ).
  7. Bending and torsion together create a plane-stress state on the surface element, and equivalent torque or equivalent bending moment converts it back into a simple design sum.
  8. A close-coiled spring stores energy almost purely in torsion of its wire, which is why its stiffness is Gd⁴/(8D³n) and it is far softer than a straight bar of the same wire.
  9. A leaf spring spreads bending stress along its length by using graduated plates, giving a compact, damped spring for road vehicles.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to derive the torsion equation from its assumptions, separate the strength check from the stiffness check, and finish a full numerical design of a shaft, helical spring or leaf spring with every quantity carrying units.

Remember and understand

Remember State the assumptions of the simple theory of torsion for circular shafts and derive the torsion equation T/J = τ/r = Gθ/L. Draw the shear stress distribution across a solid section and across a hollow section. 10 marks

Plane sections remain plane and circular after twisting; Radial lines stay straight, so shear strain varies linearly with radius; Material is homogeneous, elastic and obeys Hooke's law, so τ = Gγ; Torque equals the moment of the shear force about the axis: T = ∫τ·r dA; Substitute τ = τmax·r/R and identify J = ∫r² dA = πd⁴/32; Combine with the twist relation to get the three-part torsion equation; Sketch: straight line from zero at centre to τmax at surface; hollow shaft has a minimum value at the inner radius, not zero.

Understand Define torsional rigidity and torsional stiffness. Explain, with the two design conditions, how a shaft that is safe in strength may still be rejected. 10 marks

Torsional rigidity = GJ; torsional stiffness = GJ/L; Strength condition τmax = Tr/J ≤ τallow; Stiffness condition θ/L = T/(GJ) ≤ allowable twist per metre; Long slender shaft: stress small but twist per metre large, so strength passes and stiffness fails; Short thick shaft: stiffness passes easily but stress may exceed the limit; Practical allowable twist often quoted in degrees per metre; state the unit conversion.

Understand Describe close-coiled and open-coiled helical springs. Explain why the open-coiled spring is softer, and derive the deflection of a close-coiled spring under axial load W. 12 marks

Close-coiled: helix angle small (a few degrees), coils nearly in one plane; Open-coiled: helix angle large, load resolved into twist and bending of the wire; Close-coiled: load W acts at mean radius R = D/2, so wire carries torque T = WR and negligible bending; Strain energy U = T²L/(2GJ) with wire length L = 2πRn and J = πd⁴/32; δ = ∂U/∂W gives δ = 8WD³n/(Gd⁴); Stiffness k = Gd⁴/(8D³n) = Gd/(8C³n), C = D/d; Open-coiled spring has an extra bending term with E, so it deflects more for the same load.

Remember Explain the construction of a laminated leaf spring. What is the purpose of the graduated leaves and the master leaf? Write the expressions for maximum bending stress and central deflection. 10 marks

Stack of plates of decreasing length, clamped at the centre by a U-bolt; Master leaf is the longest, carries the eyes for mounting; Graduated leaves shorten progressively so each section has about the same stress; Ideal spring behaves like a plate of uniform strength; bending moment WL at the centre; σ = 6WL/(nbt²) with L the half span, b width, t thickness, n number of leaves; δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³) = σL²/(Et); Rebound clips, nipping and initial curvature mentioned briefly.

Apply

Apply A solid steel shaft of 60 mm diameter runs at 300 rpm with an allowable shear stress of 60 MPa. Find the torque and the power it can transmit. Then find the power carried by a hollow shaft of the same outside diameter and inner-to-outer diameter ratio 0.6 at the same stress and speed. 13 marks

T = τallow·πd³/16 for the solid shaft; Substitute d = 0.06 m and τ = 60 × 10⁶ Pa to get about 2.54 kN·m; P = 2πNT/60 with N = 300 rpm gives about 80 kW; Hollow shaft: T = τπ(D⁴ − d⁴)/(16D) with d = 0.6D; Ratio of torque capacities = (1 − 0.6⁴)/(1) compared with solid; state the percentage gain; Comment: same outside diameter, less material, more torque.

Apply A tapered shaft 1.5 m long has end diameters 80 mm and 40 mm and carries a torque of 1.2 kN·m. Take G = 80 GPa. Find the maximum shear stress, locate where it occurs, and find the angle of twist. 12 marks

Torque is the same at every section; τ = 16T/(πd³) is largest where d is smallest, that is at the 40 mm end; Compute τmax with d = 0.04 m; Twist for a linear taper: θ = (32TL)/(3πG) × (d₁² + d₁d₂ + d₂²)/(d₁³d₂³); Substitute d₁ = 0.08 m, d₂ = 0.04 m, L = 1.5 m, G = 80 × 10⁹ Pa; Answer in radians, then convert to degrees; Note that θ = TL/(GJ) with an average J would be wrong here.

Apply A shaft is subjected to a bending moment of 3 kN·m and a torque of 5 kN·m together. The allowable shear stress is 45 MPa and the allowable tensile stress is 80 MPa. Choose a suitable diameter using the maximum shear stress theory and check it by the maximum principal stress theory. 13 marks

Equivalent torque Te = √(M² + T²); Diameter from d³ = 16Te/(πτallow); Equivalent bending moment Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)]; Diameter from d³ = 32Me/(πσallow); Take the larger of the two diameters; Check the resulting maximum principal stress against 80 MPa; State that the shaft also carries an axial shear from bending, but it is zero at the surface point of maximum bending stress.

Apply Design a close-coiled helical spring of spring index 6 to absorb 500 N with a deflection of 40 mm. Take G = 80 GPa and allowable shear stress 350 MPa. Using the Wahl factor, find the wire diameter, the mean coil diameter and the number of active coils. 12 marks

C = D/d = 6, so D = 6d; Wahl factor K = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C; Stress: τmax = K·8WD/(πd³) = K·8W·6/(πd²) solve for d; Round d to a standard wire size and keep D = 6d; Deflection δ = 8WD³n/(Gd⁴) solve for n; Stiffness k = W/δ = Gd⁴/(8D³n) as a check; State that the ends contribute inactive coils.

Apply A leaf spring for a light vehicle has a span of 1.0 m, width 60 mm, and 10 leaves each 8 mm thick. It carries a central load of 4 kN. Find the maximum bending stress and the central deflection. Take E = 200 GPa. 12 marks

Half span L = 0.5 m, width b = 0.06 m, thickness t = 0.008 m, n = 10; σ = 6WL/(nbt²); Substitute and get the stress in MPa; δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³); Check with δ = σL²/(Et); Comment on whether the stress is acceptable for spring steel.

Apply A shaft ABC of length 2 m is fixed at both ends and carries a torque of 6 kN·m at the junction B, 0.8 m from A. The two portions are of the same material and diameter. Find the reacting torques at A and C and the angle of twist at B. 12 marks

Shafts in parallel: the twist at B is the same for both portions; T_A·L_A/(GJ) = T_C·L_C/(GJ); Equilibrium: T_A + T_C = 6 kN·m; Solve the two equations; the shorter portion takes the larger torque; θ_B = T_A L_A/(GJ) with the given d and G; State that the twist is measured from a fixed end and both give the same value.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A hollow shaft and a solid shaft are made of the same material and have the same mass per metre. Compare the torque they can carry at the same maximum shear stress. Evaluate the result for an inner-to-outer diameter ratio of 0.5 and comment on the practical limits of using very thin hollow shafts. 15 marks

Same mass per metre means equal cross-sectional area; Solid: A = πd²/4, T_s = τπd³/16; Hollow: A = π(D² − d²)/4, T_h = τπ(D⁴ − d⁴)/(16D); Express T_h/T_s in terms of the diameter ratio k = d/D; For k = 0.5 the hollow shaft carries roughly 1.3 times the torque; Physics: material near the axis contributes little, so removing it costs little strength; Limits: thin wall buckles and dents, poor fatigue and impact behaviour, difficult joints, larger outside diameter.

Analyze Two shafts of the same material and length are connected (i) end to end and (ii) side by side with both ends fixed. Explain how torque and twist distribute in each case, using a gearbox and a machine-tool spindle as examples. 13 marks

Series: same torque passes through both, twists add, weaker section governs stress; Effective 1/(GJ) = Σ Lᵢ/(GᵢJᵢ); stiffness drops when a thin section is added; Parallel: same twist, torque divides as G₁J₁/L₁ : G₂J₂/L₂; Stiffer member attracts more torque — a design warning, not an accident; Series example: motor shaft, coupling, gearbox input shaft; Parallel example: a shaft keyed inside a sleeve, or a two-support spindle; Failure of one member in series stops power flow; in parallel the other overloads.

Analyze A pump shaft fails repeatedly at a keyway close to the bearing, although the nominal shear stress from torque alone is well below the allowable value. Diagnose the likely causes and explain how you would redesign the shaft using combined bending and torsion. 16 marks

Nominal τ = 16T/(πd³) ignores bending from belt pull, misalignment and rotor weight; Surface element then has σ = 32M/(πd³) and τ together: plane stress; Principal stresses σ1,2 = σ/2 ± √((σ/2)² + τ²); maximum shear = √((σ/2)² + τ²); Keyway gives stress concentration, so use a higher factor on the nominal stress; Fatigue: reversed bending with steady torque needs a fatigue-based allowable stress; Redesign: increase diameter at the keyway, use a larger fillet, splines or a key with rounded ends; Check both strength and twist per metre after resizing.

Evaluate Compare a close-coiled helical spring and a laminated leaf spring as suspension elements for a road vehicle, on stiffness, space, stress distribution, damping and failure behaviour. Which would you choose for a two-wheeler and which for a truck? Justify. 15 marks

Coil spring: stiffness Gd⁴/(8D³n), stress uniform along the wire, compact, no natural damping, fails by fatigue crack and drops load; Leaf spring: stress spread along the graduated plates, also locates the axle and provides interleaf friction damping; Coil spring needs separate links and a damper; leaf spring is heavy but simple; Truck: leaf spring for load capacity, spread of stress and built-in location; Two-wheeler: coil spring with a damper for low unsprung mass and ride comfort; Both checked for strength and for stiffness (ride frequency), not strength alone.

Glossary

Angle of twist
The rotation of one end of a shaft relative to the other, in radians, given by θ = TL/(GJ).
Close-coiled spring
A helical spring with a small helix angle, in which the axial load chiefly produces torsion of the wire.
Equivalent bending moment
Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)], the bending moment that alone produces the same maximum principal stress as M and T together.
Equivalent torque
Te = √(M² + T²), the torque that alone produces the same maximum shear stress as the bending moment M and torque T acting together.
Helix angle
The angle between the wire centre line and the plane perpendicular to the axis of the spring.
Leaf spring
A spring made of a stack of flat graduated strips clamped at the centre, loaded at the ends or at the centre.
Mean coil diameter
D, the diameter of the circle through the centres of the wire cross-sections, equal to outside diameter minus wire diameter.
Modulus of rigidity
G = τ/γ, the shear counterpart of Young's modulus; about 80 GPa for steel.
Nipping
The initial curvature given to the leaves of a spring so that they fit together and share load from the start.
Open-coiled spring
A helical spring with a large helix angle, in which the axial load produces both torsion and bending of the wire.
Polar second moment of area
The quantity J = ∫r² dA over the cross-section, equal to πd⁴/32 for a solid circle and π(D⁴ − d⁴)/32 for a hollow circle.
Principal stress
A normal stress on a plane where the shear stress is zero; in a shaft under M and T these are σ1 and σ2 in the surface element.
Shaft in parallel
Shafts connected so that they twist by the same amount and share the applied torque in proportion to GJ/L.
Shaft in series
Shafts connected end to end so that the same torque passes through each and the total twist is the sum of the parts.
Shear strain
The angle, in radians, through which a right angle of an element distorts under shear stress.
Shear stress
The stress acting parallel to a face of an element; in a shaft it acts on the cross-section in the tangential direction.
Spring index
The ratio C = D/d of mean coil diameter to wire diameter.
Stiffness condition
The requirement that the twist per unit length does not exceed the value allowed for smooth running of the machine.
Strain energy in torsion
The energy stored in a twisted shaft, U = T²L/(2GJ), equal to τmax²/(4G) per unit volume for a solid shaft.
Strength condition
The requirement that the maximum shear stress in the shaft does not exceed the allowable shear stress.
Tapered shaft
A shaft whose diameter changes linearly along its length, so that stress is highest and twist is concentrated near the small end.
Torque
The twisting moment of a couple about the axis of a shaft, measured in N·m.
Torsional rigidity
The product GJ of a shaft section, the resistance of the section to twisting.
Torsional stiffness
Torque per unit angle of twist for a shaft of given length, equal to GJ/L.
Wahl factor
A factor greater than 1 that raises the simple shear stress in a spring wire to allow for wire curvature and direct shear.
