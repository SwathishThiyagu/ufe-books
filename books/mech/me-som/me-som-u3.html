3.1 Beams: types of supports and loads

A beam is a bar that carries loads sideways on its axis, so it bends. Before drawing a shear force diagram, replace the real bridge, slab or machine part by a model: a line for the beam, standard supports, standard loads. The model gives the number of unknown reactions and shows whether the three equilibrium equations can find them.

You need: Force, moment of a force about a point, and equilibrium of a rigid body from first-year mechanics.

Beam, span and what the model assumes

A beam is a bar whose length far exceeds its cross-section, carrying loads transverse — sideways — to its axis. Slabs, girders, rails, axles and machine beds are analysed as beams.

The span is the distance between two supports; on a cantilever, from the support to the free end. The effective span is measured between bearing centres: a 5 m clear gap with 200 mm bearings each side gives 5.2 m.

Five assumptions turn the real member into a line model:

  • The beam is its own straight axis; thickness and shape matter later, for stress (see Unit 4, Bending and Shear Stresses in Beams).
  • All loads act in one plane through the axis, so the beam bends in that plane only.
  • Deflections are small compared with the span.
  • Plane sections stay plane after bending.
  • Self weight is neglected or added as a uniformly distributed load.

Supports: count the movements they stop

A support stops some movements of the beam end and allows the rest. Each stopped movement brings a reaction, so count the movements prevented to get the number of unknowns.

Pin (hinge). Stops sliding in any direction in the plane, allows rotation. Two reactions: H horizontal and V vertical, in kN. A door hinge behaves like this.

Roller. Stops movement normal (perpendicular) to its surface; allows sliding along it and rotation. One reaction V, normal to the surface. On a slope it is not vertical, so resolve it into parts.

Fixed (built-in). Stops translation and rotation. Three reactions: H, V and a moment M in kN·m. A steel beam cast into a concrete wall is the usual picture.

Watch the words: some syllabi say simple support for a roller or knife edge, and simply supported beam for a pin at one end and a roller at the other. Read the term from the sketch.

Loads and their units

A point load is a force at one section, in kN. In reality it acts over a small area — a wheel on a rail — small against the span, so we draw an arrow.

A uniformly distributed load (UDL) has intensity w in kN/m over length L. Its total acts at the middle of the loaded length, not the middle of the whole beam when the load covers part of the span. Self weight is a UDL: w = ρ A g / 1000 in kN/m, with ρ the density in kg/m³, A the cross-section area in m² and g = 9.81 m/s².

W = w × L, x̄ = L / 2

W is the total of a UDL in kN; w is its intensity in kN/m; L is the loaded length in m; x̄ is the distance from the start of the load to the line of action of W, in m.

A uniformly varying load (UVL) rises linearly from zero to w over length L. Its total is ½ w L, acting at 2L/3 from the zero end — the centroid of a triangle. Split a trapezoidal load into a rectangle plus a triangle.

An applied couple is a pure moment M in kN·m — a shaft keyed into a beam. An eccentric load becomes a concentric force plus a couple; only the couple part is the applied moment.

Units carry the meaning: kN per metre of length, not per square metre. A slab pressure p in kN/m² on a strip b metres wide becomes w = p b kN/m.

Beam types and determinacy

  • Cantilever: fixed at one end, free at the other.
  • Simply supported: pin at one end, roller at the other.
  • Overhanging: two supports, the beam running past one or both (see 3.4).
  • Fixed-fixed: built in at both ends.
  • Propped cantilever: fixed at one end, a roller prop at the other.
  • Continuous: more than two supports, such as a pipeline over several piers.

A plane beam gives three equilibrium equations: ΣH = 0, ΣV = 0 and ΣM = 0. Count the unknown reactions r. If r = 3 the beam is statically determinate — equilibrium alone finds every reaction. If r > 3 it is statically indeterminate of degree r − 3, and compatibility of deflections is needed (see Unit 5, Deflection of Beams and Energy Methods).

Quick count: simply supported 2 + 1 = 3; propped cantilever 4, degree 1; fixed-fixed 6, degree 3. A cantilever's single fixed end gives three unknowns — exactly three equations, so determinate.

Sign convention: sagging is positive. A bending moment that curves the beam concave up — tension at the bottom fibre — is positive; hogging, concave down, is negative.

Worked example

Question. A beam AB is 6 m long, pinned at A and on a roller at B at the right end. It carries a 20 kN point load 2 m from A and a uniformly distributed load of 5 kN/m over the whole span. (a) Name the beam. (b) List every unknown reaction. (c) State the total of the UDL and where it acts. (d) Say whether the beam is statically determinate.

  1. Pin at A gives HA and VA; the roller at B gives VB, vertical. Unknowns r = 2 + 1 = 3.
  2. One pin and one roller at the ends, span 6 m, no overhang: a simply supported beam.
  3. UDL: W = w × L = 5 × 6 = 30 kN, acting at x̄ = L / 2 = 6 / 2 = 3 m from A.
  4. The 20 kN point load at 2 m from A adds loading but no unknown.
  5. Plane equilibrium gives three equations: ΣH = 0, ΣV = 0, ΣM = 0.
  6. r − 3 = 3 − 3 = 0, so the beam is statically determinate. The reaction arithmetic is in 3.2.

Exam traps

  • Treating a roller as a pin invents a horizontal reaction. A roller gives one reaction, normal to its surface; on a slope that reaction is not vertical.
  • The total of a UDL is w × L, not w, and it acts at the middle of the loaded length, not of the span when the load is partial.
  • Unit slips: w in kN/m times L in m gives kN; a slab pressure in kN/m² needs the strip width to become kN/m; a moment is in kN·m only when force is in kN and length in m.
  • Forgetting the moment reaction at a fixed end makes a fixed or propped beam look determinate. A fixed support always contributes three unknowns.
  • A simply supported beam needs a pin plus a roller; a simple support is one device. Draw the roller with its gap or circles.

3.2 Free body diagrams and support reactions

A beam resting on a wall and a roller looks simple, but the wall and the roller are not part of the beam. To apply equilibrium you must first cut the beam free: draw it alone and replace each support by the force it can exert. That drawing is a free body diagram, or FBD. Its reactions are the numbers a beam design starts from: the load a wall bracket must take, the push a bridge bearing sends into a pier. This chapter gives the steps, the two or three equilibrium equations, and the order that finds the reactions with the least algebra.

You need: 3.1 for the standard support and load types, and first-year statics for resolving forces and taking moments about a point.

Cut the beam free, then put back only forces

A beam sitting on a wall and a roller is not a free body. The wall pushes on it. The roller pushes on it. The equations ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 hold only for a body with every contact removed and every removed contact replaced by a force. So the first job is to cut the beam free.

  1. Draw the beam alone as a single line, at its true length, to scale if you can.
  2. Erase the supports, the wall, the floor and any hanger. Nothing touches the beam now.
  3. At each support draw the reaction that support can give. If you do not know its direction, assume upward and let a minus sign in the answer correct you.
  4. Replace each distributed load by one resultant. A UDL of w over length L becomes wL acting at mid-length. A triangular load rising to w over length L becomes wL/2, acting at one-third of L from the tall end.
  5. Keep every point load where it is, and mark its distance from one chosen end.

Now the beam is a free body carrying a few unknown arrows, and only those unknowns are missing. Every other number on the drawing is known.

Which support gives which reaction

A support can give only the forces its construction allows. A roller on a floor cannot pull the beam down, so it gives one force normal to the floor, usually vertical. A pin can push in any direction in the plane, so it gives two components, usually one horizontal and one vertical. A fixed end also resists rotation, so it gives a moment as well.

SupportReaction you drawUnknowns
Roller, or a beam end free to slideOne force normal to the surface1
Pin or hingeHorizontal and vertical components2
Fixed endHorizontal, vertical and a moment3
Short tie rodOne force along the rod1

Count the unknowns before you write any equation. A plane beam with three or fewer unknown reactions is statically determinate: the three equilibrium equations are enough to find them all. A beam with more than three unknowns is statically indeterminate, and equilibrium alone cannot finish the job; that case waits for Unit 5, Deflection of Beams and Energy Methods.

Three equations, one good order

Fix the signs first and keep them. Take upward as positive for forces, and anticlockwise as positive for moments. A force F whose line of action is at perpendicular distance d from the moment centre gives the moment F·d. A force whose line of action passes through the centre gives zero moment.

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0 about any point

ΣFx is the sum of horizontal force components in N or kN; ΣFy is the sum of vertical components; ΣM is the sum of moments in N·m or kN·m about any chosen point in the plane of the beam.

  1. Take moments about one support, say A. The reaction at A passes through A, so its moment is zero and it drops out.
  2. Solve that single equation for the reaction at the other support, B.
  3. Use ΣFy = 0 to get the reaction at A.
  4. Check with a moment equation about B. It must give the same R_A.
  5. Use ΣFx = 0 only if a horizontal load exists; with all loads vertical, the horizontal reaction at a pin is zero.

The order matters because it turns three simultaneous equations into one equation, then one substitution. Taking moments about an arbitrary point leaves both unknowns in the equation and costs time for no gain.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam AB is 6 m long. A is a pin, B is a roller on a horizontal floor. A point load of 20 kN acts 2 m from A, and a UDL of 5 kN/m covers the whole span. Draw the free body diagram and find both reactions.

  1. Free body diagram: beam line AB, 6 m long. Draw R_A vertically upward at A, R_B vertically upward at B. No horizontal load acts, so the horizontal component at the pin is zero.
  2. Replace the UDL: wL = 5 × 6 = 30 kN, acting at mid-length, that is 3 m from A.
  3. Take moments about A, anticlockwise positive: R_B × 6 − 20 × 2 − 30 × 3 = 0.
  4. So R_B × 6 = 40 + 90 = 130 kN·m, giving R_B = 130 ÷ 6 = 21.67 kN.
  5. ΣFy = 0: R_A + R_B − 20 − 30 = 0, so R_A = 50 − 21.67 = 28.33 kN.
  6. Check by moments about B: 20 × 4 + 30 × 3 = 80 + 90 = 170 kN·m, and R_A × 6 = 28.33 × 6 = 170 kN·m. Equal, so the answers are consistent.
  7. Check total force: downward 20 + 30 = 50 kN; upward 28.33 + 21.67 = 50 kN. R_A is larger because the 20 kN load sits nearer A.

Exam traps

  • Putting the resultant of a UDL at a support or at the end of the beam. For a UDL over the whole span it sits at mid-length, 3 m from A in this beam, never at A.
  • Writing the UDL as w instead of wL. A 5 kN/m load over 6 m is 30 kN in total, not 5 kN.
  • Stopping after the moment equation. That gives one reaction only; R_A needs the ΣFy step, and examiners take a mark for it.
  • Switching sign convention halfway. A moment written clockwise-positive in one line and anticlockwise-positive in the next gives a reaction that is right in size and wrong in sign.
  • Adding a horizontal reaction when every load is vertical. A roller on a horizontal floor gives only a vertical force, and the pin's horizontal component is zero here.

3.3 SFD and BMD for cantilever and simply supported beams

A beam carries transverse loads. To design it, you need the internal shear force V and bending moment M at every section, not only the reactions. Cut the beam at a distance x, keep one side as a free body, and equilibrium gives V and M at the cut. Plot V against x to get the shear force diagram (SFD), and M against x to get the bending moment diagram (BMD). These diagrams locate the maximum shear and maximum moment, the values used to choose the beam section; see Unit 4 for the stresses they cause.

You need: beam types, supports and loads from 3.1, and free body diagrams with support reactions from 3.2.

Sign convention and the section method

Take x from the left end of the beam. At a cut, the left part and right part exert equal and opposite internal actions. Use one convention for the whole chapter. A bending moment is positive when it causes sagging: the beam bends concave upward and the bottom fibres are in tension. It is negative when it causes hogging: concave downward and top fibres in tension. A positive shear force on the right-hand face of a left-hand piece acts downward; it tends to rotate that piece clockwise. The opposite face carries the opposite force.

  1. Take a section at distance x from a chosen end.
  2. Keep the left part as the free body; use the right part only when it has fewer loads, and reverse the face signs.
  3. At the cut draw V downward and M anticlockwise as the positive directions.
  4. Apply ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0 about the cut, then solve for V(x) and M(x).
V(x) = (sum of upward forces on the kept part) − (sum of downward loads on the kept part)

V(x) is the shear force at section x in kN; take upward forces as positive and downward applied loads as negative in the two sums.

Standard cases to remember

Learn the shapes first, then the end values. The table gives magnitudes; use the sign convention for the actual sign. In every case L is the span in metres, P is a point load in kN, w is a uniformly distributed load (UDL) in kN/m, and x is measured from the left end unless stated.

For a cantilever, a downward load always gives hogging moment, so the fixed-end moment is negative under the sagging-positive convention. Its magnitude is the sum of each load times its distance from the fixed end. For a simply supported beam, the BM is zero at both simple ends and positive between them for downward loads.

Drawing rules and checks

Name the two plots SFD and BMD, and label the units on both axes. These rules catch most drawing mistakes and let you sketch the diagrams before calculating every value.

  • A point load makes the SFD jump by the size of the load at that point. The BMD slope changes at the same point, so the BM curve has a kink.
  • A UDL makes the SFD a straight line with slope −w. The BMD is then a parabola.
  • BM is zero at a free end and at a simple support. At a fixed end it equals the fixed-end moment, usually hogging for a cantilever.
  • BM is maximum where SF is zero, or at a point load if SF crosses zero there. Check both sides of the point.
  • Draw the SFD first. Use its area or slope to check the BMD; see 3.5 for w = −dV/dx and V = dM/dx.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever AB is fixed at A and free at B. AB = 4 m. A UDL of 5 kN/m acts over the whole length, and a point load of 10 kN hangs at B. Draw the SFD and BMD and give the shear force and bending moment at A and at B. Use sagging moment positive.

  1. Take x from A. The UDL totals wL = 5 × 4 = 20 kN, acting at 2 m from A.
  2. Vertical equilibrium of the whole beam: R_A = 10 + 20 = 30 kN upward.
  3. Moment equilibrium about A: the loads give 10 × 4 + 20 × 2 = 40 + 40 = 80 kN·m clockwise. The fixed support supplies 80 kN·m anticlockwise. This is a hogging fixed-end moment, so M_A = −80 kN·m in the sagging-positive convention.
  4. Cut at x and keep the left part. Forces are R_A = 30 kN upward, UDL 5x downward, and V at the cut. With positive V downward: 30 − 5x − V = 0, so V = 30 − 5x kN. At A, V = 30 kN; at B, V = 10 kN.
  5. Take moments about the cut. The support moment is +80 kN·m (anticlockwise), R_A gives −30x, and the UDL gives +(5x)(x/2) = +2.5x². With positive M anticlockwise: 80 − 30x + 2.5x² + M = 0, so M = −80 + 30x − 2.5x² kN·m.
  6. At A, x = 0: M = −80 kN·m (hogging). At B, x = 4: M = −80 + 120 − 40 = 0. The BMD is a parabola from −80 kN·m to zero; the SFD is a straight line from 30 kN to 10 kN.

Answer. At A: V = 30 kN and M = −80 kN·m (hogging); at B: V = 10 kN and M = 0.

Exam traps

  • Fixed-end moment sign: a downward load on a cantilever gives hogging, so M is negative if sagging is positive. Writing +PL is a common sign error.
  • Leaving the point load out of the moment at the fixed end. Every load on the cantilever contributes load × distance from the fixed end.
  • Using the UDL total at the wrong place: wL acts at the midpoint of the loaded length, not at the free end.
  • Forgetting that a point load at the free end is not included in the shear just left of the free end; it is the boundary load that the shear at that section carries.
  • Drawing the BMD as a straight line under a UDL. Under a UDL the BM is a parabola; only the SFD is straight.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern beam design software draws SFD and BMD automatically, but it uses the same section method and checks the diagrams against the equilibrium of the whole beam. Moving loads, such as a train over a bridge, need an envelope of maximum SF and BM at every section, not one diagram. Search for influence lines, shear force envelope and moving load analysis if you meet bridge or crane girders.

3.4 SFD and BMD for overhanging beams

An overhanging beam runs past one or both supports into free air. That overhang changes the picture: the beam bends one way between the supports and the other way over the overhang, so the bending moment curve crosses zero somewhere inside the span. Engineers meet this on balcony slabs, canopy beams, and pipe racks with a projecting arm. The same three equilibrium equations still give the reactions, because the beam stays statically determinate. You only need one extra habit: measure every overhang load from the nearest support, and let the bending moment go negative there.

You need 3.2 for reactions and 3.3 for the section method and sketch shapes.

Why the overhang matters

A simply supported beam ends at its two supports, so the bending moment is zero at both ends. An overhanging beam does not end there. It continues past A or past B to a free end C, and the free end carries zero bending moment and, unless a point load or couple acts there, zero shear force as well.

Take a beam pinned at A, on a roller at B, with the overhang BC to the right. A downward load on BC pushes the right end down and tends to lift the beam off A. The support at A must pull down to hold it, so the reaction RA can come out negative. That is allowed for a pinned support, which can pull as well as push.

The bending moment at B is no longer zero. The overhang load to the right of B bends the beam downward on that side, which stretches the top fibres at B. That is hogging, and we plot it as a negative moment. The moment at a roller is zero only when nothing projects beyond it.

The beam is still statically determinate. Three unknowns RA, RB and the horizontal reaction at A are fixed by ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0. The overhang adds load, not an extra unknown.

Give it a sign rule and keep it: sagging positive, hogging negative. Then a negative answer means hogging, and nothing else needs remembering.

The four steps, and where the curves cross

  1. Draw the whole-beam free body. Put RA and RB at the supports and show every load with its distance from A, including the loads out on the overhang.
  2. Take moments about A to get RB. An overhang load at distance x from A to the right of B has lever arm x about A, so it still appears in the sum. Then ΣFy = 0 gives RA.
  3. Draw the SFD walking left to right: start at 0 at A, jump up by RA, fall linearly by the UDL to B, jump up by RB, then fall by the UDL along the overhang to 0 at the free end C. An arithmetic slip shows up as a diagram that does not close at zero.
  4. Draw the BMD from section equations, or by areas once you know 3.5. Look for zero moment: the free end, and the point of contraflexure where the curve crosses.

The shear force crosses zero once between the supports, where the sagging moment is largest. On the overhang the shear does not cross zero; it reaches zero only at the free end, where no load is applied. So the hogging moment at the support is the largest hogging value on that side.

The point of contraflexure is the section where M = 0 between the supports. There the beam changes from sagging to hogging, and the top fibres change from compression to tension. Concrete cracks there, so reinforcement is placed on both faces near it.

Between A and B, with L the span AB, M(x) = RAx − w x²/2, measured from A. Past B add the term RB(x − L). Solve M(x) = 0 for the contraflexure and V = dM/dx = 0 for the peak sagging moment.

Overhangs on both sides

When the beam overhangs at A and at B, both support moments are negative. The middle span still sags, so the curve has two contraflexure points, one near each support.

Take clockwise moments about A as positive. Then a downward load at x > 0 gives a positive moment, and a load on the left overhang (x < 0) gives a negative one. Keep the signs as the convention says instead of reasoning it out each time. ΣM about B is a good second check.

Two symmetrical overhangs of length a with a UDL w over the whole beam are a common exam case. Put the span between supports as L. Then RA = RB = w(L/2 + a) by symmetry, and the moment at each support is M = −w a²/2, exactly the cantilever moment of the overhang.

Worked example

Question. A beam is pinned at A, rests on a roller at B 4 m from A, and overhangs to a free end C, 6 m from A. A UDL of 10 kN/m acts over the whole 6 m. Find the reactions, the bending moment at B, the largest sagging bending moment and the point of contraflexure.

  1. Total load = 10 × 6 = 60 kN, acting at 3 m from A.
  2. ΣM about A: RB × 4 = 60 × 3 = 180, so RB = 45 kN.
  3. ΣFy = 0: RA = 60 − 45 = 15 kN (downward load, upward reaction).
  4. Section at x from A, 0 ≤ x ≤ 4: V = 15 − 10x, M = 15x − 5x².
  5. Section at x from A, 4 ≤ x ≤ 6: V = 15 − 10x + 45 = 60 − 10x, M = 15x − 5x² + 45(x − 4).
  6. At B, x = 4: M = 60 − 5(16) = −20 kN·m. Hogging, as expected over an overhang.
  7. Shear zero between the supports: 15 − 10x = 0 gives x = 1.5 m.
  8. Sagging peak: M = 15(1.5) − 5(1.5)² = 22.5 − 11.25 = 11.25 kN·m.
  9. Contraflexure: 15x − 5x² = 0 gives x = 3 m (the root x = 0 is the end A, not a contraflexure). The point of contraflexure is 3 m from A.

Answer. RA = 15 kN upward, RB = 45 kN upward, M at B = −20 kN·m (hogging), largest sagging moment 11.25 kN·m at x = 1.5 m, and the point of contraflexure is 3 m from A.

Exam traps

  • Writing M = 0 at the roller B out of habit. With an overhang, M at B equals minus the moment of the overhang load about B.
  • Measuring an overhang load's lever arm from the free end instead of from the support. For a UDL w on an overhang of length a, the support moment is −w a²/2, not −w a × a.
  • Dropping the variable sign of RA: with a heavy overhang, RA comes out negative, meaning the support holds the beam down. Do not 'correct' it.
  • Calling the zero-shear point the contraflexure point. Zero shear gives the peak sagging moment; M = 0 gives the contraflexure.
  • The SFD must close at zero at the free end (or at each free end), so an arithmetic slip shows up immediately as a leftover step.

3.5 Relation between load, shear force and bending moment

Load, shear and bending moment are not three separate diagrams. For a beam, the load w (kN/m, downward positive), shear V (kN) and moment M (kN·m) at section x (m) are linked by slopes: dV/dx = −w and dM/dx = V. The idea exists because once one curve is known, areas under it give the changes in the next. Designers use it to draw SFD and BMD quickly, to find the section of largest moment, and to check a computer plot before trusting it.

You need Chapter 3.3 and Chapter 3.4, plus basic differentiation and the areas of a rectangle and triangle.

One small element gives both relations

Cut a small length dx (m) from a beam. On the left face let the shear be V, acting upward. On the right face let it be V + dV, acting downward. A downward distributed load w (kN/m) acts on the element, giving total w dx. Vertical equilibrium: V = (V + dV) + w dx. Cancel V and divide by dx: dV/dx = −w. Now take moments about the left face. The right-face shear has lever arm dx and contributes (V + dV) dx. The load acts at dx/2 and contributes w dx × dx/2, which is second order in dx. The bending moments on the two faces differ by dM. Keeping first-order terms gives dM = V dx, so dM/dx = V. Differentiate again: d²M/dx² = −w. These three forms are the same statement in different clothes.

dV/dx = −w, dM/dx = V, d²M/dx² = −w

w is downward load per metre (kN/m), V is shear force (kN), M is bending moment (kN·m), and x is distance along the beam (m).

What the slopes mean

Read the slopes as instructions. Since dM/dx = V, the slope of the BMD at any section equals the shear force there. A positive V means M is increasing as x increases. A local maximum or minimum of M can occur where V = 0; compare that value with the end moments and support moments before calling it the largest. Since dV/dx = −w, the SFD is horizontal where w = 0, straight under a UDL, and parabolic under a uniformly varying load (UVL). The BMD is one degree higher: parabolic under a UDL and cubic under a UVL.

Jumps follow from equilibrium of the same element. A downward point load P makes V drop by P at that section; M stays continuous, so only its slope kinks. A concentrated couple C makes M jump by C; V stays continuous.

In integrated form, V₂ − V₁ = −(area under w from x₁ to x₂). M₂ − M₁ = signed area under the SFD from x₁ to x₂. The word signed is the whole game: area below the x-axis subtracts.

Using areas to draw and check

Use the area method as a drawing and checking tool. First find reactions from the free body diagram. Mark every point load, support and couple as a key section. Start at the left end with the known V and M. Moving right, add each jump and subtract the load area to get the next V. Then add the signed SFD area to get the next M. Locate V = 0 to place the peak or trough of M. Finally check that M returns to the correct end value and that units stay kN and kN·m.

For the figure, a 6 m simply supported beam carries 4 kN/m. Symmetry gives 12 kN at each support. V = 12 − 4x, so it falls from 12 kN to −12 kN and crosses zero at x = 3 m. M = 12x − 2x² peaks at 18 kN·m. The triangle under the SFD from 0 to 3 m has area ½ × 3 × 12 = 18 kN·m, matching the rise in M.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a UDL of 4 kN/m. Using V = dM/dx and the area method, find the reactions, the section where SF is zero and the largest BM, then check the BM by the area under the SFD.

  1. Total load = 4 kN/m × 6 m = 24 kN. By symmetry, R_A = R_B = 24/2 = 12 kN.
  2. Take x from A. For 0 ≤ x ≤ 6, V = 12 − 4x kN. Set V = 0: x = 3 m.
  3. M = ∫V dx = 12x − 2x² kN·m, with M = 0 at A. At x = 3 m, M = 12 × 3 − 2 × 3² = 36 − 18 = 18 kN·m.
  4. Area check: the SFD from x = 0 to 3 m is a triangle of base 3 m and height 12 kN. Area = ½ × 3 × 12 = 18 kN·m. This signed area equals M(3) − M(0) = 18 − 0.

Exam traps

  • Signs: use downward w positive and sagging M positive; mixing conventions flips dV/dx and dM/dx.
  • Signed areas: a negative SF reduces M, so adding magnitudes gives the wrong support or overhang moment.
  • Jumps: a point load jumps SF, not BM; a concentrated couple jumps BM, not SF.
  • Zero shear: V = 0 marks a candidate extremum, not automatically the absolute largest moment; check supports and overhangs.
  • Units: w dx gives kN and V dx gives kN·m; keeping kN/m and m avoids factor-of-1000 slips.

Takeaways

  1. Every real beam problem starts by drawing the free body diagram: replace each support with the reactions it can give, then solve ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0.
  2. A pin or roller gives one vertical reaction each; a fixed end gives a vertical reaction and a moment, which makes a cantilever solvable from the free end.
  3. The shear force at a section is the running sum of all transverse forces to one side of that section; the bending moment is the running sum of their moments.
  4. Shear force changes by jumps at point loads and by a straight-line slope under a UDL; the bending moment curve is one order smoother than the shear curve.
  5. The bending moment is maximum where the shear force is zero, or where a point load acts, or at a fixed end.
  6. In an overhanging beam part of the span is hogging, so the bending moment diagram crosses zero at a point of contraflexure.
  7. dM/dx = V and dV/dx = −w let you build the second diagram from the area under the first without writing every equation.
  8. An applied couple produces a sudden jump in the bending moment diagram but leaves the shear force diagram unchanged.

Glossary

Applied couple
A pure moment of magnitude M₀ applied at one section of a beam, measured in N·m.
Beam
A structural bar that carries loads applied perpendicular to its length and resists them mainly by bending.
Bending moment
The internal moment at a section, equal to the algebraic sum of the moments of all forces on one side about that section.
Cantilever
A beam fixed at one end, with the other end free to deflect.
Continuous beam
A single beam running over more than two supports.
Fixed beam
A beam with both ends built in, so no rotation is possible at either end.
Free body diagram
A diagram of the beam alone with all supports replaced by reaction forces and all loads shown in position.
Hogging moment
A negative bending moment that makes the beam concave downward, producing tension in the top fibres.
Overhanging beam
A beam that extends beyond one or both of its supports.
Pin support
A hinge-type support that prevents translation but allows rotation, giving two reaction components.
Point load
A concentrated force acting at a single section of a beam, measured in N or kN.
Point of contraflexure
A section of a beam where the bending moment is zero and changes sign.
Point of zero shear
A section where the shear force is zero, marking a maximum or minimum on the bending moment diagram.
Propped cantilever
A cantilever with an added simple support at some point along its length.
Roller support
A support that gives one reaction force normal to the rolling surface and allows free rotation.
Sagging moment
A positive bending moment that makes the beam concave upward, producing tension in the bottom fibres.
Shear force
The internal transverse force at a section, equal to the algebraic sum of all forces on one side of that section.
Simply supported beam
A beam with a pin support at one end and a roller support at the other, giving two vertical reactions only.
Span
The horizontal distance between two adjacent supports of a beam.
Statically determinate beam
A beam whose support reactions can be found from the three equations of equilibrium alone.
Support reaction
The force or moment that a support exerts on the beam to keep it in equilibrium.
Uniformly distributed load
A load of constant intensity w Newton per metre spread along a length of the beam.
