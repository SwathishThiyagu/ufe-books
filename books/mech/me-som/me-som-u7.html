7.1 Columns and struts: classification and end conditions

Push a long rod from its ends and it may not crush; it bows sideways and fails suddenly. That is buckling. Any member in axial compression is a strut; a vertical one is a column. Its safe load depends on its length, its section, and how its ends are held. Columns appear in building frames, press rams, truss members and scissor lifts. This chapter sorts compression members by how they fail and by their end conditions, so later formulas use the right ideal case.

You need Unit 1 for stress, strain and factor of safety, and Unit 4 for centroid and second moment of area; Euler's formula itself is in 7.2.

Strut, column and failure types

A member that carries an axial push is a compression member. If it lies at any angle, as in a roof truss, it is a strut. If it stands vertical, as a building column or a press ram, it is a column. Some syllabi use the two words for the same member. The direction on the drawing does not change the physics; the push does.

Compression members are classed by how they fail. A short column, with small length to least radius of gyration L/r, fails by crushing: the material reaches its compressive strength. A long column, with large L/r, bends sideways and fails by buckling at a load far below the crushing load. An intermediate column fails by a mixture: yielding and bending happen together. The dividing lines are not fixed numbers; they come from the material and the end conditions.

Why does sideways bending win? A real member has a small initial curve or a load not exactly on the axis. The push then has a small lever arm. Bending grows the deflection, and the deflection grows the bending. At a critical load the growth runs away. That load is the buckling load, also called the crippling load or Euler load. It is a stability limit, not a strength limit.

The same steel rod can be a tie in one bay and a strut in another. A tie is pulled and cannot buckle. A strut is pushed and must be checked for buckling.

How the ends are held

The load at which a column buckles depends on how its ends are held. A held end can stop translation, rotation or both. The five ideal cases used in textbooks are:

  • Both ends pinned (hinged): the ends cannot move sideways but can rotate; the column bows in one curve.
  • Both ends fixed: the ends cannot move or rotate; the column bows in an S shape.
  • One end fixed, one end free: a flagpole; the free top can move and rotate.
  • One end fixed, one end pinned: a clamp at the base and a hinge at the top.
  • Both ends fixed but free to move sideways: the ends keep their slope but the whole column sways.

An end that can rotate lets the column bow early. An end that is clamped forces a point of inflection and makes the column behave as if it were shorter. A free end adds a swaying cantilever action, the weakest case.

Each case replaces L by an effective length Le. L is the actual length between supports, in metres. The values of Le are in 7.3; the derivation of Euler's load is in 7.2.

Area, least I and radius of gyration

For buckling, three section properties matter. A is the area in m². I is the second moment of area about the axis of bending, in m⁴. r is the radius of gyration, in metres. The ratio Le/r is the slenderness ratio; 7.3 uses it.

r = √(I/A)

r is the least radius of gyration in metres (m); I is the least second moment of area in m⁴; A is the cross-section area in m².

A section has many centroidal axes. Buckling takes the path of least resistance, so the smallest I controls. Call it I_min; its radius is r_min. For an I-section the weak axis is usually the one through the web. A flat ruler bends easily about its thin edge, not its broad face.

Do not use the strong axis just because it gives a bigger load. A column can bend sideways in any direction, so it chooses the softest direction first. Built-up sections need checking about both axes, and the smaller r governs.

Euler's crippling load, critical load and buckling load mean the same thing: the axial push at which the straight form becomes unstable. It is not the crushing load. The Euler expression and its limits are in 7.2 and 7.3.

Worked example

Question. A steel rod of diameter d = 30 mm and length 2.0 m is used (a) as a tie rod in a roof truss and (b) as a vertical strut carrying a compressive load. Find its area, least second moment of area and least radius of gyration. State which use needs a buckling check.

  1. Convert d = 30 mm = 0.030 m. The length is L = 2.0 m.
  2. Area A = π d²/4 = π(0.030)²/4 = 7.069 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  3. Second moment I = π d⁴/64 = π(0.030)⁴/64 = 3.976 × 10⁻⁸ m⁴. For a solid round rod every centroidal axis has this same value, so it is the least I.
  4. Radius of gyration r = √(I/A) = √(3.976 × 10⁻⁸ / 7.069 × 10⁻⁴) = 7.5 × 10⁻³ m = 7.5 mm. Also r = d/4 = 30/4 = 7.5 mm.
  5. The tie is in tension; tension pulls the rod straight and cannot buckle. The strut is in compression; it can buckle about any axis, so it needs a buckling check with L and r.

Answer. A = 7.069 × 10⁻⁴ m², I = 3.976 × 10⁻⁸ m⁴, r = 7.5 mm; the strut needs the buckling check, the tie does not.

Exam traps

  • Using the strong axis. A column bends about the axis with the smallest I, so always take I_min and r_min.
  • Treating the Euler load as a crushing strength. It is a stability limit and is valid only while the stress stays below the proportional limit; see 7.3.
  • Confusing actual length L with effective length Le. The end conditions replace L by Le before Euler's formula is used.
  • Calling every compression member a column. A strut may lie at any angle; a column is vertical. Some syllabi use the words interchangeably, but the definition question expects the distinction.
  • Forgetting the unit conversion in I. A diameter in mm must be changed to m before d⁴ is computed; otherwise the error is huge.

7.2 Euler's crippling load for various end conditions

A long, straight column under axial compression can suddenly bend sideways and fail, although the material has not crushed. This sideways failure is buckling, and the load at which it first becomes possible is Euler's crippling load. For a pin-ended column it is P = π²EI/L². The same expression covers fixed, free and mixed ends when L is replaced by an effective length K L. Designers use it for struts in roof trusses, crane jacks, scaffolding and chassis links, where a thin compression member must not buckle.

You need: 7.1 for end conditions and radius of gyration, Unit 4 for the bending equation, and Unit 5 for the elastic curve of a bent beam.

The pin-ended column and the sine curve

Take a perfectly straight column of length L, pinned at both ends, and give it a small sideways deflection y at distance x from one end. The axial load P is vertical, so at that section its lever arm is y and the bending moment is M = P y. The moment acts to bend the column back toward the straight line, so the bending equation carries a minus sign.

EI d²y/dx² = −P y

E is Young's modulus (N/m²); I is the least second moment of area (m⁴); y is lateral deflection (m); x is distance along the column (m); P is axial load (N).

Put α² = P/(EI). Then d²y/dx² + α² y = 0. The solution is y = A sin αx + B cos αx. At the lower pin x = 0 and y = 0, so B = 0. At the upper pin x = L and y = 0, so A sin αL = 0. A = 0 gives the straight column. A bent shape first becomes possible when sin αL = 0, and the smallest nonzero root is αL = π. Thus α² = π²/L².

P = π²EI/L²

P is Euler's crippling load (N); E is Young's modulus (N/m²); I is the least second moment of area (m⁴); L is the pin-ended length (m).

Higher roots αL = 2π, 3π give higher buckling loads and more waves. Real columns buckle at the lowest one.

Four end conditions and effective length

Pinned ends cannot move sideways but can rotate freely. Fixed ends cannot rotate. A free end can move and rotate. Euler's differential equation is unchanged; the distance between points of zero bending moment changes. Replace L by the effective length Le = K L.

P = π²EI/(K L)²

K is the effective length factor (dimensionless); K L = Le is the effective length (m); the other symbols are as above.

The buckled shapes differ. Both ends pinned gives one half-sine bow over L. Both ends fixed gives an S-shape made of two half-sine segments of length L/2, with inflection points at L/4 and 3L/4. One fixed and one pinned gives one half-sine segment of length about 0.7L. One fixed and one free gives a quarter-wave of length 2L. The fixed-fixed column carries four times the pinned-pinned load; the fixed-free column carries one quarter.

Assumptions and real joints

Euler's result assumes a perfectly straight column, homogeneous material, load exactly along the axis, small deflection, and stress below the proportional limit. It also assumes ideal ends: frictionless pins or perfect fixity. Real columns have initial crookedness, load eccentricity and residual stress. Real bolted, welded or riveted joints rotate a little, so they sit between pinned and fixed. Use an effective length that matches the joint, not the ideal value blindly. The formula is unsafe for short, thick columns, which crush before they buckle; check slenderness limits in 7.3 and use 7.4 for the full range. Always take I about the weak axis. A safe design uses a factor of safety on the crippling load and checks both buckling and yielding.

Worked example

Question. A hollow steel tube of outside diameter D = 60 mm, inside diameter d = 50 mm and length L = 3 m is used as a column with E = 200 GPa. Find the crippling load when both ends are pinned, and when both ends are fixed.

  1. Convert: D = 60 mm = 0.060 m, d = 50 mm = 0.050 m, E = 200 GPa = 200 × 10⁹ N/m².
  2. Second moment of area: I = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64 = π(0.060⁴ − 0.050⁴)/64 = 3.294 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  3. Bending stiffness: EI = 200 × 10⁹ × 3.294 × 10⁻⁷ = 65,880 N·m².
  4. Both ends pinned: K = 1, so P = π²EI/L² = 9.8696 × 65,880 / 3² = 72,245 N.
  5. Both ends fixed: Le = L/2 = 1.5 m, so P = π²EI/(1.5)² = 9.8696 × 65,880 / 2.25 = 288,980 N.
  6. The fixed-fixed load is four times the pinned-pinned load for the same tube.

Answer. The crippling load is about 72.2 kN with both ends pinned and about 289.0 kN with both ends fixed.

Exam traps

  • Using the second moment of area about the strong axis. Euler uses the least I of the section, because the column bends about the weak axis.
  • Mixing units: E in N/m² with L in mm and I in mm⁴. Convert D, d and L to metres, or keep every length in millimetres and use E in N/mm².
  • Using actual L instead of effective length in P = π²EI/(K L)², or taking K = 0.5 for fixed-free. Fixed-free has K = 2, the largest effective length and the lowest load.
  • Treating Euler's crippling load as a working load. It is the theoretical buckling load; apply a factor of safety and check that the stress is below the proportional limit.
  • Assuming perfect fixity. Real bolted or welded joints rotate a little, so the actual load lies between the pinned and fixed predictions.

7.3 Effective length, slenderness ratio and limits of Euler's theory

Euler's buckling load looks like a single number, but it is really a curve. Divide the critical load by the area and the material constants collapse into one ratio: the effective length divided by the least radius of gyration. That number, the slenderness ratio, tells you whether a column is thin enough for Euler's formula to mean anything. It decides the design of steel columns in buildings, tubular struts in trusses, and the light compression members in a roof.

You need 7.1 for the least second moment of area and radius of gyration, and 7.2 for Euler's load and the end-condition factor K.

Effective length and slenderness ratio

The effective length Le is the length of an equivalent pin-ended column that buckles at the same load as the real column. Write Le = K L, where L is the actual length between supports (mm or m) and K is the end-condition factor from 7.2; K is 1 for pinned-pinned, 2 for fixed-free, about 0.7 for fixed-pinned and 0.5 for fixed-fixed.

The least radius of gyration is r = √(I/A), where I is the least second moment of area (mm⁴) and A is the cross-sectional area (mm²). The slenderness ratio is λ = Le/r. Both Le and r are lengths, so λ has no units. A member with large λ is slender or thin; a member with small λ is stocky. There is no single agreed boundary. Some syllabi call λ above about 80 slender; others use different limits.

Critical stress from Euler's load

Start with Euler's load P_cr = π² E I / Le² from 7.2, where E is Young's modulus (MPa or N/mm²), I is the least second moment of area (mm⁴) and Le is the effective length (mm). Write I = A r², with A the area (mm²). Then P_cr = π² E A r² / Le². Divide by A to get the average stress at buckling:

σ_cr = P_cr / A = π² E / λ²

σ_cr is the Euler critical stress (MPa); E is Young's modulus (MPa); λ = Le/r is the slenderness ratio, with no units.

The formula shows the whole design in one line: double λ and σ_cr falls to one quarter. For steel with E = 200 GPa = 200,000 MPa, λ = 180 gives σ_cr = 9.8696 × 200,000 / 180² = 60.9 MPa. At λ = 100 it is 197.4 MPa. At λ = 75 it is 350.9 MPa.

Where Euler stops working

Euler's formula assumes a perfectly straight, homogeneous column, free of initial stress, loaded exactly along its axis, with ends that are perfectly pinned or perfectly fixed. Real columns have a slight bow, load eccentricity, residual welding stress and connections that are only partly fixed. Each defect lowers the real buckling load, so Euler is an upper bound.

Euler remains valid only while σ_cr stays below the proportional limit. In simple calculations this limit is often taken as the yield stress σ_c, which is slightly unconservative: it gives λ ≈ 89 for σ_c = 250 MPa, while a proportional limit below yield would give a larger cut-off slenderness. Set σ_cr equal to this limiting stress:

σ_cr = π² E / λ² = σ_c → λ = √(π² E / σ_c)

σ_c is the limiting compressive stress, often taken as the yield stress (MPa); E is Young's modulus (MPa); λ is the slenderness ratio, with no units.

For E = 200 GPa = 200,000 MPa and σ_c = 250 MPa, λ = √(9.8696 × 200,000 / 250) = √7895.7 = 88.8, about 89. A column with λ > 89 buckles elastically and Euler applies. A column with λ < 89 reaches the yield stress before it buckles, so Euler predicts a stress the material cannot carry; such a column is short or stocky and must be designed by the Rankine-Gordon formula or a column curve (see 7.4).

Worked example

Question. A steel column has effective length Le = 4.5 m, E = 200 GPa and yield stress 250 MPa. (a) Its least radius of gyration is r = 25 mm. Find the slenderness ratio and Euler critical stress, and state whether Euler's theory applies. (b) Repeat with r = 60 mm.

  1. Convert: Le = 4.5 m = 4500 mm, E = 200 GPa = 200,000 MPa, σ_c = 250 MPa.
  2. (a) λ = Le/r = 4500/25 = 180.
  3. σ_cr = π²E/λ² = 9.8696 × 200,000 / 180² = 1,973,920 / 32,400 = 60.9 MPa.
  4. 60.9 MPa < 250 MPa, so Euler applies; the column buckles elastically.
  5. (b) λ = 4500/60 = 75.
  6. σ_cr = 9.8696 × 200,000 / 75² = 1,973,920 / 5,625 = 350.9 MPa.
  7. 350.9 MPa > 250 MPa, so Euler is invalid; treat the column as intermediate.

Answer. (a) λ = 180, σ_cr = 60.9 MPa, Euler applies. (b) λ = 75, σ_cr = 350.9 MPa, Euler is invalid.

Exam traps

  • Using the actual length L instead of the effective length Le in λ.
  • Quoting a Euler load from a stocky column whose computed σ_cr is above the yield stress.
  • Taking any radius of gyration instead of the least one; buckling happens about the weak axis.
  • Mixing units: E in GPa with Le in mm. Convert E to MPa (N/mm²) first.
  • Writing λ with units; Le and r are both lengths, so λ is dimensionless.

7.4 Rankine-Gordon formula for columns

Euler's formula is reliable for long columns and badly wrong for short ones, where a column crushes before it buckles. The Rankine-Gordon formula joins the two failures in a single expression, P = σc A/(1 + a λ²), that gives a usable failure load at every slenderness ratio. It is used for cast iron columns in old buildings, timber struts in roof trusses, and machine members whose slenderness falls in the middle band where neither Euler nor pure crushing applies.

You need 7.2 for Euler's load, 7.3 for the slenderness ratio and the Euler limit, and Unit 1 for crushing stress and factor of safety.

Two failures, one gap

A short compression member fails by crushing. The load at which the material itself gives way is σcA, where σc is the crushing stress in N/m² and A is the cross-section area in m². A long member fails earlier, by bending sideways, at Euler's load π²EI/Le².

Real columns sit between the two. Take mild steel at λ = Le/r = 60. Euler's stress is π²E/λ² = π² × 200 000/3600 = 548 MPa. That is far above the 320 MPa crushing stress of mild steel, so Euler asks for a stress the material can never reach. Plain crushing is wrong too: at that slenderness the member is visibly flexible and bends sideways before it crushes.

Rankine and Gordon fitted one curve that moves from crushing to buckling as the column grows slender. The form is empirical, chosen to match test results, not derived from elasticity.

The formula and its two limits

P = σc A / (1 + a λ²)

P is the crippling load in N; σc is the crushing strength of the material in N/m² (for mild steel, its compressive yield stress); A is the cross-section area in m²; a is a constant for the material, a pure number; λ = Le/r is the slenderness ratio, with Le the effective length in m and r the least radius of gyration in m.

The denominator 1 + aλ² is a plain number, so the units of P come entirely from σcA. Read the two ends.

  • Small λ: aλ² → 0, the denominator → 1, and P → σcA. Crushing governs, as it must for a squat block.
  • Large λ: the 1 is negligible beside aλ², so P → σcA/(aλ²) = (σc/a)(A/λ²). That is Euler's form P = π²E(A/λ²) with π²E replaced by σc/a.
  • In between, the curve bends smoothly from one limit to the other. It never rises above σcA, so it never predicts a stress the material cannot carry.

The constant σc/a plays the part of π²E. If a were exactly σc/(π²E), the large-λ tail would lie on Euler's curve. The fitted a is close to that ratio, so the formula joins Euler instead of contradicting it.

Worked example

Question. A hollow cast iron column has outside diameter D = 200 mm, inside diameter d = 150 mm and length L = 4 m. Both ends are fixed. Take σc = 550 MPa, a = 1/1600 and E = 100 GPa. Find the Rankine-Gordon crippling load and compare it with Euler's load for the same column.

  1. Area: A = π(D² − d²)/4 = π(0.2² − 0.15²)/4 = π(0.0175)/4 = 0.013744 m².
  2. Second moment of area: I = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/64 = π(0.0016 − 0.00050625)/64 = 5.369 × 10⁻⁵ m⁴.
  3. Radius of gyration: r = √(I/A) = √(5.369 × 10⁻⁵/0.013744) = √0.003906 = 0.0625 m.
  4. Both ends fixed, so Le = 0.5L = 0.5 × 4 = 2 m. Slenderness: λ = 2/0.0625 = 32, and λ² = 1024.
  5. Denominator: aλ² = 1024/1600 = 0.64, so 1 + aλ² = 1.64.
  6. Rankine-Gordon load: P = 550 × 10⁶ × 0.013744/1.64 = 7.559 × 10⁶/1.64 = 4.609 × 10⁶ N, about 4609 kN.
  7. Euler load: Pe = π²EI/Le² = π² × 100 × 10⁹ × 5.369 × 10⁻⁵/2² = 13.25 × 10⁶ N, about 13 250 kN.
  8. Euler's load is about 2.9 times the Rankine-Gordon load, so it overestimates the strength of this stocky column by a factor of 2.9. Its stress, 13.25 × 10⁶/0.013744 = 964 MPa, is far above the 550 MPa crushing stress, so Euler is unusable for this column.

Answer. The Rankine-Gordon crippling load is 4.609 × 10⁶ N ≈ 4609 kN; Euler's load is 13 250 kN, about 2.9 times the Rankine-Gordon load.

  • Feeding L/r into λ instead of Le/r. Using the full 4 m length in the example gives λ = 64, aλ² = 2.56 and P = 2123 kN, less than half the correct answer.
  • Treating a as a stress or an area. aλ² must add to the number 1, so a is a pure number; taking it in MPa breaks the formula.

  • Using I about the strong axis. r and I must be the minimum values, because the column bends about the weaker axis first.

7.5 Thin cylinders: hoop and longitudinal stresses

A closed thin cylinder under internal pressure—a boiler, an LPG cylinder, a water main—has two normal stresses in its wall. Hoop stress σ_h acts around the circumference. Longitudinal stress σ_L acts along the axis. A force balance on a cut half-shell gives σ_h = p d/(2t); a balance on the end cap gives σ_L = p d/(4t). Hoop stress is twice longitudinal stress. This is why a cylindrical pressure vessel splits along a longitudinal seam. Here p is internal pressure, d is internal diameter and t is wall thickness.

You need Unit 1 for Hooke's law, Poisson's ratio and volumetric strain, and Unit 2 for plane stress and Mohr's circle; 7.6 extends the same strain idea to spheres.

Thin wall and hoop stress

Take t ≤ d/20, that is d/t ≥ 20. Here t is wall thickness and d is internal diameter. Stress is then taken as uniform through the thickness. Some books say d/t of 20 or more. Below that, use 7.7.

Cut the cylinder along a diameter over length L. Pressure p acts on the projected rectangle d × L, giving force p d L. Two wall strips, each of area t L, carry hoop stress σ_h. Balance: 2 σ_h t L = p d L.

σ_h = p d / (2t)

p = internal pressure (Pa); d = internal diameter (m); t = wall thickness (m); σ_h = hoop stress (Pa).

For a closed end, cut across the axis. Pressure on the circular end area πd²/4 pushes the cap off. The wall ring of area π d t carries longitudinal stress σ_L. Balance: σ_L π d t = p π d²/4.

σ_L = p d / (4t) = σ_h / 2

σ_L = longitudinal stress (Pa); closed ends assumed; other symbols as above.

Closed ends matter: an open pipe has no pressure thrust on its ends, so no longitudinal pressure stress.

Strains, shear and joints

ε_h = (σ_h − ν σ_L) / E,   ε_L = (σ_L − ν σ_h) / E

ε_h, ε_L = hoop and longitudinal strain; E = Young's modulus (Pa); ν = Poisson's ratio; σ_h, σ_L as above.

The diameter grows by Δd = ε_h d. The length grows by ΔL = ε_L L; L is cylinder length. The strain route is advanced; only a few universities teach it.

Maximum shear stress through the wall is about σ_h/2. In the wall surface plane it is (σ_h − σ_L)/2 = σ_h/4. Radial stress runs from 0 at the outer surface to −p at the inner surface, so it is ignored.

A riveted or welded shell is weakened at its joint. Use joint efficiency η ≤ 1:

σ_h = p d / (2 t η)

η = joint efficiency (≤ 1); other symbols as above.

The longitudinal joint carries hoop stress, so it usually governs. The sphere in 7.6 has no such split: its stress is p d/(4t) in every direction.

Worked example

Question. A boiler shell has d = 1.2 m, t = 8 mm, p = 1.2 MPa, E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.3. Check the thin-wall limit, find σ_h and σ_L, and the increase in diameter.

  1. t = 8 mm = 0.008 m; E = 200,000 MPa.
  2. d/t = 1200/8 = 150 ≥ 20, thin.
  3. σ_h = p d/(2t) = 1.2 × 10^6 × 1.2/(2 × 0.008) = 90 MPa.
  4. σ_L = p d/(4t) = 45 MPa.
  5. ε_h = (90 − 0.3 × 45)/200,000 = 3.825 × 10^-4.
  6. Δd = ε_h d = 3.825 × 10^-4 × 1200 = 0.459 mm.

Answer. d/t = 150, σ_h = 90 MPa, σ_L = 45 MPa, and the diameter increases by 0.459 mm.

Exam traps

  • Substituting the radius value into the diameter formula. σ_h = p d/(2t) needs the diameter; σ_h = p r/t with r = d/2 gives the same number, so using r where d is written halves your answer and is easy to miss. Write the formula and the value with the same symbol.
  • Setting σ_L = σ_h. Closed ends give σ_L = σ_h/2; an open pipe gives zero.
  • Mixing p in MPa with d in metres and t in millimetres. Convert all lengths to one unit.
  • Forgetting joint efficiency η: σ_h = p d/(2tη), with η ≤ 1.
  • Taking σ_h/2 as in-surface shear. Surface maximum shear is σ_h/4; σ_h/2 is through the wall.

7.6 Thin spherical shells and change in volume

A thin spherical shell under internal pressure has the same direct stress on every plane through its centre: σ = p d/(4t). This is half the hoop stress of a cylinder with the same d, t and p. Strain is equal in every surface direction, so volume strain is 3ε. Gas holders and dished vessel ends use this.

You need 7.5 for cylinder stress and strain, and Unit 1 for volumetric strain and Hooke's law.

Cut the shell through its centre

A shell is thin when t ≤ d/20; d is internal diameter and t wall thickness, both in mm. p is internal pressure in N/mm² (MPa). The wall carries only direct tension; bending is ignored.

  1. Cut the sphere into halves through its centre.
  2. Pressure on one half is F = p × πd²/4; the curved surface projects to a circle of diameter d.
  3. The cut face is a ring of area πdt, carrying force σ × πdt.
  4. Equate: σdt = pd²/4, so σ = pd/(4t).

Any diametral plane gives the same σ. A sphere has no weak direction. No shear acts on a diametral plane, so σ is a principal stress and the other surface principal stress equals it.

σ = p d / (4t) = p r / (2t)

σ is direct stress, N/mm²; p is internal pressure, N/mm²; d is internal diameter, mm; t is wall thickness, mm; r = d/2 is internal radius, mm.

Strain and change in volume

The shell looks the same in every surface direction. The direct stress σ is the same in two perpendicular directions in the surface; radial stress is taken as zero. Hence ε = (σ/E)(1 − ν).

ε = (σ / E)(1 − ν)

ε is linear strain in any surface direction; σ = pd/(4t) is direct stress, N/mm²; E is Young's modulus, N/mm² (2×10⁵ for steel); ν is Poisson's ratio (about 0.3 for steel).

Every internal diameter grows by ε. A cube argument gives (1+ε)³ ≈ 1+3ε for small ε, so

dV / V = 3ε = 3(σ / E)(1 − ν)

V = πd³/6 is internal volume; dV is increase. With d in metres V is in m³; with d in mm V is in mm³.

A cylinder is less simple: V = (π/4)d²L, so

dV / V = 2 ε_h + ε_L

ε_h = (σ_h/E)(1 − ν/2); ε_L = (σ_L − ν σ_h)/E; σ_h = pd/(2t); σ_L = pd/(4t); V = (π/4)d²L.

Sphere against cylinder, and dished ends

With the same d, t, p and ν = 0.3, the sphere gives dV/V = 0.525 pd/(tE); the cylinder gives 0.95 pd/(tE). The sphere swells 55% as much and has half the wall stress, so it needs half the plate thickness.

A hemispherical end (or the spherical crown of a dished head) is part of a sphere welded to the cylinder. A flat end would bend and need much thicker plate. The hemispherical end carries tension at pd/(4t), half the shell's hoop stress. The two parts grow differently: shell radial strain ≈ 0.85σ_h/E (σ_h = pd/(2t)), end ≈ 0.35σ_h/E. The stiffer end restrains the shell, so bending and shear appear near the joint. A knuckle radius or thickened transition spreads it.

A sphere has least surface area for a given volume, so it needs least steel per cubic metre stored. It also sits on a small foundation ring. Large LPG and CNG terminals use spheres. Road tankers stay cylindrical because a sphere will not fit on a truck.

Worked example

Question. A thin spherical shell of internal diameter 2 m and wall thickness 10 mm holds gas at 1.5 MPa. Take E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.3. Find (i) direct stress, (ii) linear strain, (iii) volume strain, (iv) increase in volume in litres. (v) State the volume strain a thin cylinder of the same d, t and p would show.

  1. Data: p = 1.5 MPa = 1.5×10⁶ N/m², d = 2 m, t = 10 mm = 0.01 m, E = 200 GPa = 2×10¹¹ N/m², ν = 0.3. Thin check: t/d = 0.005, far below 1/20.
  2. Stress: σ = pd/(4t) = (1.5×10⁶×2)/(4×0.01) = 3×10⁶/0.04 = 75×10⁶ N/m² = 75 MPa.
  3. Strain: ε = (σ/E)(1 − ν) = (75×10⁶/(2×10¹¹))(1 − 0.3) = 3.75×10⁻⁴×0.7 = 2.625×10⁻⁴.
  4. Volume strain: dV/V = 3ε = 3×2.625×10⁻⁴ = 7.875×10⁻⁴.
  5. Volume: V = πd³/6 = π×2³/6 = 4.189 m³.
  6. Increase: dV = 7.875×10⁻⁴×4.189 = 3.30×10⁻³ m³ = 3.30 litres.
  7. Cylinder check: σ_h = pd/(2t) = 150 MPa, ε_h = ((150×10⁶)/(2×10¹¹))(1 − 0.15) = 6.375×10⁻⁴; σ_L = 75 MPa, ε_L = ((75 − 0.3×150)×10⁶)/(2×10¹¹) = 1.5×10⁻⁴; dV/V = 2(6.375×10⁻⁴) + 1.5×10⁻⁴ = 1.425×10⁻³.

Answer. σ = 75 MPa, ε = 2.625×10⁻⁴, dV/V = 7.875×10⁻⁴ with ΔV = 3.30 litres; the same-size cylinder would give dV/V = 1.425×10⁻³, about 1.8 times more.

Exam traps

  • Writing σ = pd/(2t) for a sphere. That is a cylinder's hoop stress; a sphere carries pd/(4t).
  • Using dV/V = ε_h + ε_L for a cylinder. The diameter grows in two directions, so it is 2ε_h + ε_L.
  • Dropping the Poisson factor: use (σ/E)(1 − ν) for a sphere, (σ_h/E)(1 − ν/2) for a cylinder's hoop direction.
  • Feeding r into a formula written for d. pd/(4t) = pr/(2t); mixing radius and diameter doubles or halves the answer.
  • Mixing units: p in MPa (N/mm²) with d in metres. Work in N and mm, or N and m, never half of each.

7.7 Thick cylinders: Lame's theory

A thick cylinder is one whose wall is not small compared with its radius, roughly diameter-to-thickness below 20. The thin-cylinder formula assumes hoop stress is constant through the wall; Lame's theory accepts that it is not. It writes radial stress σr = A − B/r² and hoop stress σθ = A + B/r², with constants A and B fixed by the pressures at the bore and outside. The result is used for hydraulic cylinders, gun barrels, pressure vessels, pipe autofrettage and high-pressure process equipment, where, under internal pressure, the bore is normally the danger point and the first point to yield.

You need: 7.5 for the thin-wall force balance, and see Unit 2 for principal stresses and maximum shear.

When thin theory stops working

Thin-cylinder theory balances force on a cut half-cylinder, then divides by the wall area. It gives σθ = p d/(2t), where p is internal pressure, d is internal diameter and t is wall thickness. That formula returns one number, so it assumes hoop stress is uniform through the wall. The assumption is fair while the wall is thin: d/t above about 20. Below d/t ≈ 20, material near the bore stretches more than material outside and shares the load differently. The hoop stress then varies strongly across the wall. Hydraulic press cylinders, gun barrels and high-pressure process pipes with thick walls fall in this group. The thin formula can be badly low at the bore, so it is unsafe there. Lame's theory keeps the force balance but lets stress change with radius r.

  • Use thin theory when d/t ≥ 20.
  • Use Lame's theory when d/t < 20.

Lame's equations from radial equilibrium

Take a ring of unit length along the axis, between radius r and r + dr. Radial stress σr acts on the inner and outer curved faces. Hoop stress σθ acts on the two side faces. Radial equilibrium of the ring gives dσr/dr + (σr − σθ)/r = 0. Strain compatibility with Hooke's law adds that σr + σθ is constant. Solving these conditions gives Lame's equations:

σ<sub>r</sub> = A − B/r²,   σ<sub>θ</sub> = A + B/r²

σr is radial stress (negative in compression), σθ is hoop stress, r is radius in m, A is in MPa, B is in MPa m² so that B/r² is in MPa.

A and B are constants fixed by the pressures at the two surfaces. Boundary conditions for internal pressure p only: at the bore r = ri, σr = −p; at the outer surface r = ro, σr = 0. Solving gives:

A = p r<sub>i</sub>²/(r<sub>o</sub>² − r<sub>i</sub>²),   B = p r<sub>i</sub>² r<sub>o</sub>²/(r<sub>o</sub>² − r<sub>i</sub>²)

p is internal pressure in MPa, ri is bore radius in m, ro is outer radius in m.

The hoop stress follows:

σ<sub>θ,bore</sub> = p(r<sub>o</sub>² + r<sub>i</sub>²)/(r<sub>o</sub>² − r<sub>i</sub>²),   σ<sub>θ,outer</sub> = 2p r<sub>i</sub>²/(r<sub>o</sub>² − r<sub>i</sub>²)

σθ,bore is hoop stress at the inside surface, σθ,outer is hoop stress at the outside surface.

At the bore σθ is largest. At the outer surface it is lower. For closed ends, the end pressure is carried by longitudinal stress σL = p ri²/(ro² − ri²) = A.

Bore is the danger point

At the bore, σθ is largest and σr is −p, so the difference is largest there. For a closed cylinder the three principal stresses at the bore are σθ, σL and σr. The largest shear stress is τmax = (σθ − σr)/2 = (σθ,bore + p)/2. The outer surface has lower hoop stress and zero radial stress, so it is not the danger point. Design checks therefore use the bore: yield begins there first. In the worked example, the bore has 100 MPa hoop and −60 MPa radial, giving τmax = 80 MPa; the thin formula would have said 60 MPa everywhere. Some books call this Lame's theory, some the thick-cylinder theory. Use the same sign convention throughout: tensile positive, radial compression at the bore negative.

Worked example

Question. A thick cylinder has internal radius 50 mm, external radius 100 mm and carries internal pressure 60 MPa, with no external pressure. Find the constants A and B, the hoop stress at the bore and at the outer surface, the longitudinal stress for closed ends, and the maximum shear stress at the bore.

  1. Convert radii to metres: ri = 50 mm = 0.05 m, ro = 100 mm = 0.1 m. The pressure is p = 60 MPa.
  2. Use σr = A − B/r². At the bore: A − B/0.05² = −60. At the outer surface: A − B/0.1² = 0.
  3. The outer condition gives A = B/ro² = B/0.1² = B/0.01 = 100B.
  4. Substitute in the bore condition: 100B − B/0.0025 = −60, so 100B − 400B = −60, giving −300B = −60 and B = 0.2 MPa m².
  5. Then A = 100 × 0.2 = 20 MPa.
  6. Hoop stress at the bore: σθ,bore = A + B/ri² = 20 + 0.2/0.0025 = 20 + 80 = 100 MPa.
  7. Hoop stress at the outer surface: σθ,outer = A + B/ro² = 20 + 0.2/0.01 = 20 + 20 = 40 MPa.
  8. Longitudinal stress for closed ends: σL = p ri²/(ro² − ri²) = 60 × 0.0025/0.0075 = 20 MPa, which equals A.
  9. Maximum shear at the bore: τmax = (σθ,bore − σr,bore)/2 = (100 − (−60))/2 = 80 MPa.

Answer. The constants are A = 20 MPa and B = 0.2 MPa m², the hoop stress is 100 MPa at the bore and 40 MPa at the outer surface, the longitudinal stress is 20 MPa, and the maximum shear at the bore is 80 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Writing σr = +p at the bore. Internal pressure pushes on the bore, so radial stress is compression: σr = −p at r = ri.
  • Using millimetres in B/r². B is in MPa m², so r must be in metres; 50 mm must become 0.05 m.
  • Applying σθ = p d/(2t) when d/t is below 20. The thin formula gives 60 MPa for the worked example, not the true 100 MPa at the bore.
  • Checking the outer surface for maximum shear. σθ and the difference σθ − σr are largest at the bore, so the bore is the danger point.
  • Forgetting the closed-end longitudinal stress. It is p ri²/(ro² − ri²), equal to A here, and it makes the stress state triaxial at the bore.

7.8 Compound thick cylinders and thick spheres

A compound thick cylinder is two or more tubes shrunk one onto the other. A single thick cylinder under internal pressure is worst at the bore, where hoop stress peaks, while outer layers do little work. Shrinking an outer tube on leaves compressive residual hoop stress at the bore, cancelling part of the pressure stress. Gun barrels, hydraulic press cylinders and high-pressure vessels use this. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need 7.7 for Lamé's equations, constants A and B, and the sign convention for radial stress.

The shrink fit

The inner tube's outside diameter is slightly larger than the outer tube's bore. The difference is the shrinkage allowance. Heat the outer tube until it expands, slip it over, and cool it. On cooling it grips, and a shrinkage pressure ps appears at the common surface.

That pressure holds the joint. It pushes radially outward on the inner tube and inward on the outer tube: same pressure, opposite directions. Each tube then carries its own Lamé field with ps as its only boundary load. This is the residual field, present before working pressure.

  1. Fix the shrinkage allowance and find ps from it: the outer tube's bore opens out and the inner tube's outside is squeezed down; these two radial deflections together equal the allowance.
  2. Residual hoop stress at the bore of the inner tube is compressive, written with a minus sign.
  3. Residual hoop stress at the bore of the outer tube is tensile, written with a plus sign.

Adding the pressure stresses

Run Lamé's equations again for working internal pressure p alone, with zero pressure outside. Add this field to the residual field at the same radius, using the same sign convention: σr is negative when compressive.

σ<sub>θ,final</sub>(r) = σ<sub>θ,residual</sub>(r) + σ<sub>θ,pressure</sub>(r)

σθ is the hoop stress at radius r, in N/m² (Pa); r is the radius in metres. The two hoop stresses are added algebraically at the same point using the same sign convention, so a compressive (negative) residual reduces a tensile (positive) pressure stress.

The gain: at the bore the shrink-fit compression is subtracted from the pressure tension, so peak stress drops and the same wall carries more pressure. Autofrettage does the same by over-pressurising the bore once, so the metal there yields and stays in compression.

Thick spheres

σ<sub>r</sub> = A − 2B/r³     σ<sub>θ</sub> = A + B/r³

σr is radial stress, σθ is hoop stress in any tangential direction, both in Pa; r is the radius in m; A has units of Pa and B of Pa·m³.

The constants come from the two surfaces: σr = −p at the bore r = ri, and σr = 0 at the outside r = ro. Two equations, two unknowns. A sphere carries the same hoop stress in every direction, so it is stronger than a cylinder of the same size, as in 7.6. A compound sphere is analysed the same way: residual field from the shrink fit, pressure field from the load, then add.

Worked example

Question. A compound cylinder is made from two tubes shrunk together. The shrink fit alone leaves a residual hoop stress of −45 MPa at the bore of the inner tube and +45 MPa at the bore of the outer tube. The working pressure alone would give a hoop stress of 120 MPa at the bore of the inner tube. Find the final hoop stress at the bore of the inner tube and the percentage reduction.

  1. Write the superposition: σθ,final = σθ,pressure + σθ,residual.
  2. Substitute at the inner bore: σθ,final = 120 MPa + (−45 MPa).
  3. σθ,final = 75 MPa (tension).
  4. Reduction = 45 MPa. As a fraction of the pressure-only value: 45/120 = 0.375, that is 37.5 per cent.

Exam traps

  • Adding residual and pressure stresses with the wrong signs; the residual at the inner bore is negative and must be subtracted.
  • Forgetting that shrinkage pressure pushes outward on the inner tube but inward on the outer tube, so the two bores get opposite residual hoop stresses.
  • Using working pressure where shrinkage pressure belongs, or the reverse, when computing ps.
  • Mixing diameters and radii inside B/r² or B/r³; B changes value if you switch, so stay consistent.
  • Believing a compound cylinder is just a thick cylinder of the same total wall thickness; the shrink fit is the whole point.

Remember Define the shrinkage allowance in a compound cylinder. 2 marks

Understand Why does the shrinkage pressure leave the inner bore in compression and the outer bore in tension? 3 marks

The pressure acts radially outward on the inner tube, so its circumference tries to shrink and the hoop stress is compressive; it acts inward on the outer tube, which is stretched over the inner one, so its hoop stress is tensile.

Understand State the two boundary conditions used to find A and B for a thick sphere under internal pressure p. 2 marks

Apply A compound cylinder has a residual bore hoop stress of −30 MPa and a pressure-only bore hoop stress of 90 MPa. Find the final bore hoop stress. 4 marks

Apply The same cylinder (residual −30 MPa) must keep the bore hoop stress at or below 100 MPa. What is the largest pressure-only bore hoop stress allowed? 4 marks

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Gun barrels and autofrettaged fuel-injection lines use this residual-stress trick, and modern vessels sometimes use three or more shrunk layers. Autofrettage is designed by checking where the bore stress first reaches yield and how far the plastic zone spreads. Takeaways

  1. A compression member does not fail by crushing alone: once slender, it bends sideways and the load capacity drops sharply.
  2. Every end condition uses the same Euler formula if L is replaced by the effective length kL.
  3. The slenderness ratio Lₑ/r alone fixes the critical stress through σ_cr = π²E/λ², so geometry decides, not just material.
  4. Euler's formula is only valid while the critical stress stays below the proportional limit; beyond that it grossly overestimates strength.
  5. The Rankine-Gordon formula blends crushing and buckling and stays safe at every slenderness, which is why design codes favour it.
  6. In a thin closed cylinder the hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress, so the wall is always governed by the hoop direction.
  7. A thin sphere carries σ = pd/(4t) in every direction, which is half the cylinder hoop stress for the same thickness and pressure.
  8. In a thick cylinder, hoop stress varies through the wall and is largest at the bore, so uniform-stress theory of thin shells fails.
  9. A compound (shrunk-fit) cylinder works better than a single thick tube because pre-compression at the bore cancels part of the pressure stress.

Glossary

Buckling
Loss of stability of a compression member by sideways bending at a critical load.
Column
A straight member carrying an axial compressive load, usually vertical.
Compound cylinder
Two or more tubes shrunk one onto another so that residual stresses share the pressure load.
Crippling load
The critical axial load at which a column just begins to buckle.
Effective length
The equivalent pin-ended length kL that buckles at the same load as the real column.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure load or stress to the working load or stress.
Hoop stress
The circumferential tensile stress in a shell wall due to internal pressure.
Interference
The amount by which the outer member is smaller than the inner member before assembly.
Lame's equations
The radial and hoop stress expressions for a thick cylinder, in terms of two constants A and B.
Least second moment of area
The smallest I about any centroidal axis; buckling always occurs about that axis.
Longitudinal stress
The axial tensile stress in a closed shell due to pressure on the end plates.
Proportional limit
The stress up to which stress is proportional to strain; above it Hooke's law fails.
Radial stress
The direct stress acting perpendicular to the cylinder wall; equals −p at a pressure surface.
Radius of gyration
r = √(I/A), a measure of how far the area is spread from the centroid.
Rankine constant
The material constant a = σ_c/(π²E) used in the Rankine-Gordon formula.
Secant formula
A column formula for eccentric loading that includes the secant of a term containing the eccentricity.
Shrink fit
A joint made by making the outer tube slightly smaller than the inner tube and heating it to assemble.
Slenderness ratio
The dimensionless ratio λ = Lₑ/r of effective length to radius of gyration.
Spherical shell
A shell with equal curvature in all directions; carries equal stress in every membrane direction.
Strut
A compression member in a frame or truss, of any orientation.
Thin shell
A shell whose wall thickness is small compared with its diameter, about t ≤ d/20.
Volumetric strain
The fractional change in the enclosed volume of a shell under pressure.
