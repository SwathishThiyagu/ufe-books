5.1 Elastic curve and its differential equation

A straight beam bends into a smooth curve called the elastic curve. At every section, the curvature of this curve equals the bending moment divided by EI, where E is Young's modulus and I is the second moment of area. This link turns a strength problem into a deflection problem. Designers use it to keep floors, bridges and machine shafts within serviceability limits, for example a 4 m floor beam that must not sag more than span/250. This chapter sets up the differential equation; the next chapters solve it.

You need Unit 3 (shear force and bending moment diagrams) and Unit 4 (bending stress and EI); after this chapter, 5.2 Double integration method integrates the equation.

The elastic curve and curvature

Take x along the beam axis from a chosen origin, in metres. Take y as the vertical deflection, positive upward. A loaded beam bends into a smooth curve y(x) called the elastic curve. The quantity EI is the flexural rigidity: E is Young's modulus in kN/m² (or N/m²), and I is the second moment of area in m⁴. EI has units kN·m².

Curvature measures how sharply the curve turns. It is κ = 1/R, where R is the radius of curvature in metres, so κ has units 1/m. For small slopes, κ ≈ d²y/dx². The theory of simple bending from Unit 4 gives M/I = E/R, so 1/R = M/EI.

EI d²y/dx² = M(x)

E is Young's modulus; I is the second moment of area; y is deflection positive upward; x is distance along the beam; M(x) is the bending moment at x, positive sagging.

Sign convention: y upward and sagging M positive. A sagging beam is concave up, so d²y/dx² is positive and EI y″ = M. If you take y downward while keeping sagging M positive, the same physical sag gives negative d²y/dx², so the equation becomes EI y″ = −M. Write your convention at the top of every answer.

The governing equation and boundary conditions

EI d²y/dx² = M(x) is called the governing differential equation of the elastic curve, or the equation of the elastic curve. It is second order, so two boundary conditions fix the solution. Do not integrate here; 5.2 Double integration method does that.

Differentiate once: EI y‴ = V, where V is shear force. Differentiate twice: EI y⁗ = −w, where w is the downward distributed load per unit length. These follow from dM/dx = V and dV/dx = −w.

How to set up the equation

  1. Choose x from one end and state the range, for example 0 ≤ x ≤ 4 m.
  2. Choose y positive upward or downward. Write the sign convention before any equation.
  3. Find support reactions from equilibrium, as in Unit 3.
  4. Write M(x) from the bending moment diagram. Sagging positive.
  5. Write EI y″ = M(x). Use EI y″ = −M(x) if y is downward.
  6. List boundary conditions from the table.
  7. Check units: M in kN·m and EI in kN·m² give y″ in 1/m.

For a simply supported beam with UDL w over span L, the reactions are wL/2 each. With x from the left support, M(x) = (wL/2)x − wx²/2. Sagging is positive. This is the setup used in the example below.

A cantilever fixed at x = 0 with a downward point load P at the free end x = L has M(x) = −P(L − x). The negative sign means hogging. At the fixed end, y = 0 and y′ = 0. At the free end, M = 0, so y″ = 0. The shear at the free end equals the load applied there, V = P (not zero), so y‴ = P/EI. Use y‴ = 0 at a free end only when no concentrated load acts at that end.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 4 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 6 kN/m over the whole span. EI = 5000 kN·m². Take x from the left support, y positive upward, and sagging bending moment positive. (a) Write M(x) for 0 ≤ x ≤ 4 m. (b) Find the curvature at midspan.

  1. Total load = 6 kN/m × 4 m = 24 kN. By symmetry, each reaction is 12 kN.
  2. Cut the beam at distance x from the left support. The left part has upward reaction 12 kN and downward UDL of 6x kN acting at x/2 from the cut.
  3. M(x) = 12x − (6x)(x/2) = 12x − 3x² kN·m. Positive sagging.
  4. At midspan x = 2 m: M = 12(2) − 3(2²) = 24 − 12 = 12 kN·m.
  5. Curvature = M/EI = 12 / 5000 = 0.0024 m⁻¹. Positive means concave up, consistent with sagging.

Exam traps

  • Mixing sign conventions: a sagging moment with y downward needs EI y″ = −M. Write the convention at the top of every answer.
  • Confusing curvature with radius of curvature. Curvature is κ = 1/R = M/EI; the radius is R = EI/M. A large radius means slight bending, not sharp bending.
  • Using EI in N·m² with M in kN·m without converting. Keep kN and metres together, or convert both.
  • Taking M(x) from the wrong end or with the wrong sign. Match the bending moment diagram from Unit 3.
  • Treating the small-deflection equation as exact for large sag. It assumes slopes are small.

5.2 Double integration method

The double integration method turns a bending moment diagram into a deflected shape. Start from EI y″ = M(x), integrate twice, and two constants appear. Support conditions fix those constants. The result gives slope and deflection at every section. Engineers use it to check serviceability: a floor beam that sags too much cracks finishes, and a crane runway that deflects too much jams the trolley. It is the direct route when M(x) is simple and continuous; Macaulay's method extends it to many loads.

You need 5.1 for the elastic curve equation EI y″ = M(x) and the sign convention.

The equation and the two constants

Take x along the beam from the left end, y positive upward, and sagging moment positive. The elastic curve from 5.1 is EI y″ = M(x). This is the double integration method, also called the direct integration method.

EI y″ = M(x)

y is deflection (m); y′ is slope (rad); y″ is curvature (1/m); M(x) is bending moment (kN·m); EI is flexural rigidity (kN·m²).

Integrate once: EI y′ = ∫M dx + C₁. Integrate again: EI y = ∫∫M dx dx + C₁x + C₂. C₁ has units kN·m²; C₂ has units kN·m³. The two constants are not fixed by the load. They are fixed by the supports.

If M changes formula at a point load, split the beam into regions. Give each region its own constants. At the load point, slope and deflection must be equal from both sides. Symmetry can replace one matching equation.

  1. Write M(x) for one region with no change in load formula.
  2. Integrate twice symbolically; keep C₁ and C₂.
  3. Apply two boundary conditions from the supports.
  4. Solve for C₁ and C₂, then substitute x for the required slope or deflection.

Support conditions fix C₁ and C₂

A simple support allows rotation but not deflection: y = 0. A fixed end allows neither: y = 0 and y′ = 0. Two independent conditions are enough because the equation is second order.

EndConditions

Do not use y = 0 at both ends of a cantilever. A cantilever has one fixed end and one free end. The free end gives the deflection you want; it does not supply two conditions.

Cantilever fixed at x = 0 with downward end load P at x = L: M = −P(L − x). Integration with y(0) = 0 and y′(0) = 0 gives EI y = −P x²(3L − x)/6.

y(L) = −P L³ / (3 EI)

P is the downward end load (kN); L is the cantilever length (m); EI is flexural rigidity (kN·m²); negative means downward.

For a cantilever UDL w, M = −w(L − x)²/2. The same two fixed-end conditions give free-end deflection −wL⁴/(8EI). The fixed-end conditions, not the free end, supply C₁ and C₂.

Standard cases from one integration run

For a simply supported span L under UDL w, M = w x(L − x)/2. The boundary conditions y(0) = y(L) = 0 give C₂ = 0 and C₁ = −wL³/24. Then EI y = wLx³/12 − wx⁴/24 − wL³x/24. Midspan deflection is −5wL⁴/(384EI); end slope magnitude is wL³/(24EI).

For a central point load P, M changes at L/2. Use M = Px/2 for 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2 and M = P(L − x)/2 for L/2 ≤ x ≤ L. Match y and y′ at midspan, or use symmetry y′ = 0 at midspan. The maximum deflection is −PL³/(48EI); support slope magnitude is PL²/(16EI).

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a uniformly distributed load of 4 kN/m over its whole length. EI = 6000 kN·m². Using the double integration method, find the midspan deflection and the slope at each support.

  1. Reactions: each is wL/2 = 4 × 6 / 2 = 12 kN.
  2. With x from the left support and sagging positive, M(x) = 12x − (4x²/2) = 12x − 2x² kN·m.
  3. EI y″ = 12x − 2x².
  4. EI y′ = 6x² − (2/3)x³ + C₁.
  5. EI y = 2x³ − (1/6)x⁴ + C₁x + C₂.
  6. y(0) = 0 gives C₂ = 0. y(6) = 0 gives 2(6³) − (1/6)(6⁴) + 6C₁ = 0, so 432 − 216 + 6C₁ = 0 and C₁ = −36 kN·m².
  7. At midspan x = 3 m: EI y = 2(27) − (1/6)(81) − 36(3) = 54 − 13.5 − 108 = −67.5 kN·m³. y = −67.5 / 6000 = −0.01125 m = −11.25 mm.
  8. EI y′ = 6x² − (2/3)x³ − 36. At x = 0: y′ = −36 / 6000 = −0.006 rad. At x = 6: y′ = (216 − 144 − 36) / 6000 = 36 / 6000 = 0.006 rad.

Answer. Midspan deflection is 11.25 mm downward; slope at the left support is −0.006 rad and at the right support is +0.006 rad.

Exam traps

  • Using one M(x) across a point load. The moment formula changes at the load; split the beam or use Macaulay's method.
  • Putting y = 0 into the moment equation before integrating. Boundary conditions apply to y and y′, not to M.
  • Forgetting the second fixed-end condition. A fixed end needs y = 0 and y′ = 0, not y = 0 twice.
  • Dropping the sign of the support slope. With y upward positive, the left support of a sagging beam has negative slope and the right support positive slope.
  • Mixing kN, m and mm. EI in kN·m² and w in kN/m give deflection in m; multiply by 1000 for mm.

5.3 Macaulay's method

Macaulay's method writes one bending moment equation for the whole beam, using bracket terms that switch on only after the section passes a load. One expression means one pair of integration constants and no matching of segments. It is the standard exam route for a simply supported or cantilever beam carrying several loads, and the same brackets sit inside most beam-analysis software. This chapter shows the brackets, the integration rule and the support conditions that finish the job.

You need 5.1 for EI y″ = M, 5.2 for the integrate-twice routine, and the SFD/BMD work of Unit 3 for the reactions.

Why one expression

In the double integration method you split the beam into segments between loads. Each segment needs its own M(x), its own two constants, and matching conditions at every junction. Three loads give four sets of algebra and three chances to slip.

Macaulay's method keeps one expression from the left end to the right end. The trick is a bracket that stays asleep until the section passes the load.

⟨x − a⟩ⁿ = 0 when x < a;  ⟨x − a⟩ⁿ = (x − a)ⁿ when x ≥ a

x is the distance from the left end to the section, in m; a is the distance from the left end to the load, in m; n is 0, 1 or 2 depending on the load type; the angle brackets are Macaulay's brackets.

Because the term is exactly zero up to a, adding it to M(x) changes nothing to the left of the load and adds the load's full contribution to the right. The single M(x) is then correct at every section.

Some syllabi call these Macaulay's brackets; others call the whole idea the singularity function method.

Bracket for each load type

Load on the beamTerm added to M(x)
Downward point load W at x = a−W⟨x − a⟩
Applied moment M₀ at x = a+M₀⟨x − a⟩⁰
UDL w starting at x = a and running right−(w/2)⟨x − a⟩²
UDL w from x = a to x = b (it stops at b)−(w/2)⟨x − a⟩² + (w/2)⟨x − b⟩²

The signs above suit sagging positive with downward loads. W is in kN, w in kN/m, M₀ in kN·m, a and b in m.

  • Point load: its moment about the section is W times the lever arm (x − a), and it hogs, so the term is negative.
  • Applied moment: a concentrated couple has no lever arm, so it enters with power zero. ⟨x − a⟩⁰ is 1 after a and 0 before.
  • UDL starting at a: the load w(x − a) acts at its centroid (x − a)/2 from the section, giving w(x − a)²/2.
  • UDL that ends: a UDL from a to b is a UDL starting at a plus an upward UDL of the same w starting at b. Each start needs its own bracket, which is how you switch a load off.
  • Loads left of the origin never need a bracket. Always put the origin at the left end and reuse the same sign convention.

Integrate and fit the supports

  1. Draw the free body diagram and get both reactions from ΣF and ΣM (Unit 3).
  2. Write EI d²y/dx² = M(x) with every load as a bracket term.
  3. Integrate once for slope: each ⟨x − a⟩ⁿ becomes ⟨x − a⟩ⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1). Add C₁.
  4. Integrate again for deflection and add C₁x + C₂. No bracket gets its own constant, because M(x) is a single function.
  5. Put y = 0 at each support: x = 0 gives C₂ for a beam resting at the left end, and x = L gives C₁. A cantilever fixed at x = 0 uses y = 0 and y′ = 0 instead.
  6. Substitute the x where deflection is wanted. If x < a, write 0 for that bracket — never a negative number.

One power is special. ⟨x − a⟩⁰ integrates to ⟨x − a⟩¹, not to x, because the bracket stays zero until x reaches a.

The method needs reactions that statics alone can give. In a propped cantilever or a fixed beam the reactions are themselves unknown, so the brackets are combined with an extra compatibility equation — see 5.8. For a quick deflection check on a simple load case, 5.6 is faster.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a point load of 10 kN at 2 m from the left end and a UDL of 4 kN/m over the whole span. EI = 6000 kN·m². Use Macaulay's method to find the deflection under the point load.

  1. Reactions. Total UDL = 4 × 6 = 24 kN, acting at midspan, 3 m from the left end. Taking moments about the left support A: R_B × 6 = 10 × 2 + 24 × 3 = 20 + 72 = 92 kN·m, so R_B = 15.333 kN and R_A = 10 + 24 − 15.333 = 18.667 kN.
  2. Moment expression. Origin at A, sagging positive: M(x) = 18.667x − 10⟨x − 2⟩ − (4/2)x² = 18.667x − 10⟨x − 2⟩ − 2x², in kN·m, with x in m.
  3. First integration: EI y′ = 9.333x² − 5⟨x − 2⟩² − 0.6667x³ + C₁, in kN·m².
  4. Second integration: EI y = 3.111x³ − 1.667⟨x − 2⟩³ − 0.1667x⁴ + C₁x + C₂, in kN·m³.
  5. Support at x = 0. Every x term and every bracket is zero there, so C₂ = 0.
  6. Support at x = 6. 3.111 × 216 − 1.667 × 64 − 0.1667 × 1296 + 6C₁ = 0, that is 672.0 − 106.7 − 216.0 + 6C₁ = 0, giving C₁ = −58.222 kN·m².
  7. Deflection at x = 2, under the point load. The bracket ⟨x − 2⟩ is zero here: EI y = 3.111 × 8 − 0 − 0.1667 × 16 + (−58.222) × 2 = 24.889 − 2.667 − 116.444 = −94.222 kN·m³.
  8. y = −94.222/6000 = −0.0157037 m = −15.70 mm. With y positive upward, the negative sign means the beam moves down.

Answer. The deflection under the point load is 15.70 mm downward, that is y = −15.70 mm. Check values: R_A = 18.667 kN, C₁ = −58.222 kN·m².

Exam traps

  • Putting a negative number into a bracket instead of writing zero. ⟨x − 4⟩³ at x = 1 is 0, not −27.
  • Adding a fresh constant after integrating each bracket. The whole EI y needs only C₁ and C₂.
  • Forgetting to switch off a UDL that ends partway, so the load keeps acting to the right end and the reactions no longer balance.
  • Losing the sign convention: EI y″ = M is used with y positive upward, so a sagging beam gives a negative y at midspan.
  • Trying Macaulay's method on a propped cantilever without writing the compatibility equation first — the reactions cannot be found from statics alone.

5.4 Moment-area method

The moment-area method reads slope and deflection straight off the M/EI diagram, with no integration of equations. The area under M/EI between two sections gives the change in slope between them. The first moment of that area about one section gives how far the other section sits from the tangent drawn at the first. Engineers use it to check a cantilever or a simply supported beam quickly, and examiners use it because it tests whether you understand curvature rather than algebra.

You need: 5.1 for curvature M/EI, and Unit 3 for bending moment diagrams.

The two theorems

Draw the bending moment diagram, divide every ordinate by EI, and plot M/EI along the beam. Some syllabi call the results Mohr's theorems; others call the whole procedure the area-moment method.

θ_B − θ_A = area of M/EI between A and B

θ_A, θ_B are slopes in radians at sections A and B; the area is measured in (1/m) × m = radians.

t_B/A = first moment of that area about B

t_B/A is the vertical distance of B from the tangent drawn at A, in metres; the moment is area × horizontal distance of its centroid from B.

Sign rule: take sagging moment as positive, so a sagging beam curves concave upward (it sags downward). Plot M/EI on the sagging side as positive. Then t_B/A is positive when B lies above the tangent drawn at A. Keep one sign convention for the whole problem.

Theorem 1 gives only a change of slope, so you still need one known slope. Theorem 2 gives a distance from a tangent, not a deflection: the tangent at A may itself be tilted. The deflection of B is t_B/A only when the tangent at A is horizontal, as at a fixed end or at the centre of a symmetrically loaded span.

  1. Draw the bending moment diagram for the beam.
  2. Divide the ordinates by EI to get the M/EI diagram; split it into triangles, rectangles and parabolas if EI or the load changes.
  3. Area of M/EI between two sections gives the change in slope.
  4. Take the first moment of that area about the section whose deviation you want.
  5. Use a known slope or known horizontal tangent to convert a deviation into a real slope or deflection.

Why area gives angle and moment gives deviation

Curvature is 1/R = M/EI. Take a slice of length dx. The two faces of the slice turn relative to each other by dθ = (M/EI) dx. Adding these small turns from A to B gives the total change in slope, which is Theorem 1.

For deviation, a turn dθ in a slice at distance x from B moves B by x dθ relative to the tangent to that slice. Adding x dθ over the whole length from A to B gives the deviation of B from the tangent at A. That sum is exactly the first moment of the M/EI area about B, which is Theorem 2.

The method is strongest where the M/EI diagram is simple shapes. A cantilever with an end load gives a triangle. A simply supported beam with a uniformly distributed load w over span L gives a parabola; the slope at a support is wL³/(24EI) and the central deflection is 5wL⁴/(384EI). For several loads, draw the moment diagram by parts: one triangle per point load, one parabola for the distributed load, and take areas and centroids shape by shape.

Worked pattern: cantilever with an end load

Take a cantilever fixed at A and free at B, length L, with a point load P at B. The moment at a section distance x from A is M = P(L − x), so M/EI is a straight line: PL/EI at the fixed end, zero at the free end.

Area of the triangle = ½ L (PL/EI) = PL²/(2EI). That is the slope at B, since the slope at A is zero. The centroid of a triangle with its base at the fixed end lies L/3 from A, so its distance from B is 2L/3. Then t_B/A = (PL²/2EI)(2L/3) = PL³/(3EI). The tangent at the fixed end is horizontal, so this deviation is the free-end deflection.

Numbers from the figure: L = 3 m, P = 8 kN, EI = 4000 kN·m², giving 0.009 rad and 18 mm as solved below.

5.5 Conjugate beam method

The conjugate beam method turns deflection into statics. Replace the real beam with a fictitious conjugate beam of the same length. Load it with the M/EI diagram of the real beam. Then the conjugate shear equals the real slope, and the conjugate moment equals the real deflection. It exists because statics is faster than integrating the elastic curve. It is used for quick checks on determinate beams. Only a few universities teach it.

You need: 5.1 for the elastic curve and 5.4 for M/EI areas.

The conjugate beam

Take the real beam of length L. Draw its bending moment M(x). Divide every ordinate by EI, where E is Young's modulus and I is the second moment of area. The result is the M/EI diagram, with unit 1/m. Make a conjugate beam of length L and load it with q*(x)=M(x)/EI. The conjugate shear V*(x) and moment M*(x) obey dV*/dx=q* and dM*/dx=V*. Comparing with dθ/dx=M/EI and dy/dx=θ gives V*=θ and M*=y. Here θ is real slope in radians and y is real deflection in metres. Some syllabi call this the fictitious beam method; the standard name is the conjugate beam method. Choose one sign convention: sagging M positive, downward y positive. Apply the M/EI load in the direction that keeps the signs consistent. Check one known value before trusting the rest.

Support transformation

Transform every real support. A real fixed end has θ=0 and y=0, so the conjugate must have V*=0 and M*=0: a free end. A real free end has θ≠0 and y≠0, so the conjugate must have V*≠0 and M*≠0: a fixed end. A real simple support has y=0 but θ≠0, so the conjugate has M*=0 but V*≠0: a simple support. A real internal hinge has y=0 and a possible jump in slope, so the conjugate has M*=0 and a possible jump in shear: an internal hinge. Never keep the real supports. After loading, V* at any section is the real slope, and M* is the real deflection. The method is for determinate beams: a simply supported beam with a central load, or a cantilever with an end load. Indeterminate beams need M found first; see 5.8.

Worked example

  • Swapping the results: conjugate shear gives slope, conjugate moment gives deflection.
  • Forgetting units: M/EI is 1/m, so V* is radians and M* is metres.

5.6 Method of superposition

The method of superposition solves a beam with several loads by splitting them into simple cases, finding the deflection or slope for each case from a standard result, and adding the answers algebraically. It exists because a linear elastic beam responds in proportion to load, so the effect of a combined load is the sum of the effects of its parts. Engineers use it for quick checks on floor beams, overhanging shafts and bridge girders.

You need the elastic curve (Chapter 5.1) and the moment-area method (Chapter 5.4); double integration (Chapter 5.2) gives the standard results.

Principle and standard results

For a linear elastic beam, bending moment is linear in the applied loads. Curvature is M/EI, and two integrations give slope and deflection, so both are linear in the loads. If load case 1 gives deflection δ1 at a point and load case 2 gives δ2 at the same point, the combined case gives δ1 + δ2 there. This is the superposition principle. It needs small deflections, a linear elastic material and unchanged geometry. Only a few universities teach it as a separate topic.

Splitting a combined load

Write the combined loading as a sum of standard cases. Find the deflection at the required point for each case. Take downward deflections as positive and upward deflections as negative, then add algebraically. The same rule works for slopes, positive meaning rotation of the free end downwards.

  1. Sketch each standard case alone; keep the same span and EI.
  2. Compute δ for each case at the same point.
  3. Take downward as positive and upward as negative; add algebraically.
  4. For an overhanging beam, keep the whole beam (span plus overhang) as one structure and split only the loading into standard cases. The deflection of the overhang free end equals the overhang length times the slope at the support (from the main-span cases) plus the bending deflection of the overhang under its own load.

Never add a midspan deflection from one case to a quarter-span deflection from another. The point must be the same. Superposition still holds for a stepped or tapered beam, but each standard result assumes constant EI over the span it is applied to. Divide such a beam into constant-EI segments, or use an energy method. Superposition genuinely fails only for nonlinear material, for large deflections that change the geometry, or for a change of structural system, such as a support lifting off.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6 m carries a UDL of 4 kN/m over the whole span and a central point load of 12 kN. EI = 6000 kN·m². Find the midspan deflection by superposition.

  1. Check linear elastic, small-deflection conditions; superposition is valid.
  2. Case 1, central point load: δ1 = PL³/(48EI) = (12 × 6³)/(48 × 6000) = 2592/288000 = 0.009 m = 9 mm downward.
  3. Case 2, full UDL: δ2 = 5wL⁴/(384EI) = (5 × 4 × 6⁴)/(384 × 6000) = 25920/2304000 = 0.01125 m = 11.25 mm downward.
  4. Both act at midspan and in the same direction. Take downward as positive: δ = (+9) + (+11.25) = +20.25 mm, the plus sign meaning downward.

Answer. The midspan deflection is 20.25 mm downward.

Exam traps

  • Adding deflections at different points; superposition works at one point at a time.
  • Dropping the sign when a load acts upward or opposite to another; add algebraically.
  • Using the cantilever formula for a simply supported beam, or forgetting the 5 in 5wL⁴/384EI.
  • Mixing units: L in mm with EI in kN·m² gives a wrong deflection; convert to metres first.
  • Applying superposition to a nonlinear or large-deflection problem where geometry changes.

5.7 Strain energy methods and Maxwell's reciprocal theorem

A bent beam stores energy, like a stretched bow. Write that energy in terms of the applied loads and one derivative hands you the deflection under the load. That is Castigliano's theorem. Its companion, Maxwell's reciprocal theorem, says two cross-deflections of the same beam are equal: the deflection at A due to a unit load at B equals the deflection at B due to a unit load at A. Energy methods are the quick route for determinate beams and the standard route for statically indeterminate ones, where double integration becomes heavy.

You need: strain energy under axial load (see Unit 1, Strain energy under gradual, sudden and impact loads), the bending equation and flexural rigidity EI (see Unit 4, Theory of simple bending and bending equation), and the elastic curve (see 5.1).

Strain energy stored in a bent beam

When a beam bends, fibres on one side stretch and fibres on the other side shorten. The work done by the external loads against this elastic resistance is stored in the beam and is recovered when the loads are removed. That stored work is the strain energy U, measured in joules (J) or, in beam work, in kN·m.

For a small slice of beam of length dx, the bending moment M(x) acts through the curvature it produces. The energy in that slice works out to M(x)² dx / (2EI). Adding all slices gives the strain energy of the whole beam.

U = ∫₀ᴸ M(x)² / (2EI) dx

U is the strain energy (N·m or kN·m); M(x) is the bending moment at section x (N·m or kN·m); E is Young's modulus (N/m²); I is the second moment of area about the bending axis (m⁴); EI is the flexural rigidity (N·m²); L is the beam length (m).

For pure bending, M is constant over the length, so the integral collapses to U = M²L / (2EI). For anything else you must write M as a function of x and integrate. Use the magnitude of M: it is squared, so a sagging or hogging sign disappears.

The attraction of energy is that it is a scalar. You do not chase slopes or boundary constants. A Chennai workshop checking a canopy beam can square the moment diagram, integrate to get U, then differentiate with respect to the load to read the deflection.

Castigliano's theorem

Castigliano's theorem for bending says: the partial derivative of the strain energy with respect to a load gives the displacement of the point where that load acts, measured in the direction of the load.

∂U/∂P = Δ

U is the total strain energy (kN·m); P is the applied load (kN); Δ is the deflection under P in the direction of P (m). A positive result means the deflection is in the same sense as P.

  1. Keep the load as a symbol P and write M(x) with P still inside it.
  2. Form U = ∫ M(x)² / (2EI) dx and simplify to a function of P.
  3. Differentiate U with respect to P. Only now put in the number for P.
  4. Check the answer against a standard case such as PL³/(3EI) for a cantilever.

The order matters. If you substitute the numeric P into U first, U becomes a constant and the derivative is zero. The chapter trap is exactly this.

If you want the deflection at a point where no load acts, apply a dummy unit load there, differentiate, then set the dummy to zero. That is the unit load method: Δ = ∫ M m / (EI) dx, where m is the bending moment caused by the unit load alone. It is the same idea dressed for a point of your choosing, and it saves you from inventing a real load.

The double integration method of 5.2 gives the whole elastic curve; Castigliano gives one deflection with less algebra. When several loads act, superpose their M diagrams first, then square (see 5.6).

Maxwell's reciprocal theorem and Betti's theorem

Maxwell's reciprocal theorem states that for a linear elastic beam, the deflection at point A due to a unit load at point B equals the deflection at point B due to a unit load at A. In symbols, δ_AB = δ_BA, where δ_AB means the deflection at A caused by a unit load at B.

The general form is Betti's theorem: the work done by a first system of loads moving through the displacements produced by a second system equals the work done by the second system moving through the displacements of the first. Maxwell's theorem is the special case where each system is a single unit load.

Use it as a free check or a shortcut. A simply supported beam with a unit load at midspan deflects the quarter point by an amount; put the unit load at the quarter point and the midspan deflects by the same amount. Standard deflection tables therefore carry twice the information they appear to.

The theorem is also the reason a flexibility matrix of a beam is symmetric, with the term in row A, column B equal to the term in row B, column A. That symmetry halves the number of flexibility coefficients you must compute by hand, which is why the one-redundancy unit-load equation for a propped cantilever or a fixed beam is so quick to set up (see 5.8).

  • Castigliano: differentiate energy with respect to a load to get deflection.
  • Unit load method: same derivative with a dummy unit load, then set it to zero.
  • Maxwell: two reciprocal deflections are equal.
  • Betti: work of one load system on the other's displacements is symmetric.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever of length 2 m carries a point load of 6 kN at its free end. EI = 3000 kN·m². Use Castigliano's theorem to find the free-end deflection.

  1. Measure x from the free end. At distance x the bending moment magnitude is M(x) = P x, with P kept as a symbol for now.
  2. Write the energy: U = ∫₀² (P x)² / (2EI) dx = P² / (2EI) × [x³/3]₀² = P² × 8 / (6EI) = 4P² / (3EI).
  3. Differentiate with P still symbolic: ∂U/∂P = 8P / (3EI).
  4. Substitute P = 6 kN and EI = 3000 kN·m²: ∂U/∂P = 8 × 6 / (3 × 3000) = 48 / 9000 = 0.005333 m.
  5. Convert to millimetres: 0.005333 m × 1000 = 5.333 mm. The sign is positive, so the deflection is downward, the same sense as P.
  6. Check with the standard cantilever result PL³/(3EI) = 6 × 2³ / (3 × 3000) = 48/9000 = 0.005333 m. Same value.

Answer. The free-end deflection is 5.333 mm downward.

Exam traps

  • Differentiating after putting the numeric value of P into U. U then has no P in it and the derivative is zero; keep P symbolic until the derivative is done.
  • Dropping the factor 2 in the denominator of U = ∫M²/(2EI)dx and using M²/(EI); the energy doubles, so every deflection then comes out twice as large.
  • Forgetting to square M, or squaring only part of it when two loads act; square the total moment after superposition.
  • Confusing Castigliano's theorem (derivative with respect to a load) with Maxwell's theorem (equality of two reciprocal deflections). They answer different questions.
  • Losing units: with P in kN and EI in kN·m², the derivative is in metres, not millimetres; convert at the end.

5.8 Statically indeterminate beams: propped cantilever, fixed, continuous

A beam with more supports than equilibrium needs is statically indeterminate. The extra support adds an unknown reaction, so equilibrium alone cannot find all reactions. The missing equation comes from compatibility: the deflection at the extra support must equal its known movement, usually zero. Engineers meet this in continuous floor beams, bridge girders and machine bases, where fewer supports would make the beam too flexible. This chapter solves a propped cantilever and a fixed beam by choosing one reaction as redundant, then uses Clapeyron's theorem of three moments for continuous beams.

You need: slope and deflection by superposition and strain energy methods; support reactions and bending moments from Unit 3.

Why an extra support needs compatibility

A planar beam under transverse loading has two useful equilibrium equations: sum of vertical forces and sum of moments. If axial force is ignored, the degree of static indeterminacy is D = r − 2, where r is the number of vertical reaction components and end moments, and 2 is the number of equilibrium equations. A simply supported beam has r = 2, so D = 0. A propped cantilever has r = 3, so D = 1. A fixed beam has r = 4, so D = 2.

The extra unknown is called a redundant. Remove it and the beam becomes determinate; that is the primary beam. The redundant is found by compatibility: the real deflection at that support must equal the known support movement, usually zero. This is the compatibility method.

  1. Choose one extra reaction as the redundant, such as the prop reaction or an end moment.
  2. Remove the redundant to get a determinate primary beam.
  3. Find the deflection at the redundant point due to the applied loads.
  4. Find the deflection at the same point due to a unit value of the redundant.
  5. Set the total deflection equal to the known support settlement and solve for the redundant.
  6. Use equilibrium on the real beam to find the remaining reactions and moments.

For a propped cantilever the prop reaction at B is a natural redundant. For a fixed beam the two end moments are natural redundants. For a continuous beam the interior support moment is the redundant chosen in the three-moment theorem.

Propped cantilever: prop reaction and fixed moment

Take the vertical reaction at the simple support B as redundant RB. The primary beam is a cantilever fixed at A, free at B. For a UDL w over span L, the free end of the cantilever moves down by δload = wL⁴/(8EI). The upward prop reaction RB moves the free end up by δprop = RBL³/(3EI). Compatibility at B gives δprop = δload.

R<sub>B</sub> = 3wL/8; M<sub>A</sub> = wL²/8 (hogging)

w is the UDL in kN/m; L is the span in m; RB is the prop reaction in kN; MA is the fixed-end moment at A in kN·m; EI is flexural rigidity in kN·m².

The other reaction follows from vertical equilibrium: RA = wL − RB = 5wL/8. The fixed-end moment is obtained by taking moments about A: MA = wL²/2 − RBL, which reduces to wL²/8. If B settles downward by δ, compatibility becomes wL⁴/(8EI) − RBL³/(3EI) = δ, so RB = 3wL/8 − 3EIδ/L³. The same result comes from Macaulay's method with the prop reaction left as an unknown.

Fixed beams and continuous beams

A fixed beam has both ends rotation-fixed: the slope at each end is zero, but the end moment is not. Under a central point load P, symmetry gives equal end moments. The standard result is MA = MB = PL/8, hogging, with reactions P/2 each and midspan deflection PL³/(192EI). Here P is the point load in kN, L is the span in m, and EI is flexural rigidity in kN·m².

A continuous beam has more than two supports. Clapeyron's theorem of three moments relates the moments at three consecutive supports. For spans AB = L1 and BC = L2, with sagging moments positive, the theorem is:

M<sub>A</sub>L<sub>1</sub> + 2M<sub>B</sub>(L<sub>1</sub>+L<sub>2</sub>) + M<sub>C</sub>L<sub>2</sub> = −6(A<sub>1</sub>x<sub>1</sub>/L<sub>1</sub> + A<sub>2</sub>x<sub>2</sub>/L<sub>2</sub>) − 6EI[(δ<sub>B</sub>−δ<sub>A</sub>)/L<sub>1</sub> + (δ<sub>B</sub>−δ<sub>C</sub>)/L<sub>2</sub>]

MA, MB, MC are support moments in kN·m; L1 and L2 are span lengths in m; A1 is the area of the free bending-moment diagram of span AB and A2 that of span BC; x1 is the distance of A1's centroid from A, x2 the distance of A2's centroid from C; δA, δB, δC are downward support settlements in m; EI is flexural rigidity in kN·m².

At a simply supported end M = 0. At a fixed end M is unknown; zero rotation supplies the extra equation. At an overhang, the known overhang load gives a known end moment. Apply the equation to each pair of adjacent spans, then solve the simultaneous equations.

Worked example

Question. A propped cantilever AB of span 4 m is fixed at A and simply supported at B. It carries a uniformly distributed load w = 6 kN/m over the whole span. EI = 5000 kN·m². Find the prop reaction at B and the fixed-end moment at A. Ignore axial deformation.

  1. Choose the vertical prop reaction RB as the redundant.
  2. Remove the prop. The primary beam is a cantilever fixed at A with UDL w = 6 kN/m over L = 4 m. Free-end deflection downward is δload = wL⁴/(8EI) = 6×4⁴/(8×5000) = 1536/40000 = 0.0384 m.
  3. The upward prop reaction RB gives free-end deflection upward δprop = RBL³/(3EI) = RB×4³/(3×5000) = RB×64/15000.
  4. Compatibility at B: there is no deflection at the simple support, so δprop = δload. Thus RB×64/15000 = 0.0384, giving RB = 0.0384×15000/64 = 9 kN upward.
  5. Vertical equilibrium: RA + RB = wL = 6×4 = 24 kN, so RA = 15 kN.
  6. Take moments about A: MA = wL²/2 − RBL = 6×4²/2 − 9×4 = 48 − 36 = 12 kN·m. It is hogging because it opposes sagging.

Answer. The prop reaction at B is 9 kN upward, and the fixed-end moment at A is 12 kN·m hogging.

Exam traps

  • Treating a propped cantilever as simply supported and using equilibrium alone; the prop reaction needs a compatibility equation.
  • Choosing a redundant but forgetting to remove its reaction when writing the primary-beam load case.
  • Mixing sign conventions: keep sagging positive in the three-moment equation, then hogging moments come out negative.
  • Dropping the settlement term or using support settlements with the wrong sign; equal settlement of all supports must produce no extra moment.
  • Using EI in N·mm² while w is in kN/m and L is in m; convert all quantities to one consistent set of units.

There are four unknowns: two vertical reactions and two end moments. Only two equilibrium equations are useful, so two extra equations are needed, one from each fixed-end slope condition.

Takeaways

  1. The elastic curve obeys EI d²y/dx² = M, so bending moment and curvature are the same story told in different units.
  2. Double integration is the base method; every other method is a shortcut for the constants C₁ and C₂.
  3. Macaulay's brackets let one moment equation describe a beam with many loads, which saves algebra but demands careful sign handling.
  4. The moment-area theorems convert an integral into an area and a first moment, which is fast for one or two deflections.
  5. The conjugate beam turns slope and deflection into shear and moment, so a beam you already know solves a problem you do not.
  6. Superposition works only while the material is linear elastic and deflections stay small enough not to change the load geometry.
  7. Energy methods give the deflection at a chosen point directly, without ever finding the full elastic curve.
  8. In an indeterminate beam, the extra support is removed to get a determinate base beam, and compatibility of deflection supplies the missing equation.

Glossary

Continuous beam
A single beam running over three or more supports, so the intermediate moments are statically indeterminate.
Contraflexure
A point where the bending moment changes sign, so the curvature reverses and the elastic curve has an inflection.
Deflection
The vertical displacement of a point on the axis of a beam measured from its unloaded position, in metres or millimetres.
Degree of indeterminacy
The number of extra unknown reactions beyond the available equilibrium equations.
Determinate beam
A beam whose support reactions can all be found from static equilibrium alone.
Dummy load
A fictitious load applied at a point where no real load acts, so that Castigliano's theorem yields the deflection there; set to zero after differentiation.
Fixed end moment
The bending moment that develops at a built-in end of a beam when rotation there is prevented.
Flexibility coefficient
The deflection at one point due to a unit load at another point; Maxwell's theorem says the matrix of these is symmetric.
Hogging moment
A bending moment that curves the beam concave downward, producing tension at the top and compression at the bottom.
Indeterminate beam
A beam with more unknown reactions than equilibrium equations, so compatibility of deformation is also needed.
Leaf spring
A beam made of stacked curved plates, analysed as a cantilever or simply supported beam in bending, common in older vehicle suspension.
Linear elastic
A material response in which stress is proportional to strain and all deformation disappears on unloading.
Principal of virtual work
The principle that external work done by a virtual unit load equals internal work, the basis of the unit load method.
Propped cantilever
A beam fixed at one end and simply supported at the other, indeterminate to the first degree.
Radius of curvature
The radius ρ of the circular arc that best fits the elastic curve at a section; equals EI/M.
Redundant
A reaction or internal force chosen as the unknown to be removed, solved for, and then restored.
Sagging moment
A bending moment that curves the beam concave upward, producing compression at the top and tension at the bottom.
Second moment of area
The geometric property I of a cross-section about its bending axis, in m⁴, which resists bending.
Settlement
Downward movement of a support, which enters an indeterminate analysis as a known non-zero deflection at that support.
Slope
The angle the tangent to the elastic curve makes with the original axis, in radians, equal to dy/dx.
Small deflection theory
The assumption that slopes are small enough for (dy/dx)² to be neglected compared with 1, so curvature ≈ d²y/dx².
Spring constant of a beam
The load per unit deflection at a chosen point, in N/m, equal to the reciprocal of the flexibility there.
Theorem of three moments
An equation relating the moments at three successive supports of a continuous beam to the loads, spans and settlements.
Young's modulus
The ratio of direct stress to direct strain in the linear elastic range, in N/m².
