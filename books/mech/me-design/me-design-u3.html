3.1 Shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads

A shaft rarely carries one load. A gear pulls sideways, causing bending. Power flow causes torsion. A helical gear or worm adds axial thrust. All three stresses act at the same point. Static design uses the maximum shear stress theory: combine bending and axial normal stresses with torsional shear to get the principal shear, then size the diameter. This is how shafts in pumps, gearboxes and conveyors are checked before drawing.

You need: direct, bending and torsional stresses and principal stresses from Unit 1; factor of safety from Unit 2.

The three loads and their stresses

Draw a free-body diagram of the shaft. A gear at mid-span gives a tangential force that produces the torque T and also bends the shaft; the radial force bends it further. Together they give the bending moment M at the section. A helical gear or worm also gives an axial thrust P along the shaft axis. For a solid shaft of diameter d, area A = πd²/4. Bending stress σ_b = 32M/(πd³). Torsional shear τ = 16T/(πd³). Direct stress σ_a = P/A = 4P/(πd²). Take tension positive and compression negative.

For a hollow shaft with outer diameter D and inner diameter d_i, let k = d_i/D. Area A = π/4(D² − d_i²). Section modulus Z = π/32(D⁴ − d_i⁴)/D. Polar modulus Z_p = π/16(D⁴ − d_i⁴)/D. Then σ_b = M/Z, τ = T/Z_p and σ_a = P/A. At a surface point, bending and axial stresses act along the shaft axis; torsion acts as shear. Bending tension occurs on one side and compression on the other, so check the side where axial stress adds to bending.

Combining stresses and choosing diameter

For ductile shaft material, static failure is by shear. At the critical surface point the normal stress is σ_x = σ_b + σ_a (with sign) and the shear stress is τ. The other normal stress σ_y is zero. Principal stresses are σ_1,2 = σ_x/2 ± √((σ_x/2)² + τ²). Maximum shear stress is τ_max = √((σ_x/2)² + τ²) = ½√(σ_x² + 4τ²). Set τ_max ≤ τ_allow, where τ_allow is the allowable shear stress (yield shear strength divided by factor of safety, or given).

Without axial load (P = 0), σ_x = 32M/(πd³) and τ = 16T/(πd³). Then τ_max = 16/(πd³)√(M² + T²). Define equivalent torque T_e = √(M² + T²), so τ_max = 16T_e/(πd³). With shock factors K_b and K_t from the ASME code, use K_b M and K_t T in T_e. For a hollow shaft τ_max = 16T_e D/(π(D⁴ − d_i⁴)); that is, d³ in the solid formula becomes (D⁴ − d_i⁴)/D. Solve for D. Design steps: find M from the bending-moment diagram, find T from power and speed (T = power/ω, ω = 2πN/60), identify P, apply shock factors, compute τ_max, solve for d or D, then round to a standard size.

Worked example

Question. A solid steel shaft transmits 15 kW at 900 rpm. It carries a steady bending moment of 300 N·m and an axial compressive load of 10 kN. The allowable shear stress is 40 MPa. Find the shaft diameter using the maximum shear stress theory. Take a shock factor of 1.5 on torque.

  1. Angular speed ω = 2πN/60 = 2π(900)/60 = 94.25 rad/s.
  2. Torque T = power/ω = 15000/94.25 = 159.15 N·m.
  3. Design torque T_d = K_t T = 1.5 × 159.15 = 238.73 N·m. Bending moment M = 300 N·m (no shock factor).
  4. Let d be diameter in metres. Bending stress magnitude σ_b = 32M/(πd³) = 32×300/(πd³) = 3055.8/d³ Pa.
  5. Direct compressive stress σ_a = 4P/(πd²) = 4×10000/(πd²) = 12732.4/d² Pa. On the compression side, bending compression and axial compression add: σ_x = −(σ_b + σ_a) = −(3055.8/d³ + 12732.4/d²) Pa.
  6. Torsional shear τ = 16T_d/(πd³) = 16×238.73/(πd³) = 1215.8/d³ Pa.
  7. Maximum shear stress τ_max = ½√(σ_x² + 4τ²). Set τ_max = 40×10⁶ Pa. Trial d = 0.0377 m gives τ_max ≈ 40.0 MPa. So d ≈ 37.7 mm. Adopt 38 mm.

Output

None

Answer. Minimum diameter about 37.7 mm, adopt 38 mm.

Question. Design a hollow shaft with inner-to-outer diameter ratio 0.5 for the same loading and allowable shear stress as the solid example. Find the outer diameter.

  1. Same T_d = 238.73 N·m, M = 300 N·m, P = 10 kN.

None

Answer. Outer diameter about 38.9 mm, adopt 39 mm; inner diameter 19.5 mm.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting that axial load adds a direct normal stress; using T_e = √(M² + T²) when P is not zero gives a wrong diameter.
  • Using diameter in mm in formulas that need metres, or mixing N·m with N·mm; keep all quantities in SI.
  • Mixing solid and hollow section formulas: for hollow use (D⁴ − d_i⁴)/D, not d³.
  • Adding axial stress with the wrong sign, or checking only one side of the shaft where bending tension and axial compression oppose each other.
  • Applying a shock factor to both bending and torsion when the problem specifies it only on torque.

3.2 Shaft design for rigidity and critical speed

A shaft can pass the strength check and still fail in service. If it bends too far, gear teeth mesh badly and bearings wear at one edge. If it spins near its lateral natural frequency, a tiny unbalance grows into violent whirl. So a shaft needs two more checks: rigidity, meaning limits on deflection and slope, and critical speed, the spin speed where whirl peaks. Pump shafts, turbine rotors and the motor shaft of a workshop blower all get this check before the drawing is released.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for shaft loading and combined stress, and Unit 1 for beam deflection by double integration and Macaulay's method.

Deflection and slope limits

Chapter 3.1 fixes the diameter from combined bending and torsion stress. The same diameter must also be stiff enough. Stiffness depends on the product EI, and I grows as d⁴, so doubling the diameter cuts deflection to one-sixteenth. A shaft that is strong enough can still be too flexible.

Two quantities are limited. Deflection y is the sideways movement in metres, checked at the gear or the rotor. Slope θ = dy/dx is in radians, checked at the bearings and at the gear mesh.

  • Slope at a bearing tilts the inner ring on the shaft, so the rolling elements load one edge and bearing life drops.
  • Slope at a gear mesh tilts the teeth against each other, contact moves to the tooth ends, and noise and pitting follow.
  • Deflection between gears changes the centre distance and the backlash; deflection at a seal changes the clearance, which can rub on one side.

Design offices commonly allow a slope of about 0.001 rad at a bearing and a deflection of a few hundredths to about a tenth of a millimetre at a gear mesh. Your design code fixes the exact number; the worked example uses 0.08 mm.

Deflection of a stepped shaft

δ = W L³ / (48 E I)

δ is the midspan deflection in metres; W is the load at midspan in newtons; L is the span between bearings in metres; E is Young's modulus in N/m²; I is the second moment of area of the section in m⁴.

That formula assumes a uniform simply supported shaft with one central load. Real shafts are stepped, so I changes from span to span and Macaulay's method from Unit 1 is the hand tool.

  1. Draw the free body and find the bearing reactions; gear and pulley forces come from Unit 7.
  2. Write one bending moment equation M(x) for the whole length, using Macaulay brackets so each load starts acting only after its own position.
  3. Integrate twice with the correct I for each span: E I y″ = M(x), then E I y′ = ∫M dx + C₁ and E I y = ∫∫M dx dx + C₁x + C₂.
  4. Apply y = 0 at both bearings and continuity of y and slope at every step, solve for the constants, and read y at the rotor and y′ at the bearings.

A shaft with three or four diameters takes about half a page of hand algebra. A spreadsheet or a beam element code does the same in seconds and is the normal route in industry.

Critical speed and whirl

No rotor is perfectly balanced. Its centre of mass lies a small distance e from the shaft's geometric centre. At spin speed ω rad/s the centre of mass is at radius y + e from the rotation axis, so the centrifugal force is m(y + e)ω². The bent shaft pushes back with k y, where k is the lateral stiffness in N/m and y is the whirl amplitude in metres. Equating forces gives the undamped whirl amplitude y = e r²/(1 − r²), where r = ω/ωn and ωn = √(k/m) is the lateral natural frequency in rad/s. With damping ratio ζ, y = e r²/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²). As r reaches 1 the amplitude peaks. At high speed r ≫ 1 the shaft rotates about its centre of mass and the magnitude of y tends to e.

Damping keeps the peak finite, but a shaft run at its critical speed still shakes hard enough to wipe a bearing in minutes. Keep the running speed at least about 25 % away from Nc, or pass through it quickly while starting.

For a simply supported shaft with the rotor at midspan, k = 48EI/L³ and the static deflection is δ = W/k, so k/m = g/δ.

N_c = (60 / 2π) √(g / δ)

N_c is the first critical speed in rpm; δ is the static deflection at the rotor in metres; g = 9.81 m/s²; the factor 60/2π = 9.5493 converts rad/s to rpm.

A rotor that sags 1 mm has Nc near 946 rpm; at 0.07 mm it is about 3575 rpm. Halving δ raises Nc by √2, so a small gain in stiffness buys a large gain in speed.

Rayleigh's method handles several masses. Guess a deflection shape y(x), for example by loading the shaft with all the weights at once, read yi at each mass, and compute ωn = √(g ΣWi yi / ΣWi yi²), with Wi in newtons and yi in metres. The guess gives a frequency slightly above the true one.

Dunkerley's method adds the separate effects: 1/Nc² ≈ 1/N₁² + 1/N₂² + …, where Ni is the critical speed with mass i alone. It gives a value slightly below the true one. Only a few universities teach it.

Critical speed here is lateral: it comes from bending stiffness EI and the mass. A shaft also has torsional natural frequencies, set by the torsional stiffness GJ and the polar mass moment of inertia; a torsional resonance twists the shaft with no sideways movement. These are different numbers and are checked separately.

Worked example

Question. A steel shaft of diameter 60 mm and span 1.2 m between bearings carries a rotor of mass 25 kg at midspan. Take E = 200 GPa and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the static deflection at midspan and the first critical speed, and state whether the deflection is below the permitted 0.08 mm. Neglect the shaft's own mass.

  1. Second moment of area: I = πd⁴/64 = π(0.060 m)⁴/64 = 6.362 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  2. Rotor weight: W = m g = 25 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 245.25 N.
  3. Static deflection: δ = W L³/(48 E I). Numerator = 245.25 × 1.2³ = 423.8 N·m³. Denominator = 48 × 200 × 10⁹ × 6.362 × 10⁻⁷ = 6.107 × 10⁶ N·m². δ = 423.8 / 6.107 × 10⁶ = 6.939 × 10⁻⁵ m = 0.0694 mm.
  4. Check: 0.0694 mm < 0.08 mm, so the deflection is within the permitted limit.
  5. Critical speed: N_c = (60/2π) √(g/δ) = 9.5493 × √(9.81 / 6.939 × 10⁻⁵) = 9.5493 × 375.99 = 3590.5 rpm.
  6. The shaft's own mass is neglected; counting it would add to δ and lower N_c slightly.

Exam traps

  • Putting δ in millimetres into N_c = (60/2π)√(g/δ) while g is in m/s² makes the answer √1000 ≈ 31.6 times too small. Convert δ to metres first.
  • ω_n = √(g/δ) is in rad/s. Multiply by 60/(2π) to get rpm; skipping that step is a standard mark loss.
  • Critical speed is a speed, not a stress limit. The answer must end in rpm, and a shaft can be strong and still whirl.
  • δ = W L³/(48 E I) holds only for a central load on a simply supported span. For the shaft's own weight as a uniformly distributed load w, use δ = 5wL⁴/(384 E I).
  • Lateral critical speed (bending, E and I) is not torsional resonance (twisting, G and J). Read which one the question asks.

Understand What happens to whirl amplitude above the critical speed? 3 marks

Above critical speed the shaft rotates about its centre of mass. The whirl amplitude tends to the eccentricity e; it does not fall to zero. The deflection remains about e, small only because e is small, so bearing forces are small and the shaft runs smoothly.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Large turbomachinery is checked with full rotor dynamics software that adds gyroscopic stiffening, oil-film bearing stiffness and damping, and computes a Campbell diagram of natural frequency against speed. Passing through a critical speed during run-up is routine in steam turbines and is handled by rapid acceleration. Standards such as API 617 set how many lateral and torsional modes must be shown clear of the operating speed. Search terms to start with: rotor dynamics, Campbell diagram, Dunkerley's method, API 617 lateral analysis.

3.3 Keys, keyways and splines

A key is a small piece of metal that sits in a groove cut in the shaft and in the hub of a gear, pulley or coupling. It locks the two parts so the shaft can drive the hub without slipping. A spline does the same job with many teeth, so it carries more torque. Keys allow quick assembly and repair. You meet them in every electric motor, pump, gearbox and bicycle crank. Their size is small, but a failed key stops the whole machine.

You need: torsion and shear stress from Chapter 3.1, and fits and crushing stress from Unit 1.

Key types, keyways and fit

A keyway is the slot cut in the shaft and in the hub. The key sits in the slot. A sunk key is partly in the shaft and partly in the hub; it is the common rectangular or square key. A square key has width w equal to height h. A saddle key sits on the shaft surface and depends on friction; use it for light loads. A round key is a short pin in a drilled hole; it locates a part or carries small torque. A spline is a set of many keys cut as teeth on the shaft.

  • Sunk key: rectangular or square, slots in shaft and hub.
  • Saddle key: no shaft slot, friction drive, light duty.
  • Round key: drilled hole, small torque or location.
  • Spline: many teeth, high torque, sliding hub possible.

Key material is usually mild steel for light duty and alloy steel for high torque. The key must be snug sideways in the keyway, with a small top clearance for a sunk key so the hub can sit on the shaft shoulder. Standard tables link shaft diameter to key width and height. Check the table in the design handbook before choosing a key.

Force and stresses in a rectangular key

Torque T on the shaft is passed to the key at the shaft surface. The tangential force F acts at radius d/2, so T = F d/2. Here T is torque in N·mm, F is force in N, and d is shaft diameter in mm. If power P in W and speed N in rpm are given, first find T = P × 60/(2πN) with T in N·m, then convert to N·mm.

T = F d/2 → F = 2T/d

T is torque in N·mm; F is tangential force on the key in N; d is shaft diameter in mm.

The key fails in two ways. It may shear across the plane between shaft and hub. The shear area is w L, so τ = F/(w L). It may crush on the side that bears against the keyway. Only half the key height is in the hub, so the crushing area is (h/2) L and σc = F/(h L/2). Here w is key width in mm, h is key height in mm, L is effective key length in mm, τ is shear stress in N/mm² or MPa, and σc is crushing stress in MPa. Check both against allowable values.

τ = F/(w L)

τ is shear stress in MPa; F is tangential force in N; w is key width in mm; L is key length in mm.

σc = F/(h L/2)

σc is crushing stress in MPa; F is tangential force in N; h is key height in mm; L is key length in mm.

The keyway cuts the shaft section and adds sharp corners. That raises stress in torsion and fatigue. A shaft with one keyway is weaker than a plain shaft of the same diameter; design handbooks give a factor to allow for this. Do not use the full shaft diameter without allowance when a keyway is present.

Splines and keyway effect on the shaft

Splines are many keys cut as teeth. A straight-sided spline has parallel sides; an involute spline has curved flanks like a gear tooth. Both let a hub slide along the shaft while carrying torque; gearbox sliding gears use this. Load per tooth is lower than a single key. Detailed spline stress analysis is taught in only a few universities; most syllabi stop at the idea of many-key joints. The keyway root is a stress raiser. Use a fillet at the corner where possible. For a single keyway, a common design check is to reduce the shaft's torsional strength by about one-fifth. This is a design allowance, not a precise rule; follow the handbook value for the corner radius and material.

Worked example

Question. A 50 mm diameter shaft transmits 20 kW at 600 rpm through a rectangular key 14 mm wide by 9 mm high and 70 mm long. The key material has allowable shear stress 40 MPa and allowable crushing stress 80 MPa. Check the key for shear and crushing.

  1. Angular speed ω = 2πN/60 = 2π × 600/60 = 62.83 rad/s.
  2. Torque T = P/ω = 20,000/62.83 = 318.31 N·m = 318,310 N·mm.
  3. Key force F = T/(d/2) = 318,310/25 = 12,732.4 N.
  4. Shear area As = w L = 14 × 70 = 980 mm². Shear stress τ = F/As = 12,732.4/980 = 12.99 MPa. This is less than 40 MPa, so the key is safe in shear.
  5. Crushing area Ac = (h/2) L = (9/2) × 70 = 315 mm². Crushing stress σc = F/Ac = 12,732.4/315 = 40.42 MPa. This is less than 80 MPa, so the key is safe in crushing.

Answer. The key is safe: shear stress is 12.99 MPa (limit 40 MPa) and crushing stress is 40.42 MPa (limit 80 MPa).

Exam traps

  • Using the full key height h in crushing. The bearing area is hL/2, because the key bears on half its height in the hub and half in the shaft.
  • Taking key force as T/d. The force acts at the shaft surface, so F = T/(d/2) = 2T/d.
  • Forgetting the keyway. A keyway removes material and adds a sharp corner, so the shaft is weaker in torsion than a plain shaft.
  • Checking only shear. A key often fails by crushing first; check both shear and crushing.
  • Using a key length longer than the hub. The effective length is the contact length in the hub, not the total length of the key.

3.4 Rigid and flexible couplings

A coupling is a permanent or semi-permanent joint between two shafts. It passes torque from a motor to a pump, from a gearbox to a conveyor, or from a turbine to a generator. A rigid coupling holds the two shafts in line and carries high torque with simple parts. A flexible coupling also passes torque but allows a small parallel, angular or axial misalignment and softens shock. Couplings sit behind the shaft and key: the shaft is designed first, the key fixes the hub, and the coupling joins the next shaft.

You need: shaft torsion design, keys and keyways, and Unit 1 fits and tolerances.

Rigid couplings: sleeve and flange

A rigid coupling is used when the two shafts can be lined up within a small tolerance. The sleeve coupling is a single hollow cylinder, keyed to both shaft ends. It is cheap and short, but it needs long keys and is awkward to remove. The flange coupling has two flanges, each keyed to one shaft, bolted face to face. The torque path is shaft, key, flange, bolts, second flange, key, second shaft. Some syllabi call the flange coupling a rigid coupling, and design it as one.

Use a service factor Ks for the driven machine. A smooth centrifugal pump may use Ks = 1.25 to 1.5; a crusher or a reversing mill may need 2 or more. The design torque is Td = Ks T. Transmitted torque from power P and speed N is:

T = P × 60 ÷ (2πN)

T is torque in N·m; P is power in W; N is shaft speed in rev/min; π = 3.1416.

The bolts sit on a pitch circle of radius R. If there are n bolts, and each takes equal load, the force on one bolt is Fb = Td ÷ (nR). Check the bolt in shear: τb = Fb ÷ (π db²/4) ≤ τallow, where db is bolt diameter. Check the bolt in crushing: σc = Fb ÷ (db t) ≤ σc,allow, where t is the flange thickness. The shaft is checked in torsion as in 3.1: τ = 16 Td ÷ (π d³) ≤ τallow.

Flexible couplings and alignment

A flexible coupling passes torque while allowing a small misalignment. Misalignment is measured in three ways: parallel offset between shaft centre lines, angular gap between centre lines, and axial float. Real machines have all three after thermal growth, foundation settlement or poor assembly. A flexible coupling protects bearings from these small errors and reduces shock from starts and stops.

The bushed pin coupling is a flange coupling with rubber or leather bushes on the bolts. The bush takes the bolt force over a larger area, gives torsional flexibility, and allows small parallel and angular misalignment. It is common on motor-driven pumps and conveyors. The Oldham coupling uses two hubs with slots and a central disc with matching tongues. It allows parallel offset without angular error and is used in machine tools and printing presses.

The universal joint allows angular misalignment, often up to 15° to 20° per joint. A single joint gives a fluctuating output speed when the input speed is constant. Two joints, arranged with equal angles and the yokes phased, cancel most of this fluctuation. Universal joints appear in vehicle propeller shafts and rolling mill drives. Flexible couplings are not a cure for large errors; beyond their rated misalignment, bearing loads rise quickly.

Align the shafts before the coupling is tightened. A straight edge and feeler gauge find rough parallel offset; a dial gauge on each hub finds angular and parallel error; laser alignment is now common. Check hot and cold alignment: a pump that runs at 90 °C grows upward after start. The coupling must also be balanced. An unbalanced coupling gives a once-per-revolution force, felt as vibration at shaft speed. Balance is more critical above 3000 rpm. Keep the keyway and key mass symmetrical, and use the same bolt grade and tightened torque on all bolts.

Worked example

Question. A rigid flange coupling connects a 30 kW, 1440 rpm motor to a machine shaft. The shaft diameter is 40 mm. Four bolts on a 120 mm pitch circle transmit the torque. The allowable shear stress in the bolts is 30 MPa and in the shaft is 40 MPa. Find the required bolt diameter and check the shaft. Use a service factor of 1.5.

  1. Transmitted torque: T = P × 60 ÷ (2πN) = 30 000 × 60 ÷ (2 × π × 1440) = 198.94 N·m.
  2. Design torque: Td = Ks T = 1.5 × 198.94 = 298.42 N·m.
  3. Pitch circle radius: R = 120 ÷ 2 = 60 mm = 0.060 m.
  4. Bolt force: Fb = Td ÷ (nR) = 298.42 ÷ (4 × 0.060) = 1243.4 N. Each bolt carries this tangential force.
  5. Bolt shear: τb = Fb ÷ (π db²/4). Set τb ≤ 30 MPa = 30 × 10⁶ N/m². So db ≥ √(4Fb ÷ (π τallow)) = √(4 × 1243.4 ÷ (π × 30 × 10⁶)) = 0.00726 m = 7.26 mm.
  6. Select M10. A larger bolt is chosen for standard size and crushing; the shear requirement is 7.26 mm. Crushing is checked by σc = Fb ÷ (db t) when flange thickness t and the allowable crushing stress are known.
  7. Shaft shear: τ = 16Td ÷ (πd³) = 16 × 298.42 ÷ (π × 0.040³) = 23.75 × 10⁶ N/m² = 23.75 MPa. This is below 40 MPa. A keyway raises local stress, so in a real check use the keyway factor from 3.3.

Answer. Bolt diameter required is 7.26 mm (use M10), and the 40 mm shaft has shear stress 23.75 MPa, so it is safe.

Exam traps

  • Using pitch circle diameter instead of radius in Fb = Td ÷ (nR). With D = 120 mm, R = 60 mm, not 120 mm. Using D gives half the bolt force and an unsafe design.
  • Forgetting the service factor, or applying it to power in one step and then using the rated torque for the bolts. Carry Td = Ks T through every check.
  • Treating a flexible coupling as a repair for bad alignment. It allows only its rated offset; large misalignment loads the bearings.
  • Checking bolt shear with the bush outside diameter instead of the bolt diameter. The bush spreads pressure, but the bolt shank still carries the shear.
  • Assuming a single universal joint gives constant output speed. It does not; two joints with equal angles and phased yokes are needed.

3.5 Clutches and brakes

A clutch is a friction joint you can switch on and off. It lets a running engine take up the load of a stationary gearbox without shock. A brake is the same joint with one side held still: it turns kinetic energy into heat until the wheel stops. Both are judged by the torque they carry and by the heat their rubbing surfaces can absorb. You meet them in a motorcycle, a car, a lathe, a lift and a train.

You need friction, power as torque × angular speed and unit conversion from Unit 1, plus shaft torque from Chapter 3.1; torque flow through a drivetrain is in Chapter 3.4.

One plate, many plates, and what slips

A coupling joins two shafts permanently. A clutch joins them only when you ask it to. Press the pedal and the engine spins free of the gearbox; release it and friction drags both sides to the same speed. That dragging is slip, and slip is deliberate: it lets a car start from rest without a jolt.

The rubbing parts sit in a stack. In a single-plate clutch a steel plate carries friction lining on both faces. A spring pushes it against the flywheel on one side and the pressure plate on the other, so two rubbing pairs share the axial force W, the spring force in newtons. Find the torque for one pair, then multiply by the number of pairs.

Count every friction surface, including the flywheel face and the pressure-plate face. A stack of p such members gives p − 1 rubbing pairs; the familiar single plate lined on both faces, clamped between flywheel and pressure plate, is three members and so gives 2 pairs. Stack more plates and the same W buys more torque, which keeps motorcycle and machine-tool clutches short. Running the plates in oil keeps them cool and quiet: a wet clutch.

A cone clutch replaces flat faces with a shallow cone, so the wedge effect gives a larger normal force for the same W. A centrifugal clutch lets spring-loaded shoes fly outward as speed rises and engages by itself, which is why small scooters have no clutch lever.

Uniform wear and uniform pressure

Friction torque depends on how the pressure is spread over the ring between r_i, the inner radius in metres, and r_o, the outer radius in metres. Two assumptions are used; take the one the question names.

A new clutch has pressure nearly uniform over the face. Wear rate goes as pressure × sliding speed, and sliding speed is ωr, so the outer lining wears fastest. After running in, the face settles into a state where pressure × radius is constant — uniform wear — and the highest pressure then sits at r_i, the smallest radius.

T = μ W (r_o + r_i) / 2

T is the friction torque per rubbing pair in N·m; μ is the coefficient of friction (no unit); W is the axial force in N; r_o and r_i are outer and inner radii in m.

A clutch that is new, or rarely used, has not worn into shape, so its pressure is nearly uniform over the face.

T = (2/3) μ W (r_o³ − r_i³) / (r_o² − r_i²)

the symbols are as above; the radii are in m and the bracket has units of m, so T comes out in N·m per rubbing pair.

Both formulas assume μ is constant across the face. Uniform pressure gives slightly more torque than uniform wear for the same W and radii. Use uniform wear for any clutch in service, and for brakes almost always. Multiply by n, the number of rubbing pairs: a single-plate clutch has n = 2.

Brakes: friction with one side held still

A brake holds one friction member still. The drum or disc turns with the wheel; the block or pad is fixed to the frame and pushed on by a lever, cam or hydraulic piston. The torque relation is the same: T = μ N r, with N the normal force on the drum in N and r the drum radius in m.

A block brake is a lever with a block pressing one spot of the drum. Moments about the lever pivot give N, so the lever lengths set the braking torque. If the friction force helps apply the block, the brake is self-energizing; pushed too far it becomes self-locking and will not release.

A shoe brake curves the lining around a larger arc of the drum, so the same pressure gives more torque; scooter and truck drum brakes work this way. A disc brake presses flat pads on a rotating disc, with r taken at the mean radius of the pad.

Every stop turns kinetic energy into heat in the lining and drum. For steady slipping:

E = T Δω t

E is the heat generated in J; T is the friction torque in N·m; Δω is the relative angular speed between the two members in rad/s; t is the slip time in s.

That heat must first be stored in the drum and lining, then carried away by air. When it cannot, the lining gets hot, μ falls and the brake fades — a real worry on a long downhill run. Brakes are sized by energy per stop and by how often stops repeat, not by torque alone.

Worked example

Question. A single-plate clutch has outer radius 150 mm and inner radius 100 mm. The coefficient of friction is 0.3 and the axial force is 5 kN. Find the torque capacity by uniform wear theory. Find the power transmitted at 1200 rpm. Estimate the heat per engagement if the clutch slips for 0.5 s, starting at a relative speed of 600 rpm.

  1. Convert to SI: r_o = 0.15 m, r_i = 0.10 m, W = 5000 N.
  2. Count the rubbing pairs: a single plate lined on both faces, clamped between flywheel and pressure plate, gives n = 2.
  3. Torque per pair: T₁ = μ W (r_o + r_i)/2 = 0.3 × 5000 × 0.125 = 187.5 N·m.
  4. Total torque: T = 2 × 187.5 = 375 N·m.
  5. Engine speed: ω = 2πN/60 = 2π × 1200/60 = 125.66 rad/s.
  6. Power: P = T ω = 375 × 125.66 = 47124 W, about 47.1 kW.
  7. Initial relative speed: Δω = 2π × 600/60 = 62.83 rad/s.
  8. The friction torque stays roughly constant, but the relative speed falls linearly from 62.83 rad/s to zero, so the mean relative speed is half the initial value. E ≈ T (Δω/2) t = 375 × 62.83 × 0.5 × 0.5 ≈ 5890 J.

Output

E = T Δω t is right only for steady slipping, as when a brake holds a constant speed.

Answer. The clutch carries 375 N·m, transmits about 47.1 kW at 1200 rpm, and each engagement turns about 5.9 kJ into heat.

Exam traps

  • Using the uniform pressure formula on a worn-in clutch, and forgetting the number of rubbing pairs — a single-plate clutch has n = 2, not 1.
  • Putting millimetres straight into the torque formula. T comes out in N·m only when the radii are in metres.
  • In uniform wear, the maximum pressure is at the inner radius, not the outer one; the worn face has p r = constant.
  • Quoting engine speed where relative speed is needed. During a gear change the slip speed is the difference of the two side speeds, and it falls to zero.
  • For a block brake, forgetting that the normal force N comes from lever moments about the pivot, not from the force you apply on the pedal.

Not for the exam. Takeaways

  1. A transmission shaft usually carries bending and torsion at the same time, so its diameter is fixed from the equivalent torque or equivalent bending moment, not from either load alone.
  2. The ASME code lowers the allowable shear stress to 0.30 Syt or 0.18 Sut to cover keyway stress concentration and mild shock, so a code-designed shaft is larger than a purely theoretical one.
  3. A hollow shaft with ratio k has (1 − k⁴) less material doing the same work, which is why large marine and turbine shafts are bored out.
  4. Strength alone is not enough: twist, lateral deflection and critical speed must be checked, because a strong shaft can still vibrate or misalign the gears it carries.
  5. Keep the running speed at least about 25 percent away from the first critical speed, and remember Dunkerley's value is always on the safe (low) side.
  6. A key fails in shear along the shaft-hub interface or in crushing on its side face; the shorter of the two lengths decides the key length.
  7. Splines replace the key where torque is large or the hub must move; they spread the load over many teeth and avoid a single deep keyway.
  8. A rigid coupling demands good alignment and is cheap; a flexible coupling costs more but protects bearings from misalignment, thermal growth and shock.
  9. A clutch and a brake are the same friction problem seen twice: one joins two moving parts smoothly, the other turns kinetic energy into heat that must be removed.
  10. In both clutches and brakes, uniform wear theory is the design case and uniform pressure theory is the limiting new-lining case.

Glossary

Axle
A shaft-like element that supports rotating parts but transmits no torque.
Brake
A device that slows or stops a rotating part by absorbing its kinetic energy as heat.
Clutch
A device that connects or disconnects two rotating parts while running, by friction or by positive engagement.
Coupling
A device that joins two shaft ends and passes torque from one to the other.
Endurance limit
The stress amplitude below which a steel specimen survives indefinitely in reversed fatigue loading.
Fade
Loss of braking or clutch effectiveness when the lining overheats.
Friction radius
The single radius at which the total friction force may be assumed to act, giving T = μWrf.
Key
A removable metal piece that locks a hub to a shaft so they rotate together.
Keyway
The machined slot in a shaft or hub that receives a key.
Maximum normal stress theory
A failure criterion saying failure occurs at the largest principal normal stress; it gives the equivalent bending moment.
Maximum shear stress theory
A failure criterion saying yield occurs when the largest shear stress reaches the shear yield strength; used to get equivalent torque.
Misalignment
Departure from a common axis of two shafts, either parallel offset, angular or a combination.
Natural frequency
The frequency in cycles per second at which a system oscillates freely after a disturbance.
Polar second moment of area
J = πd⁴/32 for a solid round shaft; it measures resistance to twist, in mm⁴.
Principal stress
The normal stress on a plane where shear stress is zero; found from σb and τ acting together.
Service factor
A multiplier applied to the nominal torque to allow for shock and duty of the driven machine.
Shaft
A rotating machine element of circular cross-section that transmits power and supports pulleys, gears or rotors.
Spline
One of several integral axial teeth on a shaft that engage matching grooves in a hub.
Static deflection
The bending deflection of a shaft under its own weight and the weight of the parts it carries, used to estimate critical speed.
Stress concentration factor
Kt = peak stress divided by nominal stress, caused by a notch, hole, shoulder or keyway.
Torque
A turning moment in N·mm or N·m, equal to force times the perpendicular distance from the axis.
Torsional shear stress
The shear stress set up across a shaft section by torque, largest at the outer surface.
Universal joint
A flexible coupling with a cross-shaped spider that allows a large working angle between shafts.
Whirling
Large lateral vibration of a rotating shaft when its speed approaches a natural frequency.
