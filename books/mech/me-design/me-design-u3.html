3.1 Shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads

A shaft carries power from a motor to a machine. Its pulleys and gears bend it, the power twists it, and a helical gear or worm also pushes it along its axis. Static design finds the combined stress at the outer surface, compares it with an allowable stress from a failure theory, and picks a standard diameter.

Shafts, axles and the loads they carry

A shaft rotates and transmits torque. An axle carries rotating parts, such as the wheels of a railway wagon, but transmits no torque, so it is designed for bending only. A spindle is a short shaft, as in a lathe headstock.

The torque comes from the power: T = 60P/(2πN), with P in W, N in rpm and T in N·m. The bending moment comes from the forces on the shaft. A belt pulley of diameter D with tight-side tension T1 and slack-side tension T2 pulls the shaft with T1 + T2 and passes the torque (T1 − T2)D/2. A spur gear gives a tangential force Ft = 2T/D and a radial force Fr = Ft tan 20°; both bend the shaft, and Ft also gives the torque.

  1. Treat the shaft as a beam on its bearings; find the reactions and draw the bending moment diagram.
  2. If forces act in two planes, draw a diagram for each and combine them: M = √(MV² + MH²).
  3. The section with the largest M and T fixes the diameter.

Equivalent torque, equivalent moment and the ASME code

At the surface of a solid shaft of diameter d, bending gives σb = 32M/(πd³) along the axis and torque gives τ = 16T/(πd³). With σy = 0, the principal stresses are σ1,2 = σb/2 ± √((σb/2)² + τ²).

Maximum shear stress theory (Guest), used for ductile steel: τmax = √((σb/2)² + τ²) = 16√(M² + T²)/(πd³). The bracket is the equivalent torque:

Te = √(M² + T²); τallow = 16Te/(πd³)
Te is the torque that alone gives the same maximum shear stress as M and T together.

Maximum normal stress theory (Rankine), used for brittle material: σ1 = 16[M + √(M² + T²)]/(πd³). The equivalent bending moment is Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)], and σallow = 32Me/(πd³). Where both are asked, take the larger d.

The ASME code adds shock factors and an allowable stress. Use Kb M and Kt T in place of M and T: for a rotating shaft, Kb = 1.5 and Kt = 1.0 for gradual load, up to Kb = 3.0 and Kt = 3.0 for heavy shock. The allowable shear stress is the smaller of 0.30Syt and 0.18Sut, where Syt and Sut are the yield and ultimate tensile strengths, and it is cut by 25 % when the shaft has a keyway.

Axial load and hollow shafts

An axial force P adds a direct stress σa = 4P/(πd²). It changes σx, so Te no longer covers the case. Use σx = σb + σa and τmax = ½√(σx² + 4τ²). Bending makes one side of the shaft tensile and the other compressive, so the worst point is the side where the bending stress has the same sign as the axial stress: there |σx| = σb + σa.

A hollow shaft of outer diameter do and inner diameter di, with k = di/do, has τ = 16T/(πdo³(1 − k⁴)) and σb = 32M/(πdo³(1 − k⁴)), so Te and Me work as before with d³ replaced by do³(1 − k⁴). The axial area is πdo²(1 − k²)/4. The bored-out core carries little stress, so a hollow shaft is lighter for the same strength, which is why propeller shafts are hollow.

Worked example

Question. A shaft runs on two bearings A and B, 900 mm apart. A pulley of 400 mm diameter sits 300 mm from A and receives 10 kW at 500 rpm from a vertical belt with tension ratio T1/T2 = 3. The shaft is of steel with Syt = 380 MPa and Sut = 640 MPa and has a keyway. The load is gradually applied (Kb = 1.5, Kt = 1.0). Find the shaft diameter by the ASME code. Neglect the pulley weight.

  1. Torque: T = 60P/(2πN) = 60 × 10 000/(2π × 500) = 190.99 N·m.
  2. Belt tensions: (T1 − T2) × 0.2 m = 190.99, so T1 − T2 = 954.93 N. With T1 = 3T2: T2 = 477.46 N, T1 = 1432.39 N. Downward pull on the shaft W = T1 + T2 = 1909.86 N.
  3. Reactions: RA = W × 600/900 = 1273.24 N, RB = W × 300/900 = 636.62 N.
  4. Largest bending moment, under the pulley: M = RA × 0.3 = 381.97 N·m.
  5. Allowable shear stress: 0.30 × 380 = 114 MPa and 0.18 × 640 = 115.2 MPa; take 114 MPa. With the keyway, τ = 0.75 × 114 = 85.5 MPa.
  6. Equivalent torque: Te = √((Kb M)² + (Kt T)²) = √(572.96² + 190.99²) = 603.95 N·m = 603 950 N·mm.
  7. Diameter: d³ = 16Te/(πτ) = 16 × 603 950/(π × 85.5) = 35 973 mm³, so d = 33.0 mm.

Answer. d = 33.0 mm; adopt the next standard size, 35 mm. At 35 mm, τ = 71.7 MPa ≤ 85.5 MPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A hollow shaft (di/do = 0.5) transmits 15 kW at 900 rpm with Kt = 1.5, and carries a steady bending moment of 300 N·m and an axial compression of 10 kN. τallow = 40 MPa. Find do and compare its mass with the 38 mm solid shaft that suits the same duty.

  1. Torque: T = 15 000/(2π × 900/60) = 159.15 N·m; design torque 1.5 × 159.15 = 238.73 N·m.
  2. With k = 0.5 and do in m: A = 0.5890do², Z = (π/32)do³(1 − k⁴) = 0.09204do³, Zp = 2Z. So σb = 3259.5/do³, σa = 16 977/do² and τ = 1296.9/do³ (Pa).
  3. On the compression side, |σx| = σb + σa. Setting ½√(σx² + 4τ²) = 40 MPa and solving by trial gives do = 38.9 mm.
  4. Check with do = 39 mm: σb = 54.95 MPa, σa = 11.16 MPa, σx = 66.11 MPa, τ = 21.86 MPa, τmax = ½√(66.11² + 4 × 21.86²) = 39.6 MPa ≤ 40 MPa.
  5. Mass ratio for equal length: hollow area/solid area = 39² × 0.75/38² = 0.79.

Answer. do = 39 mm and di = 19.5 mm. The hollow shaft is about 21 % lighter than the 38 mm solid shaft.

Exam traps

  • Using Te = √(M² + T²) when an axial load acts. The axial stress changes σx, so work with σx = σb + σa and τmax = ½√(σx² + 4τ²).
  • Checking the side where bending tension and axial compression partly cancel. The design point is where they add.
  • Taking the belt pull as T1 − T2. The shaft is bent by T1 + T2; only the torque uses the difference.
  • Mixing units: with τ in MPa, Te must be in N·mm.

3.2 Shaft design for rigidity and critical speed

A shaft can pass the strength check and still be rejected. If it twists too much, gears and cams fall out of step; if it bends too much, gear teeth and bearings load on one edge; and if it spins near its lateral natural frequency, a small unbalance grows into violent whirl. So a shaft gets three more checks: torsional rigidity (angle of twist), lateral rigidity (deflection and slope) and critical speed. Machine-tool spindles, long line shafts and pump and fan rotors are often decided by these checks, not by stress.

You need: the torque and diameter from shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads, and beam deflection from strength of materials.

Torsional rigidity: the angle of twist

A torque T twists a shaft of length L through an angle θ = TL/(GJ) radians, where G is the shear modulus (about 79.3 GPa for steel) and J = πd⁴/32 is the polar second moment of area. The product GJ is the torsional rigidity. Converting to degrees for a solid shaft gives a handy design form:

θ° = 584TL/(Gd⁴)

with T in N·mm, L and d in mm and G in N/mm². The 584 is 32 × 180/π².

A common guide for machine-tool and other precision drives is 0.25° of twist per metre of length; line shafts are allowed more. Since θ falls as 1/d⁴, the rigidity diameter grows only as the fourth root of the load, but it is often larger than the strength diameter for long, lightly stressed shafts. A design answer finds d from strength and from rigidity and adopts the larger.

Lateral rigidity: deflection and slope

Bending deflects a shaft sideways by y and tilts it by a slope θ = dy/dx. Slope at a bearing tilts the inner ring so the rolling elements load one edge; slope at a gear moves the tooth contact to one end; deflection between gears changes backlash and centre distance. Design guides limit the slope at a rolling bearing to about 0.001 rad and the deflection at a gear to a few hundredths of a millimetre; the job specification gives the exact value.

δ = WL³/(48EI)

for a central load W on a simply supported span L; E is Young’s modulus (200 GPa for steel) and I = πd⁴/64. For the shaft’s own weight as a uniform load w per metre, δ = 5wL⁴/(384EI).

A stepped shaft with several loads is solved with the beam methods of strength of materials (Macaulay brackets or the area-moment method): one moment equation, integrated twice with the correct I for each step, with y = 0 at both bearings. Because I grows as d⁴, doubling the diameter cuts the deflection to one-sixteenth.

Critical speed, Dunkerley and Rayleigh

No rotor is perfectly balanced: its centre of mass sits a small distance e from the shaft axis. Spinning at ω, the shaft bends by y, the centrifugal force m(y + e)ω² is balanced by the shaft stiffness ky, and y = e r²/(1 − r²) with r = ω/ωn and ωn = √(k/m). As r → 1 the deflection grows without limit (damping keeps it finite but large); well above it the rotor spins about its centre of mass and y tends to e. The speed where ω = ωn is the critical (whirling) speed.

For a mass on a simply supported shaft, k/m = g/δ, where δ is the static deflection under the mass:

Nc = (60/2π)√(g/δ) rpm

with g = 9.81 m/s² and δ in metres. A 1 mm sag gives 946 rpm.

Dunkerley’s method combines the critical speeds found for each load alone, including the shaft’s own weight: 1/Nc² = 1/N1² + 1/N2² + … + 1/Ns². For a uniform shaft alone, the frequency is fs = 0.5615/√δs Hz, with δs the mid-span deflection under its own weight in metres. Dunkerley gives a value slightly below the true one. Rayleigh’s method uses the deflections yi under all the loads together: ωn = √(g ΣWi yi/ΣWi yi²); it gives a value slightly above the true one. Keep the running speed at least 20 to 25 % away from Nc, or pass through it quickly when starting.

Worked example

Question. A line shaft transmits 20 kW at 300 rpm. The allowable shear stress is 40 MPa, G = 79.3 GPa, and the twist must not exceed 0.25° per metre. Find the shaft diameter.

  1. Torque: T = 60 × 20 000/(2π × 300) = 636.62 N·m = 636 620 N·mm.
  2. Strength: d³ = 16T/(πτ) = 16 × 636 620/(π × 40) = 81 057 mm³, so d = 43.3 mm.
  3. Rigidity, for L = 1000 mm and θ = 0.25°: d⁴ = 584TL/(Gθ) = 584 × 636 620 × 1000/(79 300 × 0.25) = 1.875 × 10⁷ mm⁴, so d = 65.8 mm.
  4. Rigidity governs. Adopt 70 mm.
  5. Check at 70 mm: θ = 584 × 636 620 × 1000/(79 300 × 70⁴) = 0.195° per metre ≤ 0.25°, and τ = 16 × 636 620/(π × 70³) = 9.45 MPa, far below 40 MPa.

Answer. d = 70 mm (rigidity needs 65.8 mm, strength only 43.3 mm). The low stress shows how much a rigidity limit can control a long shaft.

Worked example 2

Question. A steel shaft of 60 mm diameter runs on bearings 1.2 m apart and carries a 25 kg rotor at mid-span. E = 200 GPa and the steel density is 7850 kg/m³. Find the critical speed from the rotor alone, then include the shaft’s own mass by Dunkerley’s method, and state a safe running speed.

  1. I = π × 0.06⁴/64 = 6.362 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴; EI = 1.272 × 10⁵ N·m².
  2. Rotor: W = 25 × 9.81 = 245.25 N; δ = WL³/(48EI) = 245.25 × 1.728/(48 × 1.272 × 10⁵) = 6.939 × 10⁻⁵ m (0.069 mm).
  3. N1 = 9.5493 × √(9.81/6.939 × 10⁻⁵) = 9.5493 × 376.0 = 3590 rpm.
  4. Shaft weight: mass per metre = 7850 × π × 0.06²/4 = 22.20 kg/m, so w = 217.7 N/m; δs = 5wL⁴/(384EI) = 5 × 217.7 × 2.0736/(384 × 1.272 × 10⁵) = 4.62 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  5. Ns = 60 × 0.5615/√(4.62 × 10⁻⁵) = 60 × 82.60 = 4956 rpm.
  6. Dunkerley: 1/Nc² = 1/3590² + 1/4956², so Nc = 2908 rpm.
  7. Safe running speed: below 0.75 × 2908 ≈ 2180 rpm, or above about 1.25 × 2908 ≈ 3630 rpm with a quick run-through.

Answer. The rotor alone gives 3590 rpm, but with the shaft’s own mass the critical speed is about 2908 rpm. A 2900 rpm motor speed would sit right on it; run below about 2180 rpm, or stiffen the shaft.

Exam traps

  • Putting δ in millimetres into Nc = (60/2π)√(g/δ) with g in m/s². The answer comes out √1000 ≈ 31.6 times too small. Convert δ to metres.
  • Forgetting the 60/(2π) factor: √(g/δ) is in rad/s, not rpm.
  • Ignoring the shaft’s own weight in Dunkerley’s sum. For a heavy shaft it can lower Nc by a fifth or more, as in the worked example.
  • Giving only the strength diameter when a twist limit is stated. Find both and adopt the larger.
  • Saying the deflection falls to zero above the critical speed. It tends to the eccentricity e; the rotor then spins about its centre of mass.

3.3 Keys, keyways and splines

A key is a small steel piece that sits in matching slots in a shaft and a hub, so the shaft can drive a gear, pulley or coupling without slipping. The slot is the keyway. A spline does the same job with many teeth cut on the shaft, so it carries more torque and can let the hub slide. A key is sized from two checks, shear and crushing, and the keyway it needs weakens the shaft, so both are part of shaft design. Every motor, pump, gearbox and bicycle crank uses one.

You need: torque and shaft diameter from shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads.

Types of keys

  • Sunk keys sit half in the shaft and half in the hub. A rectangular key has width w = d/4 and height h = 2w/3 (the common case); a square key has h = w = d/4. A parallel key has no taper; a taper key has a 1 in 100 taper on its top and is driven in, often with a gib head so it can be pulled out.
  • A feather key is a parallel key fixed to the shaft or hub so the hub can slide along the shaft, as for a gear-shift clutch.
  • A Woodruff key is a half disc in a curved seat. It tilts to match a tapered shaft end and is common in machine tools and car engines, but its deep seat weakens the shaft.
  • Saddle keys (flat or hollow) need no shaft keyway and drive by friction only, so they suit light loads.
  • Tangent keys are a pair of taper keys at 90° for heavy reversing torque, as on flywheels. A round key is a pin in a drilled hole for small torque. A Kennedy key is a pair of square keys set diagonally for heavy duty.

Key widths and heights come from standard tables for each shaft range; for a 44 to 50 mm shaft the standard rectangular key is 14 mm × 9 mm. The key fits snugly on its sides and has a small clearance at the top, so the torque passes through the side faces.

Forces and stresses in a sunk key

The torque T reaches the key as a tangential force at the shaft surface: F = 2T/d. That force can fail the key in two ways.

  • Shear across the plane where shaft and hub meet: area wl, so τ = F/(wl) = 2T/(dwl).
  • Crushing of the side face: half the height bears on the hub, area (h/2)l, so σc = F/((h/2)l) = 4T/(dhl).

Here l is the key length in contact with the hub. Design takes the larger length from the two checks and makes the hub at least that long. The hub is often of cast iron, so its keyway is checked for crushing with the hub’s own allowable stress. For a square key in a material whose crushing strength is twice its shear strength, the two checks give the same length. Making the key as strong as the shaft in shear (wlτd/2 = πd³τ/16) gives l = πd²/(8w), which is 1.571d for w = d/4: that is why key lengths of about 1.5d are common.

Effect of the keyway, and splines

A keyway removes metal and leaves sharp corners, so the shaft is weaker. Moore’s formula gives the strength factor of a keyed shaft compared with a plain one: e = 1 − 0.2(w/d) − 1.1(h1/d), where h1 is the keyway depth in the shaft (about h/2). A round-ended (profile) keyway cut by an end mill has a higher stress-concentration factor than a sled-runner keyway cut by a disc cutter. The ASME code covers any keyway with a 25 % lower allowable stress.

A spline shaft has several (often 4, 6, 10 or 16) teeth cut on it. Straight-sided splines have parallel flanks; involute splines have gear-like flanks that centre themselves and are stronger. A sliding fit lets the hub move along the shaft, as in a gearbox; a fixed fit does not. The torque capacity follows from the side-face pressure: T = p n h l Rm, where n is the number of splines, h = (D − d)/2 the tooth height, l the engaged length and Rm = (D + d)/4 the mean radius. Splines spread the load and avoid one deep keyway, so they carry more torque in the same space.

Worked example

Question. A 50 mm shaft transmits 20 kW at 600 rpm through a standard 14 mm × 9 mm rectangular sunk key. The key steel has allowable shear stress 40 MPa and allowable crushing stress 80 MPa. Find the key length, and the strength factor of the keyed shaft taking the keyway depth as h/2.

  1. Torque: T = 60 × 20 000/(2π × 600) = 318.31 N·m = 318 310 N·mm.
  2. Force at the shaft surface: F = 2T/d = 2 × 318 310/50 = 12 732 N.
  3. Length for shear: l = F/(wτ) = 12 732/(14 × 40) = 22.7 mm.
  4. Length for crushing: l = F/((h/2)σc) = 12 732/(4.5 × 80) = 35.4 mm.
  5. Crushing governs. Adopt l = 40 mm; then τ = 12 732/(14 × 40) = 22.7 MPa and σc = 12 732/(4.5 × 40) = 70.7 MPa, both within limits.
  6. Moore’s factor: e = 1 − 0.2 × 14/50 − 1.1 × 4.5/50 = 1 − 0.056 − 0.099 = 0.845.

Answer. Key 14 × 9 × 40 mm. The keyway leaves the shaft with about 84.5 % of the strength of a plain 50 mm shaft.

Exam traps

  • Using the full height h for crushing. Only half the key bears on the hub, so the area is (h/2)l.
  • Taking the key force as T/d. It acts at the shaft surface: F = T/(d/2) = 2T/d.
  • Checking only shear. A rectangular key usually needs more length for crushing, as in the worked example.
  • Choosing a key longer than the hub. The length that counts is the length in contact with the hub.
  • Calling a Woodruff key the strongest. Its deep seat weakens the shaft most; it is chosen for easy assembly on tapered ends.

3.4 Rigid and flexible couplings

A coupling joins the ends of two shafts so that torque passes from one to the other, for example from a motor to a pump. A rigid coupling holds the shafts in exact line and is simple and strong, but it passes every misalignment into the bearings. A flexible coupling allows a small parallel, angular or axial error and softens shock. The design follows the torque through every part (shaft, key, hub, flange, bolts or pins) and checks each for shear, crushing or bearing pressure.

You need: the key checks in keys, keyways and splines.

Rigid couplings: sleeve, clamp and flange

  • Sleeve (muff) coupling: a cast-iron sleeve keyed over both shaft ends. Usual proportions: outer diameter D = 2d + 13 mm and length L = 3.5d. The sleeve is checked as a hollow shaft in torsion. It is cheap, but one shaft must be moved to remove it.
  • Clamp (split-muff) coupling: the sleeve is split along the axis and bolted round the shafts, so it can be fitted without moving them; friction and the key share the torque.
  • Flange coupling: a flange keyed on each shaft, the two flanges bolted face to face. The protected type has a rim that covers the bolt heads and nuts, so nothing projects to catch clothing. The marine type has the flanges forged on the shafts.

Usual proportions of a cast-iron protected flange coupling for shaft diameter d: hub diameter 2d, hub length 1.5d, bolt pitch circle diameter D1 = 3d, flange outer diameter 4d, flange thickness t = 0.5d, protecting rim 0.25d. The torque path is shaft → key → hub → flange → bolts → second flange → key → second shaft, and each link has its own check:

  1. Shaft: τ = 16T/(πd³). Hub as a hollow shaft: τ = 16T D/(π(D⁴ − d⁴)) with D = 2d.
  2. Key: shear and crushing, as for any sunk key, over the hub length.
  3. Flange shear at the hub: T = πD²tτ/2.
  4. Bolts (fitted in reamed holes): force per bolt F = 2T/(nD1); shear τ = F/(πdb²/4); crushing σc = F/(db t).

Use the design torque T = Ks × rated torque, with a service factor Ks of about 1.25 to 1.5 for a smooth pump and 2 or more for a crusher.

Flexible couplings

Shafts never stay in perfect line: foundations settle and hot machines grow. Misalignment has three parts: parallel offset of the axes, angular misalignment and axial float.

  • Bushed-pin flexible coupling: a flange coupling whose bolts are replaced by pins carrying rubber bushes in one flange. The rubber deflects, so the coupling takes small parallel and angular errors and damps shock. The pin force is F = 2T/(nD1); the bush is sized for a bearing pressure of about 1 N/mm²: F = p Db lb; the pin is checked in shear and in bending, since F acts at the middle of the bush, away from the flange.
  • Oldham coupling: two slotted hubs and a centre disc with two tongues at 90°; it allows a parallel offset.
  • Universal (Hooke’s) joint: two yokes on a cross; it allows a large angle, but one joint gives an output speed that fluctuates twice per turn. Two joints with equal angles and the yokes in phase cancel the fluctuation, as on a truck propeller shaft.
  • Gear, grid, disc and jaw couplings are chosen from maker tables by torque, bore and speed.

A flexible coupling is not a cure for poor installation. Align the shafts first with a dial gauge or laser, allowing for the hot growth of the machine.

Worked example

Question. Design a cast-iron protected flange coupling for a steel shaft transmitting 15 kW at 200 rpm. Allowable stresses: shaft, key and bolts 40 MPa in shear and 80 MPa in crushing; cast iron 15 MPa in shear. Use four bolts and the usual proportions.

  1. Torque: T = 60 × 15 000/(2π × 200) = 716.2 N·m = 716 200 N·mm.

  4. Hub: τ = 16 × 716 200 × 100/(π(100⁴ − 50⁴)) = 3.89 MPa ≤ 15 MPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A bushed-pin coupling for a 30 kW, 720 rpm drive has six pins on a 160 mm pitch circle. Each pin is 14 mm in diameter and carries a rubber bush of 30 mm outer diameter; the pin load acts at the middle of the bush, 20 mm from the flange face. The bush pressure must not exceed 1 N/mm². Find the bush length and the stresses in the pin.

  1. Torque: T = 60 × 30 000/(2π × 720) = 397.9 N·m = 397 900 N·mm.
  2. Force per pin: F = 2T/(nD1) = 2 × 397 900/(6 × 160) = 829 N.
  3. Bush length: lb = F/(pDb) = 829/(1 × 30) = 27.6 mm; adopt 30 mm.
  4. Pin shear: τ = 829/(π × 14²/4) = 5.4 MPa.
  5. Pin bending: M = 829 × 20 = 16 580 N·mm; σb = 32M/(πd³) = 32 × 16 580/(π × 14³) = 61.5 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using the pitch circle diameter where the radius belongs: F = T/(nR) = 2T/(nD1). Mixing them halves or doubles the bolt force.
  • Checking the bolts in shear only. Flange crushing on the bolt, the hub and the flange shear at the hub are all part of the answer.
  • Checking a bushed pin in shear only. The force acts at the middle of the bush, so the pin also bends, and bending usually governs.
  • Forgetting the service factor, or using the rated torque for some checks and the design torque for others.
  • Saying a single universal joint gives a constant output speed. It needs a second joint at the same angle, in phase.

3.5 Clutches and brakes

A clutch is a friction joint that can be switched on and off while the shafts run. It lets a running engine pick up a stationary gearbox without a jolt. A brake is the same joint with one side held still: it turns kinetic energy into heat until the wheel stops. Both are designed from the friction torque their rubbing faces can carry and from the heat those faces must absorb. You meet them on a motorcycle, a car, a lathe, a lift and a train.

You need: torque from power and speed, as in shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads.

Plates, pairs and kinds of clutch

In a single-plate clutch a steel plate lined on both faces is clamped between the engine flywheel and a pressure plate by springs giving the axial force W. That makes two rubbing pairs (n = 2). In general, count the friction surfaces that slip: a stack of driving and driven plates gives n = (number of plates in the stack) − 1, counting the flywheel and pressure plate as plates. A multi-plate clutch gets more torque from the same W and diameter, which keeps motorcycle and machine-tool clutches small; run in oil it is a wet clutch.

A cone clutch uses a shallow cone of semi-angle α, so the normal force is W/sin α and the same spring gives more torque. A centrifugal clutch has shoes that fly out as speed rises and engage by themselves, which is why a scooter needs no clutch lever.

Uniform pressure and uniform wear

The friction torque depends on how the pressure p is spread over the ring between the inner radius ri and the outer radius ro. Wear rate goes as pressure × rubbing speed, and rubbing speed is ωr, so a new face with uniform pressure wears fastest at the outer edge. After running in, the face settles where p × r is constant: uniform wear. The pressure is then highest at the inner radius, pmax = W/(2πri(ro − ri)).

T = μWn(ro + ri)/2

uniform wear (any clutch in service), with μ the coefficient of friction, W in N and radii in m, giving T in N·m.

T = (2/3)μWn(ro³ − ri³)/(ro² − ri²)
uniform pressure (a new, accurately flat lining).

Uniform pressure puts more of the force at large radius, so it gives slightly more torque; a designer uses uniform wear, the lower and safer value. A ratio ro/ri of about 1.25 to 1.5 keeps the two close and the lining well used.

Brakes and the heat they make

A brake holds one friction member still: the drum or disc turns with the wheel and the block or pad is fixed to the frame. Its torque is T = μNr, with N the normal force and r the drum radius. In a block brake a lever presses one block on the drum, and N comes from moments about the lever pivot. If the friction force helps press the block on, the brake is self-energising; if it does so enough that no lever force is needed, it is self-locking and will not release. A shoe brake wraps the lining over a wider arc, and a disc brake presses pads on a disc at the pad’s mean radius.

E = T ωrel t

is the heat in joules for steady slip at relative speed ωrel (rad/s) for t seconds. When the slip speed falls steadily to zero, as in a clutch engagement or a stop, use the mean speed ωrel/2.

The heat is first stored in the drum or plate, then carried away by air. If stops come too fast, the lining gets hot, μ falls and the brake fades, a real danger on a long downhill run. So brakes and clutches are sized by energy per stop and stops per hour, not by torque alone.

Worked example

Question. A single-plate clutch, both sides effective, has ro = 150 mm, ri = 100 mm, μ = 0.3 and spring force W = 5 kN. Find the torque by uniform wear and by uniform pressure, the power at 1200 rpm, the largest lining pressure, and the heat per engagement if it slips for 0.5 s from a relative speed of 600 rpm with the slip speed falling steadily to zero.

  1. Uniform wear: T = μWn(ro + ri)/2 = 0.3 × 5000 × 2 × 0.25/2 = 375 N·m.
  2. Uniform pressure: T = (2/3) × 0.3 × 5000 × 2 × (0.15³ − 0.10³)/(0.15² − 0.10²) = 2000 × 0.002375/0.0125 = 380 N·m.
  3. Power by uniform wear: ω = 2π × 1200/60 = 125.66 rad/s, P = Tω = 375 × 125.66 = 47 124 W = 47.1 kW.
  4. Largest pressure (at ri): pmax = W/(2πri(ro − ri)) = 5000/(2π × 0.10 × 0.05) = 0.159 MPa.
  5. Slip: ωrel = 2π × 600/60 = 62.83 rad/s at the start, falling to zero, so the mean is 31.42 rad/s.
  6. Heat: E = T × mean ωrel × t = 375 × 31.42 × 0.5 = 5890 J.

Answer. Torque 375 N·m by uniform wear (380 N·m by uniform pressure), 47.1 kW at 1200 rpm, pmax = 0.159 MPa at the inner radius, about 5.9 kJ of heat per engagement.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the number of pairs: a single-plate clutch lined on both faces has n = 2, not 1.
  • Putting millimetres into the torque formulas: radii in metres give N·m; in millimetres you get N·mm.
  • Placing the maximum pressure of uniform wear at the outer radius. Since p × r is constant, it is at the inner radius.
  • Using the full starting slip speed for the whole engagement. If the slip falls steadily to zero, the mean is half.
  • Taking the hand or pedal force as the normal force on a block brake. N comes from moments about the lever pivot.

3.6 Connecting rods and crankshafts

The connecting rod links the piston to the crank, and the crankshaft turns the rod’s push into torque. The rod is a column that can buckle, with a bearing at each end; the crankshaft is a shaft under bending and twisting whose worst load changes as it turns. Design takes the peak gas force, sizes the rod as a column and its ends for bearing pressure, then checks the crankshaft in the two positions that load it most.

You need: combined bending and torsion from shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads, and Rankine’s column formula from strength of materials.

Forces on the connecting rod and its I-section

The peak gas force on the piston is Fp = (π/4)D²pmax, with D the bore and pmax the peak cylinder pressure. Near top dead centre, where pmax acts, the rod is almost in line with the cylinder, so the rod force Fc = Fp/cos φ is taken as Fp (φ is the rod’s angle to the cylinder axis). The rod is then a strut that can buckle.

Rods of high-speed engines use an I-section of web and flange thickness t, width B = 4t and depth H = 5t. Then A = 11t², Ixx = 419t⁴/12, Iyy = 131t⁴/12 and kxx² = Ixx/A = 3.17t². In the plane of motion the rod is pinned at both ends (buckling about xx, length L); out of that plane its ends are nearly fixed (about yy, length L/2). Equal strength needs Ixx = 4Iyy; the I-section gives 3.2, so it is designed about xx.

Pcr = σc A/(1 + a(L/kxx)²)
Rankine’s formula: σc is the crushing stress (about 330 MPa for steel), a = 1/7500 for steel, L the rod length between centres. Take Pcr = (factor of safety) × Fp, with 5 to 6 for rods.

The depth H is usually tapered: about 1.1H to 1.25H at the big end and 0.75H to 0.9H at the small end. At high speed the rod’s own mass swings sideways and bends it (whipping); the largest inertia bending moment is M = m ω²rL²/(9√3), with m the rod mass per unit length and r the crank radius. Some syllabi ask for this check.

Small end, big end and cap bolts

Each end is a plain bearing sized for bearing pressure pb: Fp = d l pb, with d the pin diameter and l its length. The small end (piston pin) runs slowly and oscillates, so typical values are pb ≈ 12 to 15 MPa with l/d ≈ 1.5 to 2; the big end (crankpin) takes about 10 MPa with l/d ≈ 1.0 to 1.25. The big-end cap bolts carry the inertia force of the piston and rod at top dead centre of the exhaust stroke, Fi = mr ω² r(1 + 1/nr), where mr is the reciprocating mass and nr = L/r; they are sized on their core area in tension.

Crankshafts: two positions to check

A centre crankshaft has the crankpin between two main bearings; an overhung (side) crankshaft has it outside one bearing. A crankshaft is checked in two positions:

  1. Crank at top dead centre: the full gas force acts on the crankpin with no torque. Find the bearing reactions, then size the crankpin by bearing pressure (Fp = dc lc pb) and check it in bending, M = R1 b1, σb = 32M/(πdc³). The webs see direct compression plus bending.
  2. Crank at the angle of maximum torque (about 25° to 35° after top dead centre): the rod force splits into a tangential part Ft, which gives the torque Ft r, and a radial part Fr. Here the crankpin and the shaft next to the web carry bending and torsion together, and the equivalent torque √(M² + T²) gives the diameter.

The shaft under the flywheel is checked for the flywheel weight and belt pull in bending plus the torque. Generous pin-web fillets keep the stress concentration down.

Worked example

Question. Design the I-section of a steel connecting rod for an engine with bore 100 mm, peak gas pressure 4 MPa and rod length 240 mm between centres. Take a factor of safety of 6, σc = 330 MPa and a = 1/7500. Also find the small-end pin and big-end crankpin sizes for pb = 12.5 MPa with l/d = 2 and pb = 10 MPa with l/d = 1.

  1. Gas force: Fp = (π/4) × 100² × 4 = 31 416 N.
  2. Buckling load needed: Pcr = 6 × 31 416 = 188 496 N.
  3. Rankine with A = 11t², kxx² = 3.17t²: 188 496 = 330 × 11t²/(1 + (1/7500) × 240²/(3.17t²)).
  4. Simplify: 3630t² = 188 496 + 456 070/t², so 3630t⁴ − 188 496t² − 456 060 = 0. Solving the quadratic in t² gives t² = 54.24 mm², t = 7.37 mm.
  5. Adopt t = 8 mm: B = 32 mm, H = 40 mm, A = 704 mm², kxx² = 203.2 mm². Then Pcr = 330 × 704/(1 + 240²/(7500 × 203.2)) = 223 860 N, a factor of safety of 7.1.

Question. The same engine has a centre crankshaft with the crankpin centre 100 mm from each main bearing centre. Exam traps

  • Designing the rod for crushing (σc A) alone. It is a column, so Rankine’s formula with the slenderness L/kxx sets the size.
  • Using kyy and length L together. Buckling about yy has equivalent length L/2; the I-section is designed about xx with length L.
  • Forgetting the factor of safety on the gas force before entering Rankine’s formula.
  • Sizing the crankpin by bearing pressure and stopping. At top dead centre it must also pass the bending check, as the second example shows.
  • Checking the crankshaft only at top dead centre. The maximum-torque position adds torsion and can govern the shaft next to the web.

Takeaways

  1. A shaft is sized at the section with the largest bending moment and torque, from the equivalent torque (ductile steel) or the equivalent bending moment, whichever gives the larger diameter.
  2. The ASME code multiplies M and T by shock factors and limits τ to the smaller of 0.30Syt and 0.18Sut, cut by a quarter for a keyway.
  3. An axial load changes σx, so the maximum shear stress must be found from σb + σa on the side where they add, not from Te.
  4. Long or fast shafts are often decided by twist, deflection or critical speed, not stress; run at least 20 to 25 % away from Nc.
  5. A key is checked in shear on w × l and in crushing on (h/2) × l; for a rectangular key crushing usually needs the longer length.
  6. A flange coupling is checked link by link: shaft, key, hub, flange and bolts. A bushed pin must also pass a bending check.
  7. Clutch torque is μWn times a friction radius; uniform wear gives the lower, design value.
  8. A connecting rod is a column: Rankine’s formula about the xx axis with a factor of safety of 5 to 6 sets its I-section, and bearing pressure sets its ends.
  9. A crankshaft is checked at top dead centre (bending) and at maximum torque (bending plus torsion); the crankpin must pass both bearing and bending checks.

Understand Derive the equivalent torque and the equivalent bending moment for a solid shaft under a bending moment M and a torque T, name the failure theory behind each, and find the diameter for M = 500 N·m and T = 400 N·m with τallow = 40 MPa and σallow = 80 MPa. 13 marks

Points to cover. Stresses at the surface; Principal stresses; Maximum shear stress theory gives Te; Maximum normal stress theory gives Me; Numerical example and choice.

Model answer.

  1. Data and aim: a solid shaft of diameter d carries a bending moment M and a torque T at the same section. We want a single equivalent load for each failure theory, then the diameter for M = 500 N·m, T = 400 N·m, τallow = 40 MPa and σallow = 80 MPa.
  2. At the outer surface the bending stress along the axis is σb = 32M/(πd³) (from σ = M/Z with Z = πd³/32) and the torsional shear stress is τ = 16T/(πd³) (from τ = T/Zp with Zp = πd³/16). The radial stress is zero, so the element is in plane stress with σx = σb, σy = 0 and τxy = τ.
  3. Principal stresses from Mohr’s circle:
    σ1,2 = σb/2 ± √((σb/2)² + τ²)
τmax = √((σb/2)² + τ²)
  4. Maximum shear stress theory (Guest), for ductile material: substitute σb and τ in τmax.
    τmax = √((16M/πd³)² + (16T/πd³)²) = (16/πd³)√(M² + T²)
so Te = √(M² + T²) and τallow = 16Te/(πd³)
  5. Te is the torque that, acting alone, would cause the same maximum shear stress as M and T together.
  6. Maximum normal stress theory (Rankine), for brittle material: substitute in σ1.
    σ1 = 16M/πd³ + (16/πd³)√(M² + T²) = (32/πd³) × ½[M + √(M² + T²)]
so Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)] and σallow = 32Me/(πd³)
  7. Me is the bending moment that, acting alone, would cause the same maximum normal stress.
  8. Numbers: M = 500 000 N·mm, T = 400 000 N·mm.
    Te = √(500² + 400²) = 640.3 N·m;  Me = ½(500 + 640.3) = 570.2 N·m
from Te: d³ = 16 × 640 300/(π × 40) = 81 527 mm³, d = 43.4 mm
from Me: d³ = 32 × 570 200/(π × 80) = 72 600 mm³, d = 41.7 mm
  9. Take the larger, 43.4 mm, and adopt the standard 45 mm. For steel shafts the maximum shear stress theory governs and is the basis of the ASME code; the normal stress theory suits cast iron.
  10. Why two theories: ductile steel yields by shear on 45° planes, so its failure is predicted by the largest shear stress; brittle cast iron breaks by tension, so its failure is predicted by the largest normal stress. For the same M and T, Me is always at least half of Te plus half of M, so the two theories give similar diameters when M dominates and different ones when T dominates.
  11. Use in design: find the critical section from the bending moment diagram (the section with the largest combination of M and T), multiply by Kb and Kt if the ASME code is asked, then use Te (and Me if asked) at that section.
  12. Check: at 45 mm, τ = 16 × 640 300/(π × 45³) = 35.8 MPa ≤ 40 MPa.

Model answer.

  1. Data from this unit: line shaft 20 kW at 300 rpm, τallow = 40 MPa, G = 79.3 GPa, twist limit 0.25° per metre; rotor shaft 60 mm diameter, 1.2 m span, 25 kg rotor at mid-span, E = 200 GPa.
  2. Strength checks keep stress below an allowable value. Stiffness is a separate property: deflection and twist depend on E, G and the fourth power of d, not on the allowable stress, so a shaft can be safe in stress yet twist or bend too much for the parts it carries.
  3. Torsional rigidity: a torque T twists a length L by θ = TL/(GJ) radians, with J = πd⁴/32. In degrees for a solid shaft, θ = 584TL/(Gd⁴). Too much twist puts gears, cams and timing out of step and lets a long shaft wind up and spring back. A common limit for machine-tool and precision drives is 0.25° per metre; line shafts are allowed more.
  4. Lateral rigidity: bending deflects the shaft by y and tilts it by a slope. Slope at a rolling bearing (guide about 0.001 rad) loads the rollers on one edge; deflection at a gear (a few hundredths of a millimetre) moves the tooth contact and changes backlash. Deflection is found by beam theory: δ = WL³/(48EI) for a central load, Macaulay’s method for stepped shafts.

    T = 636 620 N·mm
strength: d³ = 16T/(πτ) = 81 057 mm³, d = 43.3 mm
rigidity: d⁴ = 584 × 636 620 × 1000/(79 300 × 0.25) = 1.875 × 10⁷ mm⁴, d = 65.8 mm

Model answer.

  1. Symbols: W axial spring force (N), μ coefficient of friction, ro and ri outer and inner radii of the friction ring (m), p pressure (N/m²), n number of rubbing pairs (2 for a single plate lined on both faces).

  5. Uniform wear:
    W = ∫ (C/r) 2π r dr = 2πC(ro − ri), so C = W/(2π(ro − ri))

T = μ W n (ro + ri)/2

pmax (wear) = 5000/(2π × 0.10 × 0.05) = 0.159 MPa at ri

Apply A single-plate clutch, both sides effective, must transmit 15 kW at 1000 rpm. μ = 0.3, the largest lining pressure is 0.1 MPa and ro/ri = 1.4. Find ri, ro and the spring force by uniform wear theory. 13 marks

Points to cover. Torque; Uniform wear: pmax at ri, W = 2πpmax ri(ro − ri); Torque equation in ri; Solve and round; Recheck torque and pressure.

Model answer.

  1. Data: P = 15 kW, N = 1000 rpm, μ = 0.3, pmax = 0.1 MPa (N/mm²), ro = 1.4ri, n = 2. Uniform wear theory is used because it is the run-in condition and gives the lower torque.
  2. Torque:
    T = 60 × 15 000/(2π × 1000) = 143.24 N·m = 143 240 N·mm
  3. Uniform wear: p r = C, with the largest pressure at ri, so C = pmax ri and W = 2πC(ro − ri) = 2πpmax ri(ro − ri).
  4. Torque with n = 2 and ro = 1.4ri:
    T = μWn(ro + ri)/2 = μ n π pmax ri(ro − ri)(ro + ri)
  = 0.3 × 2 × π × 0.1 × ri × 0.4ri × 2.4ri = 0.18096 ri³ (N·mm, ri in mm)
ri³ = 143 240/0.18096 = 791 550 mm³, ri = 92.5 mm, ro = 129.5 mm
  5. Round up: ri = 95 mm and ro = 133 mm (ratio 1.4).
  6. Spring force and torque with the rounded radii:
    W = 2π × 0.1 × 95 × 38 = 2268 N
T = 0.3 × 2268 × 2 × (133 + 95)/2 = 155 150 N·mm = 155.1 N·m ≥ 143.2 N·m
  7. The largest pressure stays at 0.1 MPa at ri (it is set by W), and the pressure at ro is 0.1 × 95/133 = 0.071 MPa.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A shaft that is strong enough on paper keeps cracking at its keyway within weeks. Analyse the likely causes, using the stress and rigidity ideas of this unit, and recommend design changes. 13 marks

Points to cover. Static check versus fatigue; Keyway stress concentration and Moore’s factor; Code allowances and shock factors; Vibration from rigidity and critical speed; Recommended changes.

Model answer.

  1. What is known: the shaft passed the static check by the equivalent torque, yet cracks start at the keyway within weeks. The analysis asks why a static pass did not prevent the failure and what the design missed.
  2. A crack in weeks is fatigue, not static overload: the shaft passed a static check with Te but failed under millions of stress reversals. Rotating bending reverses fully every turn, and torque often fluctuates.
  3. The keyway is the weak spot. Its sharp root corners raise the local stress two to three times above nominal; Moore’s factor for the 14 × 9 keyway in a 50 mm shaft is 0.845 even before fatigue is considered. A profile keyway cut by an end mill has a sharper end than a sled-runner keyway.
  4. Code allowances may have been skipped: the ASME code cuts the allowable shear stress by 25 % for a keyway and raises M and T by Kb and Kt for shock. A design with Kb = Kt = 1 for a shock-loaded drive, or with no keyway reduction, is undersized by 25 to 50 %.
  5. Shock or misalignment adds load: a rigid coupling to a misaligned machine, or a hub loose on its key, turns every revolution into a hammer blow on the keyway side.
  6. Rigidity and critical speed: a slender shaft that deflects at a gear, or runs near Nc, vibrates, and the vibration adds bending stress cycles. The shaft of the critical-speed example runs safely below 2180 rpm but would whirl near 2900 rpm.
  7. Recommended changes, in order of effect:
    ChangeWhy
    Larger diameter at the keywaystress falls as 1/d³; stiffness rises as d⁴
    Generous root fillet, sled-runner keywaylower stress concentration
    Splines or a keyless locking deviceno single deep notch
    Correct Kb, Kt and the keyway factordesign for the real duty
    Flexible coupling, alignment, tight key fitremoves shock and misalignment loads
    Fatigue check with Goodman or Soderberguses the endurance limit, not yield
  8. How to confirm: inspect the fracture face for beach marks (fatigue) starting at a keyway corner, measure the actual torque and vibration in service, and check the key fit and coupling alignment before redesigning.
  9. Check: raising the shaft from 50 to 55 mm cuts the nominal stress by (50/55)³ = 0.75, a 25 % reduction, which alone restores the margin the code’s keyway allowance asks for.
Evaluate A motor drives a gearbox whose shaft centre is 0.3 mm off line and 0.2° skewed. Evaluate a rigid flange coupling against a bushed-pin coupling for a 30 kW, 720 rpm drive, using this unit’s numbers. 13 marks

Points to cover. What misalignment does to a rigid coupling; How the bushed pin absorbs it; Bushed-pin numbers: pin force, bush length, pin stresses; Bearing life and vibration; Recommendation.

Model answer.

  1. Data: misalignment 0.3 mm parallel and 0.2° angular; drive 30 kW at 720 rpm, torque 397.9 N·m; candidate couplings: a rigid flange coupling and a bushed-pin coupling with six 14 mm pins on a 160 mm circle and 30 mm bushes.
  2. With a rigid coupling the two shafts are forced into line. The 0.3 mm offset and 0.2° angle are taken up by bending the shafts and loading the bearings, and the bending reverses every revolution, so the bearings see an extra cyclic radial load and the shafts an extra bending stress cycle at 12 Hz (720 rpm).
  3. Rolling bearing life falls roughly as the cube of the load (for ball bearings), so even a modest extra radial load shortens life sharply; misalignment also shows as vibration at once and twice the shaft speed, and as coupling bolt fretting.
  4. A bushed-pin coupling lets the rubber bushes deflect: offset and angle become small rubber strains, and only a small restoring force reaches the bearings. The rubber also damps torque shock at start-up.
  5. Sizing the bushed pin (six pins on a 160 mm circle, 14 mm pins, 30 mm bushes, load 20 mm from the flange):
    T = 397.9 N·m;  F = 2T/(nD1) = 829 N per pin
bush: lb = 829/(1 × 30) = 27.6 → 30 mm
pin: τ = 5.4 MPa, σb = 32 × 16 580/(π × 14³) = 61.5 MPa, τmax = 31.2 MPa
  6. These are modest stresses for a steel pin, and the rubber rated for 1 N/mm² is the limiting element; maker tables give the allowable offset and angle for each size, typically a few tenths of a millimetre and under a degree, which covers this case.
  7. Cost and upkeep: the rigid coupling is cheaper and needs no spares; the bushed pin needs bush replacement every few years. Neither replaces alignment: correct the error first, then choose the coupling for what remains after thermal growth.
  8. Evaluation:
    CriterionRigid flangeBushed pin
    Takes 0.3 mm, 0.2°no: loads bearingsyes, within rating
    Shock dampingnonegood
    Cost, partslowmoderate, bushes wear
    Suits separate machinespoorlywell
  9. Effect on critical speed: a rigid coupling makes the two shafts act as one stiffer, longer beam, while a flexible coupling separates them dynamically; either way, the shaft critical speeds should be checked against 720 rpm with the coupling mass included.
  10. Recommendation: the bushed-pin coupling, after aligning the machines as closely as practicable. Check: its pins run at 31.2 MPa maximum shear and its bushes at 0.92 N/mm² with 30 mm length (829/(30 × 30)), both within limits.

Glossary

Axle
A member that supports rotating parts but transmits no torque; designed for bending only.
Brake fade
Loss of braking torque as hot lining loses friction.
Bushed-pin coupling
A flexible coupling with rubber-bushed pins that absorb small misalignment and shock.
Centre crankshaft
A crankshaft with the crankpin between two main bearings.
Critical speed
The speed at which a shaft’s rotation matches its lateral natural frequency and whirl peaks.
Dunkerley’s method
An estimate of the critical speed from the separate critical speeds of each load and the shaft; slightly low.
Equivalent bending moment
Me = ½[M + √(M² + T²)]: the moment that alone gives the same maximum normal stress.
Equivalent torque
Te = √(M² + T²): the torque that alone gives the same maximum shear stress as M and T together.
Feather key
A parallel key that lets the hub slide along the shaft while turning with it.
Muff coupling
A rigid coupling: a sleeve keyed over both shaft ends.
Oldham coupling
A coupling with a tongued centre disc that allows a parallel offset of the shafts.
Protected flange coupling
A rigid flange coupling whose rims cover the bolt heads and nuts.
Rankine’s formula
A column formula combining crushing and buckling: P = σc A/(1 + a(L/k)²).
Rayleigh’s method
An estimate of the critical speed from the static deflection curve under all loads; slightly high.
Saddle key
A key with no shaft keyway that drives by friction alone.
Self-locking brake
A brake in which friction alone holds the block on, so it will not release.
Service factor
A multiplier on the rated torque for shock and the duty of the driven machine.
Shaft
A rotating member that transmits torque and carries gears, pulleys or rotors.
Shock factors
Kb and Kt in the ASME code, multiplying the bending moment and torque for the type of loading.
Spindle
A short shaft, such as the main shaft of a machine tool.
Spline
One of several teeth cut on a shaft that mesh with grooves in a hub.
Sunk key
A key fitted half in the shaft and half in the hub keyway.
Torsional rigidity
GJ; the resistance of a shaft to twist, which sets the angle θ = TL/(GJ).
Uniform wear
The run-in state of a friction face in which pressure × radius is constant.
Universal joint
A cross-and-yoke joint that transmits torque between shafts at an angle.
Whipping
Bending of a connecting rod by the inertia of its own mass at high speed.
Whirling
Large lateral bending of a rotating shaft near its critical speed.
Woodruff key
A half-disc key in a curved seat, used on tapered shaft ends.
