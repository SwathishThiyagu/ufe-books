5.1 Lubricants: types and properties

A lubricant is anything you put between two surfaces to keep them from touching. Oil, grease and solid films such as graphite all do the main job, cutting friction and wear; flowing oil also carries heat away, and grease also seals out dirt. Which one you pick, and how thick it must be, is settled mainly by one property: viscosity, the resistance of the fluid to flow. It must be high enough to hold a film under load and low enough to flow into the gap. This chapter sets up the words the bearing chapters use.

You need: SI units and density from Foundations: Design Process and Stresses.

What a lubricant does

Two clean steel surfaces pressed together touch only at microscopic high spots. The real area of contact is tiny, so the pressure at those spots is enormous, they cold-weld, and sliding tears metal away. A lubricant works by putting a film between the surfaces so metal never meets metal.

  • Reduce friction: replace metal-to-metal rubbing with the low shear of a fluid layer.
  • Carry away heat: oil flowing through a bearing lifts friction heat to the sump or cooler.
  • Protect from corrosion: the film keeps air and water off the metal.
  • Seal and clean: grease in a housing keeps dust and water out, and flowing oil carries wear debris to the filter.

When the film is thick enough to fully separate the surfaces, friction is just the shear of the fluid, and the bearing is called hydrodynamic (Chapter 5.3). When the film is a few molecules thick and high spots still touch, that is boundary lubrication; it depends on oiliness, not on viscosity.

Types of lubricants

Mineral oils are refined from crude petroleum. They are cheap, sold in a wide range of viscosities, and are the default for general machinery such as gearboxes, pumps and electric motor bearings. Their viscosity index is moderate and they oxidise faster than synthetics when hot.

Synthetic oils are built from chosen chemicals: polyalphaolefins, esters, silicone oils, glycols. They cost more but hold a higher viscosity index, work over a wider temperature range, evaporate less and last longer. Refrigeration compressors, hot gearboxes and fire-resistant hydraulic systems use them.

Greases are semi-solid: oil held in a soap or clay thickener, plus additives. Lithium soap grease is the common one. Grease stays where it is put, seals the housing and needs little attention, so it suits sealed ball bearings and chassis points. It cannot carry heat away and it churns at high speed, so fast bearings use oil.

Solid lubricants are graphite, molybdenum disulphide (MoS₂) and PTFE. They are used where oil would boil, freeze or be washed out: kiln car bearings, vacuum, very high load, or a bush that can never be re-lubricated. Graphite needs some moisture in the air to slide well; MoS₂ does not, so MoS₂ is the choice in vacuum.

Viscosity, the deciding property

τ = μ · du/dy

τ is the shear stress in the fluid, in Pa; du/dy is the velocity gradient across the film, in 1/s; μ is the dynamic viscosity, in Pa·s, which is the same as N·s/m².

ν = μ / ρ

ν is the kinematic viscosity, in m²/s; μ is the dynamic viscosity, in Pa·s; ρ is the density of the oil, in kg/m³. Oil data sheets quote ν in centistokes, where 1 cSt = 10⁻⁶ m²/s.

Two viscosities exist because they answer two questions. The shear stress inside a film depends on μ alone, so bearing formulas use μ. The rate at which an oil drains through a tube under its own weight depends on μ/ρ, and the standard viscometers time exactly that flow, so data sheets quote ν. Water at 20 °C is about 1 cSt; a medium machine oil is tens of cSt.

Both values fall steeply as temperature rises. Too thin and the film breaks and metal touches. Too thick and the oil will not flow into the bearing, and churning loss raises the temperature further. Viscosity is therefore matched to speed, load and running temperature, not picked because the oil looks thick.

The viscosity index (VI) is a number that says how little the viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI means the viscosity changes less. Plain mineral oils sit near 100; many synthetics are above 130. A high VI does not mean a thick oil.

Other properties on the data sheet

Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour above the oil gives a momentary flash when a test flame is passed over it; at the fire point, a little higher, the vapour keeps burning. A high flash point means safer running near hot surfaces.

Pour point is the lowest temperature at which the oil still pours. Cloud point is the temperature at which wax crystals first appear and the oil turns cloudy; it sits above the pour point. Both matter for a machine that starts cold.

Oiliness, or lubricity, is the ability of the oil to cling to metal and lower friction when the film is thin, as in boundary lubrication. It comes from the oil's own molecules and from additives, not from viscosity.

Oxidation stability is the resistance to reacting with air when hot; a poor oil forms sludge and acids. Turbine oils must separate from water quickly, while cutting oils must form a stable emulsion with it.

SAE 30, SAE 15W-40 and ISO VG 46 are grade labels. Each label stands for a band of viscosity measured at stated temperatures. The number in the label is not a viscosity in Pa·s.

Worked example

Question. An oil sample drawn from a gearbox at 40 °C has dynamic viscosity μ = 0.05 Pa·s and density ρ = 900 kg/m³. (a) Find its kinematic viscosity in m²/s and in cSt. (b) A handbook lists the same density as 0.9 g/cm³. Show what a student gets by putting 0.9 directly into ν = μ/ρ. (c) The same oil fills a 0.1 mm gap under a plate of area 0.01 m² that slides at 2 m/s over a fixed plate. Find the shear stress in the oil and the force needed to slide the plate.

  1. Data in SI: μ = 0.05 Pa·s and ρ = 900 kg/m³.
  2. Kinematic viscosity: ν = μ/ρ = 0.05/900 = 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  3. In centistokes: 1 cSt = 10⁻⁶ m²/s, so ν = 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ / 10⁻⁶ = 55.56 cSt.
  4. Part (b), the slip: 0.9 g/cm³ is the same density as 900 kg/m³, but 0.05/0.9 = 0.0556 is a mixed-unit number, 1000 times larger than the true 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s. Convert density to kg/m³ first.
  5. Part (c): with a thin film the velocity rises in a straight line from 0 at the fixed plate to U = 2 m/s at the moving plate, so du/dy = U/h = 2/(0.1 × 10⁻³) = 20 000 s⁻¹.
  6. Shear stress: τ = μ du/dy = 0.05 × 20 000 = 1000 Pa.
  7. Force: F = τ A = 1000 × 0.01 = 10 N.
  8. Sanity check: 55.6 cSt is about 56 times water, the range of a medium machine oil.

Answer. ν = 5.56 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s = 55.6 cSt (the density must be in kg/m³, or the answer is 1000 times too large); τ = 1000 Pa and F = 10 N.

Exam traps

  • Swapping μ and ν, or writing 55.6 cSt = 55.6 Pa·s. Centistokes is kinematic; pascal-second is dynamic.
  • Putting density in g/cm³ straight into ν = μ/ρ with μ in Pa·s. The answer is then 1000 times too large and in no unit at all.
  • Treating a high viscosity index as a thick oil. VI says how little viscosity changes with temperature; it says nothing about the value at 40 °C.
  • Confusing cloud point with pour point. The oil turns cloudy first, and stops pouring at a lower temperature.
  • Reading SAE 30 as a viscosity of 30 Pa·s. It is a grade label covering a viscosity band measured at stated temperatures.

5.2 Sliding contact and rolling contact bearings

A bearing holds a rotating shaft in place and lets it turn with as little resistance as possible. There are two families. Sliding contact bearings let one surface rub over another, separated by a film of oil. Rolling contact bearings put balls or rollers between the surfaces so they roll instead of rub. The choice fixes friction, starting torque, load capacity, speed limit, noise, space and cost. You meet both every day: the bush at the tail end of a pump shaft, and the sealed ball bearing inside a ceiling fan.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for viscosity and how a lubricant film carries load.

Sliding contact bearings and their materials

A sliding contact bearing, also called a plain bearing, carries load on two surfaces that slide over each other. A film of lubricant between them does the real work; when the film is complete, the metal never touches metal. A handful of shapes cover most applications.

  • Journal bearing: the shaft end (the journal) turns inside a sleeve, and the load is radial, at right angles to the axis. This is the commonest bearing in a pump or a turbine.
  • Bush: the replaceable sleeve itself. The same word is used for a one-piece plain bearing pressed into a housing.
  • Thrust bearing: takes load along the axis of the shaft.
  • Pivot: a small hemisphere or cone on the shaft end resting on a flat or conical seat, for light axial loads in instruments.
  • Collar: a ring fixed on the shaft that bears against a flat face of the housing, giving more rubbing area than a pivot.

Bearing materials are softer than the shaft, so they wear first and can embed grit instead of scoring the journal. Babbitt, a tin- or lead-base white metal, is soft and conforms well; it is cast as a thin lining on a steel shell for high speed. Bronze is stronger and is used as a solid bush or a lining for heavier loads at moderate speed. Cast iron is cheap and is used as a solid bush at low speed and light load.

p = W / (L d)

p is the bearing pressure in N/mm²; W is the radial load in N; L is the bearing length in mm; d is the journal diameter in mm. The product L d is the projected area.

Rolling contact bearings

A rolling contact bearing puts hardened steel balls or rollers between an inner ring and an outer ring. The rings sit on the shaft and in the housing, the rolling elements carry the load, and a cage keeps them evenly spaced. Because the contact rolls, friction and starting torque are far lower than in a plain bearing. Some syllabi call these antifriction bearings; the name means low friction, not zero friction.

  • Ball bearing: balls touch the rings at points, so it runs fast and takes radial load plus light axial load. This is the bearing inside a ceiling fan or a small motor.
  • Cylindrical roller bearing: rollers touch along a line, so radial capacity is higher. The plain type, with ribs on one ring only, takes no axial load; types with ribs on both rings take a light axial load in one direction.
  • Needle roller bearing: very thin, long rollers. It fits a small radial space, often without an inner ring, and suits oscillating joints such as a universal joint.
  • Tapered roller bearing: rollers and raceways are cones, so it takes radial and axial load together. Vehicle hubs and gearbox shafts use it.
  • Spherical roller bearing: two rows of barrel-shaped rollers in a spherical outer race. Heavy load, and it tolerates small misalignment between shaft and housing.

Comparing the two families

The friction torque in either bearing can be written with one coefficient, and the power lost follows from it. Use this only for an estimate.

Lubrication regimes

How well a sliding bearing performs depends on how much oil sits between the surfaces. Three regimes are named.

  • Boundary lubrication: the film is thinner than the surface roughness, so high spots touch. Friction is high, μ around 0.05 to 0.15. This is the state at starting, at very low speed and under very heavy load. Additives in the oil, not its viscosity, do the protecting here.
  • Mixed lubrication: part of the load is carried by a film and part by touching asperities. Most machines pass through this band while speeding up.
  • Hydrodynamic lubrication: a full film separates the surfaces and the pressure in the oil carries the whole load. Friction is low, μ around 0.002 to 0.01, and there is no wear. Chapter 5.3 shows how the rotating shaft builds that pressure.

Rolling bearings also run on a film, but a very thin one squeezed between curved surfaces; it is called an elastohydrodynamic film and it is why the right grease matters even though the metal looks dry.

Worked example

Question. A shaft of diameter d = 100 mm carries a steady radial load W = 20 kN at n = 900 rpm. Compare the friction power lost in (a) a plain bearing with μ = 0.01 and (b) a rolling contact bearing with μ = 0.002. Use T = μ W r and P = T ω.

  1. Data: d = 100 mm = 0.1 m, so radius r = d/2 = 0.05 m. W = 20 kN = 20 000 N. n = 900 rpm.
  2. Convert speed to rad/s: ω = 2πn/60 = 2π × 900/60 = 94.25 rad/s.
  3. Plain bearing torque: T = μ W r = 0.01 × 20 000 × 0.05 = 10 N·m.
  4. Plain bearing power: P = T ω = 10 × 94.25 = 942.5 W.
  5. Rolling bearing torque: T = 0.002 × 20 000 × 0.05 = 2 N·m.
  6. Rolling bearing power: P = 2 × 94.25 = 188.5 W.
  7. Difference: 942.5 − 188.5 = 754.0 W, which runs all day as heat in the plain bearing.

Answer. The rolling bearing loses 188.5 W against 942.5 W for the plain bearing, about 754 W less.

Exam traps

  • Using the shaft diameter instead of the radius 0.05 m in T = μ W r, which doubles the torque and the power.
  • Feeding rpm straight into P = T ω. Convert first: ω = 2πn/60, and only then multiply.
  • Reading 'antifriction bearing' as 'no friction'. A rolling bearing still has friction, and its starting torque is low but never zero.
  • Treating bush, babbitt and bronze as the same kind of thing: a bush is a shape, babbitt is a lining material, and bronze can be either a solid bush or a lining.
  • Assuming a roller bearing is always the better choice. A plain cylindrical roller bearing carries no axial load, so it cannot locate a shaft along its axis on its own.

5.3 Hydrodynamic journal bearings

A hydrodynamic journal bearing carries a rotating shaft on a film of oil. The shaft turns inside a slightly larger sleeve. It drags oil into a gap that narrows in the direction of rotation. Viscous drag pushes oil through that wedge, pressure rises, and the pressure balances the load. Metal surfaces stay apart while the shaft runs. You meet these bearings in steam turbines, pumps, motors, gearboxes and crankshafts. The design question is simple to state: can the oil film stay thick enough at the running speed, load and viscosity?

You need: Chapter 5.1 for viscosity and Chapter 5.2 for sliding contact.

Geometry and the oil wedge

A hydrodynamic journal bearing is a plain sleeve around a shaft. The journal diameter D and bearing length L set the size; both are in mm. The journal radius is r = D/2. The radial clearance c is the small difference between the bearing radius and the journal radius. It is often a small fraction of a millimetre. Diametral clearance is twice the radial clearance; do not use it as c.

The shaft centre sits off the bearing centre by the eccentricity e. The eccentricity ratio is ε = e/c, a pure number. The closest gap is the minimum film thickness h₀.

ε = e / c, h₀ = c − e = c(1 − ε)

e is the journal centre shift from the bearing centre, c is the radial clearance, ε is the eccentricity ratio, and h₀ is the minimum film thickness; e, c and h₀ use the same length unit.

When the shaft turns, oil sticking to the journal is dragged into the clearance. The gap narrows in the direction of rotation, so the oil flow must pass a smaller area. Pressure rises in this wedge. The pressure peak lies between the start of the wedge and the minimum film; the resultant pressure balances the external load. The journal centre moves until this balance is reached. The angle between the load line and the line joining the centres is the attitude angle. Reynolds equation gives the film pressure; here we use its results and do not derive it. Some syllabi call these fluid-film bearings.

Regimes, pressure and oil supply

Three lubrication regimes matter. In boundary lubrication the oil film is too thin; high spots touch and carry load, so wear occurs. In mixed lubrication the film carries part of the load and high spots carry part. In hydrodynamic lubrication a full film separates the surfaces, and pressure in the oil carries the whole load. This chapter is about the third regime.

The pressure distribution is not uniform. Positive pressure builds in the converging half of the clearance. In the diverging half the pressure falls toward ambient and the oil may cavitate (form vapour pockets). Cavities cannot carry tensile load, so the positive region does the useful work. Oil grooves supply oil to the bearing. Put feed grooves in a low-pressure or unloaded region. A circumferential groove running across the loaded film lets pressure escape and lowers load capacity. Supply must be continuous; starvation collapses the film.

Starts and stops cause the most wear. At zero speed there is no hydrodynamic film, so the surfaces run in boundary or mixed contact. Large machines start the oil pump before the shaft and stop the shaft before the pump. Frequent starts shorten bearing life. Hydrodynamic action needs enough speed, enough viscosity and enough oil; when any one falls, the film thins.

What the design must check

A design must keep h₀ above the combined surface roughness, any expected debris and a safety margin. h₀ depends on c, ε, viscosity, speed, load, L/D and temperature. A smaller c raises load capacity but lowers oil flow and raises temperature. A larger c raises oil flow and cooling, but h₀ rises with c only up to an optimum; beyond it the journal runs more eccentric and the film thins again. A long bearing (large L/D) carries more load but is sensitive to misalignment; a short bearing leaks oil from its ends.

Viscosity must be taken at the operating temperature. Hot oil is thinner, so a bearing that is safe when cold may fail when hot. Friction power becomes heat, and that heat lowers viscosity. Design is therefore iterative: choose c, L/D and oil grade, estimate ε and h₀, then check temperature and oil flow. The Sommerfeld number (Chapter 5.4) groups speed, viscosity, load and clearance; the Raimondi and Boyd charts (Chapter 5.5) give ε, h₀, friction and flow.

Petroff's equation and the friction curve

A lightly loaded or very fast journal hardly moves off centre, so the film is close to a uniform ring of thickness c. The oil is then sheared like the film between two plates: velocity U = 2πrn at the journal, zero at the bush. Shear stress τ = μU/c acts on the whole journal surface 2πrL, and the friction torque is that force times r.

T = 4π² μ n r³ L / c, f = 2π² (μn/p)(r/c)

T is the friction torque in N·m; μ in Pa·s; n in rev/s; r, L and c in m; p = W/(2rL) is the bearing pressure in Pa; f = T/(W r) is the friction coefficient. This is Petroff's equation. It ignores eccentricity, so it underestimates friction in a heavily loaded bearing.

Plot f against the bearing modulus μN/p (N in rpm) and you get the friction curve. At small μN/p the film is thin and friction is high (boundary and mixed); friction falls to a minimum, then rises in a straight line once the film is full, as Petroff predicts. A common textbook rule keeps the working μN/p about three times the value at the minimum, so a dip in speed or a hot spell does not push the bearing back into mixed lubrication.

Worked example

Question. A journal bearing has journal diameter D = 50 mm, radial clearance c = 0.05 mm and eccentricity ratio ε = 0.6. Find the eccentricity e and the minimum film thickness h₀. Give h₀ in mm and in μm.

  1. Data: D = 50 mm (not needed for e and h₀), c = 0.05 mm, ε = 0.6.
  2. From ε = e/c, e = ε c = 0.6 × 0.05 mm = 0.03 mm.
  3. h₀ = c − e = 0.05 mm − 0.03 mm = 0.02 mm.
  4. Alternative: h₀ = c(1 − ε) = 0.05 × (1 − 0.6) = 0.02 mm.
  5. Convert: h₀ = 0.02 × 1000 μm = 20 μm.

Answer. e = 0.03 mm and h₀ = 0.02 mm = 20 μm.

Worked example 2

Question. A lightly loaded fan bearing has journal diameter 60 mm, length 60 mm and radial clearance 0.06 mm. It carries 1.8 kN at 3000 rpm with oil of μ = 0.015 Pa·s. Use Petroff's equation to find the friction coefficient, the friction torque and the power lost.

  1. Data in SI: r = 0.03 m, L = 0.06 m, c = 0.06 × 10⁻³ m, so r/c = 500. n = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s. W = 1800 N.
  2. Bearing pressure: p = W/(2rL) = 1800/(0.06 × 0.06) = 5.0 × 10⁵ Pa = 0.5 MPa.
  3. μn/p = 0.015 × 50/(5.0 × 10⁵) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶.
  4. f = 2π² × 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 500 = 19.739 × 7.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.0148.
  5. Torque: T = f W r = 0.0148 × 1800 × 0.03 = 0.799 N·m. Check with T = 4π²μnr³L/c = 39.48 × 0.015 × 50 × 2.7 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.06/(6 × 10⁻⁵) = 0.799 N·m.
  6. Power: P = 2πnT = 2π × 50 × 0.799 = 251 W.
  7. Is Petroff fair here? S = (μn/p)(r/c)² = 1.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 250 000 = 0.375, a light load with the journal near centre, so yes.

Answer. f = 0.0148, T = 0.799 N·m and the friction power is about 251 W.

Exam traps

  • Radial versus diametral clearance: if a drawing gives 0.10 mm on diameter, c = 0.05 mm. Use c in h₀ = c(1 − ε).
  • h₀ is the smallest gap, not the centre shift e. With c = 0.05 mm and ε = 0.6, e = 0.03 mm but h₀ = 0.02 mm.
  • Using Petroff's equation for a heavily loaded bearing. It assumes a centred journal and gives too little friction once ε is large.
  • An oil-filled bearing at rest is not hydrodynamic. It runs in boundary or mixed contact until speed builds.
  • A groove in the loaded film lowers load capacity; feed oil to the unloaded or low-pressure side.

5.4 Sommerfeld number

Four things decide how a journal bearing behaves: shaft speed, oil viscosity, load and clearance. Change one and the film thickness, friction and oil flow all move. The Sommerfeld number packs all four into a single dimensionless group, so one set of charts can describe every bearing of a given length-to-diameter ratio. It is the number you look up on the Raimondi and Boyd charts, the number you quote when comparing two bearing designs, and the quickest check on whether a bearing is running with a safe oil film or is about to rub.

You need: the oil-film picture of a hydrodynamic journal bearing from Chapter 5.3, and viscosity and its units from Chapter 5.1.

Four variables, one group

Speed, viscosity, load and clearance act together. Charts drawn against four separate variables would need a library of curves, one for every size and every oil. That is unworkable in a design office.

Dimensional analysis removes the size and the units. The group μn/p has no dimensions: (Pa·s × 1/s) ÷ Pa leaves nothing. The group r/c has none either, because both are lengths in metres. Combine them and one number carries the effect of speed, viscosity, load and clearance at once.

Two bearings with the same value of that number and the same length-to-diameter ratio behave alike, even if one is a 25 mm pump shaft and the other a 250 mm turbine shaft. A four-variable problem becomes a one-variable problem, and a single chart covers every bearing of a given L/D.

The definition and its units

S = (μ n / p) (r/c)²

S is the Sommerfeld number, dimensionless. μ is the dynamic viscosity of the oil at its working temperature, in Pa·s. n is shaft speed in revolutions per second (rev/s). p is the bearing pressure in Pa, p = W/(L D), where W is the load in N, L is the bearing length in m and D is the journal diameter in m. r is the journal radius in m, r = D/2. c is the radial clearance in m, the gap on one side.

Substituting p = W/(L D) removes the pressure and gives the form used when you know the load directly:

S = μ n L D (r/c)² / W

the symbols are as above; μn is a pressure (Pa), so μn × LD/W is (pressure × area)/force, a pure number. S stays dimensionless whatever units you use, as long as you are consistent.

Some books call the group μn/p the bearing characteristic number or bearing modulus. The Sommerfeld number is that group multiplied by (r/c)². Keep the two names apart.

The ratio r/c sits inside a square, so it dominates. For a machined bearing r/c typically lies between 500 and 1000 (c/r = 0.001 to 0.002). At r/c = 500 the square is 250 000, which is why S usually lands between 0.01 and 1 even though μn/p is of the order of 10⁻⁷.

A few books print 1/S under the same name. Before reading any chart, check which way its curves run.

What a high or low S means

Eccentricity e is the offset of the journal centre from the bush centre; the eccentricity ratio is ε = e/c, and the minimum film thickness is h₀ = c(1 − ε). High S means small ε and a thick film.

  • S rises in direct proportion to viscosity μ and to speed n.
  • S falls as load W rises, and falls as the square of the clearance c grows.
  • Small S: the journal is pushed close to the bush, the minimum film is thin, and the risk of metal-to-metal contact and wear is high.
  • Large S: the journal floats higher, eccentricity falls and the film thickens, but the film shears more oil and generates more heat to be carried away.

The design aim is a middle band. Very small S gives a thin, hot, wearing bearing; very large S wastes power in churning viscous oil. For the same L/D, a bearing running at higher S generally sits at lower eccentricity, so the same eccentricity chart serves every size once you plot against S.

This is why the Sommerfeld number is the horizontal axis of the design charts in Chapter 5.5. You compute S, follow it up to the curve for your L/D, and read eccentricity, minimum film thickness, friction and oil flow.

Getting the inputs right

Three inputs cause most of the lost marks.

  1. Speed: use revolutions per second. A shaft at 1500 rpm is at 25 rev/s, not 1500.
  2. Pressure: use the projected area L × D. The oil film pressure acts on the projected rectangle of the journal, not on the curved surface πDL.
  3. Viscosity: use the value at the oil's operating temperature inside the bearing, not the ISO VG grade number or the value at 40 °C from a supplier table.

Clearance is radial: c = (bush bore − journal diameter)/2. Using the diametral clearance halves the ratio r/c and quarters S. Keep every length in metres and every pressure in pascals and the group stays dimensionless.

Worked example

Question. A full journal bearing carries a steady load W = 10 kN. Journal diameter D = 50 mm, bearing length L = 50 mm, radial clearance c = 0.05 mm. The shaft runs at 1500 rpm and the oil viscosity at the working temperature is μ = 0.02 Pa·s. Calculate the Sommerfeld number.

  1. Convert the speed to revolutions per second: n = 1500/60 = 25 rev/s.
  2. Journal radius: r = D/2 = 0.050/2 = 0.025 m. Radial clearance: c = 0.05 mm = 5 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  3. Projected area: L × D = 0.050 × 0.050 = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m².
  4. Bearing pressure: p = W/(L D) = 10 000/(2.5 × 10⁻³) = 4 × 10⁶ Pa = 4 MPa.
  5. Clearance ratio: r/c = 0.025/(5 × 10⁻⁵) = 500.
  6. Viscous term: μn/p = (0.02 × 25)/(4 × 10⁶) = 0.5/(4 × 10⁶) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁷.
  7. Square the ratio: (r/c)² = 500² = 250 000.
  8. S = 1.25 × 10⁻⁷ × 250 000 = 0.03125.

Answer. The Sommerfeld number is S = 0.03125, dimensionless.

Exam traps

  • Putting n in rpm. The group needs revolutions per second; 1500 rpm is 25 rev/s, and using 1500 inflates S sixty times.
  • Using πDL as the area. Bearing pressure acts on the projected area L × D, the rectangle you see when you look at the bearing from the side; πDL is the curved surface of the journal and makes p π times too small.
  • Feeding in the diametral clearance instead of the radial clearance. That halves r/c and cuts S to one quarter.
  • Forgetting to square r/c. The ratio is around 500, so dropping the square changes S by a factor of 250 000.
  • Taking viscosity at 40 °C from a grade table. The bearing runs hotter and thinner; S must use the viscosity at the operating temperature.

5.5 Raimondi and Boyd design charts

The Raimondi and Boyd charts turn two numbers, the Sommerfeld number S and the length-to-diameter ratio L/D, into everything else a journal bearing design needs: how far off centre the journal sits, the thinnest oil film, the friction, the oil flow and, through a heat balance, the temperature rise. They exist because the film pressure in a bearing of finite length has no closed-form solution; it was solved numerically once and published as curves and tables. With the table for L/D = 1 and five minutes, you can check a bearing for a pump, a gearbox or a turbine.

You need: the Sommerfeld number from Chapter 5.4 and the minimum film thickness idea from Chapter 5.3.

What the charts give you

Oil in a loaded journal bearing is dragged through a gap that narrows in the direction of rotation, and the Reynolds equation gives the pressure in that film. In a bearing of finite length oil also escapes at the two ends, so the pressure falls near each end, and that end effect rules out a clean formula. Raimondi and Boyd solved the equation by computer for a full 360° bearing and published the answers as curves against S, one curve for each L/D.

The input is the Sommerfeld number of Chapter 5.4, S = (μn/p)(r/c)², with μ in Pa·s at the film temperature, n in rev/s, p = W/(LD) in Pa and r/c the clearance ratio. The outputs, all dimensionless, are listed below for L/D = 1, the most common choice. Design data books print the same rows; between two rows, interpolate in proportion to S.

Read the columns like this. ε is the eccentricity ratio and h₀/c = 1 − ε the film thickness ratio. φ is the attitude angle between the load line and the line of centres. (r/c)f is the friction variable, so f = (r/c)f ÷ (r/c). Q/(rcnL) is the flow variable, the total oil pumped into the film. Qs/Q is the share of that flow that leaks out at the ends. p/pmax gives the peak film pressure pmax = p ÷ (p/pmax). Notice the trend: as S falls, ε rises and the film thins.

The curves assume a steady load, a Newtonian oil of one viscosity, no misalignment and no oil groove inside the loaded arc. Break one of these and the chart value is a first guess, not the truth.

The design procedure

  1. Fix L/D, usually between 0.5 and 1.5; L/D = 1 is the common first choice.
  2. Compute S with the viscosity at the expected mean film temperature, not the tank temperature.
  3. Read ε at that S on the curve for your L/D, and find h₀ = c(1 − ε), with c the radial clearance.
  4. Read (r/c)f; then f = (r/c)f ÷ (r/c), the torque T = f W r and the power lost Pf = 2πnT.
  5. Read the flow variable; then Q = [Q/(rcnL)] × r c n L in m³/s. That sizes the supply.
  6. Find the temperature rise from the heat balance below, and check h₀ and the temperature against the limits.

The heat balance assumes all the friction heat leaves in the oil flow: Pf = ρ cp Q ΔT. Substitute the chart variables and the lengths cancel, leaving ΔT = 4π p (r/c)f ÷ [ρ cp × Q/(rcnL)]. For a typical mineral oil, ρ = 860 kg/m³ and cp = 1.76 kJ/(kg·K), so 4π/(ρcp) = 8.30 × 10⁻⁶ and, with p in MPa:

ΔT = 8.30 p (r/c)f / [Q/(rcnL)]

ΔT is the oil temperature rise in °C, p the bearing pressure in MPa, (r/c)f and Q/(rcnL) the chart readings. Some texts divide by (1 − ½ Qs/Q) as well, on the view that the side leakage leaves at the mean temperature; that gives a larger ΔT, so state which form you use.

The temperature loop and the limits

The charts were drawn for one viscosity, yet the oil that carries the load also heats up, and hot oil is thinner. So the last step feeds back into the first. Take the inlet temperature, add half of ΔT for the mean film temperature, look up the viscosity there and recompute S. Read the table again. Two passes usually settle it.

Two checks close the design. The film must be thicker than the combined roughness and the dirt that passes the filter; a widely used rule (Trumpler) asks for h₀ ≥ 0.005 + 0.00004 D, in mm with D in mm. And the bearing metal runs hotter than the oil leaving it, so keep the outlet oil well below the oil's limit. The charts also lose accuracy at very high ε, above about 0.95, and in turbulent or heavily grooved bearings.

5.6 Ball and roller bearings: load ratings and life

A rolling bearing fails by fatigue, not at one fixed load. Heavy load shortens life. Makers give two numbers: C, the basic dynamic load rating, and C₀, the basic static load rating. From C and the equivalent load P you get L₁₀, the life reached by 90 of 100 identical bearings. This chapter computes L₁₀ = (C/P)p in million revolutions, then in hours, and handles a load that changes through the day.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for bearing parts, and fatigue from Failure Theories and Fatigue Design.

Two ratings: C₀ and C

A ball or roller bearing rarely snaps. It fails by fatigue: after many revolutions a small piece of raceway metal flakes off, called spalling. Life depends on load. Roughly, double the load and a ball bearing lasts one-eighth as long. Standards give two ratings.

  • Basic static load rating C₀, in N: the load that leaves a total permanent deformation of 0.0001 times the rolling-element diameter (ball or roller) at the most heavily loaded contact. Use C₀ for stationary, slow, or heavy shock at rest.
  • Basic dynamic load rating C, in N: the load at which identical bearings reach a rating life of one million (10⁶) revolutions with 90% healthy. C is capacity, not breaking load.

Both come from the catalogue, not from derivation. For a 25 mm bore deep-groove ball bearing, C is roughly 15 kN and C₀ roughly 8 kN. Use them to form the ratio C/P.

Equivalent dynamic load P

A real bearing carries radial load Fr, in N, perpendicular to the shaft axis, and axial or thrust load Fa, in N, along the axis. Fold both into one equivalent dynamic load P.

P = X Fr + Y Fa

P in N; Fr radial load in N; Fa axial load in N; X radial factor, Y thrust factor, from the catalogue for that bearing.

P is the pure radial load giving the same fatigue as the actual mix. For pure radial load, X = 1, Y = 0, so P = Fr. For pure thrust load, X = 0, so P = Y Fa.

Single-row deep-groove ball bearings take small thrust. Catalogue tables compare Fa/Fr with threshold e and Fa/C₀. If Fa/Fr ≤ e, X = 1, Y = 0, so P = Fr. Above e, X = 0.56 and Y comes from the table, typically 1 to 2. Use tables for the chosen bearing type.

Life in revolutions and in hours

L10 = (C/P)p

L10 is rating life in million revolutions; C and P are in the same unit (N or kN); p = 3 for ball bearings, p = 10/3 for roller bearings.

The subscript 10 is a probability, not a count. L10 is the life 90% of a batch reaches; about 10% fail earlier. C = 30 kN, P = 6 kN gives L10 = (30/6)³ = 125 million revolutions. The same C on a roller bearing gives 510/3 ≈ 214 million, because a roller spreads contact over a line.

L10h = 10⁶ (C/P)p ÷ (60 n)

L10h is the life in hours; n is the speed in rpm; 10⁶ turns million revolutions into revolutions and 60 n is the revolutions per hour.

At n = 1500 rpm: L10h = 10⁶ × 125/(60 × 1500) = 1389 hours, about 58 days of continuous running. At half speed the hours double.

When the load and speed change in steps, fatigue adds up revolution by revolution. The mean equivalent load Pe is the steady load that does the same damage: Pe = [ (P1p n1 t1 + P2p n2 t2 + …) ÷ (n1 t1 + n2 t2 + …) ]1/p, where Pi in N is the load in step i, ni its speed in rpm and ti its share of the time. The bearing then runs at the mean speed nm = Σ ni ti.

For a reliability R other than 90 %, multiply L10 by the life adjustment factor a1 of ISO 281: 0.64 for 95 % and 0.25 for 99 %. Some textbooks use the Weibull form L/L10 = [ln(1/R) ÷ ln(1/0.9)]1/1.17 instead, which gives 0.54 for 95 %; use the one your data book uses.

Worked example

Question. A deep-groove ball bearing on a pump shaft has C = 30 kN. The shaft runs at n = 1500 rpm. The equivalent dynamic load is P = 6 kN. Find L10 in million revolutions and in hours.

  1. Check units: C = 30 kN and P = 6 kN are both in kN, so C/P is a pure number.
  2. Ball bearing: p = 3.
  3. C/P = 30/6 = 5.
  4. L10 = 5³ = 125 million revolutions.
  5. Revolutions per hour: 60 × 1500 = 90 000.

Life-modification methods multiply it by factors for lubrication, contamination and steel fatigue limit, so catalogues quote longer lives. Condition monitoring uses vibration and temperature to estimate used L₁₀ life and replace a bearing before failure. Search ISO 281, life modification factor and vibration signature analysis.

5.7 Selection of rolling contact bearings

A catalogue lists dozens of bearings that share the same bore, so the designer needs one number that says which of them can do the job. That number is the required basic dynamic load rating Creq. You find the equivalent load from the radial and axial forces, convert the life wanted in hours into million revolutions, and compute Creq. Then you pick the smallest bearing whose catalogue rating C is at least Creq, and you check static safety and the limiting speed. Pumps, fans, gearboxes, electric motors and machine tool spindles are all chosen this way.

You need: Chapter 5.6 for C, P and the L10 life equation, and Chapter 5.2 for the rolling bearing types.

Selection means turning load and life into one number

A rolling bearing is not designed from a blank page; it is chosen from a catalogue. For each bearing the catalogue gives a basic dynamic load rating C: the steady load, in newtons, that would give a rating life of one million revolutions. The machine gives you a load and a life in hours. Selection is the step that turns those two into a single number you can compare with the catalogue.

The life equation of Chapter 5.6 links C, P and life. Inverting it gives the rating you must ask the catalogue for.

Creq = P · L₁₀1/p

Creq is the required basic dynamic load rating in N; P is the equivalent dynamic load in N; L₁₀ is the rating life in million revolutions; p = 3 for ball bearings and 10/3 for roller bearings.

The life hidden inside C is counted in million revolutions, so the hours must be converted before they enter the formula.

L₁₀ = 60 n L10h / 10⁶

n is the speed in rev/min; L10h is the required life in hours; 60 changes rev/min into rev per hour and 10⁶ changes revolutions into millions.

Choose the smallest bearing in the size range whose C is at least Creq. A larger C costs more, weighs more and needs a bigger housing, so an oversized bearing is wasted money. The rating is not the only test, though: the static rating and the limiting speed are checked as well.

The equivalent load P

Bearings are rated for one kind of load: pure radial, steady, applied through the centre. A real bearing carries a radial load Fr and often an axial load Fa. P is the single radial load that would wear the bearing out in the same number of revolutions as the real combination.

P = X Fr + Y Fa

Fr and Fa are the radial and axial loads in N; X is the radial factor and Y the axial factor, read from the table for that bearing type.

For a single-row deep-groove ball bearing the factors depend on Fa/C₀, the thrust compared with the static rating. If Fa/Fr ≤ e, then X = 1 and Y = 0, so P = Fr. Static check, speed check, and the order of work

A bearing that turns very slowly, stands still, or takes a heavy shock can fail with no fatigue at all. The rolling elements press dents into the race, called brinelling, and the bearing runs rough for the rest of its life. Against this the catalogue gives a basic static load rating C₀, the load at which a fixed small permanent deformation appears.

P₀ = 0.6 Fr + 0.5 Fa, but never less than Fr

P₀ is the equivalent static load in N for a deep-groove ball bearing; other types have their own factors in the catalogue.

The static safety factor is s₀ = C₀ ÷ P₀. Use about 1.5 to 2 for smooth, quiet running, 2 to 3 for ordinary machinery, and 3 or more where shock is expected. Every catalogue entry also carries a limiting speed, lower for grease and higher for oil; running above it heats the bearing faster than the housing can shed heat.

  1. Find Fr and Fa on the bearing from the shaft loads (shaft design gives the reactions).
  2. Fix the required life L10h and convert it to L₁₀ in million revolutions.
  3. Assume a trial bearing of the right bore and read its C and C₀.
  4. Find Fa/C₀, then e and Y from the table; compute P = X Fr + Y Fa.
  5. Compute Creq = P L₁₀1/p and compare with C; move up a size if C is too small.
  6. Check C₀/P₀ against the static safety factor, and check the limiting speed.

Worked example

Question. A deep-groove ball bearing of 45 mm bore carries Fr = 5 kN and Fa = 2 kN at 1200 rev/min and must give L10h = 10 000 h in ordinary machinery (s₀ = 2). Two bearings of that bore are available: A with C = 33.2 kN, C₀ = 21.6 kN, and B with C = 55.3 kN, C₀ = 31.5 kN. Select the bearing.

Exam traps

  • Putting L10h straight into C = P L1/3. The life inside the rating is in million revolutions, so 10 000 h at 1200 rev/min becomes 720, not 10 000.
  • Using Y when Fa/Fr ≤ e. Below the threshold Y = 0 and X = 1, so the axial load drops out; adding Y Fa anyway inflates P and over-sizes the bearing.
  • Reading e and Y for one bearing and then choosing another. Fa/C₀ changes with the bearing, so recompute P for the bearing you finally pick.
  • Skipping the static check. A shock-loaded or slowly turning bearing can brinell its races even when C ≥ Creq.
  • Taking p = 3 for a roller bearing. It is 10/3, and the same P and L₁₀ then give a smaller Creq than a ball bearing would.

5.8 Seals and gaskets

A seal keeps the oil in and the dirt, water and process fluid out. Every bearing in this unit needs one: a journal bearing loses its film if the oil leaks away, and a rolling bearing fails early once grit gets in. Static seals, such as gaskets and O-rings, close a joint between parts that do not move against each other; dynamic seals, such as lip seals, face seals and labyrinths, close the gap round a moving shaft. Designing a gasket joint means finding the bolt load that seats the gasket and keeps it tight under pressure.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for lubricants and Chapter 5.2 for the bearing types a seal protects.

Static and dynamic seals

A leak needs a path: the roughness between two bolted faces, or the running clearance round a shaft. A contact seal fills the path with a soft material pressed hard against both faces; a clearance seal makes it so long and narrow that little fluid gets through.

  • Gasket: a flat ring of compressible material clamped between flanges by bolts, on pipe flanges, pump casings and covers.
  • O-ring: an elastomer ring of round section squeezed in a groove, in a cover joint or on a slowly moving rod.
  • Lip seal (oil seal): a rubber lip held on the shaft by a garter spring, facing the oil; the standard gearbox and motor shaft seal.
  • Mechanical face seal: a spring presses a rotating carbon ring against a fixed ring of silicon carbide or ceramic, both lapped flat; used on pumps.
  • Labyrinth seal: fins with a small running gap and no contact. It never wears and suits steam turbines, but always leaks a little.
  • Gland packing: braided rings squeezed round a shaft by a gland, on valves and slow pumps; it must drip slightly to stay cool.

Gasket joints: seating load and operating load

A gasket seals only after it has been crushed into the roughness of both flanges. The least contact stress that does this is the seating stress y, in MPa. Under pressure the fluid pushes the flanges apart and unloads the gasket, so the bolts must also keep a residual stress of m times the fluid pressure on it; m is the gasket factor. Both come from tables for each material. The pressure vessel code method uses the effective seating width b and the diameter G of the load reaction.

Wm2 = π b G y

Wm2 is the bolt load in N needed to seat the gasket before pressure is applied; b and G in mm; y in MPa.

Wm1 = (π/4) G² p + 2π b G m p

Wm1 is the bolt load in N needed under the design pressure p (MPa). The first term is the end force H of the pressure acting inside G; the second, Hp, keeps the gasket tight.

The bolts are sized for the larger of the two loads: total bolt core area Am = Wm ÷ σb, with σb the allowable bolt stress. Use an even number of bolts at a close pitch, and tighten them in a criss-cross order so the gasket is crushed evenly.

O-rings, lip seals and face seals

An O-ring seals because it is squeezed: the groove is shallower than the ring section d, and the fluid pressure then pushes the ring against the low-pressure wall of the groove, which tightens the seal. Handbooks give squeeze ranges near 15 to 30 per cent of d for static seals and less for moving ones, to keep friction down. The groove must also leave room for the rubber to spread and swell: the ring section should fill well under about 85 per cent of the groove area. Above roughly 10 MPa, or with a large clearance, a hard backup ring stops the rubber being extruded into the gap.

squeeze = (d − h)/d, fill = (πd²/4)/(w h)

d is the O-ring cross-section diameter, h the groove depth (the gland depth including any clearance), w the groove width, all in mm.

A lip seal is chosen by shaft diameter and surface speed; the shaft under the lip must be hard and finely ground, and the lip must face the fluid it holds back. Beyond a moderate speed and pressure a mechanical face seal takes over: its leak path is the micron gap between two lapped faces, and the spring and fluid pressure together set the face load.

Worked example

Question. A pressure vessel cover is sealed with a flat compressed-fibre gasket, inside diameter 300 mm and outside diameter 320 mm, with m = 2.00 and y = 11 MPa. The design pressure is 2 MPa and the allowable bolt stress is 138 MPa. Find the seating load, the operating load, the bolt area and a suitable set of bolts.

  1. Gasket width N = (320 − 300)/2 = 10 mm, so b₀ = N/2 = 5 mm. Since 5 ≤ 6.4 mm, b = 5 mm and G = mean diameter = (300 + 320)/2 = 310 mm.
  2. Seating load: Wm2 = π b G y = π × 5 × 310 × 11 = 53 564 N = 53.6 kN.
  3. Pressure end force: H = (π/4) G² p = 0.7854 × 310² × 2 = 150 954 N.

Exam traps

  • Sizing the bolts for the seating load only. They must carry the larger of Wm1 and Wm2; in the example the pressure case (189.9 kN) governs.
  • Using the outside diameter for G. For a narrow gasket (b₀ ≤ 6.4 mm) G is the mean diameter of the gasket.
  • Forgetting the factor 2π in Hp = 2π b G m p, or putting b = N instead of b₀ = N/2.
  • Thinking more O-ring squeeze is always better. Too much squeeze, or a groove filled above about 85 %, overloads the rubber and it cracks or extrudes.

Takeaways

  1. A lubricant does its job only if it stays between the surfaces, and viscosity is the property that decides whether it can.
  2. Viscosity falls as oil gets hotter, so a lubrication design has to be checked at the running temperature, not the cold start.
  3. Sliding bearings are quiet and cheap but need a full oil film; rolling bearings have low starting friction and need almost no attention.
  4. In a journal bearing the shaft's own rotation drags oil into a wedge, and that converging gap is what builds the pressure that carries the load.
  5. The Sommerfeld number collapses speed, viscosity, clearance and load into one number, which is why a single family of charts serves many bearings.
  6. Raimondi and Boyd charts turn S and L/D into eccentricity, film thickness, friction and oil flow; a heat balance on that flow gives the temperature rise.
  7. Bearing life falls steeply with load: life goes as (C/P)³ for ball bearings, so a 25% overload can halve or worse the life.
  8. Bearing selection is a loop: guess a bearing, compute P, get required C, pick from the catalogue, then recheck C₀, speed limit and temperature.
  9. Seals protect every bearing: gaskets and O-rings close static joints, lip seals, face seals and labyrinths close the gap round a shaft, and a gasket joint is bolted for the larger of its seating and operating loads.

Model answer.

  1. Dynamic viscosity. A fluid sheared between a fixed plate and a moving plate develops a shear stress proportional to the velocity gradient: τ = μ du/dy. The constant μ is the dynamic viscosity, in Pa·s (N·s/m²). It is the property that sets the friction and the film pressure in a bearing.
  2. Kinematic viscosity. ν = μ/ρ, in m²/s; oil data sheets quote it in centistokes, 1 cSt = 10⁻⁶ m²/s. Example: an oil with μ = 0.05 Pa·s and ρ = 900 kg/m³ has ν = 0.05/900 = 5.56 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s = 55.6 cSt. Standard viscometers time gravity flow, which is why ν is the catalogue value.
  3. Effect of temperature. The viscosity of every oil falls steeply as it warms: a machine oil can lose most of its viscosity between a cold start and its running temperature. Too thin and the film breaks and metal touches; too thick and the oil will not reach the bearing and churning raises the temperature.
  4. Viscosity index (VI). VI is a number that says how little the viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI means a flat viscosity–temperature curve. It does not mean a thick oil: two oils with the same 40 °C viscosity can have VI 95 and VI 150, and the second keeps more of its viscosity when hot and stiffens less when cold.
  5. The Chennai machine. The machine runs hot, perhaps an ambient of 38 °C plus its own friction heat. The design point is the hot end: the viscosity at the running temperature must still keep the film thickness above the roughness. A fairly heavy grade is chosen, and cold starting is no problem.
  6. The hill-station machine. The same heavy grade, started on a 5 °C morning, is so thick that it pours slowly (its pour point may be close), the pump struggles and the bearing runs starved for the first minutes, which is when most wear happens. In service the machine may never reach the temperature the grade was chosen for, so friction loss stays high.
  7. What the designer does. (a) Choose a high-VI or multigrade oil (for example a 15W-40 type): it behaves like a thin oil when cold and a thicker one when hot. (b) Check the viscosity at both ends of the temperature range, not only at 40 °C. (c) Add a sump heater or a warm-up run for cold sites, or an oil cooler and thermostat for hot ones. (d) Check the pour point is well below the coldest start.
  8. Closing check. A selection is correct when, at the hottest running temperature, the computed film thickness still meets the roughness rule, and at the coldest start the oil still pours and reaches the bearing within seconds.

Understand Explain how a hydrodynamic oil film is generated in a journal bearing. Define eccentricity ratio, minimum film thickness and attitude angle, and state the condition for full film lubrication. 12 marks

Points to cover. Journal drags oil into a converging wedge; Pressure rises in the converging part, falls and may cavitate in the diverging part; resultant balances the load; Journal centre takes an equilibrium offset e; ε = e/c, h₀ = c(1 − ε), attitude angle φ; Full film: h₀ well above combined roughness; needs speed, viscosity and supply.

Model answer.

  1. Geometry. The journal of radius r turns in a bush of slightly larger radius; the difference is the radial clearance c, a fraction of a millimetre. Under load W the journal centre moves off the bush centre by the eccentricity e, so the gap is no longer uniform.
  2. Wedge action. Oil sticks to the journal surface and is dragged round with it. On the side where the gap narrows in the direction of rotation, the oil is forced into a converging passage. To pass the same flow through a smaller section, the pressure must rise. This is the hydrodynamic wedge.
  3. Pressure field. Pressure builds from the oil inlet, peaks a little before the minimum gap and falls back to ambient soon after it. In the diverging part the pressure would go below ambient, so the oil releases vapour and air (cavitation); that part carries almost no load. The resultant of the positive pressure balances W.
  4. Equilibrium. The journal centre settles where the film force equals the load. It does not sit straight below the bush centre: it is displaced at an angle to the load line, in the direction of rotation.
  5. Definitions. Eccentricity ratio ε = e/c (0 for a centred journal, 1 for metal contact). Minimum film thickness h₀ = c − e = c(1 − ε). Attitude angle φ is the angle between the load line and the line joining the two centres. Example: c = 0.05 mm and ε = 0.6 give e = 0.03 mm and h₀ = 0.02 mm = 20 μm.
  6. Condition for a full film. The film is complete when h₀ exceeds the combined roughness of journal and bush plus the largest dirt particle the filter passes, with a margin. A widely used rule asks for h₀ ≥ 0.005 + 0.00004 D mm; for D = 50 mm that is 7 μm, so the 20 μm film above is safe.
  7. What it needs. Enough speed (the film collapses at start and stop), enough viscosity at the running temperature, a continuous oil supply fed into the unloaded side, and no groove across the loaded arc, which would let the pressure escape.
  8. Check. If speed doubles at the same load and oil, the Sommerfeld number doubles, ε falls and h₀ rises; if the oil heats and thins, the reverse happens. A design is therefore checked at the hottest running condition.

Understand Classify seals used in machines. Describe a gasket, an O-ring, a lip seal, a mechanical face seal and a labyrinth seal, and state where each is used. 10 marks

Points to cover. Static against dynamic; contact against clearance; Gasket: seating stress y and gasket factor m; O-ring: squeeze and groove fill; Lip seal: lip and garter spring, faces the oil; Face seal: lapped faces, spring load; Labyrinth: no contact, small steady leak; Selection by motion, speed, pressure and leakage allowed.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose. A seal keeps lubricant in and dirt, water or process fluid out. A bearing that loses its oil or takes in grit fails long before its rated life, so the seal is part of the bearing design.
  2. Classification. Static seals close a joint between parts that do not move relative to each other: gaskets, O-rings in cover grooves, liquid sealants. Dynamic seals close the gap round a moving shaft or rod: lip seals, mechanical face seals, O-rings on slow rods, labyrinths, gland packing. Contact seals press a soft or lapped surface against the moving part; clearance seals leave a small gap and accept a small leak.
  3. Gasket. A compressible ring clamped between flanges. It must first be crushed to its seating stress y to fill the flange roughness, and under pressure it must keep m times the fluid pressure. Used on pipe flanges, pump casings, engine heads and covers.
  4. O-ring. A ring of round section squeezed in a groove, typically by 15 to 30 per cent of its section for a static seal, with the groove filled well under 85 per cent. Example: d = 3.53 mm in a 2.80 mm deep, 4.80 mm wide groove gives squeeze 21 % and fill 73 %. Used on covers, hydraulic cylinders and valves.
  5. Lip seal. A rubber lip, held on the shaft by a garter spring, runs on a thin oil film; the lip faces the oil. A ground, hard shaft is needed. Used on gearbox, motor and axle shafts at moderate surface speed and low pressure.
  6. Mechanical face seal. A spring presses a rotating carbon ring against a stationary silicon carbide or ceramic ring; the leak path is the micron gap between lapped faces. Used on pumps for water, fuel and chemicals.
  7. Labyrinth seal. Fins with a small radial gap and no contact; each fin throttles the leak. No wear and no friction heat, so it suits steam turbines and compressors at high speed, but it always leaks a little.
  8. Gland packing. Braided rings squeezed by a gland nut on valves and slow pumps; it is allowed to drip to stay cool.
  9. Selection. Pick by motion (none, rotary, sliding), surface speed, pressure, temperature and how much leakage is allowed: gasket or O-ring for a cover, lip seal for an oil-bath gearbox shaft, face seal for a pump, labyrinth for a turbine.

Analyze Explain why the Sommerfeld number alone does not fix a journal bearing design, and how temperature rise, oil supply and L/D ratio are handled in the design loop. 13 marks

Points to cover. S needs μ, and μ depends on the film temperature the bearing itself creates; Friction heats the oil; viscosity falls; S falls; ε rises; Loop: assume temperature, μ, S, table, friction and flow, ΔT, compare; Separate curves for each L/D because of side leakage; Supply must replace the flow the table gives; Final checks on h₀ and temperature.

Model answer.

  1. S is only the input. S = (μn/p)(r/c)² fixes the operating point on a chart only once μ is known, and μ is the viscosity at the mean film temperature, which is not known until friction and flow have been found. So S is part of a loop, not the end of the design.
  2. The physical chain. Friction power heats the oil; hot oil is thinner; lower μ gives a smaller S; smaller S means larger ε and a thinner film; a thinner film can raise friction further. The design must find the temperature at which heat generated equals heat carried away.
  3. The loop, step by step.
    StepAction
    1Assume a mean film temperature, for example inlet + 10 °C
    2Read μ at that temperature from the oil chart
    3Compute S and enter the curve for the chosen L/D
    4Read ε, (r/c)f and Q/(rcnL)
    5ΔT = 8.30 p (r/c)f / [Q/(rcnL)], p in MPa
    6New mean temperature = inlet + ΔT/2; if it differs from step 1, repeat
  4. A worked pass. For the bearing of the Raimondi and Boyd chapter (D = L = 50 mm, c = 0.05 mm, W = 10 kN, 25 rev/s, μ = 0.02 Pa·s) S = 0.03125, ε = 0.85, h₀ = 7.4 μm, friction 107 W, Q = 7.32 cm³/s and ΔT = 9.7 °C.   5. Oil supply. The flow variable gives the oil the film itself pumps; the supply groove and pump must deliver at least that (7.3 cm³/s above), at the unloaded side. Too little supply starves the film and the chart answer no longer holds.
  6. L/D. End leakage depends on length, so each L/D has its own curve.   8. Conclusion. S collapses four variables into one, but viscosity ties it to temperature, so a design is fixed only when S, ε, friction, flow and ΔT agree with each other at one temperature.

Model answer.

  1. Scale of the problem. Three months is about one twelfth of three years. Since life goes as (C/P)³, a load only 121/3 = 2.3 times the design value would explain it on its own, so load is the first suspect, but other causes are more common.

A longer bearing (larger L/D) lowers p for the same load, raises S and the film, but runs hotter and suffers from shaft slope at its ends; a shorter one leaks more oil and runs cooler but carries less. Values from about 0.5 to 1.5 are used, L/D = 1 being the usual compromise; each L/D needs its own curve.
  7. Recommendation. For this steady load at moderate speed keep L/D = 1 and choose c = 0.04 mm (c/r = 0.0016, inside the usual 0.001 to 0.002), which gives the thickest film and a 12.9 °C rise that a 40 °C inlet easily absorbs. If the temperature is a worry, keep c = 0.05 mm and use a thicker oil to restore the margin.

Glossary

Application (service) factor
A multiplier on the calculated load that allows for shock, vibration and duty of the driven machine.
Attitude angle
The angle between the load direction and the line joining bush centre to shaft centre in a loaded journal bearing.
Babbitt
A soft white-metal lining alloy of tin or lead with antimony and copper, used on steel shells because it conforms to the shaft and embeds dirt.
Basic rating life
The life in millions of revolutions that a rolling bearing reaches or exceeds with 90% reliability under a stated load.
Bearing
A machine element that supports a moving part, carries its load and reduces friction between the moving and fixed surfaces.
Bearing life in hours
L₁₀ in revolutions converted to running time, L10h = 10⁶ × L₁₀ ÷ (60 n) hours with n in rpm.
Bush (sleeve)
The replaceable ring of bearing metal in which a journal turns or slides.
Clearance ratio
The ratio c/r of radial clearance to journal radius; it appears squared in the Sommerfeld number.
Deep groove ball bearing
The commonest ball bearing, with raceway grooves that take radial load and either direction of axial load.
Extreme pressure (EP) additive
A chemical added to oil or grease that reacts with the metal under high contact pressure to form a low-shear surface film.
Gasket
A compressible ring clamped between flanges to seal a static joint; it must be crushed to its seating stress before it seals.
Gasket factor m
The multiple of the fluid pressure that must stay on a gasket under pressure for the joint to stay tight.
Hydrostatic lubrication
A full fluid film supplied by an external pump at pressure, used where the speed is too low to build hydrodynamic pressure.
Journal
The portion of a shaft that turns inside a bearing.
Labyrinth seal
A non-contact seal of fins with small running gaps that throttle the leak; it never wears but always leaks a little.
Lip seal
A rubber lip held on a rotating shaft by a garter spring, facing the oil it holds back; the common gearbox and motor shaft seal.
Lubricant
Any substance placed between sliding or rolling surfaces to keep them apart and reduce friction and wear.
Mechanical face seal
A shaft seal in which a spring holds a rotating ring against a stationary ring, both lapped flat; used on pumps.
O-ring
An elastomer ring of round section squeezed in a groove; fluid pressure pushes it against the groove wall and tightens the seal.
Oil groove and oil hole
Passages cut in the bush to distribute oil to the loaded zone; they are kept out of the load-carrying region so the film is not broken.
Oil whirl
An unstable self-excited motion of a lightly loaded high-speed journal in which the shaft centre circles inside the bush.
Oiliness
The property of an oil that lets it cling to and lubricate surfaces under boundary conditions, separate from viscosity.
Projected bearing pressure P
Load divided by the projected area, P = W/(L·d), used as the nominal pressure in bearing design.
Radial clearance c
Half the difference between bush bore and journal diameter, the space available for the oil film when the shaft is centred.
Raimondi and Boyd charts
A set of dimensionless design curves for full journal bearings giving eccentricity, film thickness, friction, oil flow and temperature rise against the Sommerfeld number for various L/D.
Reliability adjustment factor a₁
A multiplying factor applied to the L₁₀ life when a reliability other than 90% is required; it is below 1 for higher reliability.
Rolling element
A hardened ball, cylindrical roller, needle or tapered roller that separates the bearing rings with rolling rather than sliding contact.
Static equivalent load P₀
P₀ = X₀ Fr + Y₀ Fa, the load to compare against C₀ when the bearing is stationary or very slow.
Viscosity grade (ISO VG)
A standard number for an oil's kinematic viscosity at 40 °C, such as ISO VG 32 or VG 68, used to specify lubricants.
