5.1 Lubricants: types and properties

A lubricant is anything you put between two surfaces to stop them touching. Oil, grease, graphite powder and synthetic fluid all do the same four jobs: cut friction, carry heat away, keep the metal from rusting, and seal out dirt. Which one you pick, and how thick it must be, is settled mainly by one property: viscosity. Viscosity is the resistance of the fluid to flow. It must be high enough to hold a film under load and low enough to flow into the gap. This chapter sets up the words the bearing chapters use.

You need: Unit 1, Foundations: Design Process and Stresses, for SI units, density and the way material properties are quoted.

What a lubricant does

Two clean steel surfaces pressed together touch only at microscopic high spots. The real area of contact is tiny, so the pressure at those spots is enormous, they cold-weld, and sliding tears metal away. A lubricant works by putting a film between the surfaces so metal never meets metal.

  • Reduce friction: replace metal-to-metal rubbing with the low shear of a fluid layer.
  • Carry away heat: oil flowing through a bearing lifts friction heat to the sump or cooler.
  • Protect from corrosion: the film keeps air and water off the metal.
  • Seal and clean: grease in a housing keeps dust and water out, and flowing oil carries wear debris to the filter.

When the film is thick enough to fully separate the surfaces, friction is just the shear of the fluid, and the bearing is called hydrodynamic (Chapter 5.3). When the film is a few molecules thick and high spots still touch, that is boundary lubrication; it depends on oiliness, not on viscosity.

Types of lubricants

Mineral oils are refined from crude petroleum. They are cheap, sold in a wide range of viscosities, and are the default for general machinery such as gearboxes, pumps and electric motor bearings. Their viscosity index is moderate and they oxidise faster than synthetics when hot.

Synthetic oils are built from chosen chemicals: polyalphaolefins, esters, silicone oils, glycols. They cost more but hold a higher viscosity index, work over a wider temperature range, evaporate less and last longer. Refrigeration compressors, hot gearboxes and fire-resistant hydraulic systems use them.

Greases are semi-solid: oil held in a soap or clay thickener, plus additives. Lithium soap grease is the common one. Grease stays where it is put, seals the housing and needs little attention, so it suits sealed ball bearings and chassis points. It cannot carry heat away and it churns at high speed, so fast bearings use oil.

Solid lubricants are graphite, molybdenum disulphide (MoS₂) and PTFE. They are used where oil would boil, freeze or be washed out: kiln car bearings, vacuum, very high load, or a bush that can never be re-lubricated. Graphite needs some moisture in the air to slide well; MoS₂ does not, so MoS₂ is the choice in vacuum.

Viscosity, the deciding property

τ = μ · du/dy

τ is the shear stress in the fluid, in Pa; du/dy is the velocity gradient across the film, in 1/s; μ is the dynamic viscosity, in Pa·s, which is the same as N·s/m².

ν = μ / ρ

ν is the kinematic viscosity, in m²/s; μ is the dynamic viscosity, in Pa·s; ρ is the density of the oil, in kg/m³. Oil data sheets quote ν in centistokes, where 1 cSt = 10⁻⁶ m²/s.

Two viscosities exist because they answer two questions. The shear inside the film depends on μ alone. The flow of oil into the gap, and its inertia, depend on μ/ρ, so data sheets use ν. Water at 20 °C is about 1 cSt; a medium machine oil is tens of cSt.

Both values fall steeply as temperature rises. Too thin and the film breaks and metal touches. Too thick and the oil will not flow into the bearing, and churning loss raises the temperature further. Viscosity is therefore matched to speed, load and running temperature, not picked because the oil looks thick.

The viscosity index (VI) is a number that says how little the viscosity changes with temperature. A high VI means the viscosity changes less. Mineral oils are usually below 100; synthetics are often above 130. A high VI does not mean a thick oil.

Other properties on the data sheet

Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour above the oil gives a momentary flash when a test flame is passed over it. Fire point is a little higher, where the vapour keeps burning. A high flash point means safer operation near hot surfaces and less loss by evaporation.

Pour point is the lowest temperature at which the oil still pours. Cloud point is the temperature at which wax crystals first appear and the oil turns cloudy; it sits above the pour point. Both matter for a machine that starts cold.

Oiliness, or lubricity, is the ability of the oil to cling to metal and lower friction when the film is thin, as in boundary lubrication. It comes from the oil's own molecules and from additives, not from viscosity.

Oxidation stability is the resistance to reacting with air when hot; a poor oil forms sludge and acids. Emulsification is the tendency to mix with water: turbine oils must separate from water quickly, while cutting oils must form a stable emulsion.

SAE 30, SAE 15W-40 and ISO VG 46 are grade labels. Each label stands for a band of viscosity measured at stated temperatures. The number in the label is not a viscosity in Pa·s.

Worked example

Question. An oil sample drawn from a gearbox at 40 °C has dynamic viscosity μ = 0.05 Pa·s and density ρ = 900 kg/m³. (a) Find its kinematic viscosity in m²/s and in cSt. (b) A handbook lists the same density as 0.9 g/cm³. Show what a student gets by putting 0.9 directly into ν = μ/ρ, and give the correct result in stokes.

  1. Write the data in SI: μ = 0.05 Pa·s and ρ = 900 kg/m³.
  2. Choose the relation: ν = μ/ρ, because oil data sheets are quoted as kinematic viscosity.
  3. Substitute: ν = 0.05/900 = 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s.
  4. Convert to centistokes: 1 cSt = 10⁻⁶ m²/s, so ν = 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ / 10⁻⁶ = 55.56 cSt.
  5. Part (b), the slip: 900 kg/m³ = 0.9 g/cm³, so the handbook value is correct but in other units. Using 0.9 with μ in Pa·s gives 0.0556, a mixed-unit number with no meaning; it is 1000 times too large.
  6. The correct route in cgs: 0.05 Pa·s = 0.5 poise, so ν = 0.5/0.9 = 0.556 stokes = 55.6 cSt, the same answer.
  7. Sanity check: 55.6 cSt is about 56 times water and is the range of a medium machine oil.

Answer. ν = 5.56 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s, which is 55.6 cSt; the density must be in kg/m³ (0.9 g/cm³ is 900 kg/m³) or the answer is wrong by a factor of 1000.

Exam traps

  • Swapping μ and ν, or writing 55.6 cSt = 55.6 Pa·s. Centistokes is kinematic; pascal-second is dynamic.
  • Putting density in g/cm³ straight into ν = μ/ρ with μ in Pa·s. The answer is then 1000 times too large and in no unit at all.
  • Treating a high viscosity index as a thick oil. VI says how little viscosity changes with temperature; it says nothing about the value at 40 °C.
  • Confusing cloud point with pour point. The oil turns cloudy first, and stops pouring at a lower temperature.
  • Reading SAE 30 as a viscosity of 30 Pa·s. It is a grade label covering a viscosity band measured at stated temperatures.

5.2 Sliding contact and rolling contact bearings

A bearing holds a rotating shaft in place and lets it turn with as little resistance as possible. There are two families. Sliding contact bearings let one surface rub over another, separated by a film of oil. Rolling contact bearings put balls or rollers between the surfaces so they roll instead of rub. The choice fixes friction, starting torque, load capacity, speed limit, noise, space and cost. You meet both every day: the bush at the tail end of a pump shaft, and the sealed ball bearing inside a ceiling fan.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for viscosity and how a lubricant film carries load, and Unit 1, Foundations: Design Process and Stresses, for units and stress.

Sliding contact bearings and their materials

A sliding contact bearing, also called a plain bearing, carries load on two surfaces that slide over each other. A film of lubricant between them does the real work; when the film is complete, the metal never touches metal. A handful of shapes cover most applications.

  • Journal bearing: the shaft end (the journal) turns inside a sleeve, and the load is radial, at right angles to the axis. This is the commonest bearing in a pump or a turbine.
  • Bush: the replaceable sleeve itself. The same word is used for a one-piece plain bearing pressed into a housing.
  • Thrust bearing: takes load along the axis of the shaft.
  • Pivot: a small hemisphere or cone on the shaft end resting on a flat or conical seat, for light axial loads in instruments.
  • Collar: a ring fixed on the shaft that bears against a flat face of the housing, giving more rubbing area than a pivot.

Bearing materials are softer than the shaft, so they wear first and can embed grit instead of scoring the journal. Babbitt, a tin- or lead-base white metal, is soft and conforms well; it suits thin linings at high speed. Bronze is stronger and carries heavier loads at moderate speed. Cast iron is cheap, works at low speed and light load, and needs steady lubrication.

p = W / (L d)

p is the bearing pressure in N/mm²; W is the radial load in N; L is the bearing length in mm; d is the journal diameter in mm. The product L d is the projected area.

Rolling contact bearings

A rolling contact bearing puts hardened steel balls or rollers between an inner ring and an outer ring. The rings sit on the shaft and in the housing, the rolling elements carry the load, and a cage keeps them evenly spaced. Because the contact rolls, friction and starting torque are far lower than in a plain bearing. Some syllabi call these antifriction bearings; the name means low friction, not zero friction.

  • Ball bearing: balls touch the rings at points, so it runs fast and takes radial load plus light axial load. This is the bearing inside a ceiling fan or a small motor.
  • Cylindrical roller bearing: rollers touch along a line, so radial capacity is higher, but it takes no axial load unless flanges are added.
  • Needle roller bearing: very thin, long rollers. It fits a small radial space, often without an inner ring, and suits oscillating joints such as a universal joint.
  • Tapered roller bearing: rollers and raceways are cones, so it takes radial and axial load together. Vehicle hubs and gearbox shafts use it.
  • Spherical roller bearing: two rows of barrel-shaped rollers in a spherical outer race. Heavy load, and it tolerates small misalignment between shaft and housing.

Comparing the two families

The friction torque in either bearing can be written with one coefficient, and the power lost follows from it. Use this only for an estimate; a real bearing catalogue gives its own friction figure.

T = μ W r and P = T ω

T is friction torque in N·m; μ is the coefficient of friction; W is the load in N; r is the shaft radius in m (r = d/2); P is friction power in W; ω is angular speed in rad/s, ω = 2πn/60 with n in rpm.

Lubrication regimes

How well a sliding bearing performs depends on how much oil sits between the surfaces. Three regimes are named.

  • Boundary lubrication: the film is thinner than the surface roughness, so high spots touch. Friction is high, μ around 0.05 to 0.15. This is the state at starting, at very low speed and under very heavy load. Additives in the oil, not its viscosity, do the protecting here.
  • Mixed lubrication: part of the load is carried by a film and part by touching asperities. Most machines pass through this band while speeding up.
  • Hydrodynamic lubrication: a full film separates the surfaces and the pressure in the oil carries the whole load. Friction is low, μ around 0.001 to 0.01, and there is no wear. Chapter 5.3 shows how the rotating shaft builds that pressure.

Rolling bearings also run on a film, but a very thin one squeezed between curved surfaces; it is called an elastohydrodynamic film and it is why the right grease matters even though the metal looks dry.

Worked example

Question. A shaft of diameter d = 100 mm carries a steady radial load W = 20 kN at n = 900 rpm. Compare the friction power lost in (a) a sliding contact bearing with μ = 0.02 and (b) a rolling contact bearing with μ = 0.002. Use T = μ W r and P = T ω.

Python program.

import math

d = 0.100
r = d / 2
W = 20000.0
n = 900.0
w = 2 * math.pi * n / 60

for name, mu in (("sliding", 0.02), ("rolling", 0.002)):
    T = mu * W * r
    P = T * w
    print(name, "T =", round(T, 2), "N m", "P =", round(P, 2), "W")
  1. Data: d = 100 mm = 0.1 m, so radius r = d/2 = 0.05 m. W = 20 kN = 20 000 N. n = 900 rpm.
  2. Convert speed to rad/s: ω = 2πn/60 = 2π × 900/60 = 94.2478 rad/s.
  3. Sliding bearing torque: T = μ W r = 0.02 × 20 000 × 0.05 = 20 N·m.
  4. Sliding bearing power: P = T ω = 20 × 94.2478 = 1884.96 W, about 1.885 kW.
  5. Rolling bearing torque: T = 0.002 × 20 000 × 0.05 = 2 N·m.
  6. Rolling bearing power: P = 2 × 94.2478 = 188.50 W.
  7. Extra power lost by the sliding bearing = 1884.96 − 188.50 = 1696.46 W = 1.70 kW.

Answer. The rolling bearing loses 188.50 W against 1884.96 W for the sliding bearing, a saving of about 1.70 kW.

Exam traps

  • Using the shaft diameter instead of the radius 0.05 m in T = μ W r, which doubles the torque and the power.
  • Feeding rpm straight into P = T ω. Convert first: ω = 2πn/60, and only then multiply.
  • Reading 'antifriction bearing' as 'no friction'. A rolling bearing still has friction, and its starting torque is low but never zero.
  • Treating bush, babbitt and bronze as the same kind of thing: a bush is a shape, babbitt and bronze are lining materials.
  • Assuming a roller bearing is always the better choice. A cylindrical roller bearing carries no axial load and cannot match a ball bearing at high speed.

5.3 Hydrodynamic journal bearings

A hydrodynamic journal bearing carries a rotating shaft on a film of oil. The shaft turns inside a slightly larger sleeve. It drags oil into a gap that narrows in the direction of rotation. Viscous drag pushes oil through that wedge, pressure rises, and the pressure balances the load. Metal surfaces stay apart while the shaft runs. You meet these bearings in steam turbines, pumps, motors, gearboxes and crankshafts. The design question is simple to state: can the oil film stay thick enough at the running speed, load and viscosity?

You need: Chapter 5.1 for viscosity and Chapter 5.2 for sliding contact.

Geometry and the oil wedge

A hydrodynamic journal bearing is a plain sleeve around a shaft. The journal diameter D and bearing length L set the size; both are in mm. The journal radius is r = D/2. The radial clearance c is the small difference between the bearing radius and the journal radius. It is often a small fraction of a millimetre. Diametral clearance is twice the radial clearance; do not use it as c.

The shaft centre sits off the bearing centre by the eccentricity e. The eccentricity ratio is ε = e/c, a pure number. The closest gap is the minimum film thickness hmin.

ε = e / c,   h<sub>min</sub> = c − e = c(1 − ε)

e is the journal centre shift from the bearing centre, c is the radial clearance, ε is the eccentricity ratio, and hmin is the minimum film thickness; e, c and hmin use the same length unit.

When the shaft turns, oil sticking to the journal is dragged into the clearance. The gap narrows in the direction of rotation, so the oil flow must pass a smaller area. Pressure rises in this wedge. The pressure peak lies between the start of the wedge and the minimum film; the resultant pressure balances the external load. The journal centre moves until this balance is reached. The angle between the load line and the line joining the centres is the attitude angle. Reynolds equation gives the film pressure; here we use its results and do not derive it. Some syllabi call these fluid-film bearings.

Regimes, pressure and oil supply

Three lubrication regimes matter. In boundary lubrication the oil film is too thin; high spots touch and carry load, so wear occurs. In mixed lubrication the film carries part of the load and high spots carry part. In hydrodynamic lubrication a full film separates the surfaces, and pressure in the oil carries the whole load. This chapter is about the third regime.

The pressure distribution is not uniform. Positive pressure builds in the converging half of the clearance. In the diverging half the pressure falls toward ambient and the oil may cavitate (form vapour pockets). Cavities cannot carry tensile load, so the positive region does the useful work. Oil grooves supply oil to the bearing. Put feed grooves in a low-pressure or unloaded region. A circumferential groove running across the loaded film lets pressure escape and lowers load capacity. Supply must be continuous; starvation collapses the film.

Starts and stops cause the most wear. At zero speed there is no hydrodynamic film, so the surfaces run in boundary or mixed contact. Large machines start the oil pump before the shaft and stop the shaft before the pump. Frequent starts shorten bearing life. Hydrodynamic action needs enough speed, enough viscosity and enough oil; when any one falls, the film thins.

What the design must check

A design must keep hmin above the combined surface roughness, any expected debris and a safety margin. hmin depends on c, ε, viscosity, speed, load, L/D and temperature. A smaller c raises pressure and load capacity but lowers hmin and raises temperature. A larger c raises oil flow and hmin but lowers load capacity. A long bearing (large L/D) carries more load but is sensitive to misalignment; a short bearing leaks oil from its ends.

Viscosity must be taken at the operating temperature. Hot oil is thinner, so a bearing that is safe when cold may fail when hot. Friction power becomes heat, and that heat lowers viscosity. Design is therefore iterative: choose c, L/D and oil grade, estimate ε and hmin, then check temperature and oil flow. The Sommerfeld number (Chapter 5.4) groups speed, viscosity, load and clearance; the Raimondi and Boyd charts (Chapter 5.5) give ε, hmin, friction and flow.

Worked example

Question. A journal bearing has journal diameter D = 50 mm, radial clearance c = 0.05 mm and eccentricity ratio ε = 0.6. Find the eccentricity e and the minimum film thickness h_min. Give h_min in mm and in μm.

  3. h_min = c − e = 0.05 mm − 0.03 mm = 0.02 mm.
  4. Alternative: h_min = c(1 − ε) = 0.05 × (1 − 0.6) = 0.02 mm.
  5. Convert: h_min = 0.02 × 1000 μm = 20 μm.

0.02

Answer. e = 0.03 mm and h_min = 0.02 mm = 20 μm.

Exam traps

  • Radial versus diametral clearance: if a drawing gives 0.10 mm on diameter, c = 0.05 mm. Use c in h_min = c(1 − ε).
  • h_min is radial, not diametral. It is the smallest gap, not the centre shift e.
  • ε = e/c must be less than 1 for a continuous film. A negative h_min means the data or the sign is wrong.
  • An oil-filled bearing at rest is not hydrodynamic. It runs in boundary or mixed contact until speed builds.
  • A groove in the loaded film lowers load capacity; feed oil to the unloaded or low-pressure side.

5.4 Sommerfeld number

Four things decide how a journal bearing behaves: shaft speed, oil viscosity, load and clearance. Change one and the film thickness, friction and oil flow all move. The Sommerfeld number packs all four into a single dimensionless group, so one set of charts can describe every bearing of a given length-to-diameter ratio. It is the number you look up on the Raimondi and Boyd charts, the number you quote when comparing two bearing designs, and the quickest check on whether a bearing is running with a safe oil film or is about to rub.

You need: the oil-film picture of a hydrodynamic journal bearing from Chapter 5.3, and viscosity and its units from Chapter 5.1.

Four variables, one group

Speed, viscosity, load and clearance act together. Charts drawn against four separate variables would need a library of curves, one for every size and every oil. That is unworkable in a design office.

Dimensional analysis removes the size and the units. The group μn/p has no dimensions: (Pa·s × 1/s) ÷ Pa leaves nothing. The group r/c has none either, because both are lengths in metres. Combine them and one number carries the effect of speed, viscosity, load and clearance at once.

Two bearings with the same value of that number and the same length-to-diameter ratio behave alike, even if one is a 25 mm pump shaft and the other a 250 mm turbine shaft. A four-variable problem becomes a one-variable problem, and a single chart covers every bearing of a given L/D.

The definition and its units

S = (μ n / p) (r/c)²

S is the Sommerfeld number, dimensionless. μ is the dynamic viscosity of the oil at its working temperature, in Pa·s. n is shaft speed in revolutions per second (rev/s). p is the bearing pressure in Pa, p = W/(L D), where W is the load in N, L is the bearing length in m and D is the journal diameter in m. r is the journal radius in m, r = D/2. c is the radial clearance in m, the gap on one side.

Substituting p = W/(L D) removes the pressure and gives the form used when you know the load directly:

S = μ n L D (r/c)² / W

the symbols are as above; μn is a pressure (Pa), so μn × LD/W is (pressure × area)/force, a pure number. S stays dimensionless whatever units you use, as long as you are consistent.

Some books call the group μn/p the bearing characteristic number or bearing modulus. The Sommerfeld number is that group multiplied by (r/c)². Keep the two names apart.

The ratio r/c sits inside a square, so it dominates. For a machined bearing r/c typically lies between 500 and 1000 (c/r = 0.001 to 0.002). At r/c = 500 the square is 250 000, which is why S usually lands between 0.01 and 1 even though μn/p is of the order of 10⁻⁷.

A few books print 1/S under the same name. Before reading any chart, check which way its curves run.

What a high or low S means

Eccentricity e is the offset of the journal centre from the bush centre; the eccentricity ratio is ε = e/c, and the minimum film thickness is h₀ = c(1 − ε). High S means small ε and a thick film.

  • S rises in direct proportion to viscosity μ and to speed n.
  • S falls as load W rises, and falls as the square of the clearance c grows.
  • Small S: the journal is pushed close to the bush, the minimum film is thin, and the risk of metal-to-metal contact and wear is high.
  • Large S: the journal floats higher, eccentricity falls and the film thickens, but the film shears more oil and generates more heat to be carried away.

The design aim is a middle band. Very small S gives a thin, hot, wearing bearing; very large S wastes power in churning viscous oil. For the same L/D, a bearing running at higher S generally sits at lower eccentricity, so the same eccentricity chart serves every size once you plot against S.

This is why the Sommerfeld number is the horizontal axis of the design charts in Chapter 5.5. You compute S, follow it up to the curve for your L/D, and read eccentricity, minimum film thickness, friction and oil flow.

Getting the inputs right

Three inputs cause most of the lost marks.

  1. Speed: use revolutions per second. A shaft at 1500 rpm is at 25 rev/s, not 1500.
  2. Pressure: use the projected area L × D. The oil film pressure acts on the projected rectangle of the journal, not on the curved surface πDL.
  3. Viscosity: use the value at the oil's operating temperature inside the bearing, not the ISO VG grade number or the value at 40 °C from a supplier table.

Clearance is radial: c = (bush bore − journal diameter)/2. Using the diametral clearance halves the ratio r/c and quarters S. Keep every length in metres and every pressure in pascals and the group stays dimensionless.

Worked example

Question. A full journal bearing carries a steady load W = 10 kN. Journal diameter D = 50 mm, bearing length L = 50 mm, radial clearance c = 0.05 mm. The shaft runs at 1500 rpm and the oil viscosity at the working temperature is μ = 0.02 Pa·s. Calculate the Sommerfeld number.

  1. Convert the speed to revolutions per second: n = 1500/60 = 25 rev/s.
  2. Journal radius: r = D/2 = 0.050/2 = 0.025 m. Radial clearance: c = 0.05 mm = 5 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  3. Projected area: L × D = 0.050 × 0.050 = 2.5 × 10⁻³ m².
  4. Bearing pressure: p = W/(L D) = 10 000/(2.5 × 10⁻³) = 4 × 10⁶ Pa = 4 MPa.
  5. Clearance ratio: r/c = 0.025/(5 × 10⁻⁵) = 500.
  6. Viscous term: μn/p = (0.02 × 25)/(4 × 10⁶) = 0.5/(4 × 10⁶) = 1.25 × 10⁻⁷.
  7. Square the ratio: (r/c)² = 500² = 250 000.
  8. S = 1.25 × 10⁻⁷ × 250 000 = 0.03125.

Answer. The Sommerfeld number is S = 0.03125, dimensionless.

Exam traps

  • Putting n in rpm. The group needs revolutions per second; 1500 rpm is 25 rev/s, and using 1500 inflates S sixty times.
  • Using πDL as the projected area. Hydrodynamic bearing pressure acts on L × D, the rectangle you see in side view, looking at right angles to the shaft axis — not on the curved surface πDL that you would see looking along the axis.
  • Feeding in the diametral clearance instead of the radial clearance. That halves r/c and cuts S to one quarter.
  • Forgetting to square r/c. The ratio is around 500, so dropping the square changes S by a factor of 250 000.
  • Taking viscosity at 40 °C from a grade table. The bearing runs hotter and thinner; S must use the viscosity at the operating temperature.

5.5 Raimondi and Boyd design charts

The Raimondi and Boyd charts turn two numbers — the Sommerfeld number S and the length-to-diameter ratio L/D — into everything else a journal bearing needs: how far off centre the shaft sits, the thinnest oil film, the friction torque, the oil flow and the temperature rise. They exist because the oil-film pressure in a bearing of finite length has no closed-form solution; it was solved numerically once and published as curves. A designer with a chart and five minutes can size a bearing for a pump, a gearbox or a turbine.

You need: the Sommerfeld number from Chapter 5.4 and the minimum film thickness idea from Chapter 5.3.

What the charts give you

Oil in a loaded journal bearing is dragged through a gap that narrows in the direction of rotation. The Reynolds equation gives the pressure in that film. In a bearing of finite length, oil also escapes sideways at the two ends, so the pressure drops near each end — and that end effect kills any clean formula. Raimondi and Boyd solved the equation numerically over a wide range of running conditions and published the answers as families of curves. Every curve in a chart is one value of L/D; every point on a curve is one Sommerfeld number S.

The Sommerfeld number is S = (r/c)² (μ n / P). Here μ is the oil viscosity in Pa·s, n the shaft speed in rev/s, r the journal radius, c the radial clearance and P = W/(L d) the unit bearing pressure in Pa, with W the load, L the bearing length and d the journal diameter.

The design procedure

  1. Fix L/D between 0.5 and 1.5. Long bearings (L/D above 1) carry more load but run hotter and hate misalignment; short bearings leak more oil. L/D = 1 is a common first choice.
  2. Compute S = (r/c)² (μ n / P) as in Chapter 5.4, using the oil viscosity at the temperature you expect in the film, not in the supply tank.

The temperature loop and the limits

The charts were drawn for a fixed viscosity, yet the oil that carries the load also gets hot, and hot oil is thinner. So the last chart feeds back into the first. Take the inlet oil temperature, add the chart's ΔT to get the outlet temperature, average the two for the mean film temperature, look up the viscosity there and recompute S. Then read the charts once more. Two passes usually settle it; a third moves the answer very little. Only a few universities ask for the full loop in an exam — run it as a check that the first answer is not badly off.

Two limits. Bearing metal runs hotter than the oil leaving the bearing, so never quote the oil outlet temperature as the bearing temperature. And the charts lose accuracy at very high eccentricity, around ε above 0.95, where the film is thinner than the surface roughness, and in turbulent or heavily grooved bearings.

Worked example

Question. A 50 mm diameter journal bearing carries a steady load W = 10 kN at n = 25 rev/s. The radial clearance is c = 0.05 mm and L/D = 1. The Sommerfeld number is S = 0.031.   2. Clearance ratio r/c = 25/0.05 = 500.
  3. Minimum film thickness: h_min = c(1 − ε) = 0.05 × (1 − 0.62) = 0.05 × 0.38 = 0.019 mm = 19 μm.
  4. Friction coefficient: f = (r/c)f ÷ (r/c) = 1.9/500 = 0.0038.
  5. Friction torque: T = f W r = 0.0038 × 10 000 × 0.025 = 0.95 N·m.
  6. Angular speed: ω = 2π n = 2π × 25 = 157.08 rad/s.
  7. Power lost as heat: P_loss = T ω = 0.95 × 157.08 = 149.23 W, about 0.15 kW.
  8. Check: 19 μm of film is comfortable against the roughness of a ground journal, so the design is acceptable; the next step is to confirm the oil can carry 149 W away.

Exam traps

  • S is dimensionless: put n in rev/s and viscosity in Pa·s. Feed rpm into S and every chart reading is wrong.
  • (r/c)f is not f. The chart gives the friction variable; divide by r/c (500 in the example) to get f = 0.0038.
  • Read the curve for your own L/D. The same S on the L/D = 0.5 curve gives a different ε than on the L/D = 1 curve.
  • h_min uses the radial clearance c, half the diametral clearance C. Using C doubles the film thickness you report.
  • Use viscosity at the working film temperature, not at the supply temperature; the chart answer is optimistic otherwise.

5.6 Ball and roller bearings: load ratings and life

A rolling bearing fails by fatigue, not at one fixed load. Heavy load shortens life. Makers give two numbers: C, the basic dynamic load rating, and C0, the basic static load rating. From C and equivalent load P you get L10, the life reached by 90 of 100 bearings. This chapter computes L10 = (C/P)^p in million revolutions, then in hours.

You need: Need Chapter 5.2 for bearing parts and Unit 2 for fatigue.

Two ratings: C0 and C

A ball or roller bearing rarely snaps. It fails by fatigue: after many revolutions a small piece of raceway metal flakes off, called spalling. Life depends on load. Roughly, double the load and a ball bearing lasts one-eighth as long. Standards give two ratings.

  • Basic static load rating C0, in N: the load that leaves a permanent dent of 0.0001 times the ball diameter at the most heavily loaded contact. Use C0 for stationary, slow, or heavy shock at rest.
  • Basic dynamic load rating C, in N: the load at which identical bearings reach a rating life of one million (10⁶) revolutions with 90% healthy. C is capacity, not breaking load.

Both come from the catalogue, not from derivation. For a 25 mm bore deep-groove ball bearing, C is about 14 kN and C0 about 8 kN. Use them to form the ratio C/P.

Equivalent dynamic load P

A real bearing carries radial load Fr, in N, perpendicular to the shaft axis, and axial or thrust load Fa, in N, along the axis. Fold both into one equivalent dynamic load P.

P = X Fr + Y Fa

P in N; Fr radial load in N; Fa axial load in N; X radial factor, Y thrust factor, from the catalogue for that bearing.

P is the pure radial load giving the same fatigue as the actual mix. For pure radial load, X = 1, Y = 0, so P = Fr. For pure thrust load, X = 0, so P = Y Fa.

Single-row deep-groove ball bearings take small thrust. Catalogue tables compare Fa/Fr with threshold e and Fa/C0. If Fa/Fr ≤ e, X = 1, Y = 0, so P = Fr. Above e, X = 0.56 and Y comes from the table, typically 1 to 2. Use tables for the chosen bearing type.

Life in revolutions and in hours

L10 = (C/P)p

L10 is rating life in million revolutions; C and P are in the same unit (N or kN); p = 3 for ball bearings, p = 10/3 for roller bearings.

The subscript 10 is a probability, not a count. L10 is the life 90% of a batch reaches; about 10% fail earlier. C = 30 kN, P = 6 kN gives L10 = (30/6)³ = 125 million revolutions. The same C on a roller bearing gives 510/3 ≈ 214 million, because a roller spreads contact over a line.

L<sub>10h</sub> = (10⁶/(60 n))(C/P)<sup>p</sup>

L10h is life in hours; n is speed in rpm; 10⁶ converts million revolutions to revolutions; 60 converts minutes to hours.

At n = 1500 rpm: L10h = 10⁶ × 125/(60 × 1500) = 1389 hours, about 58 days of continuous running. At half speed the hours double.

For stepped load and speed, use Pe = [ (P1p n1 t1 + P2p n2 t2 + …)/(n1 t1 + n2 t2 + …) ]1/p, where ti is time in each step. For reliability other than 90%, multiply L10 by a1: about 0.62 for 95%, 0.21 for 99%.

Worked example

Question. A deep-groove ball bearing on a pump shaft has C = 30 kN. The shaft runs at n = 1500 rpm. The equivalent dynamic load is P = 6 kN. Find L10 in million revolutions and in hours.

  1. Check units: C = 30 kN and P = 6 kN are both kN, so C/P is dimensionless.

  3. C/P = 30/6 = 5.

  5. L10h = 10⁶ × L10/(60 n) = 10⁶ × 125/(60 × 1500).
  6. 60 × 1500 = 90 000 revolutions per hour.

Answer. L10 = 125 million revolutions, which at 1500 rpm is L10h ≈ 1389 hours.

Exam traps

  • Mixing newtons and kilonewtons inside C/P. Convert C and P to the same unit before dividing.
  • Using p = 3 for a roller bearing. Rollers take p = 10/3, and the same C/P then gives a longer life.
  • Putting Fr in place of P when the bearing also carries thrust. If Fa/Fr > e, P > Fr and life drops.
  • Dropping the 10⁶ when converting L10 to L10h; the answer becomes a million times too small.
  • Reading L10 as the life every bearing reaches or as 10% reliability. It is the 90% reliability life.
  • Using C0 in the life formula. C0 guards against permanent denting at rest; only C enters L10.

5.7 Selection of rolling contact bearings

A catalogue lists dozens of bearings that share the same bore, so the designer needs one number that says which of them can do the job. That number is the required basic dynamic load rating C_req. You find the equivalent load from the radial and axial forces, fix the life wanted in hours, convert that life into million revolutions, and compute C_req. Then you pick the smallest bearing whose catalogue rating C is at least C_req, and you check static safety and the limiting speed. Pumps, fans, gearboxes, electric motors and machine tool spindles are all chosen this way.

You need: Chapter 5.6 for C, P and the L10 life equation, and Chapter 5.2 for the rolling bearing types.

Selection means turning load and life into one number

A rolling bearing is not designed from a blank page; it is chosen from a catalogue. For each bearing the catalogue gives a basic dynamic load rating C: the steady load, in newtons, that would give a rating life of one million revolutions. The machine gives you a load and a life in hours. Selection is the step that turns those two into a single number you can compare with the catalogue.

The life equation of Chapter 5.6 links C, P and life. Inverting it gives the rating you must ask the catalogue for.

C_req = P × L10^(1/p)

C_req is the required basic dynamic load rating in N; P is the equivalent dynamic load in N; L10 is the rating life in million revolutions; p = 3 for ball bearings and p = 10/3 for roller bearings.

The life hidden inside C is counted in million revolutions, so the hours must be converted before they enter the formula.

L10 = 60 × n × L10h ÷ 10⁶

n is the speed in rev/min; L10h is the required life in hours; 60 changes rev/min into rev per hour and 10⁶ changes revolutions into millions; L10 is in million revolutions.

Choose the smallest bearing in the size range whose C is at least C_req. A larger C costs more, weighs more and needs a bigger housing, so an oversized bearing is wasted money. The rating is not the only test, though: the static rating and the limiting speed are checked as well.

The equivalent load P

Bearings are rated for one kind of load: pure radial, steady, applied through the centre. A real bearing carries a radial load F_r and often an axial load F_a. P is the single radial load that would wear the bearing out in the same number of revolutions as the real combination.

P = X × F_r + Y × F_a

F_r is the radial load in N; F_a is the axial load in N; X is the radial factor and Y the axial factor, both read from the catalogue table for that bearing; P is in N.

Static check, speed check, and the order of work

A bearing that turns very slowly, stands still, or takes a heavy shock can fail with no fatigue at all. The rolling elements press dents into the race, called brinelling, and the bearing runs rough for the rest of its life. Against this the catalogue gives a basic static load rating C0, the load at which a fixed small permanent deformation appears.

P0 = F_r   or   P0 = 0.6 × F_r + 0.5 × F_a, whichever is greater

P0 is the equivalent static load in N; the second form applies to radial ball and roller bearings that also carry thrust.

The static safety factor is s0 = C0 ÷ P0. Use about 1.5 to 2 for smooth, quiet running, 2 to 3 for ordinary machinery, and 3 or more where shock is expected. Every catalogue entry also carries a limiting speed, lower for grease and higher for oil; running above it heats the bearing faster than the housing can shed heat.

  2. Choose a trial bearing type for the duty.
  3. Read e, X and Y from the catalogue for that bearing.
  4. Compute P = X F_r + Y F_a.
  5. Fix the required life L10h and convert it to L10 in million revolutions.
  6. Compute C_req = P L10^(1/p).
  7. Pick a bearing with C ≥ C_req and the bore the shaft needs.
  8. Check C0/P0 against the static safety factor, and check the limiting speed.

  6. Choose a catalogue bearing in the right bore with C ≥ 52 kN.
  7. Static load: 0.6 F_r + 0.5 F_a = 0.6 × 5000 + 0.5 × 2000 = 3000 + 1000 = 4000 N. F_r = 5000 N is greater, so P0 = 5000 N.
  8. With s0 = 2 for ordinary running, C0 must be at least 2 × 5000 = 10 000 N, and the limiting speed must exceed 1200 rev/min.

Answer. P = 5800 N, L10 = 720 million revolutions, C_req = 51 984.3 N ≈ 52 kN; the chosen bearing needs C ≥ 52 kN, C0 ≥ 10 kN and a limiting speed above 1200 rev/min.

Exam traps

  • Putting L10h straight into C = P L^(1/3). The life inside the rating is in million revolutions, so 10 000 h at 1200 rev/min becomes 720, not 10 000.
  • Using Y when F_a/F_r ≤ e. Below the threshold Y = 0 and X = 1, so the axial load drops out; adding Y F_a anyway inflates P and over-sizes the bearing.
  • Skipping the static check. A shock-loaded or slowly turning bearing can brinell its races even when C ≥ C_req, so compare C0 with P0 and use the static safety factor.
  • Taking p = 3 for a roller bearing. It is 10/3, and the same P and L10 then give a smaller C_req than a ball bearing would.
  • Mixing N and kN in P, or forgetting the 60 in the hours-to-revolutions conversion; both give a wrong C_req by a factor that looks plausible on the page.

Check yourself

Remember What does the basic dynamic load rating C of a bearing mean? 2 marks

The steady load in newtons that gives a rating life of one million revolutions; the catalogue lists it for every bearing.

Remember State the equivalent static load for a radial ball bearing carrying thrust. 2 marks

P0 is the greater of F_r and 0.6 F_r + 0.5 F_a.

Understand Why must L10h be converted to million revolutions before C_req is computed? 3 marks

C is defined for a life of one million revolutions, so the life in the rating equation must be in that unit. Using 10 000 h in place of 720 million rev gives 10 000^(1/3) ≈ 21.5 instead of 8.96, so C_req comes out about 2.4 times too large and the bearing is badly over-sized.

Understand Why does the axial load stop mattering below the ratio e? 3 marks

Below e the thrust is small compared with the bearing's static capacity, the load zone hardly changes, and life is set by the radial load alone. So Y = 0 and P = F_r.

Apply A ball bearing carries F_r = 4 kN and no thrust at n = 900 rev/min, with a required life of 20 000 h. Find C_req. 5 marks

F_a = 0, so P = 4000 N. L10 = 60 × 900 × 20 000/10⁶ = 1080 million revolutions. C_req = 4000 × 1080^(1/3) = 4000 × 10.26 = 41 040 N ≈ 41 kN.

Apply A roller bearing has P = 6 kN and a required life of 500 million revolutions. Find C_req. 4 marks

p = 10/3, so C_req = 6000 × 500^(3/10) = 6000 × 6.45 = 38 700 N ≈ 38.7 kN.

Analyze A bearing chosen on the dynamic rating fails within weeks, and its races show brinelling. What was missed? 5 marks

The static check. Slow, stationary or shock loading dents the races even when C ≥ C_req, so C0/P0 must reach the static safety factor. The limiting speed, misalignment and fits are the other suspects.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Standard L10 gives 90% reliability; for higher reliability an adjustment factor a1 below 1 multiplies the life, so the same bearing must be bigger. Modified rating life standards go further and correct C for oil film thickness, contamination and the fatigue load limit of the steel. In industry, bearing firms offer selection software and vibration-based condition monitoring that replaces a fixed life estimate with measured wear. Search for modified rating life, reliability adjustment factor, and bearing vibration signature analysis.

Takeaways

  1. A lubricant does its job only if it stays between the surfaces, and viscosity is the property that decides whether it can.
  2. Viscosity falls as oil gets hotter, so a lubrication design has to be checked at the running temperature, not the cold start.
  3. Sliding bearings are quiet and cheap but need a full oil film; rolling bearings have low starting friction and need almost no attention.
  4. In a journal bearing the shaft's own rotation drags oil into a wedge, and that converging gap is what builds the pressure that carries the load.
  5. The Sommerfeld number collapses speed, viscosity, clearance and load into one number, which is why a single family of charts serves many bearings.
  6. Raimondi and Boyd charts turn S and L/D into eccentricity, film thickness, friction, oil flow and temperature rise — the five numbers a bearing design needs.
  7. Bearing life falls steeply with load: life goes as (C/P)³ for ball bearings, so a 25% overload can halve or worse the life.
  8. Bearing selection is a loop: guess a bearing, compute P, get required C, pick from the catalogue, then recheck C₀, speed limit and temperature.

Remember and understand

Understand Define viscosity and viscosity index. Explain why the same oil may be right for a machine in a Chennai summer and wrong in a cold hill station, and what the designer does about it. 10 marks

Viscosity definition with μ = τ ÷ (du/dy), units Pa·s; kinematic viscosity ν = μ/ρ; Viscosity–temperature curve: viscosity drops sharply on heating, rises on cooling; Viscosity index as a measure of flatness of that curve; high VI preferred; Hot running temperature fixes the minimum viscosity that keeps h₀ large enough; Cold start fixes the maximum viscosity that still allows oil to reach the bearing; Practical answers: multigrade oil, high-VI oil, warm-up, oil cooler, thermostat.

Understand Compare sliding contact and rolling contact bearings under load capacity, friction, starting torque, space, noise, cost and maintenance. Place boundary, mixed and hydrodynamic lubrication on the same picture. 12 marks

Construction: journal and bush versus inner ring, outer ring and rolling elements; Friction: sliding bearings have higher starting friction; rolling bearings have low starting torque; Load capacity per unit size; shock capacity; damping and noise; Radial space: rolling bearings need more radial depth, needle rollers less; Lubrication regimes: boundary at low speed or high load, mixed in between, hydrodynamic at speed; Where each is used: journal bearing in engines and turbines, rolling bearing in electric motors, fans, gearboxes.

Understand Explain how a hydrodynamic oil film is generated in a journal bearing. Define eccentricity ratio, minimum film thickness and attitude angle, and state the condition for full film lubrication. 12 marks

Shaft rotates and drags oil into the converging wedge; oil is incompressible; Pressure rises in the converging region and falls in the diverging region; the resultant balances the load; Equilibrium position: shaft centre offset by e in the direction of rotation; Eccentricity ratio ε = e/c, h₀ = c(1 − ε), attitude angle between load line and line of centres; Full film condition: h₀ well above the combined surface roughness; otherwise boundary or mixed; Requires continuous supply of oil and enough speed and viscosity.

Understand Define basic dynamic load rating C and basic static load rating C₀ of a rolling bearing. Explain the physical meaning of each and when each governs the selection. 10 marks

C: load giving 1 million revolutions L10 life, for a rotating bearing; C₀: load causing permanent deformation of 0.0001 times the element diameter at the most loaded contact; C₀ governs for static or very slow, heavily loaded bearings, and for shock loads; C governs for normal rotating service through the life equation; Both are catalogue values for the specific bearing number; Check that the chosen C and C₀ are both adequate, and also check the speed limit.

Apply

Apply A journal of 60 mm diameter and 60 mm length runs at 1200 rpm with SAE 30 oil of μ = 0.020 Pa·s at the working temperature. Radial clearance is 0.06 mm and the load is 6 kN. Compute the Sommerfeld number and the projected bearing pressure. 10 marks

r = 30 mm = 0.03 m, c = 0.06 mm, r/c = 500, (r/c)² = 250000; N = 1200/60 = 20 rev/s; Projected area = L × d = 0.06 × 0.06 = 3.6 × 10⁻³ m²; P = 6000 ÷ 3.6 × 10⁻³ = 1.67 MPa; S = (r/c)² × μN/P = 250000 × (0.02 × 20) ÷ 1.67 × 10⁶; S ≈ 0.060; state that S is dimensionless and check it lies inside the chart range.

Apply For a journal bearing with L/D = 1 and Sommerfeld number 0.12, use the Raimondi and Boyd charts to obtain the eccentricity ratio, minimum film thickness, friction variable and oil flow, given c = 0.05 mm, d = 50 mm, N = 1500 rpm. Explain each chart reading. 13 marks

Enter the ε chart at S = 0.12 for L/D = 1, read ε and h₀ = c(1 − ε); Enter the friction variable chart (r/c)f versus S, obtain f, then friction torque T = f·W·r; Enter the flow variable chart, obtain Q/(r·c·N·L), then Q in m³/s; Read the temperature-rise chart or use the heat balance ΔT = friction power ÷ (ρ·C_p·Q); Check h₀ against the surface roughness for full film; State the interpolation and the fact that charts are for finite-length bearings and given boundary conditions.

Apply A deep groove ball bearing carries a radial load of 5 kN at 900 rpm and must last 10 000 hours. Find the required basic dynamic load rating. Take p = 3. 10 marks

Revolutions in the life: L = 60 × N × h = 60 × 900 × 10000 = 5.4 × 10⁸ rev; In million revolutions: L10 = 540; L10 = (C/P)³ → C = P × (L10)^(1/3); C = 5 × (540)^(1/3) ≈ 5 × 8.14 ≈ 40.7 kN; Choose the next catalogue bearing with C ≥ 40.7 kN; Check C₀ and the limiting speed for that bearing.

Apply A bearing carries F_r = 4 kN and F_a = 1.6 kN. From the catalogue X = 0.56 and Y = 1.5, and the application factor is 1.3. Compute the equivalent dynamic load and select the life at 1200 rpm for a bearing with C = 35 kN. 10 marks

P = X·F_r + Y·F_a = 0.56 × 4000 + 1.5 × 1600 = 2240 + 2400 = 4640 N; Apply the service factor: P = 1.3 × 4640 ≈ 6032 N; L10 = (C/P)³ = (35000/6032)³ ≈ 195 million revolutions; In hours: L = 10⁶ × 195 ÷ (60 × 1200) ≈ 2710 h; Comment that increasing C or reducing load raises life steeply.

Apply Compare a hydrodynamic journal bearing and a deep groove ball bearing for the two ends of a 7.5 kW, 1440 rpm induction motor. Give the calculation steps you would use for each and justify the choice. 13 marks

Load estimate for the motor shaft end; both radial and some axial; Rolling bearing route: P, life in hours, required C, catalogue selection, C₀ and speed check; Journal bearing route: choose L/D and clearance, oil, assume temperature, S, charts, h₀ and ΔT, iterate on clearance; Compare starting friction, noise, maintenance, radial space and cost; Rolling bearings suit small motors: low starting torque, quiet enough, sealed for life; Journal bearings suit very large or very high-speed machines where life in hours is unreachable for rolling elements; State a clear recommendation with reasons.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Explain why the Sommerfeld number alone does not fix a journal bearing design, and how temperature rise, oil supply and L/D ratio are handled in the design loop. 13 marks

S depends on μ, which depends on the oil temperature that the bearing itself creates; Friction power heats the oil; viscosity falls; S falls; the operating point moves; Iterative loop: assume a temperature, get μ, compute S, read charts, compute friction and flow, compute ΔT, check the assumed temperature; Side leakage at the ends reduces pressure for small L/D; Raimondi and Boyd curves are given separately for each L/D; Large L/D raises load capacity but also raises temperature and misalignment sensitivity; Convergence check and the final safe value of h₀.

Analyze A newly installed rolling bearing on a pump fails in three months instead of the expected three years. List the likely causes and explain how each one is detected and corrected. 12 marks

Overload or underestimated P from axial load, unbalance or belt pull; Wrong or insufficient lubricant, or grease loss; check colour,quantity, operating temperature; Contamination: water, dust, grit; check seals and breathing; Misalignment or shaft deflection causing edge loading; Incorrect fits: too tight removes internal clearance, too loose allows creep and fretting; Improper mounting damage, false brinelling during idle vibration; Diagnosis from the failure pattern on raceways plus vibration and temperature records; Corrections: reselect with the real P, better seals, alignment check, correct fits.

Evaluate Discuss the effect of radial clearance c and length-to-diameter ratio L/D on the performance of a hydrodynamic journal bearing. How would you choose both for a steady load at moderate speed? 13 marks

S carries (r/c)², so clearance changes S strongly and shifts the whole operating point; Small clearance: high load capacity but high friction and temperature, tighter tolerances needed; Large clearance: more oil flow and cooler running, but lower capacity and possible whirl; L/D near 1 is the usual compromise; low L/D leaks oil and loses pressure, high L/D overheats and misaligns; Use the Raimondi and Boyd charts at several L/D to compare h₀, friction and ΔT; Check h₀ against roughness, and temperature against oil life; Recommend a clearance range from standard tables and confirm by the temperature-rise check.

Evaluate Design a full hydrodynamic journal bearing for a steady load of 20 kN at 1000 rpm: choose the oil and L/D, compute the Sommerfeld number, use the charts to fix clearance and check film thickness and temperature rise. State all assumptions. 16 marks

Choose L/D near 1 and a trial diameter from the allowable bearing pressure; Pick an oil grade and estimate its viscosity at the expected oil temperature; Select clearance from standard practice for that diameter; Compute P, then S; Read ε, h₀, friction variable and flow from the charts for the chosen L/D; Heat balance: friction power equals the heat carried by the oil flow plus that lost to the surroundings; find ΔT; Iterate the assumed temperature until it matches the computed one; Check h₀ against combined roughness for full film; state assumptions and safety margins.

Glossary

Application (service) factor
A multiplier on the calculated load that allows for shock, vibration and duty of the driven machine.
Attitude angle
The angle between the load direction and the line joining bush centre to shaft centre in a loaded journal bearing.
Babbitt
A soft white-metal lining alloy of tin or lead with antimony and copper, used on steel shells because it conforms to the shaft and embeds dirt.
Basic rating life
The life in millions of revolutions that a rolling bearing reaches or exceeds with 90% reliability under a stated load.
Bearing
A machine element that supports a moving part, carries its load and reduces friction between the moving and fixed surfaces.
Bearing life in hours
L10 in revolutions converted to running time, L = 10⁶ × L10 ÷ (60 × N) hours with N in rpm.
Bush (sleeve)
The replaceable ring of bearing metal in which a journal turns or slides.
Clearance ratio
The ratio c/r of radial clearance to journal radius; it appears squared in the Sommerfeld number.
Collar
A raised ring on a shaft that bears against a thrust surface to take axial load.
Deep groove ball bearing
The commonest ball bearing, with raceway grooves that take radial load and either direction of axial load.
Extreme pressure (EP) additive
A chemical added to oil or grease that reacts with the metal under high contact pressure to form a low-shear surface film.
Hydrostatic lubrication
A full fluid film supplied by an external pump at pressure, used where the speed is too low to build hydrodynamic pressure.
Journal
The portion of a shaft that turns inside a bearing.
Lubricant
Any substance placed between sliding or rolling surfaces to keep them apart and reduce friction and wear.
Oil groove and oil hole
Passages cut in the bush to distribute oil to the loaded zone; they are kept out of the load-carrying region so the film is not broken.
Oil whirl
An unstable self-excited motion of a lightly loaded high-speed journal in which the shaft centre circles inside the bush.
Oiliness
The property of an oil that lets it cling to and lubricate surfaces under boundary conditions, separate from viscosity.
Projected bearing pressure P
Load divided by the projected area, P = W/(L·d), used as the nominal pressure in bearing design.
Radial clearance c
Half the difference between bush bore and journal diameter, the space available for the oil film when the shaft is centred.
Raimondi and Boyd charts
A set of dimensionless design curves for full journal bearings giving eccentricity, film thickness, friction, oil flow and temperature rise against the Sommerfeld number for various L/D.
Reliability adjustment factor a₁
A multiplying factor applied to the L10 life when a reliability other than 90% is required; it is below 1 for higher reliability.
Rolling element
A hardened ball, cylindrical roller, needle or tapered roller that separates the bearing rings with rolling rather than sliding contact.
Static equivalent load P₀
P₀ = X₀·F_r + Y₀·F_a, the load to compare against C₀ when the bearing is stationary or very slow.
Viscosity grade (ISO VG)
A standard number for an oil's kinematic viscosity at 40 °C, such as ISO VG 32 or VG 68, used to specify lubricants.
