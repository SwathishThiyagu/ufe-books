7.1 Gear types, nomenclature and law of gearing

Gears transmit rotation and torque between shafts at a fixed ratio. The law of gearing says the common normal at every contact point must pass through the pitch point. The involute profile meets this condition, so the ratio stays constant even when the centre distance changes slightly. You see gears in a UPI payment machine's paper drive, in a two-wheeler gearbox and in a Chennai pump set. This chapter names the parts of a tooth and shows why the involute is the standard shape.

You need: school geometry of circles and triangles, and the relation v = πdn/60 from Unit 6, Design of flat belt drives.

Gear families by shaft arrangement

Gears are chosen first by how their shafts sit. Spur gears have straight teeth and parallel shafts. They are simple and efficient, but the whole tooth engages at once, so they are noisy at high speed. Helical gears also have parallel shafts, but the teeth are cut at a helix angle. Contact starts at one end and slides along the tooth, so the drive is quieter and carries more load; the price is an axial thrust that the bearings must take. Bevel gears sit on cones and connect intersecting shafts, usually at 90°. A worm and worm wheel connect crossed shafts, usually at right angles, and give a large reduction in one step through sliding contact. A rack is a gear with infinite pitch radius: it turns rotation into straight-line motion, as in a car steering rack or a sliding door opener. An internal gear has teeth cut on the inside of a ring, so the pinion sits inside it and the pair is compact.

Tooth nomenclature and the module

Every gear calculation starts at the pitch circle. It is the imaginary circle on which the teeth roll without slipping. Let d be the pitch circle diameter in mm and z the number of teeth. The module is m = d/z, in mm. Module is the standard index of tooth size: a 4 mm module means 4 mm of pitch diameter for every tooth. The circular pitch is the distance from one tooth to the next measured along the pitch circle: p = πd/z = πm, in mm. A 4 mm module therefore has p = 12.57 mm. In inch units, diametral pitch P = z/d with d in inches; P is teeth per inch of pitch diameter, and m = 25.4/P. Some syllabi call m the metric module and P the English module.

Tooth height follows the module. Standard full-depth teeth use addendum h_a = m above the pitch circle and dedendum h_f = 1.25m below it. Clearance is c = 0.25m, working depth is 2m and whole depth is 2.25m. The pressure angle φ is the angle between the common normal at the pitch point and the common tangent; 20° is the standard. The base circle diameter is d_b = d cos φ, because the involute is generated from this smaller circle. Face width b is the tooth length along the shaft. For two external gears the centre distance is a = m(z1+z2)/2; for an internal pair use a = m(z2−z1)/2 with z2 the internal gear.

The law of gearing and the involute

The law of gearing fixes the shape of a tooth. At any instant a pair of teeth touches at a point. Draw the common normal to the two profiles at that point. The law says this normal must cut the line joining the two shaft centres at a fixed point, the pitch point, for the speed ratio to stay constant. If the normal wanders, the ratio changes tooth by tooth, and the drive vibrates. Notice that it is the common normal, not the common tangent.

The involute curve satisfies the law. Imagine a taut string unwinding from a base circle; the end traces an involute. At every contact point the common normal is the string itself, tangent to both base circles, and it always passes through the pitch point. The velocity ratio is therefore constant: ω1/ω2 = d2/d1 = z2/z1, where ω is angular speed in rad/s. A small change in centre distance does not change this ratio for an involute pair, because the base circles do not change; only the pressure angle shifts. That tolerance to mounting error is why involute gears dominate.

Interference occurs when contact tries to happen on the non-involute part of a tooth, below the base circle. A small pinion with a large gear is the usual case. The cutter then removes material from the pinion root, called undercutting, and the tooth becomes weak. Minimum tooth numbers and profile shift are the standard cures; tooth stress itself is covered in Chapter 7.3.

Worked example

Question. A 20° involute spur pair has z1 = 20, z2 = 60 and module 4 mm. Find both pitch diameters, the centre distance, both base circle diameters and the velocity ratio.

  1. Module is m = d/z, so d1 = m z1 = 4 × 20 = 80 mm and d2 = m z2 = 4 × 60 = 240 mm.
  2. External centre distance is a = (d1 + d2)/2 = (80 + 240)/2 = 160 mm.
  3. Base circle diameter is d_b = d cos φ. With φ = 20°, cos 20° = 0.93969. Then d_b1 = 80 × 0.93969 = 75.175 mm ≈ 75.2 mm and d_b2 = 240 × 0.93969 = 225.526 mm ≈ 225.5 mm.
  4. Velocity ratio is ω1/ω2 = z2/z1 = 60/20 = 3. The pinion turns three times for one turn of the gear.

Answer. The pitch diameters are 80 mm and 240 mm, the centre distance is 160 mm, the base circle diameters are 75.2 mm and 225.5 mm, and the velocity ratio is 3.

Exam traps

  • Module uses the pitch circle: m = d/z. Using the addendum circle or the base circle gives a wrong module.
  • Circular pitch is p = πm, not m. Module is not the tooth thickness or the pitch.
  • The law of gearing is about the common normal at the contact point, not the common tangent. Examiners test this wording.
  • Diametral pitch is in inch units: P = z/d with d in inches, and m = 25.4/P. Mixing mm with P gives a wrong module.
  • For an internal gear pair the centre distance is m(z2 − z1)/2. Adding the tooth numbers is wrong.

7.2 Gear materials

A gear fails in one of two places. The root cracks in bending, or the flank pits and wears away. One material must resist both, and the two demands pull against each other: a hard surface needs a soft, tough core underneath. Gear material choice is therefore a system of core strength, surface hardness and heat treatment, matched to load, speed and cost. That choice decides whether a pump gearbox in a Chennai water plant runs ten years or two.

You need: material properties and factor of safety from Unit 1, and module and pitch diameter from Chapter 7.1.

Three demands on one material

A tooth is a cantilever. Root bending stress is carried by the whole section, so the core must be strong and tough. A hard, brittle core chips at the root under shock. The flank carries repeated rolling with sliding, and fails by pitting and wear. That is a hardness problem. One material must do both jobs, and still machine well and keep its shape through heat treatment.

Surface hardness is measured as BHN, the Brinell hardness number: a 10 mm steel ball pressed at 3000 kgf, giving hardness in kgf/mm². BHN is not strength in MPa. As a rough estimate for steel, ultimate tensile strength ≈ 3.4 × BHN in MPa, but this comes from a hardness test and is never a design value. The allowable bending stress comes from Chapter 7.3.

The pinion rule matters more than any single number. A 20-tooth pinion driving a 60-tooth gear makes three times the cycles, because it meets every gear tooth once per revolution. Fatigue damage accumulates with cycles, so an equally hard pair wears out first on the pinion. Make the pinion harder by 30–50 BHN. Both wheels then reach the end of life together.

Steels and their heat treatment

Plain carbon steels with 0.3–0.6% carbon are the shop-floor default. C45 has 0.45% carbon; normalised it is about 220 BHN, and quenched and tempered it reaches 250–300 BHN. It is cheap, machines freely and serves up to about 5 m/s peripheral speed. Above roughly 350 BHN, through-hardening distorts the gear and makes finish machining difficult.

Alloy steels add chromium for hardenability, nickel for core toughness and molybdenum to control tempering and case depth. Through-hardened 40Cr1 or 42CrMo4 reach 300–350 BHN in sections too large for plain carbon steel.

  1. Case carburising: a low-carbon steel (0.15–0.25% C with Cr, Ni, Mo) is held at 900–930°C in carbon-rich gas. Carbon diffuses to a depth of 0.8–1.5 mm; the gear is then quenched and tempered. Surface 58–62 HRC, core 30–40 HRC. Chosen for high load and speeds above 5 m/s.
  2. Nitriding: ammonia gas at 500–550°C for many hours, with no quench. Case 0.3–0.5 mm at 500–600 HV. Distortion is very small, so it suits internal gears and large rings that cannot be ground after hardening.
  3. Induction hardening: a coil heats the flanks and they are quenched at once. 50–55 HRC over a tough core, used on large module gears.

Distortion is the hidden cost. A carburised gear must be ground after hardening, so leave 0.1–0.2 mm of stock on each flank and write 'grind after hardening' on the drawing.

Cast iron, bronze and plastics

Grey cast iron (FG 200, FG 260) contains flake graphite that damps vibration and breaks chips. It machines easily and runs quietly, but its bending strength is low, about 200 MPa. Use it for large, slow gears below about 3–5 m/s, running with a steel pinion. Never use it for a small high-speed pinion.

Bronze belongs to worm wheels, not spur gears. A worm drive slides rather than rolls, so the wheel must be soft, embeddable and low in friction. The standard pair is a phosphor bronze or gunmetal wheel with a hardened, ground steel worm. The bronze is always the softer member.

  • Nylon gears run dry, absorb shock and cost little. They creep, swell with moisture and conduct heat badly, so keep load and speed low.
  • Bakelite (phenolic laminate) is stiffer and used in timing and cam gears.
  • Sintered powder-metallurgy gears have 10–15% porosity that holds oil, and are cheap in mass production of small gears.

Worked example

Question. A 7.5 kW motor at 1440 rpm drives a 20-tooth pinion of module 3 mm with moderate shock. Find the pitch line velocity and the tangential load, and recommend a material pair.

  1. Pitch diameter: d = m z = 3 × 20 = 60 mm = 0.060 m.
  2. Pitch line velocity: v = π d N / 60 = π × 0.060 × 1440 / 60 = 4.52 m/s.
  3. Angular velocity: ω = 2π N / 60 = 2π × 1440 / 60 = 150.8 rad/s.
  4. Torque: T = P / ω = 7500 / 150.8 = 49.74 N·m.
  5. Tangential load: Ft = 2T / d = 2 × 49.74 / 0.060 = 1658 N, about 1.66 kN.
  6. Speed is below 5 m/s with moderate shock, so a through-hardened C45 pinion at about 250 BHN running with an FG 200 cast iron gear is a common shop-floor choice. Above 5 m/s, switch to a case-carburised 20MnCr5 pinion at 58–62 HRC with a hardened alloy gear 30–50 BHN softer.

Answer. Pitch line velocity is 4.52 m/s and tangential load is 1.66 kN; use a through-hardened C45 pinion at 250 BHN with a grey cast iron gear, moving to a case-carburised alloy pinion above 5 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Choosing the hardest steel for both wheels. The pinion needs the extra 30–50 BHN, and a hard case needs a tough core; a fully hardened tooth chips at the root.
  • Reading BHN as strength in MPa. 250 BHN is roughly 850 MPa tensile strength for steel as an estimate, but the allowable bending stress is a fraction of that.
  • Forgetting grinding stock. A carburised gear distorts; leave 0.1–0.2 mm per flank and specify 'grind after hardening'.
  • Pairing bronze with bronze in a worm drive, or a soft worm with a hard wheel. The wheel is the sacrificial member and the worm is hardened and ground.
  • Using cast iron for a high-speed pinion because it runs quietly. Damping does not repair low bending strength.

7.3 Spur gear design: Lewis and Buckingham equations

A spur tooth carries the whole load near its tip, like a short cantilever fixed at the root. Lewis turns that picture into one line: the root bending stress must stay below the allowable stress. Buckingham then adds the part Lewis misses — the extra load from speed, impact and tooth errors — and checks surface wear. You use these equations to choose module and face width for a gear pair in a gearbox, a machine tool, or an electric vehicle drive.

You need: bending stress and factor of safety from Unit 1, fatigue ideas from Unit 2, and the involute geometry of Chapter 7.1.

Lewis: the tooth as a cantilever

A spur tooth is a short cantilever. The tangential force Ft acts at the tooth tip — the worst case, with one tooth taking the whole load — and the root fillet is the fixed end. The bending stress at the root is highest, so the root sets the tooth size. Lewis writes the allowable tangential load as:

Ft = σb b m y

Ft is the allowable tangential load in N. σb is the allowable bending stress in N/mm². b is the face width in mm. m is the module in mm. y is the Lewis form factor, dimensionless, from tooth count and pressure angle.

An equivalent form is Ft = σb b π m y′, where y′ = y/π (about 0.102 when y = 0.32). Books that put π inside the formula tabulate this smaller factor; the two tabulated factors differ by the factor π. Use one form only. The right-hand side of either form is the beam strength: the load the tooth can carry at the allowable stress. It is a capacity, not the load applied by the gearbox.

For standard 20° full-depth involute teeth, y falls as the tooth count falls, because with fewer teeth the flanks are more sharply curved and the root is relatively thin (undercut below about 17 teeth), so the same load bends the tooth more. Face width is usually b = 8m to 12m. Narrower than 8m gives high bending stress. Wider than 12m does not share load evenly because of shaft deflection and misalignment.

Buckingham: dynamic and wear loads

Lewis is static. At speed, tooth errors, inertia and impact add a dynamic increment. Buckingham writes the total dynamic load Fd as:

Fd = Ft + (21v(Cb + Ft))/(21v + √(Cb + Ft))

Fd is the dynamic load in N. Ft is the tangential load in N. v is pitch line velocity in m/s. b is face width in mm. C is the deformation factor in N/mm², from material and tooth accuracy.

Two conventions exist. The AGMA/Shigley dynamic factor Kv is greater than 1: Ft,eff = Kv Ft. The velocity factor Cv in many Indian textbooks is less than 1: Ft,eff = Ft/Cv. Use one convention only; never apply both the velocity factor and the Buckingham Fd in the same check. The dynamic load is what the tooth actually feels.

Surfaces also fail by pitting. The limiting wear load is:

Fw = d1 b Q K, Q = 2z2/(z1 + z2)

Fw is the limiting wear load in N. d1 is the pinion pitch diameter in mm. b is face width in mm. z1 and z2 are pinion and gear tooth counts. K is the load-stress factor in N/mm², from the material pair and surface hardness.

Two checks close the design: Fd ≤ beam strength (σb b m y) and Fd ≤ Fw. Lewis sizing with Ft is only a first pass. Some syllabi call Fd the Buckingham dynamic load and Fw the limiting load for wear. If wear fails, raise surface hardness or size; if bending fails, raise m or b.

Design order

  1. Choose z1 and z2 from the ratio. Choose a standard module m and set b = 8m to 12m.
  2. Find pitch line velocity v = πd1n/60, with d1 = z1m. Convert d1 to metres so v is in m/s.
  3. Find Ft = P/v, with P in watts. Use Ft to size the tooth by Lewis as a first pass: Ft ≤ σb b m y.
  4. Compute Fd from the Buckingham equation and Fw from the wear equation.
  5. Accept if Fd ≤ beam strength (σb b m y) and Fd ≤ Fw. Otherwise increase m, b, or surface hardness and repeat.

The order matters: the Lewis equation sizes the tooth, then the dynamic and wear checks test the surface and the speed effect. The worked example does only the first sizing step.

Worked example

Question. A 20° full-depth spur pinion has 20 teeth, allowable bending stress 120 MPa and face width 10m. It transmits 10 kW at 1200 rpm. Take the Lewis form factor y = 0.32. Find the module by Lewis sizing. This first pass takes Cv = 1.

  1. Let m be the module in mm. Pitch diameter d1 = z1m = 20m mm.
  2. Pitch line velocity v = π(0.020m)(1200)/60 = 1.256637m m/s, with m in mm.
  3. Tangential force Ft = P/v = 10000/(1.256637m) = 7957.75/m N.
  4. Lewis beam strength = σb b m y = 120 × (10m) × m × 0.32 = 384.0m² N.
  5. Set beam strength equal to Ft: 384.0m² = 7957.75/m → m³ = 20.7233 → m = 2.747 mm.
  6. Choose the standard module m = 3.0 mm.

Answer. The minimum module by Lewis is 2.747 mm, so choose m = 3.0 mm.

Exam traps

  • Lewis beam strength is capacity, not demand. Do not put Ft = σb b m y and call Ft the applied load; compare the applied tangential force with this capacity.
  • Using d1 in millimetres instead of metres inside v = πd1n/60 makes v 1000 times too large and Ft 1000 times too small. Always put d1 in metres when v must come out in m/s.
  • Keep m and b in mm and v in m/s. The 0.020m conversion from mm to m is a favourite place for a factor-of-1000 slip.
  • y and y′ are not the same: with the π inside the formula the factor is y/π, roughly a third of the other.
  • The final checks compare both beam strength and wear load with the dynamic load Fd; the static Ft only gives the first size.

7.4 Helical gear design

A helical gear has teeth cut on a slant, not parallel to the shaft. The slant makes contact begin at one end of the tooth and roll across the face, so the load is shared over a longer line. The gear runs quieter and carries more load than a spur gear of the same size. The same slant creates an axial thrust. That thrust changes the shaft and bearing design, and it is the main reason helical gears are not just better spur gears. They are common in vehicle and industrial gearboxes.

You need: Chapter 7.3 for the Lewis equation and Chapter 7.1 for involute profiles and gear nomenclature.

Helix angle and the two modules

A spur tooth meets its mate across the full face at once. A helical tooth is cut at a helix angle ψ to the shaft axis, so contact begins at one end of the face and travels across it. The contact line is longer and the load is shared more gradually. The result is quieter running, less impact and more load capacity at the same size. The price is an axial thrust that a spur gear does not have.

Two planes matter. The normal plane is perpendicular to the tooth; the transverse plane is perpendicular to the shaft. The standard cutter is defined in the normal plane, so the normal module mn is the standard value. The transverse module is mt = mn / cos ψ. Module is the pitch diameter per tooth, in millimetres. The transverse pitch is pt = π mt. The axial pitch, the distance between corresponding points of adjacent teeth along the shaft, is px = pt / tan ψ.

The pressure angle also changes with the plane. If φn is the normal pressure angle and φt is the transverse pressure angle, tan φn = tan φt cos ψ. Standard values are mn and φn; the designer calculates mt and φt for the transverse plane. Typical helix angles are 8° to 20°. Larger angles give smoother contact but larger thrust.

Virtual teeth and modified Lewis

The normal plane cuts the pitch cylinder in an ellipse, not a true circle, so the tooth curvature in that plane is larger than the pitch radius. A spur gear with that curvature would have more teeth. That equivalent spur gear has the virtual, or formative, number of teeth zv = z / cos³ ψ, where z is the actual number of teeth and ψ is the helix angle. For ψ = 15° and z = 20, zv = 22.2, so the tooth behaves like a 22-tooth spur tooth. Some syllabi write β for the helix angle and call zv the formative number of teeth.

The Lewis form factor y is read for zv, not z. The modified Lewis equation for helical gears is σ = Ft / (Cv b π mn y). Here σ is the bending stress at the tooth root in N/mm², Ft is the tangential load at the pitch circle in N, b is the face width in mm, mn is the normal module in mm, y is the tooth form factor for zv, and Cv is the velocity factor from Chapter 7.3. The factor y comes from the standard tooth form and is about 0.1 for common tooth numbers; the π appears because y is defined through the circular pitch. Some tables list Y = πy instead, with values around 0.3 to 0.4; then use σ = Ft / (Cv b mn Y). Never mix a table of y with the form of the equation that expects Y. Use mn because the Lewis cantilever is taken on the normal tooth section. Using mt here overestimates the tooth thickness and gives an unsafe design.

Helical gears also have overlap. The transverse contact ratio εα is found as for a spur gear in the transverse plane. The overlap ratio is εβ = b sin ψ / (π mn). Total contact ratio is ε = εα + εβ. Overlap keeps more than one tooth in contact and smooths the drive. A total ratio above about 2 is common in quiet gearboxes.

Forces, thrust and hand of helix

The torque T on a gear gives the tangential force Ft = 2T / d = P / v. T is torque in N·mm, d is pitch diameter in mm, P is power in W and v is pitch line velocity in m/s; use consistent units. The diameter is d = mt z = mn z / cos ψ. The same Ft acts at the pitch circle.

The tooth also pushes radially and axially. The radial force is Fr = Ft tan φt = Ft tan φn / cos ψ. The axial thrust is Fa = Ft tan ψ. This thrust pushes the shaft along its axis, so the bearings must take it. A ball bearing with deep grooves takes little thrust; angular contact or tapered roller bearings are used. The shaft also bends under Ft and Fr and carries an axial load Fa. See Unit 5 for bearing selection.

Helices have a hand, right or left. Two parallel shafts need gears of opposite hand to mesh. The direction of thrust depends on hand and direction of rotation, so reverse rotation reverses Fa. A double helical gear, or herringbone gear, has two opposite helices on one body; the two thrusts cancel inside the gear and the bearings take almost no axial load. It costs more to make and needs space.

Worked example

Question. A helical pair has mn = 3 mm, z1 = 20, z2 = 80, ψ = 15°, b = 45 mm, and transmits 11 kW at 1450 rpm. Find mt, d1, v, Ft, Fa and zv1.

  1. Helix angle ψ = 15°. Normal module mn = 3 mm. Transverse module mt = mn / cos ψ = 3 / cos 15° = 3 / 0.9659 = 3.106 mm.
  2. Pitch diameter d1 = mt z1 = 3.106 × 20 = 62.12 mm, use 62.1 mm.
  3. Pitch line velocity v = π d1 n1 / 60 with d1 in metres: v = π × 0.0621 × 1450 / 60 = 4.71 m/s.
  4. Tangential force Ft = P / v = 11000 / 4.71 = 2335 N.
  5. Axial thrust Fa = Ft tan ψ = 2335 × tan 15° = 2335 × 0.2679 = 626 N.
  6. Virtual teeth zv1 = z1 / cos³ ψ = 20 / (0.9659)³ = 20 / 0.9010 = 22.2.
  7. The radial force is Fr = Ft tan φn / cos ψ; φn is not given, so it is not calculated here.

Answer. The pair has mt = 3.106 mm, d1 = 62.1 mm, v = 4.71 m/s, Ft = 2335 N, Fa = 626 N and zv1 = 22.2.

Exam traps

  • Using mt in the modified Lewis equation. The Lewis section is normal, so use mn and y for zv.
  • Forgetting Fa = Ft tan ψ in shaft and bearing loads. The gear looks like a spur gear in side view but the axial thrust is real.
  • Using z instead of zv = z / cos³ ψ for the Lewis form factor, and using Fr = Ft tan φn without dividing by cos ψ.
  • Degree/radian slip and hand errors: tan ψ needs ψ in the calculator's unit; gears on parallel shafts need opposite hand.

7.5 Straight bevel gear design

A bevel gear pair transmits power between shafts whose axes meet, usually at 90°. The teeth lie on a cone, so tooth size changes from the outer end to the apex. Design converts each cone into an equivalent spur gear on the back cone, then applies the Lewis and Buckingham equations at the mean section where the load acts. You meet this in a hand drill, a vehicle differential and a right-angle gearbox. The aim is to size teeth and face width without using the large-end dimensions for load.

You need: Lewis and Buckingham from Chapter 7.3 and basic trigonometry (tan, sin, cos).

Nomenclature on the pitch cone

In a bevel pair the two pitch cones roll on each other without slip. The pitch cone angle γ1 of the pinion is the half-angle of its pitch cone measured from the shaft axis; γ2 is the same for the gear. The shaft angle Σ is the angle between the two axes. For Σ = 90°, tan γ1 = z1/z2 and γ2 = 90° − γ1. The cone angles add to Σ, not to 180°.

Cone distance R is the slant length from the pitch cone apex to the large end. For a 90° pair, R = √((d1/2)² + (d2/2)²), where d1 and d2 are the pitch diameters at the large end. In general R = d1/(2 sin γ1). The face width b is the tooth length measured along the cone. Keep b ≤ R/3; a wider face twists under load and does not share load evenly.

Dimensions are given at the large end, called the back or crown. Addendum and dedendum are measured there on the back cone, not along the shaft. Because the tooth tapers, the tooth at the small end is smaller. For strength, use the section at the middle of the face, the mean section.

Virtual teeth: unroll the back cone into a plane. The tooth then looks like a spur tooth on a larger circle. Its number is zv = z / cos γ for each gear. Use zv in the Lewis form factor. Spiral bevel gears have curved teeth and run quieter; hypoid gears have offset axes.

Load at the mean section

Torque is carried at the mean radius, not the outer radius. The mean cone distance is Rm = R − b/2. The mean radius of the pinion is rm1 = Rm sin γ1, also rm1 = (d1/2)(Rm/R). The mean module is mm = m Rm/R, where m is the module at the large end. For the gear use the same Rm and its own cone angle.

The tangential force at the mean radius is Wt = 2T / rm, with T in N·mm and rm in mm. For power P in kW and speed N in rev/min, T = 60×106 P / (2π N) N·mm.

σ = Wt / (b · mm · Y)

σ is bending stress (N/mm²), Wt is tangential force at the mean radius (N), b is face width (mm), mm is mean module (mm), and Y is the Lewis form factor for zv.

Buckingham adds dynamic load: Wd = Wt + 21 v (b C + Wt) / (21 v + √(b C + Wt)). Here v is pitch-line velocity at the mean radius in m/s, b is face width in mm, C is the deformation factor in N/mm², and Wt, Wd are in N.

Straight bevel teeth also produce axial thrust. For pressure angle φ, Wa ≈ Wt tan φ sin γ and radial force Wr ≈ Wt tan φ cos γ. The thrust goes into the bearings; see Unit 5.

Design steps

  1. Fix the ratio i = z2/z1 and choose z1 above the undercut limit for the virtual tooth count. Choose the large-end module m from the standard series.
  2. Compute γ1 = tan⁻¹(z1/z2), γ2 = 90° − γ1, d1 = m z1, d2 = m z2 and R = √((d1/2)² + (d2/2)²). Take b ≤ R/3.
  3. Compute Rm = R − b/2, mm = m Rm/R, rm = Rm sin γ1 and Wt = 2T/rm.
  4. Read Y for zv = z/cos γ. Use σ = Wt/(b mm Y) to size b or mm. Change the trial value until σ is below the allowable stress.
  5. Compute Wd by Buckingham at the mean velocity v = π dm n / 60000, where dm is the mean pitch diameter in mm, n is in rev/min, and v is in m/s. Check the tooth for Wd and for wear.
  6. Compute Wa and Wr and give them to the bearing design in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A 90° straight bevel pair has z1 = 20, z2 = 40, module 4 mm at the large end and face width 30 mm. Find d1, d2, γ1, γ2, R, the mean cone distance, the mean module and the pinion virtual teeth.

  1. Large-end pitch diameters: d1 = m z1 = 4 × 20 = 80 mm; d2 = m z2 = 4 × 40 = 160 mm.
  2. Pinion cone angle: tan γ1 = z1/z2 = 20/40 = 0.5, so γ1 = 26.565°. Gear cone angle: γ2 = 90° − 26.565° = 63.435°.
  3. Cone distance: R = √((80/2)² + (160/2)²) = √(40² + 80²) = √8000 = 89.44 mm.
  4. Mean cone distance: Rm = R − b/2 = 89.44 − 30/2 = 89.44 − 15 = 74.44 mm. R/3 = 89.44/3 = 29.81 mm, so b = 30 mm is just over the limit (or use b = 29 mm); the example keeps 30 mm for round numbers.
  5. Mean module: mm = m Rm/R = 4 × 74.44/89.44 = 3.33 mm.
  6. Pinion virtual teeth: zv1 = z1/cos γ1 = 20/cos 26.565° = 20/0.8944 = 22.36.

Answer. d1 = 80 mm, d2 = 160 mm, γ1 = 26.57°, γ2 = 63.43°, R = 89.44 mm, Rm = 74.44 mm, mm = 3.33 mm and zv1 = 22.36.

Exam traps

  • Using the actual tooth count z in the Lewis form factor instead of the virtual tooth count zv = z/cos γ.
  • Taking the tangential load at the outer radius instead of the mean radius. At the same torque, the outer radius gives a smaller force, so the design is unsafe.
  • Adding cone angles to 180° instead of to the shaft angle. For a 90° pair, γ1 + γ2 = 90°.
  • Using the large-end module m in the Lewis equation when the load acts at the mean section. Use mm = m Rm/R.
  • Making the face width larger than R/3. The tooth becomes stiff at one end and lightly loaded at the other, so the load is not shared as assumed.

7.6 Worm and worm gear design

A worm drive is a screw (the worm) turning a specially shaped gear (the worm wheel). One step gives a large reduction, often 10:1 to 80:1, because the worm has only one to four starts while the wheel has many teeth. Contact is mostly sliding, so friction, wear and heat decide the size more than bending strength. You meet worm drives in lifts, conveyor gearboxes, steering gears and indexing tables. This chapter fixes the geometry, then uses sliding velocity, efficiency, self-locking, and the strength, wear and heat checks.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for gear nomenclature and the law of gearing, Chapter 7.2 for gear materials, and Unit 5 for lubrication and friction.

Geometry: the worm sets the size

A worm is a screw with zw starts; its mating worm wheel has z2 teeth. The axial pitch px is the distance between corresponding points on adjacent threads. With module m, px = πm. The lead L is the axial distance the worm advances in one turn: L = zw px = zw πm. The worm reference diameter d1 is not m zw. It is fixed by the diametral quotient q: d1 = q m. The wheel pitch diameter follows the usual rule: d2 = m z2. The lead angle λ is measured on the worm reference cylinder: tan λ = L/(πd1) = zw/q. The ratio is i = n1/n2 = z2/zw, where n1 and n2 are in rpm. Some syllabi call q the worm factor. A small q gives a slender worm (small d1) and a large lead angle λ; a large q gives a thick, stiff worm and a small λ. A single-start worm has zw = 1; a two-start worm doubles L and raises λ.

Sliding, friction and self-locking

At the mesh the worm thread slides along the wheel tooth. The worm pitch-line velocity is v1 = π d1 n1/60, with d1 in metres and n1 in rpm, giving v1 in m/s. The sliding velocity is larger: vs = v1/cos λ. Friction is described by the friction angle φ, where μ = tan φ and μ is the coefficient of friction. The efficiency is η = tan λ / tan(λ + φ). This formula uses φ, not the pressure angle α. A drive is self-locking when λ < φ: the wheel cannot turn the worm. Self-locking is useful in a hoist holding a load, but it also means low efficiency. Materials follow the sliding duty: the worm is hardened and ground steel, and the wheel is phosphor bronze. Lubrication and friction are covered in Unit 5.

Checks that size the drive

  1. Lewis bending on the wheel tooth. Use the tangential load Wt, the wheel module m and the face width b. The worm tooth is stronger, so the wheel is the weaker member.
  2. Wear. Compare the contact stress or the limiting wear load for the steel-on-bronze pair with the actual load. Wear sets the face width and the wheel material.
  3. Heat. The power lost to friction is approximately (1 − η)P, where P is the input power. This heat must leave through the housing and lubricant. A small gearbox can overheat even when the teeth are strong.

Use the wheel Lewis form factor for the actual number of teeth z2, not for zw. Detailed thermal rating is in Chapter 7.8.

Worked example

Question. A single-start worm has zw = 1, z2 = 40, module m = 5 mm, diametral quotient q = 10 and speed n1 = 1440 rpm. Find the worm diameter d1, wheel diameter d2, lead L, lead angle λ, worm pitch-line velocity v1 and sliding velocity vs.

  1. d1 = q m = 10 × 5 = 50 mm.
  2. d2 = m z2 = 5 × 40 = 200 mm.
  3. Single start: L = zw πm = 1 × π × 5 = 15.708 mm.
  4. tan λ = L/(πd1) = 15.708/(π × 50) = 0.1000, so λ = 5.711°.
  5. v1 = πd1n1/60 = π(0.050)(1440)/60 = 3.77 m/s.
  6. vs = v1/cos λ = 3.77/cos 5.711° = 3.789 m/s.

Answer. d1 = 50 mm, d2 = 200 mm, L = 15.708 mm, λ = 5.711°, v1 = 3.77 m/s, vs = 3.789 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using d1 = m zw is wrong; d1 = q m. The worm is a screw, not a spur gear.
  • Reversing the ratio: i = z2/zw, not zw/z2.
  • Using pressure angle α in η = tan λ/tan(λ + φ). The formula uses friction angle φ.
  • Forgetting starts: L = zw πm. For zw = 2 or 4, L is not πm.
  • Self-locking is λ < φ, not λ > φ. It gives low efficiency and is not a sign of good design.

7.7 Gear trains, including epicyclic gear trains

A gear train is several gear pairs in series. Each pair changes speed and torque by its tooth ratio, and the train multiplies the ratios. An epicyclic train adds a carrier that lets one gear roll around another, so one input can give two outputs or a large reduction in a small box. Gear trains appear in a car differential, a lathe back gear, a planetary hub and a hand drill.

You need: gear nomenclature and the law of gearing from Chapter 7.1, and relative angular velocity from mechanics.

Simple, compound and reverted trains

A gear train is a set of gears that carries motion from one shaft to another. A simple train has one gear on each shaft, so the gears mesh in one line. A compound train has at least one shaft with two gears that turn together. A reverted train is a compound train whose first and last shafts share one axis; a lathe back gear is an example.

For one pair, driver z₁ teeth drives driven z₂ teeth. The driven speed is ω₂ = −(z₁/z₂)ω₁ for external teeth; the minus sign means opposite directions. An internal pair has no minus sign. Define velocity ratio i = ω_first/ω_last. For a train, i = (z₂ z₄ …)/(z₁ z₃ …), where z₁, z₃ are drivers and z₂, z₄ are driven gears. Each external mesh reverses direction; an internal mesh does not. If i is greater than 1, the output is slower and has more torque, ignoring losses.

Example: drivers 20 and 30 teeth, driven 40 and 60 teeth. i = (40×60)/(20×30)=4, so the output turns at one quarter of the input speed. Two external meshes give no net reversal. For a reverted train with the same module, the centre distance condition is z₁+z₂ = z₃+z₄. This is the usual exam check.

Epicyclic train: parts and tabulation

An epicyclic train has a central sun gear, one or more planet gears, an internal ring gear and a carrier. The carrier pins the planets and turns about the same axis as the sun. Each planet meshes with the sun and with the ring. Constrain two members: fix one member and drive a second; the third then has one definite speed and is the output. For example, fix the ring and drive the sun, and the carrier is the output. Some syllabi call this a planetary gear train.

For a planet to touch both the sun and the ring, the tooth numbers must satisfy z_R = z_S + 2z_P. With the numbers used here, 60 = 24 + 2×18.

Do not use z_driven/z_driver directly. Hold the carrier fixed and find relative turns. Give the sun +1 turn. The planet turns −z_S/z_P because the sun-planet mesh is external. The ring turns −z_S/z_R: the planet teeth cancel, and the internal mesh keeps planet and ring in the same direction. Add c turns of the carrier to every member. The sun becomes 1+c; the ring becomes −z_S/z_R + c. If the sun is fixed, 1+c=0, so c=−1 and the ring is −(1+z_S/z_R) turns. This is the tabulation method.

Where epicyclic trains are used

A car differential is epicyclic with bevel planets. The engine drives the carrier. On a straight road the planets do not spin on their pins, so both half shafts turn with the carrier. In a turn the inner wheel slows, the outer speeds up, and the average stays at carrier speed. This lets the car turn without wheel slip.

A lathe back gear is a reverted train, not epicyclic; it gives low spindle speed and high torque for heavy cuts. A planetary hub in an excavator or electric vehicle uses a small sun and several planets inside a ring, so a large reduction fits inside the wheel. The fixed ring and driven carrier give high torque at low speed. Automatic gearboxes use clutches to hold different members and select ratios; see Chapter 7.9.

7.8 Gear life and gearbox design

A gear tooth does not see one fixed load for its whole life. It sees a load spectrum: full load in the morning, part load at night, shocks at starting. The tooth collects fatigue damage cycle by cycle. Gear life design turns that spectrum into one equivalent load, sizes the gears, then checks shafts, bearings, heat and lubrication. The same method sizes a gearbox for a textile mill, a crane or an electric vehicle reducer.

You need: spur gear design, helical gear design, fatigue S-N and Miner rule from Unit 2, and bearing life and lubrication from Unit 5.

Life in cycles, not hours

Gear life is counted in load cycles on one tooth. A gear running at n rpm for Lh hours gives N = 60 n Lh cycles. A gear at 1200 rpm for 8,000 h sees 5.76×108 cycles.

N = 60 n Lh

n is gear speed in rpm; Lh is design life in hours; N is load cycles on one tooth.

The S-N curve plots alternating stress S against cycles to failure N. On log-log axes it falls with a slope; for steel it becomes flat below the endurance limit Se. Bending fatigue and contact fatigue have separate S-N curves, because one starts at the tooth root and the other at the tooth surface.

Miner's rule adds damage fractions: D = Σ ni / Ni ≤ 1. ni is cycles at stress level i; Ni is cycles to failure at that stress from the S-N curve. D reaches 1 at predicted failure. Use D below 1 for reliability; a reliability factor raises the required size.

Design life in hours comes from the customer: 10,000 h for a workshop hoist, more for a power plant. Convert every duty point to cycles before comparing.

Equivalent load and service factor

A load spectrum lists power Pi or torque Ti and the fraction of life pi at that level. For contact fatigue the equivalent power is Peq = (Σ pi Pi3)1/3. Pi is power in kW; pi is a fraction that sums to 1; the exponent 3 comes from the contact S-N slope. The result is the steady power that gives the same contact damage as the spectrum. Some syllabi call it the cubic mean load.

P<sub>eq</sub> = (Σ p<sub>i</sub> P<sub>i</sub><sup>3</sup>)<sup>1/3</sup>

Peq is equivalent contact power in kW; Pi is power at duty point i in kW; pi is the fraction of life at that point.

For a torque spectrum replace Pi by Ti. For bending fatigue the exponent is different, so do not use the contact equivalent load for a bending check unless the same exponent applies. Apply a service factor Ks to the nominal equivalent load: Pdesign = Ks Peq. Ks accounts for shock, starting, reversing and application severity; it is selected from the application, not from the gear.

Do not use the maximum load for the whole life. That over-sizes and hides bearing or heat limits. Do not use the arithmetic average either; it under-sizes because damage grows with the cube of contact load.

Gearbox layout, heat and lubrication

A single-stage gearbox is simple and efficient; it suits ratios up to about 4 to 6. A two-stage gearbox splits the total ratio i into i = i1 i2. Equal split i1 = i2 = √i keeps the stages similar in size. More stages add losses and cost, so use them only when the ratio or package needs it.

The housing carries bearing bores, holds oil and conducts heat to the air. It is ribbed for stiffness. Oil level reaches the lowest gear so splash lubrication works at moderate speed. At high speed use forced lubrication with a pump and oil cooler. Seals at the shafts keep oil in and dirt out; a breather stops pressure build-up.

ΔT = Pin (1 − η) / (h A)

ΔT is oil-to-air temperature rise in K; Pin is input power in W; η is efficiency; h is heat transfer coefficient in W/(m²K); A is housing area in m².

At steady state heat generated equals heat lost: Pin(1 − η) = h A ΔT. If ΔT is too high, add fins, a fan or an oil cooler. Check bearings by their own life formula in Unit 5. A gearbox can fail first on bearing life, seal leakage or oil temperature, not tooth bending.

Worked example

Question. A gearbox runs at 1000 rpm for 10,000 h. It transmits 10 kW for 60% of the time, 6 kW for 30% and 2 kW for 10%. For contact fatigue take life exponent m = 3. Find the equivalent power and the total cycles on a gear tooth.

  1. Fractions sum: 0.60 + 0.30 + 0.10 = 1.00.
  2. Take m = 3. Compute each term: 0.60 × 10³ = 0.60 × 1000 = 600; 0.30 × 6³ = 0.30 × 216 = 64.8; 0.10 × 2³ = 0.10 × 8 = 0.8.
  3. Sum = 600 + 64.8 + 0.8 = 665.6 kW³.
  4. Peq = 665.61/3 = ∛665.6 = 8.73 kW (cube root, not division by 3).
  5. Cycles N = 60 n Lh = 60 × 1000 × 10,000 = 6.0×10⁸ cycles.

Answer. The equivalent power is 8.73 kW and the tooth sees 6.0×10⁸ cycles in 10,000 h.

Exam traps

  • Using the maximum power for the whole life over-sizes the gears and hides the real heat or bearing limit.
  • Using the arithmetic average power under-sizes contact fatigue; contact damage follows the cube of load.
  • Counting life in hours and forgetting speed: cycles N = 60 n Lh.
  • Using the contact exponent 3 for a bending check without changing to the bending exponent.
  • Forgetting the service factor, or applying it after the gear size is fixed.

7.9 Multi-speed gearboxes and ray diagrams

A multi-speed gearbox turns one input speed into a set of stepped output speeds. It places gear groups in series, and each group multiplies the number of ratios. A lathe, drill press or conveyor needs these steps because the best cutting speed or belt speed changes with the job. The speeds follow a geometric progression, and a ray diagram plots them on a log scale so the step size and any overlap are visible at a glance.

You need: Chapter 7.7 for train ratios and Chapter 7.8 for gearbox layout.

Speed range and geometric steps

A gearbox is described by its lowest and highest spindle speeds. The speed range compares them by a ratio, not by a subtraction.

Rₙ = n_max / n_min, φ = (Rₙ)^(1/(z−1))

Rₙ is the speed range (unitless); n_max is the highest spindle speed in rpm; n_min is the lowest spindle speed in rpm; φ is the step ratio (unitless); z is the number of spindle speeds.

Geometric progression means n₂ = n₁φ, n₃ = n₁φ², and so on. Each step is the same percentage change. An arithmetic progression adds a constant rpm, so the jump from 100 to 150 rpm is 50 percent but from 500 to 550 rpm is only 10 percent. The machine would be coarse at low speed and wastefully fine at high speed.

Common standard φ values are 1.06, 1.12, 1.25, 1.41, 1.58 and 2.00. Choose the nearest standard value; then adjust n_min or n_max slightly if the calculated φ falls between two standards.

Ray diagram and structure formula

A ray diagram, also called a speed diagram, has spindle speed on a vertical log scale and shaft positions along the horizontal. The input speed is one point on the first shaft line. Each gear group sends rays to the next shaft line. The vertical rise of a ray equals log φ for that step. Parallel rays mean equal ratio steps; rays that cross or land on the same speed show a wrong layout.

z = p₁ × p₂ × ... × pₖ

z is the total number of spindle speeds; p₁, p₂, ..., pₖ are the numbers of ratios in gear groups 1 to k.

The structure formula counts the ratios. For example 6 = 2 × 3 means two ratios in group 1 and three ratios in group 2; 6 = 3 × 2 reverses the order. The order changes the ray slopes and shaft sizes even though the output speeds are the same.

Keep each group speed ratio r_g = output speed / input speed between 1/4 and 4. A larger reduction in one group needs big gears or a long shaft centre distance. Only a few universities teach detailed structure formulae; the product rule above is enough for reading a ray diagram.

Grouping, sliding gears and Norton drives

Grouping means choosing which ratios sit in which group. The aim is to keep every group inside the ratio limit and to avoid crossed rays. A gearing diagram is the side view of the same layout: it shows the actual gear pairs that mesh for each speed.

A sliding gear drive has a cluster of gears that slides along a splined shaft. Moving the cluster meshes a different pair and selects a new ratio. It is simple and cheap, but the gears cannot be shifted under load. A Norton drive uses a cone of gears on one shaft and a tumbler gear on the other; the tumbler swings to pick a ratio. It gives many speeds in a small space but needs careful lubrication.

In a machine tool, six or nine speeds cover a wide range while keeping every gear pair small. The same idea appears in a car manual gearbox: first gear gives a large reduction for torque, top gear gives a ratio near 1 for speed.

Worked example

Question. A six-speed gearbox has minimum speed n_min = 100 rpm and step ratio φ = 1.41. Find all six spindle speeds, the maximum speed and the speed range. Use the geometric progression.

  1. Number the speeds n₁ to n₆. Set n₁ = n_min = 100 rpm.
  2. n₂ = 100 × 1.41 = 141 rpm.
  3. n₃ = 100 × 1.41² = 100 × 1.9881 = 198.8 ≈ 199 rpm.
  4. n₄ = 100 × 1.41³ = 100 × 2.8032 = 280.3 ≈ 280 rpm.
  5. n₅ = 100 × 1.41⁴ = 100 × 3.9525 = 395.3 ≈ 395 rpm.
  6. n₆ = 100 × 1.41⁵ = 100 × 5.5731 = 557.3 ≈ 557 rpm.
  7. Maximum speed n_max = 557 rpm. Speed range Rₙ = 557 / 100 = 5.57.

Answer. The six speeds are 100, 141, 199, 280, 395 and 557 rpm; maximum 557 rpm; range 5.57.

Exam traps

  • Using an arithmetic progression because the numbers look neat; gearbox speeds are geometric.
  • Treating the speed range as n_max − n_min instead of n_max / n_min.
  • Putting z instead of z−1 in the denominator of the φ formula; there are z−1 intervals between z speeds.
  • Accepting a ray diagram where rays cross or two output speeds coincide; that means the layout is wrong.
  • Ignoring the 1/4 to 4 ratio limit for one group; a very large reduction in one group makes the gears and shaft spacing too large.

Takeaways

  1. A gear pair transmits a truly constant ratio only because the involute keeps the common normal fixed through the pitch point.
  2. Gear material is a system: a tough core for bending fatigue and a hard surface for pitting resistance, chosen with heat treatment and cost together.
  3. Lewis treats the tooth as a cantilever and sizes the module; Buckingham then adds the dynamic load of speed and the wear load of surface stress, and the larger of the two checks governs.
  4. Helical teeth carry more load quietly because contact line is longer, but the axial thrust they create must be handed to the bearings and the shaft.
  5. Bevel gear teeth are designed as spur teeth of a virtual gear on the back cone, using the mean cone distance for the load.
  6. In a worm drive, sliding contact makes friction, wear and heat the size-setting factors, not bending strength.
  7. Every gear train is solved pair by pair; an epicyclic train needs the extra step of adding the arm rotation to the relative rotations.
  8. A gearbox is sized by an equivalent life load from the load spectrum, then checked for bearings, lubrication and heat.
  9. A multi-speed gearbox is a product of gear groups, and the ray diagram shows at a glance whether the speed steps are geometric and whether groups overlap.

Glossary

Addendum
Radial height of a tooth above the pitch circle, equal to one module in standard teeth.
Backlash
The small clearance between the non-driving flanks of meshing teeth, allowing lubrication and thermal growth.
Circular pitch
Arc length on the pitch circle between corresponding points of adjacent teeth, equal to π times the module.
Cone distance
The slant length from the apex to the large end of a bevel gear pitch cone.
Contact ratio
The average number of tooth pairs in contact, equal to the arc of contact divided by the circular pitch.
Dedendum
Radial depth of a tooth below the pitch circle, equal to 1.25 module in standard full-depth teeth.
Dynamic load
The additional tooth load caused by impact and mesh errors at running speed.
Epicyclic gear train
A train in which one or more gears move around a central axis while carried by a rotating arm.
Equivalent load
A single steady load that causes the same fatigue damage as a varying load history over the same number of cycles.
Gear
A toothed wheel that transmits motion and torque to another toothed wheel by successive contact of teeth.
Helix angle
The angle a helical tooth makes with the shaft axis.
Involute
The curve traced by a point on a taut string unwound from a base circle; its normal is always tangent to the base circle.
Lead angle
The angle of the worm thread helix with the plane of rotation, controlling efficiency and self-locking.
Lewis form factor
A factor depending on tooth number that gives the strength of the parabolic equivalent tooth.
Module
The pitch circle diameter in mm divided by the number of teeth; the basic size unit of gear teeth.
Pinion
The smaller gear of a meshing pair, usually the driver.
Pitch circle
The theoretical circle on which the gear ratio is calculated and on which the pitch point lies.
Pitting
Fatigue failure of the tooth surface appearing as small craters on the flank.
Pressure angle
The angle between the line of action and the common tangent to the pitch circles, usually 20°.
Progression ratio
The constant factor between consecutive spindle speeds in a geometric speed series.
Ray diagram
A log-scale speed chart in which each gear pair appears as a straight ray, used to design multi-speed gearboxes.
Train value
The ratio of the product of driving teeth to the product of driven teeth for a whole train.
Undercutting
Removal of material at the tooth root by the cutter when the tooth count is too low, weakening the tooth.
Virtual number of teeth
The tooth count of the equivalent spur gear used in strength formulas for helical and bevel gears.
Wear load
The limiting tangential load set by surface contact stress, beyond which pitting starts.
