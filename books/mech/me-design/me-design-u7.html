7.1 Gear types, nomenclature and law of gearing

Gears pass rotation and torque from one shaft to another at a fixed speed ratio, with no slip. The law of gearing says what tooth shape makes the ratio truly constant: at every instant the common normal at the contact point must pass through one fixed point, the pitch point. The involute profile meets this law and also forgives a small error in centre distance, so almost every power gear uses it. Gears sit in every two-wheeler gearbox and in the pump set on a Chennai terrace.

You need: school geometry of circles and tangents, and the pitch-line speed v = πdn/60 used for belt drives.

Gear families by shaft arrangement

Gears are grouped by how their shafts sit. Spur gears have straight teeth and join parallel shafts; they are simple and efficient but noisy at high speed, because a whole tooth width engages at once. Helical gears also join parallel shafts, with teeth at a helix angle: they run quieter and carry more load, but push the shaft axially. Bevel gears are cut on cones and join intersecting shafts, usually at 90°. A worm and worm wheel join crossed shafts and give a large reduction in one step. A rack is a gear of infinite radius that turns rotation into straight-line motion, as in a car steering rack. An internal gear has its teeth inside a ring; the pinion runs inside it and both turn the same way.

Tooth nomenclature and the module

Every calculation starts at the pitch circle: the imaginary circle on which the two gears roll without slipping. Let d be the pitch circle diameter in mm and z the number of teeth. The module is m = d/z, in mm; a 4 mm module means 4 mm of pitch diameter for every tooth, and modules come from a standard series (1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 mm). The circular pitch is the distance from a point on one tooth to the same point on the next, measured along the pitch circle: p = πd/z = πm. In inch units the diametral pitch is P = z/d with d in inches (teeth per inch of diameter), so m = 25.4/P.

Tooth heights follow the module. For standard 20° full-depth teeth the addendum (pitch circle to tip) is ha = m and the dedendum (pitch circle to root) is hf = 1.25m. The addendum circle passes through the tips, da = d + 2m; the dedendum circle passes through the roots, df = d − 2.5m. Clearance is c = 0.25m, working depth 2m and whole depth 2.25m. On the pitch circle the tooth thickness and the space width are each p/2 in theory; the space is made slightly wider, and the gap between the non-driving flanks of a meshing pair is the backlash. Face width b is the tooth length along the shaft.

The line of action is the common tangent to the two base circles; tooth contact always lies on it. The pressure angle φ is the angle between this line and the common tangent to the two pitch circles at the pitch point; 20° is standard, 14½° is old practice. The base circle, db = d cos φ, is the circle the involute grows from. For two external gears the centre distance is a = m(z1 + z2)/2; for an internal pair a = m(z2 − z1)/2, with z2 the internal gear.

The law of gearing, the involute and contact ratio

At any instant two teeth touch at one point. Draw the common normal to the two profiles there. If this normal cuts the line of centres O1O2 at a fixed point P, similar triangles give ω1/ω2 = O2P/O1P, a constant. That is the law of gearing. If the normal wanders, the ratio changes tooth by tooth and the drive vibrates.

An involute is the curve traced by the end of a taut string unwound from a base circle. Its normal at every point is tangent to the base circle. For two involute teeth the common normal is therefore the common tangent to both base circles, a line fixed in space, so it always cuts O1O2 at the same point P and ω1/ω2 = d2/d1 = z2/z1. If the centre distance grows a little, the base circles do not change, so the ratio does not change either; only the working pressure angle and the backlash grow.

Contact begins where the gear's addendum circle cuts the line of action and ends where the pinion's addendum circle cuts it. That length is the path of contact; divided by the base pitch pb = πm cos φ it gives the contact ratio, the average number of tooth pairs in mesh. It must be more than 1, and 1.4 to 1.8 is usual for spur gears. Interference happens when a gear tip would touch the pinion below its base circle, where there is no involute. During cutting the cutter then removes metal from the pinion root; this undercutting is the damage interference does, and it weakens the tooth. A 20° full-depth pinion needs about 17 or more teeth to escape it, or a profile shift. Tooth strength itself is the subject of spur gear design.

Worked example

Question. A 20° full-depth involute spur pair has z1 = 20, z2 = 60 and module 4 mm. Find both pitch diameters, the centre distance, both base circle diameters and the velocity ratio.

  1. Pitch diameters: d1 = m z1 = 4 × 20 = 80 mm and d2 = m z2 = 4 × 60 = 240 mm.
  2. Centre distance (external pair): a = (d1 + d2)/2 = (80 + 240)/2 = 160 mm.
  3. Base circles: db = d cos φ, with cos 20° = 0.93969. db1 = 80 × 0.93969 = 75.18 mm and db2 = 240 × 0.93969 = 225.53 mm.
  4. Velocity ratio: ω1/ω2 = z2/z1 = 60/20 = 3. The pinion turns three times for one turn of the gear.

Answer. d1 = 80 mm, d2 = 240 mm, a = 160 mm, db1 = 75.18 mm, db2 = 225.53 mm, velocity ratio 3.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same pair (addendum = m = 4 mm) find the path of approach, the path of recess, the path of contact and the contact ratio.

  1. Radii: r1 = 40 mm, r2 = 120 mm; addendum radii ra1 = 44 mm, ra2 = 124 mm; base radii rb1 = 37.588 mm, rb2 = 112.763 mm.
  2. Path of approach (set by the gear tip): √(ra2² − rb2²) − r2 sin φ = √(124² − 112.763²) − 120 × 0.34202 = 51.580 − 41.042 = 10.537 mm.
  3. Path of recess (set by the pinion tip): √(ra1² − rb1²) − r1 sin φ = √(44² − 37.588²) − 40 × 0.34202 = 22.873 − 13.681 = 9.192 mm.
  4. Path of contact = 10.537 + 9.192 = 19.729 mm.
  5. Base pitch pb = πm cos φ = π × 4 × 0.93969 = 11.809 mm.
  6. Contact ratio = 19.729/11.809 = 1.67.

Answer. Approach 10.54 mm, recess 9.19 mm, path of contact 19.73 mm, contact ratio 1.67: two pairs of teeth share the load for about two thirds of the time.

Exam traps

  • Module uses the pitch circle: m = d/z. Using the tip (addendum) diameter or the base diameter gives a wrong module.
  • Contact ratio is the path of contact over the base pitch πm cos φ, or the arc of contact over p = πm; a path over p is wrong.
  • The law of gearing is about the common normal at the contact point, not the common tangent. Examiners test this wording.
  • Diametral pitch is in inch units: P = z/d with d in inches, and m = 25.4/P. Mixing mm with P gives nonsense.
  • Undercutting is not a cure; it is the damage interference causes when a pinion has too few teeth. The cures are more teeth, a profile shift or a stub tooth.

7.2 Gear materials

A gear tooth fails in one of two places. The root cracks in bending fatigue, or the flank pits and wears. One material must resist both, and the two demands pull against each other: a hard surface tends to be brittle, while bending needs a tough core. Gear material choice is therefore a package of core strength, surface hardness and heat treatment, matched to load, speed and cost. It decides whether a Chennai water-plant gearbox lasts ten years or two.

You need: tensile strength and hardness of steels (selection of materials), and module and pitch diameter from gear nomenclature.

Three demands on one material

A tooth is a short cantilever. The root bending stress is carried by the whole section, so the core must be strong and tough; a hard, brittle core chips at the root under shock. The flank carries repeated rolling with sliding and fails by pitting and wear, which is a surface hardness problem.

Surface hardness is usually quoted as BHN, the Brinell hardness number: a 10 mm hardened ball is pressed in with 3000 kgf and the load is divided by the dent area, so BHN is in kgf/mm². Very hard cases are quoted in HRC (Rockwell C) or HV (Vickers): 58 HRC is about 600 BHN on the same scale. For steel the ultimate tensile strength is roughly 3.4 × BHN in MPa; that is an estimate from a hardness test, not a design value. Many Indian textbooks take the allowable bending stress of a gear tooth as σb = σut/3, and the wear of a steel pair through the load-stress factor K = 0.16(BHN/100)² N/mm²; both are used in spur gear design.

The pinion rule matters more than any single number. When a 20-tooth pinion drives a 60-tooth gear, the pinion turns three times for each turn of the gear, so each pinion tooth is loaded three times as often. Fatigue damage grows with the number of cycles, so with equal hardness the pinion pits first. For through-hardened pairs make the pinion about 30 to 50 BHN harder than the gear, so both reach the end of their life together. A case-hardened pinion is usually far harder than its gear anyway.

Steels and their heat treatment

Plain carbon steels with 0.3 to 0.6 % carbon (C40, C45, 40C8) are the shop-floor default. C45 normalised is about 200 to 230 BHN; hardened and tempered it reaches 250 to 300 BHN. It is cheap and machines freely. Above about 350 BHN a through-hardened gear is hard to cut and distorts, so harder surfaces come from case treatments instead. Alloy steels add chromium for hardenability, nickel for core toughness and molybdenum against temper brittleness; through-hardened 40Cr1 or 42CrMo4 keeps 300 to 350 BHN in sections too thick for plain carbon steel.

  • Case carburising: a low-carbon alloy steel (0.15 to 0.25 % C, for example 20MnCr5) is held at about 900 to 930 °C in a carbon-rich gas, so carbon diffuses 0.8 to 1.5 mm into the surface; the gear is then quenched and tempered. Surface 58 to 62 HRC, core 30 to 40 HRC. Chosen for heavy load, shock and high speed, as in vehicle gearboxes.
  • Nitriding: ammonia gas at about 500 to 550 °C for many hours, with no quench. The case is thin, 0.3 to 0.6 mm, and very hard, roughly 600 to 1000 HV depending on the steel. Distortion is very small, so it suits internal gears and large rings that cannot be ground after hardening.
  • Induction or flame hardening: the flanks are heated quickly and quenched at once, giving about 50 to 55 HRC over a tough core. Used on large-module gears made of medium-carbon steel.

Distortion is the hidden cost of hardening. A carburised gear must be ground after hardening, so the drawing leaves grinding stock on each flank and says 'grind after hardening'.

Cast iron, bronze and non-metals

Grey cast iron (FG 200, FG 260; the number is the minimum tensile strength in MPa) has flake graphite that damps vibration and breaks chips. It is cheap and quiet but weak in tension and shock. Use it for large, slow gears, usually running with a steel pinion; never for a small high-speed pinion.

Bronze belongs to worm wheels. A worm drive slides rather than rolls, so the wheel must be conformable, resist seizure and have low friction against steel. The standard pair is a phosphor bronze wheel with a hardened, ground steel worm; the bronze is always the softer member.

  • Nylon and acetal gears run quietly with little lubrication and absorb shock, but they creep, swell with moisture and get rid of heat badly, so keep load and speed low (food mixers, printers).
  • Phenolic laminates and sintered powder-metal gears serve quiet timing drives and cheap, mass-produced small gears.

Worked example

Question. A 7.5 kW motor at 1440 rpm drives a 20-tooth pinion of module 3 mm through a 1:3 pair with moderate shock. Find the pitch line velocity, the torque and the tangential load, and recommend a material pair.

  1. Pitch diameter: d1 = m z1 = 3 × 20 = 60 mm = 0.060 m.
  2. Pitch line velocity: v = πd1n/60 = π × 0.060 × 1440/60 = 4.52 m/s.
  3. Angular speed: ω = 2πn/60 = 2π × 1440/60 = 150.80 rad/s. Torque: T = P/ω = 7500/150.80 = 49.74 N·m.
  4. Tangential load: Ft = 2T/d1 = 2 × 49.74/0.060 = 1658 N. Check: P/v = 7500/4.524 = 1658 N.
  5. Choice: the speed is moderate and the shock is moderate, so a through-hardened steel pair is enough: a C45 pinion at about 300 BHN with a C45 gear at about 250 BHN (50 BHN softer, because the pinion sees three times the cycles). If the box must be smaller or the shock grows, change to a case-carburised 20MnCr5 pinion.

Answer. v = 4.52 m/s, T = 49.74 N·m, Ft = 1.66 kN; a through-hardened C45 pair, pinion about 300 BHN and gear about 250 BHN.

Worked example 2

Question. A 40C8 steel pinion (σut = 600 N/mm²) runs with a gear of the same steel. Both are hardened to 300 BHN. Find the allowable bending stress, the load-stress factor K, and how much the wear capacity rises if the surfaces are hardened to 380 BHN instead. The pinion has 20 teeth, runs at 1440 rpm with ratio 3, for 10 000 h: find the cycles on a pinion tooth and on a gear tooth.

  1. Allowable bending stress: σb = σut/3 = 600/3 = 200 N/mm².
  2. Load-stress factor at 300 BHN: K = 0.16(300/100)² = 0.16 × 9 = 1.44 N/mm².
  3. At 380 BHN: K = 0.16 × 3.8² = 0.16 × 14.44 = 2.31 N/mm². The wear load is proportional to K, so the wear capacity rises by 2.31/1.44 = 1.60 times, that is 60 %, with the same size of gear.
  4. Pinion cycles: N = 60 n Lh = 60 × 1440 × 10 000 = 8.64 × 10⁸.
  5. Gear speed = 1440/3 = 480 rpm, so gear cycles = 60 × 480 × 10 000 = 2.88 × 10⁸, a third of the pinion's.

Answer. σb = 200 N/mm²; K = 1.44 N/mm² at 300 BHN and 2.31 N/mm² at 380 BHN (60 % more wear capacity); 8.64 × 10⁸ pinion cycles against 2.88 × 10⁸ gear cycles.

Exam traps

  • Choosing the hardest steel for both wheels. A hard case needs a tough core; a tooth hardened right through is brittle and chips at the root under shock.
  • Reading BHN as a strength in MPa. 300 BHN suggests about 1000 MPa tensile strength for steel, but only as an estimate, and the allowable bending stress is a fraction of the strength.
  • Mixing hardness scales: 58 HRC is about 600 BHN. Compare pinion and gear hardness on one scale.
  • Pairing bronze with bronze in a worm drive, or a soft worm with a hard wheel. The wheel is the bronze member and the worm is hardened and ground.
  • Using cast iron for a small high-speed pinion because it runs quietly. Damping does not make up for its low tensile and shock strength.

7.3 Spur gear design: Lewis and Buckingham equations

A spur tooth is a short cantilever fixed at the root. Lewis turned that picture into one line that gives the beam strength of the tooth, the largest tangential load it can carry in bending. Buckingham added what Lewis misses: the extra dynamic load from tooth errors at speed, and the limiting load for surface wear. These numbers, set against the real load with a factor of safety, fix the module and face width of every spur pair, from a lathe headstock to an electric-vehicle reducer.

You need: module and pitch diameter, allowable stress and hardness of gear materials, and the bending of a cantilever.

Lewis: the tooth as a cantilever

Take the worst case: one tooth carries the whole load, and the load acts at the tip, where it gives the largest bending moment at the root. Lewis fitted a parabola of uniform strength inside the tooth, touching the root fillets. The result is the beam strength of the tooth:

Sb = σb b m Y

Here Sb is the beam strength, the largest tangential load in N the tooth can carry in bending; σb is the allowable bending stress in N/mm² (σut/3 in many Indian textbooks); b is the face width in mm; m is the module in mm; Y is the Lewis form factor, a number that depends only on the tooth form and the number of teeth.

For 20° full-depth involute teeth a common fit is Y = 0.484 − 2.87/z, which gives Y = 0.3405 at 20 teeth and 0.4362 at 60 teeth; tables differ slightly (0.322 at 20 teeth in Shigley's), so use your data book's. Some books write Sb = σb b p y with p = πm; their factor is y = Y/π, about 0.11, and must never be mixed with Y.

Y grows with the number of teeth, because a small gear's teeth are thinner at the root. So when pinion and gear are of the same material, the pinion is weaker, and the design is done on the member with the smaller product σb Y. Face width is usually 8m to 12m, often 10m; wider teeth do not share the load evenly because shafts deflect and teeth are never perfectly aligned.

The load the tooth must carry

The tangential force at the pitch circle is Ft = P/v, with P the power in W and v = πd1n_1/60 the pitch line velocity in m/s (d1 in metres, n1 in rpm). Two things make the real load larger. A service factor Cs (1 for smooth duty, about 1.25 to 1.75 for moderate to heavy shock) allows for the driver and the machine. A velocity factor allows for the impact of tooth errors at speed: for ordinary cut gears below about 10 m/s, Cv = 3/(3 + v).

Feff = Cs Ft / Cv, Sb ≥ fs Feff Feff is the effective load in N and fs the factor of safety, often 1.5. Since v depends on m, m is found by trial.

Shigley and AGMA use instead a dynamic factor Kv greater than 1 that multiplies Ft; do not mix the two conventions.

Buckingham: dynamic load and wear

Tooth errors e and tooth stiffness make the gears separate and strike again; Buckingham's estimate of this extra load is:

Fd = 21v(Ceb + Ft) / (21v + √(Ceb + Ft)) Fd is the dynamic (incremental) load in N; v is in m/s; e is the sum of the tooth errors of the pair in mm; b is the face width in mm; C is the deformation factor in N/mm², 11 400 for two steel gears with 20° full-depth teeth. Then Feff = Cs Ft + Fd, and the bending check is Sb ≥ fs Feff.

Surfaces fail by pitting under repeated contact stress. Buckingham's wear strength, the largest load the flanks can carry for a long life, is:

Sw = b Q d1 K, Q = 2z2/(z1 + z2) Sw is in N; d1 is the pinion pitch diameter in mm; Q is the ratio factor (for an internal gear Q = 2z2/(z2 − z1)); K is the load-stress factor in N/mm², 0.16(BHN/100)² for a steel pair. The wear check is Sw ≥ fs Feff.

The design order is: size m from the Lewis check with Cv and round it up to a standard module; then find Fd, Feff = Cs Ft + Fd and Sw, and check both Sb and Sw against fs Feff. If wear fails, raise the surface hardness or the size; if bending fails, raise m or b, or cut the tooth errors.

Worked example

Question. A pair of 20° full-depth spur gears transmits 10 kW at 1200 rpm of the pinion; z1 = 20, z2 = 60. Both gears are 40C8 steel, σut = 600 N/mm². Take b = 10m, service factor 1.25, factor of safety 1.5 and Cv = 3/(3 + v). Find the module by the Lewis equation.

  1. Same material, so the pinion is weaker: Y1 = 0.484 − 2.87/20 = 0.3405 (gear: 0.4362). σb = 600/3 = 200 N/mm².
  2. In terms of m (mm): d1 = 20m, v = π(0.020m)(1200)/60 = 1.2566m m/s, Ft = 10 000/(1.2566m) = 7957.7/m N, Sb = 200 × 10m × m × 0.3405 = 681m² N.
  3. Trial m = 3.5 mm: v = 4.398 m/s, Ft = 2274 N, Cv = 3/7.398 = 0.4055, Feff = 1.25 × 2274/0.4055 = 7009 N, fs Feff = 10 513 N; Sb = 681 × 3.5² = 8342 N. Too weak.
  4. Trial m = 4 mm: v = 5.027 m/s, Ft = 1989 N, Cv = 3/8.027 = 0.3738, Feff = 1.25 × 1989/0.3738 = 6653 N, fs Feff = 9980 N; Sb = 681 × 16 = 10 896 N. Strong enough (solving exactly gives m = 3.86 mm).
  5. So m = 4 mm, b = 40 mm, d1 = 80 mm, d2 = 240 mm, a = 160 mm.

Answer. m = 4 mm (standard), b = 40 mm; the exact minimum is 3.86 mm.

Worked example 2

Question. Check the pair of the first example (m = 4 mm, b = 40 mm, 300 BHN surfaces) by Buckingham. The sum of tooth errors is e = 0.015 mm and C = 11 400 N/mm². Is the design safe in bending and in wear? If not, what surface hardness is needed?

  1. From the first example: v = 5.027 m/s, Ft = 1989 N, Sb = 10 896 N.
  2. Ceb = 11 400 × 0.015 × 40 = 6840 N; Ceb + Ft = 8829 N; √8829 = 93.97; 21v = 105.56.
  3. Fd = 105.56 × 8829/(105.56 + 93.97) = 931 990/199.53 = 4671 N.
  4. Feff = Cs Ft + Fd = 1.25 × 1989 + 4671 = 7158 N.
  5. Bending: Sb/Feff = 10 896/7158 = 1.52 ≥ 1.5. Safe.
  6. Wear: Q = 2 × 60/80 = 1.5, K = 0.16 × 3² = 1.44 N/mm², Sw = 40 × 1.5 × 80 × 1.44 = 6912 N. Sw/Feff = 0.97: the flanks would pit.
  7. Needed: Sw = 1.5 × 7158 = 10 737 N, so K = 10 737/(40 × 1.5 × 80) = 2.24 N/mm² and BHN = 100√(2.24/0.16) = 374. Specify 380 BHN: K = 2.31, Sw = 11 090 N, Sw/Feff = 1.55. Safe.

Answer. Bending is safe (factor 1.52) but wear governs: at 300 BHN the wear factor is only 0.97, so harden the flanks to about 380 BHN (factor 1.55).

Exam traps

  • The beam strength σb b m Y is a capacity, not the applied load. Compare it with fs times the effective load.
  • Putting d1 in mm into v = πd1n/60: v comes out 1000 times too large.
  • Mixing the two Lewis forms: Y (about 0.3) goes with σb b m Y; y = Y/π (about 0.1) goes with σb b πm y.
  • Using Cv and Buckingham's Fd together: Cv is for the first sizing only; then Feff = Cs Ft + Fd.
  • Checking only bending. In many designs wear governs, as in the second example; the cure is surface hardness, not a larger module.

7.4 Helical gear design

A helical gear has its teeth cut on a slant to the shaft axis. Contact begins at one end of a tooth and spreads across the face, so several teeth share the load and each engages gently: the drive is quieter and stronger than a spur pair of the same size. The same slant pushes the shaft along its axis, and that axial thrust changes the shaft and bearing design. Helical pairs are the default in vehicle gearboxes, electric-vehicle reducers and industrial speed reducers.

You need: the Lewis and Buckingham checks of spur gear design.

Helix angle and the two modules

The teeth lie at the helix angle ψ to the shaft axis; 15° to 25° is usual (some syllabi write β). Two planes matter. The normal plane is perpendicular to the tooth; the transverse plane is perpendicular to the shaft. The hob cuts the tooth in the normal plane, so the normal module mn and the normal pressure angle φn are the standard values. In the transverse plane:

mt = mn / cos ψ, d = mt z = mn z / cos ψ mt is the transverse module in mm and d the pitch diameter in mm. The pressure angles are related by tan φn = tan φt cos ψ. The axial pitch is pa = πmn/sin ψ.

Gears on parallel shafts must have opposite hands: one right-hand, one left-hand. The centre distance is a = mn(z1 + z2)/(2 cos ψ), so a small change of ψ lets the designer hit a round centre distance without changing the module.

Virtual teeth and the strength checks

Cut a helical tooth by the normal plane. The plane meets the pitch cylinder in an ellipse whose radius of curvature at the tooth is r/cos²ψ, larger than the pitch radius r. In that plane the tooth looks like the tooth of a bigger spur gear with the virtual (formative) number of teeth zv = z/cos³ψ. The Lewis form factor is read for zv, and the tooth section is measured with mn:

Sb = σb b mn Y (Y for zv) Sb is the beam strength in N, b the face width in mm (often 10mn to 15mn). For the first sizing, helical teeth engage more gently than spur teeth, so the velocity factor is larger: Cv = 5.6/(5.6 + √v), and Feff = Cs Ft/Cv.

Buckingham's dynamic load and the wear strength take the helix angle into account:

Fd = 21v(Ceb cos²ψ + Ft) cos ψ / (21v + √(Ceb cos²ψ + Ft)) with the symbols of the spur gear check; then Feff = Cs Ft + Fd.

Sw = b Q d1 K / cos²ψ

with Q = 2z2/(z1 + z2) and K = 0.16(BHN/100)² for a steel pair. Both checks need a factor of safety, Sb ≥ fs Feff and Sw ≥ fs Feff.

The overlap ratio b sin ψ/(πmn) counts how many axial pitches fit in the face width. Above 1, there is always a second tooth in contact along the face, which is where the quiet running comes from.

Forces, thrust and bearings

The tooth force has three components. With Ft = P/v = 2T/d:

Fr = Ft tan φn / cos ψ, Fa = Ft tan ψ Fr is the radial (separating) force and Fa the axial thrust, both in N.

The thrust pushes the shaft along its axis, so one bearing on each shaft must be able to take it (an angular contact ball or taper roller bearing); the other is left free to float. Reversing the rotation, or the hand, reverses the thrust. A double helical (herringbone) gear has two opposite helices on one body: the two thrusts cancel inside the gear and the bearings see almost none, so ψ can be 30° to 45°; it costs more to make. Choosing ψ is a trade: a larger angle gives smoother contact and more overlap but more thrust. The bearing loads go to the selection of gearbox bearings and of rolling contact bearings.

7.5 Straight bevel gear design

A bevel gear pair joins shafts whose axes meet, usually at 90°. The teeth are cut on cones, so a tooth is large at the outer end and shrinks towards the cone apex. Design treats each bevel gear as an equivalent spur gear drawn on its back cone, applies the Lewis and Buckingham checks with a correction for the taper, and takes the tooth load at the middle of the face. Straight bevel gears drive hand drills, differentials and right-angle drives.

You need: the Lewis and Buckingham checks of spur gear design.

Nomenclature on the pitch cone

The two pitch cones roll on each other without slip and share one apex. The pitch angle γ is the half-angle of a pitch cone, measured from the gear's own axis; γ1 for the pinion, γ2 for the gear. The shaft angle Σ = γ1 + γ2. For Σ = 90°, tan γ1 = z1/z2 and γ2 = 90° − γ1. Some syllabi write δ for the pitch angle.

Sizes are given at the large end. The module m, the pitch diameters d = mz, the addendum and the dedendum are all measured there. The cone distance R (some books write A0) is the slant length from the apex to the large end of the pitch cone: R = d1/(2 sin γ1), which for 90° shafts is √((d1/2)² + (d2/2)²). The face width b is measured along the cone; keep b ≤ R/3 (or about 10m, whichever is smaller), because the tooth tapers to nothing at the apex and a wide face only adds weak, small-end tooth.

The back cone is the cone at the large end whose surface is at right angles to the pitch cone. Unrolled flat, it shows the teeth as spur teeth on a circle of radius r/cos γ. That equivalent spur gear has the virtual (formative) number of teeth zv = z/cos γ. This is the Tredgold approximation; Y is read for zv.

Strength, dynamic load and wear

The tooth force acts near the middle of the face. The mean radius of the pinion is rm1 = d1/2 − (b sin γ1)/2, and the tangential force is Ft = T/rm1 with T in N·mm. Because the tooth shrinks towards the apex, the beam strength of the large-end tooth is reduced by the factor (1 − b/R):

Sb = σb b m Y (1 − b/R)

Sb is in N; σb in N/mm²; b, m and R in mm; Y for zv. The first sizing uses Cv = 3/(3 + v) and Feff = Cs Ft/Cv, with v the pitch line velocity at the large end.

Buckingham's dynamic load is the spur gear formula, Fd = 21v(Ceb + Ft)/(21v + √(Ceb + Ft)), and Feff = Cs Ft + Fd. The wear strength uses the virtual gears and a factor 0.75 for the shorter, tapering contact:

Sw = 0.75 b Q′ d1 K / cos γ1 with Q′ = 2zv2/(zv1 + zv2), d1 the large-end pinion diameter in mm and K = 0.16(BHN/100)² for steel. Both checks need S ≥ fs Feff.

Straight bevel teeth also push the shafts apart and along their axes. For pressure angle φ, the pinion carries an axial thrust Fa1 = Ft tan φ sin γ1 and a radial force Fr1 = Ft tan φ cos γ1. For a 90° pair the pinion's axial force is the gear's radial force and the other way round. The pinion's thrust always points away from the cone apex, towards its large end.

Design steps

  1. Fix the ratio and choose z1 so that zv1 is above the undercut limit; find γ1, γ2 and zv1, zv2.
  2. Choose the weaker member from σb Y; size the module with Sb = fs Cs Ft/Cv, using b = R/3 or 10m, and round up to a standard module.
  3. With the module fixed, find d1, d2, R, b ≤ R/3, rm1 and Ft.
  4. Find Fd and Feff = Cs Ft + Fd; check Sb and Sw against fs Feff.
  5. Find Fa and Fr on each shaft and pass them to the bearing selection.

Spiral bevel and hypoid gears (curved teeth, offset axes) follow their own standards.

7.6 Worm and worm gear design

A worm drive is a screw, the worm, turning a gear, the worm wheel, on a shaft at right angles to it. One stage gives a large reduction, 10:1 to 80:1, because the worm has only one to four threads (starts) while the wheel has many teeth. Contact is mostly sliding, so friction, wear and heat set the size more than tooth bending does. Worm drives turn lift winches, conveyor reducers, steering boxes and indexing tables.

You need: the Lewis equation of spur gear design, the steel-bronze pair of gear materials, and the efficiency of a power screw.

Geometry: the worm sets the size

Let the worm have z1 starts and the wheel z2 teeth. The speed ratio is i = n1/n2 = z2/z1. The worm's axial pitch pa equals the wheel's circular pitch, πm, where m is the module (axial module of the worm, transverse module of the wheel). The lead is the distance the thread advances in one turn: L = z1 pa = z1πm.

The worm's pitch diameter is not m z1. It is set by the diametral quotient q (some syllabi call it the worm factor): d1 = qm, with q usually 8 to 12. The wheel diameter is d2 = m z2 and the centre distance a = (d1 + d2)/2. The lead angle λ is the angle between the thread and a plane normal to the worm axis, measured on the pitch cylinder:

tan λ = L/(πd1) = z1/q

So a small q gives a slender worm and a large lead angle; a large q gives a thick, stiff worm and a small lead angle. Adding starts raises the lead and the lead angle.

Sliding, efficiency and self-locking

The worm's pitch line velocity is v1 = πd1n_1/60 (d1 in m). The thread slides along the wheel tooth at the rubbing velocity vs = v1/cos λ, larger than v1. Friction is written as a coefficient μ, or as a friction angle φ = tan⁻¹μ; μ falls as vs rises and is about 0.02 to 0.05 for a well-lubricated steel worm on a bronze wheel.

η = (cos αn − μ tan λ)/(cos αn + μ cot λ) η is the efficiency with the worm driving and αn the normal pressure angle (often 20°). If αn is ignored, as many books do, it becomes η = tan λ/tan(λ + φ), the efficiency of a square-threaded screw.

The drive is self-locking when the wheel cannot turn the worm, roughly when λ < φ (more exactly when μ > cos αn tan λ). A self-locking hoist holds its load without a brake, but its forward efficiency is then below 50 %, so much of the power becomes heat. The worm is case-hardened and ground steel; the wheel is phosphor bronze, because the pair must slide without seizing.

Forces, strength and heat

The worm's tangential force Ft1 = 2T1/d1 equals the axial thrust on the wheel. The wheel's tangential force Ft2 = 2T2/d2 equals the axial thrust on the worm. The two are related through λ and μ: Ft2 = Ft1 (cos αn cos λ − μ sin λ)/(cos αn sin λ + μ cos λ). The separating force is Fr = Ft1 sin αn/(cos αn sin λ + μ cos λ).

  • Bending: the bronze wheel tooth is the weaker. A common textbook form is Sb = σb b mn Y, with mn = m cos λ, Y read for z2, and a velocity factor Cv = 6/(6 + v2) on the wheel's pitch line velocity v2; check Sb ≥ Ft2/Cv.
  • Wear: the contact stress of the steel-bronze pair limits the face width; data books give a limiting wear load in terms of d2, b and a wear factor for the pair and lead angle.
  • Heat: the power lost is (1 − η)P. In steady running it must leave through the housing: (1 − η)P = k A (t − t0), where k is the heat transfer coefficient in W/(m²K), A the housing area in m², t the oil temperature and t0 the air temperature. Oil should stay below about 80 °C; a fan on the worm shaft roughly doubles k.

A small worm box often has strong teeth and still overheats, so for continuous duty the heat check usually decides the catalogue power. The same heat balance is used for whole gearboxes in gearbox design.

7.7 Gear trains, including epicyclic gear trains

A gear train is several gear pairs working one after another. Each pair changes speed and torque by its tooth ratio, and the train multiplies the ratios. An epicyclic train adds an arm, or carrier, that carries a gear round another, so the train has two inputs or one large reduction in a small box. Gear trains drive lathe back gears, car differentials, planetary hubs of excavators and the automatic gearbox of a car.

You need: speed ratio and centre distance from gear nomenclature.

Simple, compound and reverted trains

A simple train has one gear on each shaft. The gears in the middle are idlers: they change the direction of rotation but not the overall ratio. A compound train has at least one shaft carrying two gears fixed together, so large ratios fit in a small space. A reverted train is a compound train whose first and last shafts are in line, as in a lathe back gear or a clock.

For one external pair the driven speed is n2 = −(z1/z2) n1; the minus sign means the direction reverses. An internal pair keeps the direction. For the whole train:

nlast/nfirst = ± (product of driver teeth)/(product of driven teeth) The ratio is called the train value; its inverse is the speed ratio i. Each external mesh reverses the direction, so count them for the sign.

Example: drivers of 20 and 30 teeth, driven gears of 40 and 60 teeth. Train value = (20 × 30)/(40 × 60) = 1/4, so the output turns at a quarter of the input speed; two external meshes leave the direction unchanged. In a reverted train with one module the centre distances must match: z1 + z2 = z3 + z4. With two different modules the condition is m1(z1 + z2) = m2(z3 + z4).

Epicyclic trains and the tabular method

A simple epicyclic train has a sun S on the central axis, planets P that mesh with the sun and with an internal ring (annulus) R, and an arm or carrier A that holds the planet pins and turns about the central axis. For the planets to fit, zR = zS + 2zP. Several planets share the load; equal spacing needs (zS + zR)/(number of planets) to be a whole number. Some syllabi call this a planetary train.

The train has two degrees of freedom. Fix one member and drive a second, and the third has one definite speed; lock any two members together and the whole train turns as one solid block. The planets both spin and orbit, so the plain tooth ratio does not work. The tabular method handles it in two steps: first hold the arm and turn the sun x revolutions; then turn everything, arm included, by y revolutions. Counter-clockwise is taken as positive.

Torques and uses

If friction is ignored, the three external torques on the train balance and the power balances: TS + TR + TA = 0 and TS nS + TR nR + TA nA = 0. A fixed member turns at zero speed, so it does no work, but it carries a holding torque. This torque is what a brake band or clutch in an automatic gearbox must hold.

In a car differential the crown wheel drives the cage (the arm) and bevel planets drive the two half shafts. On a straight road the planets do not spin on their pins and both wheels turn at the cage speed; in a turn the inner wheel slows and the outer speeds up, and their average stays equal to the cage speed. In a planetary hub the ring is fixed, the sun is driven and the arm is the output, which gives 3:1 to 10:1 in one stage inside a wheel. A lathe back gear is reverted, not epicyclic. Automatic gearboxes use clutches and brakes to hold different members of planetary sets and so select ratios; the stepped gearboxes of machine tools are taken up in multi-speed gearboxes.

Exam traps

  • Using the plain tooth ratio for an epicyclic train. Hold the arm first, then add the arm motion to every member.
  • Treating every mesh as a reversal. External meshes reverse; a planet and an internal ring turn the same way.
  • Confusing a planet's absolute speed with its spin on its pin: spin = absolute speed − arm speed.
  • Saying that fixing two members drives the third. Fixing (or locking) two members locks the whole train; fix one and drive one.
  • Forgetting the holding torque on the fixed member. It carries torque but no power; the three torques add to zero.

7.8 Gear life and gearbox design

A gear tooth does not see one fixed load all its life. It sees a load spectrum: full load for some hours, part load for others, a shock at each start. The tooth collects fatigue damage cycle by cycle, so gear life design turns the spectrum into one equivalent load, sizes the gears for it, and then checks the rest of the gearbox: ratio split, shafts, bearings, heat and lubrication. The same method sizes the reducer of a textile mill, a crane hoist or an electric-vehicle drive.

You need: the S-N curve and endurance limit from fatigue design, and the checks of spur gear design.

Life in cycles, not hours

Gear life is counted in load cycles on one tooth. A gear turning at n rpm for Lh hours meets the load N = 60 n Lh times; a gear at 1200 rpm for 8000 h sees 5.76 × 10⁸ cycles. Because the pinion turns faster, its teeth collect more cycles than the gear's, which is the reason for the harder pinion in gear materials.

The S-N curve plots the alternating stress against the cycles to failure. On log-log axes it falls as a straight line, which can be written Fk N = constant for a load F, with k the life exponent; for many steels it flattens at the endurance limit. Bending fatigue starts at the root fillet and contact fatigue (pitting) at the flank, so each has its own curve and exponent. For contact the load-life exponent is often taken as 3, because the contact stress grows only with the square root of the load.

Miner's rule adds the damage of each load level: D = Σ ni/Ni, where ni is the number of cycles spent at level i and Ni the cycles to failure at that level from the S-N curve. Failure is predicted when D reaches 1; designers keep D below 1 for reliability.

Equivalent load and service factor

Let a load spectrum give the power Pi (or torque Ti) at each duty point and the fraction pi of the life spent there, at a constant speed. Miner's rule with the life exponent k gives the steady load that does the same damage:

Peq = (Σ pi Pik)1/k Peq is the equivalent power in kW; the fractions pi add to 1; k = 3 for contact fatigue. For a torque spectrum put Ti in place of Pi. If the speed changes between duty points, use cycle fractions ni/Σni instead of time fractions.

For bending the exponent is larger, so do not reuse the contact Peq for a bending check. The design load is Pdesign = Cs Peq, where the service factor Cs allows for the driver and the machine (shock, starting, reversing). Two easy mistakes go in opposite directions: the maximum load for the whole life over-sizes the gears, and the simple average under-sizes them, because damage grows with the cube of the contact load.

Gearbox layout, heat and lubrication

One stage of spur or helical gears suits ratios up to about 5 or 6; beyond that the pinion becomes too small or the gear too large. A two-stage box splits the ratio, i = i1 i2. An equal split, i1 = i2 = √i, keeps the stages similar; many designers give the first (fast) stage a slightly larger ratio so that the two gear wheels dip equally into the oil. The second stage carries i1 times the torque of the first, so its gears are larger.

The housing holds the bearing bores in line, holds the oil and passes heat to the air; ribs stiffen it. At moderate speed the larger gears dip into the oil and splash it on to the teeth and bearings; at high speed a pump sprays oil on the mesh and a cooler may be added. Seals keep oil in and dust out, and a breather stops pressure building up. In steady running the heat made equals the heat lost:

Pin (1 − η) = k A (t − t0) Pin is the input power in W, η the overall efficiency (about 0.98 per spur or helical stage), k the heat transfer coefficient in W/(m²K), A the housing area in m², t the oil temperature and t0 the air temperature.

A gearbox can fail first on bearing life, oil temperature or a leaking seal, not on its teeth, so each gets its own check; bearings are selected from their load ratings and life in the unit on bearings.

7.9 Multi-speed gearboxes and ray diagrams

A multi-speed gearbox turns one input speed into a set of stepped output speeds. It places gear groups in series, and the number of speeds is the product of the number of ratios in each group. A lathe, drilling machine or milling machine needs these steps because the best spindle speed changes with the job diameter and the material. The speeds follow a geometric progression, and a ray diagram, drawn on a log scale, shows every ratio and every speed of the design at a glance, so the designer can check it before fixing tooth numbers.

You need: speed ratios of gear pairs and compound trains from gear trains.

Speed range and geometric steps

Let the spindle have z speeds from nmin to nmax. The speed range is a ratio, Rn = nmax/nmin. Spaced geometrically, each speed is the one below it times the step ratio φ (progression ratio):

φ = (nmax/nmin)1/(z − 1) There are z − 1 steps between z speeds; φ has no unit. Then n2 = n1φ, n3 = n1φ², and so on.

Geometric steps give the same percentage change everywhere. With arithmetic steps of 50 rpm, going from 100 to 150 rpm is a 50 % jump but 500 to 550 rpm only 10 %, so the machine would be coarse at low speeds and needlessly fine at high speeds. The cutting speed v = πDn/1000 (m/min, D in mm) is what the job needs, and a geometric series loses the same fraction of it at every step.

Standard step ratios come from the preferred number series: 1.06, 1.12, 1.26, 1.41, 1.58 and 2.0 (R40, R20, R10, R20/3, R5 and R10/3 series); 1.26 and 1.41 are the most common in machine tools. The standard speeds are the rounded series values, for example 100, 140, 200, 280, 400, 560 rpm for φ = 1.41. If the calculated φ falls between two standard values, take the nearer one and adjust nmax.

Structure formula and ray diagram

The structure formula writes the number of speeds as a product, z = p1 × p2 × …, where p1 is the number of ratios in the first group (nearest the motor), p2 in the second, and so on. Each group also has a characteristic x, the number of steps of φ between neighbouring ratios of that group, written in brackets: 6 = 3(1) 2(3) means a first group of three ratios one step apart and a second group of two ratios three steps apart. The first group with x = 1 is the basic group; the next has x equal to the number of speeds produced before it.

A ray diagram (speed chart) draws one vertical line per shaft and one horizontal line per standard speed, spaced equally because the scale is logarithmic in φ. A gear pair is a ray from its input speed on one shaft to its output speed on the next. A horizontal ray is a 1:1 pair; a ray falling by j speed lines has the ratio 1/φj. Rays of one group from one point fan out, with neighbouring rays x speed lines apart; rays of the same pair drawn from different points are parallel, because they are the same ratio.

Rules that keep the gears sensible: in any group the reduction should be no more than 1/4 and the step-up no more than 2 (so a group's ratios span at most 8); keep each shaft as fast as possible for as long as possible, so put the big reductions in the last group, where the torque is largest anyway; and give the groups near the motor the most ratios (p1 ≥ p2 ≥ …) so that the slower, heavier shafts carry fewer gears. The diagram must give every output speed once, with no two rays landing on the same speed.

From the ray diagram to the gearbox

Within one group all pairs share the same two shafts, so they must have the same centre distance: with one module, zdriver + zdriven is the same for every pair of the group. The tooth numbers come from the ratios and a chosen sum, with no gear below about 17 teeth. The actual speeds may differ a little from the standard ones; a deviation within ±10(φ − 1) % is accepted, ±4.1 % for φ = 1.41.

The gearing (kinematic) diagram is the side view of the same design: shafts, the gears on each, and which pairs mesh for each speed. Speeds are selected by sliding gear clusters on splined shafts, cheap but shifted only when stopped, by dog clutches, or by friction clutches that can shift under load. A Norton gearbox, used for feed and thread-cutting drives, has a cone of gears on one shaft and a swinging tumbler gear that meshes with any one of them, giving many ratios in a small space. A car gearbox uses the same idea with a few widely spaced ratios.

Takeaways

  1. A gear pair runs at a truly constant ratio because the involute keeps the common normal fixed through the pitch point; the contact ratio says how many teeth share the load.
  2. A gear material is a package: a tough core for root bending and a hard surface for pitting, with the pinion harder because it sees ratio-times more cycles.
  3. Lewis sizes the module (Sb = σb b m Y with a velocity factor); Buckingham then checks the same tooth for dynamic load and wear, and either check can govern.
  4. Helical gears are designed on the normal section with zv = z/cos³ψ; they run quieter and carry more, but push the shaft with Ft tan ψ.
  5. Bevel teeth are designed as spur teeth on the back cone (zv = z/cos γ), with the load at the mean radius and the taper factor (1 − b/R).
  6. In a worm drive the lead angle sets the efficiency; friction heat, not tooth strength, usually limits a worm box.
  7. Epicyclic trains are solved by holding the arm, then adding the arm's motion; a fixed member carries a holding torque but no power.
  8. Gear life is counted in cycles; a load spectrum becomes one equivalent load through Miner's rule, and the gearbox is then checked for ratio split, bearings and heat.
  9. A multi-speed gearbox multiplies group ratios; the ray diagram shows every speed and ratio, and tooth numbers follow from equal tooth sums in each group.

Understand State the law of gearing. With a neat sketch of two involute teeth in contact, show that the common normal at the point of contact always passes through the pitch point, and hence that the angular velocity ratio stays constant throughout the mesh. 13 marks

Points to cover. Statement: the common normal at the point of contact must pass through a fixed pitch point P on the line of centres; Proof: velocities along the common normal are equal, similar triangles give ω1/ω2 = O2P/O1P; Involute property: its normal is tangent to the base circle; For two involutes the common normal is the fixed common tangent of the base circles, so P is fixed and the ratio constant; Bonus: ratio unchanged by a small change of centre distance.

Model answer.

  1. Statement. For two gears to turn at a constant angular velocity ratio, the common normal to the tooth profiles at the point of contact must always pass through a fixed point P on the line of centres O1O_{2}. P is the pitch point, where the two pitch circles touch.
  2. Sketch. Draw centres O1 (pinion) and O2 (gear), the line of centres, the two pitch circles touching at P, the two base circles, and two involute flanks touching at a point C. Through C draw the common tangent and the common normal N1N_{2}; mark the feet of the perpendiculars from O1 and O2 on the normal as N1 and N2.
    O1 ●
       \  N1
        \ /
   ------P------  common tangent of the pitch circles
        / \
      N2   (line N1-C-N2 is the common normal)
O2 ●
  3. Velocities at C. As part of the pinion, C moves with v1 = ω1 · O1C, perpendicular to O1C. As part of the gear, it moves with v2 = ω2 · O2C, perpendicular to O2C. The two profiles stay in contact without digging into each other or separating, so their velocity components along the common normal must be equal: v1 cos α1 = v2 cos α2, where α1 and α2 are the angles between each velocity and the normal.
  4. Turning that into the ratio. v1 cos α1 = ω1 · O1C cos α1 = ω1 · O1N_1, because O1N_1 is the projection of O1C perpendicular to the normal. In the same way v2 cos α2 = ω2 · O2N_{2}. Hence ω1 · O1N_1 = ω2 · O2N_2 and ω1/ω2 = O2N_2/O1N_{1}.
  5. Similar triangles. Triangles O1N_1P and O2N_2P are similar (right angles at N1 and N2, equal vertical angles at P), so O2N_2/O1N_1 = O2P/O1P. Therefore ω1/ω2 = O2P/O1P. The ratio is constant only if P is a fixed point, which is the law of gearing.
  6. The involute satisfies it. An involute is traced by the end of a taut string unwound from the base circle, so its normal at any point is the string itself, tangent to the base circle. At C the normal to the pinion flank is tangent to the pinion base circle and the normal to the gear flank is tangent to the gear base circle. Both are the same line, so the common normal is the common internal tangent N1N_2 of the two base circles.
  7. The normal is fixed in space. The base circles do not move as the gears turn, so their common tangent is the same line at every instant. Contact moves along this line (the line of action), and the line always cuts O1O_2 at the same point P. Hence ω1/ω2 = O2P/O1P = r2/r1 = rb2/rb1 = z2/z1 at every position of the mesh.
  8. Check with numbers. For the pair z1 = 20, z2 = 60, m = 4 mm: rb1 = 37.59 mm and rb2 = 112.76 mm, and 112.76/37.59 = 3.00 = 60/20. If the centre distance is opened from 160 to 160.5 mm, the base radii do not change, so the ratio stays 3.00; only the working pressure angle rises (cos φ′ = 160 cos 20°/160.5, φ′ = 20.5°) and the backlash grows. This tolerance is why involute teeth are standard.
Remember Define module, circular pitch, diametral pitch, pressure angle, addendum, dedendum, backlash and contact ratio for a spur gear pair. Give the relations between module, circular pitch and number of teeth, and state the standard pressure angle. 8 marks

Points to cover. m = d/z in mm; p = πm; P = z/d (inch), m = 25.4/P; Pressure angle between line of action and common tangent at P; 20° standard; Addendum m, dedendum 1.25m for 20° full depth; Backlash: gap between non-driving flanks; Contact ratio = path of contact/base pitch, above 1.

Model answer.

  1. Module m = d/z, the pitch circle diameter per tooth, in mm (d in mm, z the number of teeth). Standard first-choice modules are 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10 mm.
  2. Circular pitch p: the distance from a point on one tooth to the same point on the next, measured along the pitch circle: p = πd/z = πm. For m = 4 mm, p = 12.57 mm.
  3. Diametral pitch P: the number of teeth per inch of pitch diameter, P = z/d with d in inches. It is the inch-system size index, related to the module by m = 25.4/P; a 10-pitch gear has m = 2.54 mm. Note p × P = π.
  4. Pressure angle φ: the angle between the line of action (the common normal to the teeth, tangent to both base circles) and the common tangent to the pitch circles at the pitch point. 20° is the standard; 14½° is old practice and 25° is used for some heavy-duty gears. The base circle diameter is db = d cos φ.
  5. Addendum ha: the radial height of the tooth above the pitch circle, m for standard 20° full-depth teeth, so the tip (addendum) circle is da = d + 2m. Dedendum hf: the radial depth below the pitch circle, 1.25m, so the root circle is df = d − 2.5m. The extra 0.25m is the clearance between the tip of one gear and the root of the other.
  6. Backlash: the gap between the non-driving flanks of a meshing pair, measured on the pitch circle. It is made by thinning the teeth slightly so the pair does not jam through heat growth, errors and oil film; too much backlash gives knocking when the load reverses.
  7. Contact ratio: the average number of tooth pairs in contact, equal to the path of contact divided by the base pitch πm cos φ (or the arc of contact divided by p). It must exceed 1 for smooth transfer of load; 1.4 to 1.8 is usual for spur gears.

Understand A gear material must supply core strength, surface hardness and machinability at acceptable cost. Discuss how through-hardened steel, case-carburised steel, cast iron and phosphor bronze meet these needs, and explain why the pinion is made harder than the gear. 10 marks

Points to cover. Core strength resists root bending; surface hardness resists pitting; Through-hardened carbon and alloy steels: simple, 250–350 BHN; Case carburising: hard case, tough core, grinding needed; Cast iron: cheap, quiet, weak in tension and shock; bronze for worm wheels; Pinion harder because it sees ratio-times more cycles; K = 0.16(BHN/100)².

Model answer.

  1. The three needs. Bending fatigue starts at the root fillet, where the whole tooth section carries the load, so the core must be strong and tough. Pitting starts on the flanks under repeated contact stress, so the surface must be hard. The gear must also be machinable and keep its shape through heat treatment, at a cost that suits the duty.
  2. Through-hardened steels (C45, 40C8, 40Cr1, 42CrMo4). The whole gear is hardened and tempered to 250 to 350 BHN. Core and surface have the same hardness, so the tooth is tough and the process is cheap, and the gear can be cut after treatment. Above about 350 BHN cutting becomes hard and distortion grows, so the surface hardness, and with it the wear capacity, is limited. Typical allowable bending stress σb = σut/3, about 200 N/mm² for σut = 600 N/mm².
  3. Case-carburised steels (20MnCr5, 15Ni4Cr1). Carbon is diffused 0.8 to 1.5 mm into a low-carbon alloy steel at about 900 to 930 °C, then the gear is quenched and tempered: case 58 to 62 HRC, core 30 to 40 HRC. This gives the best of both needs, a very hard flank and a tough core, so it is used for heavy load, shock and high speed (vehicle gearboxes). The price is distortion: the teeth must be ground after hardening, which adds cost. Nitriding and induction hardening are other ways to harden only the surface.
  4. Grey cast iron (FG 200, FG 260). Cheap, casts to shape, machines well and damps noise, and its graphite helps it run with little lubrication. Its tensile and shock strength are low, so it suits large, slow, lightly loaded wheels running with a steel pinion, never a small high-speed pinion.
  5. Phosphor bronze is used for worm wheels. The worm slides on the wheel, so the wheel must be conformable and resist seizure against a hard, ground steel worm. Non-metals (nylon, laminates) serve quiet, light drives.
  6. Why the pinion is harder. With ratio i, each pinion tooth is loaded i times as often as a gear tooth. For a 20/60 pair at 1440 rpm for 10 000 h the pinion sees 60 × 1440 × 10 000 = 8.64 × 10⁸ cycles and the gear 2.88 × 10⁸. Equal hardness would make the pinion pit first, so a through-hardened pinion is made about 30 to 50 BHN harder (for example 300 BHN pinion, 250 BHN gear).
  7. Effect on design numbers. The wear factor K = 0.16(BHN/100)² rises from 1.44 N/mm² at 300 BHN to 2.31 N/mm² at 380 BHN, a 60 % rise in wear strength for the same size. Hence the rule: if bending governs, use more module or a stronger core; if wear governs, use a harder surface.

Model answer.

Apply A machine tool spindle needs six speeds from 100 rpm to 1000 rpm in geometric progression. Choose the step ratio, the structure and the group order, draw the ray diagram, and fix the tooth numbers of each pair with one module so that the shaft centre distances are equal. 14 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Bending fatigue at the root and pitting of the flank are the two competing limits on a spur gear. Compare them in terms of where the stress acts, what resists each, and how load, speed and surface hardness decide which one governs. Use the pair m = 4 mm, b = 40 mm, z = 20/60, 10 kW at 1200 rpm to illustrate. 13 marks

Points to cover. Bending: tensile stress at the root fillet, resisted by core strength; Sb = σb b m Y; Pitting: contact stress on the flank, resisted by surface hardness; Sw = b Q d1 K; Speed raises the dynamic load for both; Numbers: bending 1.52, wear 0.97 at 300 BHN, 1.55 at 380 BHN; Design response for each.

Model answer.

  1. Where the stress acts. Bending: the tooth is a cantilever and the largest tensile stress is at the root fillet on the loaded side; fatigue cracks start there and the tooth breaks off. Pitting: the flanks are pressed together under Hertz contact stress, highest just below the surface near the pitch line; repeated cycles start fine cracks that lift out small pits, then the flank wears and gets noisy.
  2. What resists each. Bending depends on the strength and toughness of the core and on tooth size: Sb = σb b m Y grows with the module. Pitting depends on surface hardness and on the radii of curvature: Sw = b Q d1 K, with K = 0.16(BHN/100)², grows with the pinion diameter and the square of the hardness, but not with the module directly.
  3. Effect of load and speed. Both checks are made against Feff = Cs Ft + Fd. Higher speed raises Buckingham's Fd for both. Higher torque raises Ft for both. Contact damage grows with about the cube of the load, so a load spectrum shifts the balance towards pitting.
  4. Illustration. For the pair (v = 5.027 m/s, Ft = 1989 N, e = 0.015 mm, Cs = 1.25): Fd = 4671 N and Feff = 7158 N.
    CheckStrength (N)Factor of safety
    Bending, Sb = 200 × 40 × 4 × 0.340510 8961.52
    Wear at 300 BHN, Sw = 40 × 1.5 × 80 × 1.4469120.97
    Wear at 380 BHN, K = 2.3111 0901.55
  5. Reading it. At 300 BHN the root is safe but the flanks would pit: wear governs. Raising the surface hardness to 380 BHN multiplies K by 1.6 and makes wear safe without touching the module. If instead bending had failed, a larger module or wider face, or a tougher core, would be the cure; a harder case does little for the root.
  6. How the balance moves. Case hardening (58 to 62 HRC) raises K so much that bending becomes the limit; then the designer raises the module or shot-peens the root. Through-hardened gears at high load usually stay wear-limited. Small, fine-pitch pinions with many teeth are bending-limited; large, slow, heavily loaded gears are wear-limited.
  7. Conclusion. Check both every time, report the smaller factor of safety, and cure each limit with its own remedy: module and core for bending, hardness and diameter for pitting.
Evaluate A crane gearbox runs at constant speed and carries 20 kW for 10 % of its life and 8 kW for 90 %. Using Miner's rule with a contact life exponent of 3, find the equivalent power. Evaluate what happens to the equivalent power and to the life of the same gears if the heavy lifts become 20 % of the time. 12 marks

Points to cover. Fk N = constant; Miner D = Σ ni/Ni; Peq = (Σ pi Pi³)1/3; Numbers for 10 % and 20 %; Life ∝ Peq−3; Design response.

Model answer.

  1. Basis. Along the sloping part of the S-N curve, Pk N = constant, with k = 3 for contact fatigue. Miner's rule adds the damage fractions D = Σ ni/Ni and predicts failure at D = 1.
  2. Equivalent load. At constant speed the cycle fractions equal the time fractions pi. A steady Peq for the same total cycles gives the same damage when Peq³ = Σ pi Pi³, so Peq = (Σ pi Pi³)1/3.

Evaluate A conveyor needs a 20:1 reduction from a 10 kW, 1440 rpm motor in two spur stages. Discuss how you would split the ratio, choose materials, carry the torques forward, check the heat for a 1.0 m² housing (k = 15 W/(m²K), air at 35 °C), and use a speed chart for a two-speed version whose second output speed is one standard step above 72 rpm. 16 marks

Points to cover. Split: equal √20 = 4.47 or a slightly larger first stage; Tooth numbers and speeds; Torques with 98 % per stage; Materials per stage; Bearings and heat; Two-speed version: change gears in stage 1 and the speed chart.

Model answer.

  1. Ratio split. i = 1440/72 = 20. An equal split, √20 = 4.47, makes the two stages similar; a slightly larger first-stage ratio keeps the two large wheels a similar size so both dip in the oil. Choose stage 1 = 20/90 (4.5) and stage 2 = 18/80 (4.444): overall 20.0, shafts at 1440, 320 and 72 rpm.

Evaluate Compare an epicyclic gear train with a sliding-gear train for the spindle drive of a machine tool. Cover the speeds obtainable, the number of gear pairs needed, compactness, efficiency and manufacture, and state which you would choose for six spindle speeds from 100 to 560 rpm. 13 marks

Points to cover. Sliding-gear box: geometric steps from groups in series, one pair per speed path; Epicyclic: large ratio in a small coaxial box, ratios by holding members with clutches or brakes; Pairs and compactness; Efficiency and manufacture; Recommendation for 6 speeds.

Model answer.

  1. Speeds. A sliding-gear box gives exactly the geometric series the designer chooses: groups in series multiply the ratios, 3 × 2 = 6 speeds. An epicyclic set gives a few fixed ratios per set, depending on which member is held: with zS = 24, zR = 60, ring fixed gives 1 + 60/24 = 3.5, sun fixed gives 1 + 24/60 = 1.4, and locking two members gives 1. Six geometric speeds need two or three sets with clutches and brakes.
  2. Number of gear pairs. For 6 = 3(1) 2(3) with φ = 1.41 the sliding box needs five pairs (36/36, 30/42, 24/48; 38/38, 20/56), ten gears on three shafts. An epicyclic solution needs a sun, three planets and a ring per set, plus clutches and brakes for each ratio.
  3. Compactness. The epicyclic set is coaxial and shares the load among three planets, so it carries more torque in a smaller diameter and is ideal where space is tight (wheel hubs, automatic gearboxes). The sliding box needs three parallel shafts and a larger housing, but the space is usually available in a machine-tool headstock.
  4. Efficiency. Each spur pair loses about 2 %; a speed path through two pairs is about 96 % efficient. An epicyclic set has more meshes in parallel plus planet bearing and clutch drag losses, which are similar at the ratios a machine tool needs.
  5. Manufacture and use. Sliding gears are ordinary spur gears, cheap to make, easy to service, but shifted only when the spindle is stopped. Epicyclic sets need accurate internal gears, careful planet spacing ((zS + zR)/number of planets a whole number) and clutches, but can change ratio under load, which matters for automatic drives.
  6. Recommendation. For six speeds from 100 to 560 rpm on a conventional machine tool, the sliding-gear box is the better choice: it gives the exact standard speeds 100, 140, 200, 280, 400, 560 with five simple pairs, costs less and is easy to repair; shifting at rest is acceptable. Choose epicyclic sets only where the drive must be coaxial and compact or must shift under load.
  7. Size check. With m = 3 mm, the epicyclic set zS = 24, zP = 18, zR = 60 gives 3.5 inside a ring of pitch diameter 180 mm, all on one axis; three planets fit because (24 + 60)/3 = 28. A single spur pair for 3.5, for example 20/70 at m = 3 mm, needs a centre distance of 135 mm and an overall width of 30 + 135 + 105 = 270 mm. The epicyclic set is about two-thirds the width and puts the output on the input axis.

Glossary

Addendum
The radial height of a tooth above the pitch circle, one module for standard teeth.
Back cone
The cone at the large end of a bevel gear whose elements are perpendicular to the pitch cone; unrolled, it shows the teeth as spur teeth.
Backlash
The gap between the non-driving flanks of meshing teeth.
Beam strength
The largest tangential load a tooth can carry in bending at the allowable stress.
Circular pitch
The arc on the pitch circle from a point on one tooth to the same point on the next, equal to π times the module.
Contact ratio
The average number of tooth pairs in contact: the path of contact divided by the base pitch.
Dedendum
The radial depth of a tooth below the pitch circle, 1.25 module for standard full-depth teeth.
Diametral quotient
The ratio of worm pitch diameter to module, q = d/m.
Dynamic load
The extra tooth load caused by tooth errors and impact at running speed, estimated by Buckingham's equation.
Epicyclic gear train
A train in which planet gears carried by an arm roll round a central sun gear, usually inside a ring gear.
Equivalent load
A single steady load that causes the same fatigue damage as a varying load over the same number of cycles.
Gear
A toothed wheel that transmits motion and torque to another by successive contact of teeth.
Helix angle
The angle a helical tooth makes with the shaft axis.
Involute
The curve traced by the end of a taut string unwound from a base circle; its normal is always tangent to the base circle.
Lewis form factor
A number that depends on the tooth form and number of teeth and gives the bending strength of the tooth treated as a cantilever.
Module
The pitch circle diameter in mm divided by the number of teeth; the size index of gear teeth.
Pinion
The smaller gear of a meshing pair, usually the driver.
Pitch circle
The imaginary circle on which two meshing gears roll without slipping; the ratio and the module are based on it.
Pitting
Fatigue failure of the tooth surface, seen as small craters on the flank.
Pressure angle
The angle between the line of action and the common tangent to the pitch circles, usually 20°.
Ray diagram
A speed chart on a log scale in which each gear pair is a straight ray between two shafts.
Self-locking
The state of a worm drive in which the wheel cannot drive the worm, roughly when the lead angle is less than the friction angle.
Service factor
A factor above 1 that allows for shock and the character of the driver and the driven machine.
Step ratio
The constant factor between consecutive spindle speeds of a geometric series.
Structure formula
The number of speeds written as a product of the number of ratios in each gear group, with each group characteristic.
Undercutting
Removal of metal at the root of a pinion with too few teeth during cutting, which weakens the tooth; it is the damage caused by interference.
Velocity factor
A factor below 1 that allows for the dynamic load of tooth errors at speed in the first sizing of a gear.
Virtual number of teeth
The tooth count of the equivalent spur gear used in strength formulas for helical and bevel gears.
Wear strength
The largest tangential load the tooth flanks can carry without pitting over a long life.
