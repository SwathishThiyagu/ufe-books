6.1 Flexible drives: types, belt materials and construction

A flexible drive carries power from one shaft to another through a belt, chain or rope that bends around pulleys or sprockets. Because the member bends, the shafts can sit far apart and need only rough alignment, which a gear pair cannot do. Belts grip by friction and allow a little slip, which soaks up shock; chains and timing belts grip by teeth or rollers and hold the speed ratio exact. Belts, chains and wire ropes run mills, conveyors, motorcycles, lifts and generator sets across Indian plants.

You need: Unit 1 for density, material properties and the factor of safety, and Unit 3 for the shaft carrying the pulley load; stress design of the belt itself follows in 6.2.

Three families, one job

A flexible drive has three parts: a driving pulley or sprocket on the input shaft, a driven one on the output shaft, and one continuous member wrapped round both. Torque enters at the driving pulley, travels along the member as tension, and leaves as torque at the driven shaft. The small pulley is often, but not always, the driver. Nothing rigid connects the shafts, so they can sit a metre or ten metres apart and be out of line by a degree or two without stopping the drive.

The members divide by how they grip. Flat, V and round belts grip by friction on the pulley and slip a little when overloaded. Timing belts have moulded teeth that mesh with grooves in the pulley; chains have rollers that seat in sprocket teeth. Neither of those slips. Wire and fibre ropes carry load only in tension and run on drums or large sheaves.

Against a gear pair the trade is clear. Gears give an exact velocity ratio in a small rigid box, but need a short centre distance, accurate alignment, continuous lubrication and a sealed casing. A flexible drive is cheap and tolerant of misalignment; belt drives are also quiet and absorb shock, while chain and timing-belt drives are noisier and pass shock through more readily. In exchange you pay in slip (belts), extra shaft bending load and floor space.

Belt materials

Leather is the oldest belt material and still the textbook reference. Hides are oak-tanned — tanned with tannin from bark — which makes them tough and slightly stiff. Standard design values: density about 1000 kg/m³, and coefficient of friction μ = 0.25 to 0.4 for leather on a cast-iron pulley. Leather takes high tensile stress and runs quietly, but it stretches with use, loses strength when wet, and needs occasional dressing with a friction compound.

Cotton canvas, woven into a heavy cloth called duck and bonded with rubber, is the workhorse of flat belting on Indian floor-mounted drives. Several plies of canvas carry the load; the rubber holds them together and gives the friction face. Balata, a latex from a tropical tree, impregnates cotton belting to make it waterproof with high, stable friction, and some imported belting still quotes a balata surface.

High-strength belts use synthetic cords. Nylon is strong and absorbs shock but stretches; polyester is stiffer and holds its length; aramid cord gives very high strength with low stretch where the belt must be narrow and fast. The cover is rubber or polyurethane, chosen for grip and resistance to oil and grit.

How a belt is built

A flat belt is a stack of layers. Tension cords carry the pull and sit as close as possible to the neutral axis — the plane inside the belt that neither stretches nor shortens when the belt bends. Above and below the cords sit compression rubber and fabric plies, with a cover on each face for friction and to keep oil out. Cords near the neutral axis see little bending stress, so the belt runs on a small pulley without fatigue.

A flat belt must be joined into a loop. A skived and cemented joint (ends cut to a taper, glued and pressed) runs smoothly and keeps most of the belt strength. Metal lacing threaded through punched holes is quick to fit but heavier and weaker, and slaps at every pulley; hinged wire hooks serve for temporary repairs. The joint is the weakest section, so the allowable tension is reduced by a joint efficiency.

V-belts are moulded endless, with a trapezoidal cross-section and a layer of tension cord just under the top face. Standard sections run A, B, C, D and E, with top widths of about 13 mm (A) up to 38 mm (E); each section covers a range of pulley diameters and powers. The groove angle in the pulley is 34° to 40°, narrower for small pulleys, because a belt bent round a small pulley bulges and needs a tighter wedge to keep full side contact.

T_c = m v², where m = ρ b t

T_c is the centrifugal tension in N, pulling on the belt even when it carries no load; m is the mass per metre length in kg/m; v is belt speed in m/s; ρ is belt material density in kg/m³; b is belt width in m; t is belt thickness in m.

Worked example

Question. A leather belt is 100 mm wide and 6 mm thick and runs at 15 m/s over a cast-iron pulley. Take the density of leather as 1000 kg/m³. Find the mass per metre length of the belt and the centrifugal tension set up in it.

  1. Convert to SI: width b = 100 mm = 0.1 m; thickness t = 6 mm = 0.006 m; speed v = 15 m/s; density ρ = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Volume of one metre of belt = b × t × 1 m = 0.1 × 0.006 × 1 = 6 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  3. Mass per metre length m = ρ b t = 1000 × 0.1 × 0.006 = 0.6 kg per metre.
  4. Centrifugal tension T_c = m v² = 0.6 × (15)² = 0.6 × 225 = 135 N.
  5. The belt carries this 135 N whether or not it transmits power. In 6.2 it is subtracted from the total tension before the friction ratio e^(μθ) is applied, so it never helps transmit torque.

Answer. Mass per metre length = 0.6 kg/m and centrifugal tension = 135 N.

Exam traps

  • Assuming a V-belt grips on its flat bottom face. It grips on the two sloping sides; the bottom must clear the groove bottom, or the wedge action is lost.
  • Forgetting that centrifugal tension grows as the square of speed: 15 m/s to 30 m/s takes T_c from 135 N to 540 N, not 270 N.
  • Using a steel-on-steel friction value. Leather on cast iron gives μ = 0.25 to 0.4; 0.1 is far too low and 0.6 far too optimistic.
  • Applying the belt-friction ratio e^(μθ) to a timing belt. It drives by tooth engagement, and its rating comes from tooth shear, not friction.
  • Ignoring the joint. The allowable tension is multiplied by a joint efficiency, and a laced joint is weaker and heavier than a cemented one.

Analyze A machine needs an exact 2:1 speed ratio over a 1.5 m centre distance at moderate power, with no lubrication service available. Choose between a flat belt, a chain and a timing belt, and justify. 5 marks

A flat belt slips, so the ratio is only approximate — reject. A chain is exact and suits 1.5 m, but needs lubrication and gets noisy at speed. A timing belt is exact, oil-free and quiet at this centre distance. Choose the timing belt; take the chain only if torque is high or the drive is hot and dirty.

Analyze A cheap V-belt is run on a pulley whose grooves have worn deeper and wider. Explain what happens to the drive. 4 marks

The belt sinks until its bottom touches the groove bottom. Contact moves from the two sloping sides to the flat bottom, the wedge factor 1/sin β is lost, and effective friction drops to about μ. The belt slips, heats and wears fast, and delivered power falls sharply.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Timing belts now drive printers, engine camshafts and e-rickshaw drives, where a wide, quiet, oil-free belt replaces a gear pair. Aramid and carbon cord belts carry torques leather could not touch. Plants check belt health by vibration measurement and infrared thermography rather than by eye.

6.2 Design of flat belt drives

A flat belt is a loop of flexible material that grips a pulley by friction over part of its rim. The driver pulls the tight side; the slack side returns. The two tensions are tied by T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ), so the belt can pull only as hard as friction allows. Design means choosing a width b so that the tight-side tension plus centrifugal tension stays below the safe strength σbt. Flat belts drive line shafts, conveyors, and small machines where shafts are far apart and some slip is acceptable.

You need 6.1 for belt materials, thickness and density, and Unit 1 for direct stress and factor of safety.

Grip and the arc of contact

A flat belt transmits power only where it touches the pulley. The driver pulls the tight side and feeds the slack side. The arc of contact is the angle over which the belt touches a pulley. The smaller pulley has the smaller arc, so it sets the friction limit.

Use d and D for the small and large pulley diameters in metres, C for the centre distance in metres, and N₁, N₂ for speeds in rpm. Ignoring slip, N₁d = N₂D. With slip fraction s, N₂ = N₁(d/D)(1 − s); s is the fraction of belt speed lost.

θd = 180° − 2 sin⁻¹((D − d)/(2C))

θd is the arc of contact on the smaller pulley of an open belt, in degrees; d and D are pulley diameters; C is the centre distance.

θd = 180° + 2 sin⁻¹((D + d)/(2C))

θd is the arc of contact on the smaller pulley of a crossed belt, in degrees; the belt crosses between the pulleys.

Convert θd to radians before using e^(μθ). Belt length for an open drive is L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)²/(4C). For a crossed drive, L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D + d)²/(4C).

Tensions, centrifugal force and power

Let T₁ be the tight-side tension and T₂ the slack-side tension that produce friction, in N. The centrifugal tension is Tc = m v², where m is the belt mass per metre in kg/m and v is belt speed in m/s. For width b in metres, thickness t in metres and density ρ in kg/m³, m = ρbt.

T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ)

μ is the coefficient of friction; θ is the arc of contact on the smaller pulley in radians; e = 2.718.

The safe tension is T = σbt, where σ is the safe stress in N/m². The tight side carries T₁ plus Tc, so T₁ + Tc ≤ T. Power is P = (T₁ − T₂)v. Initial tension is T₀ = (T₁ + T₂)/2 + Tc.

At fixed T and μθ, P = (1 − 1/e^(μθ))(T − m v²)v. Differentiate with respect to v. Maximum power occurs when Tc = m v² = T/3, so v = √(T/(3m)). For T = 1000 N and m = 0.6 kg/m, v = √(1000/(3×0.6)) = 23.6 m/s. Above this speed, Tc grows as v² and the useful grip falls.

Design steps, creep and slip

  1. Find v from the smaller pulley: v = πdN₁/60.
  2. Find θd for the smaller pulley and convert it to radians.
  3. Compute μθ and e^(μθ).
  4. Find the driving tension difference: T₁ − T₂ = P/v.
  5. Solve T₁ and T₂ using the ratio T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ).
  6. Compute m = ρbt and Tc = m v². Use b in metres here.
  7. Set T₁ + Tc = σbt and solve for b.
  8. Round b up to a standard width. Check T₀ and the pulley face width.

Creep is the stretch on the tight side and contraction on the slack side. It makes the driven pulley lag slightly even with good friction. Slip is gross sliding when T₁/T₂ exceeds e^(μθ). Both reduce the velocity ratio. A flat belt is chosen when some slip is acceptable and the shafts are far apart.

Worked example

Question. A 15 kW motor at 1440 rpm drives a line shaft at 480 rpm through an open flat belt. The pulley diameters are 200 mm and 600 mm, the centre distance is 2 m, μ = 0.3, belt thickness is 10 mm, density is 1000 kg/m³, and safe stress is 2.5 N/mm². Find the belt width.

  1. Check velocity ratio without slip: N₁d = 1440 × 0.200 = 288; N₂D = 480 × 0.600 = 288. The speeds are consistent with these diameters.
  2. Belt speed: v = πdN₁/60 = π × 0.200 × 1440/60 = 15.08 m/s.
  3. Arc on the small pulley: θd = 180° − 2 sin⁻¹((0.600 − 0.200)/(2 × 2.0)) = 180° − 2 sin⁻¹(0.1) = 168.52° = 2.94 rad.
  4. Friction factor: μθ = 0.3 × 2.94 = 0.882; e^(μθ) = 2.42.
  5. Driving tension difference: T₁ − T₂ = P/v = 15000/15.08 = 994.72 N.
  6. With T₁/T₂ = 2.42, T₂ = 994.72/(2.42 − 1) = 702.17 N and T₁ = 1696.89 N.
  7. Belt mass per metre: m = ρbt. With b in mm, t = 0.010 m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³: m = 1000 × (b/1000) × 0.010 = 0.010b kg/m. Tc = m v² = 0.010b × 15.08² = 2.27b N.
  8. Safe tension: T = σbt = 2.5 × b × 10 = 25b N (σ = 2.5 N/mm², t = 10 mm).
  9. Strength condition: T₁ + Tc = T → 1696.89 + 2.27b = 25b → 22.73b = 1696.89 → b = 74.67 mm.
  10. Take the next standard width: b = 80 mm.

Answer. The required belt width is 74.67 mm; use a standard 80 mm wide flat belt.

Exam traps

  • Using 180° as the arc of contact. Use the smaller pulley's arc; for an open belt it is less than 180°.
  • Feeding degrees into e^(μθ). Convert θ to radians first.
  • Forgetting centrifugal tension Tc. At high speed it can use a large part of the safe tension.
  • Mixing millimetres and metres in m = ρbt. Use b and t in metres for m, then convert the answer.
  • Treating T₁ alone as the safe tension. The strength limit is T₁ + Tc = σbt.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern flat belts are often polyurethane or aramid-corded and run at high speed with small pulleys. Synchronous belts replace friction with teeth, so they do not slip and do not need the e^(μθ) limit. Conveyor belts add idlers, take-ups and cleaning systems, which change the design from a simple two-pulley problem. 6.3 Design of V-belt drives

A V-belt sits in a grooved pulley and wedges into it. The wedge raises the effective friction from μ to about μ / sin β, roughly three times, so the drive grips with a short centre distance and small pulleys. The price is that one belt carries only a few kilowatts; the drive is rated as a number of standard belts. V-belts run pumps, compressors, fans and machine tools, including many Indian workshop drives.

You need 6.2 for the tension ratio and power relation, and 6.1 for belt construction.

The wedge that multiplies friction

A flat belt grips only on the pulley rim. A V-belt grips on its two side faces, which press against the sides of a groove. The wedge action makes the normal force larger than the belt pull, so the friction that can be developed is larger. The same tension ratio as a flat belt applies, with μ replaced by the effective friction μ′.

μ′ = μ / sin β

β is the half groove angle; μ is the real coefficient of friction between belt and pulley; μ′ is the effective friction.

(T<sub>1</sub> − T<sub>c</sub>) / (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>c</sub>) = e<sup>(μθ / sin β)</sup>

T1 is tight-side tension in N; T2 is slack-side tension in N; Tc is centrifugal tension in N; θ is arc of contact on the small pulley in radians; e = 2.718.

For 2β = 38°, β = 19°, sin 19° = 0.326, so μ′ ≈ 3.07 μ. This is why a V-drive can use a centre distance shorter than a flat-belt drive and a small pulley that would slip with a flat belt. Groove angle is 34° to 40°; small pulleys use the narrower angle so the belt still bears on both sides after bending. A V-flat drive runs a V-belt on one grooved pulley and one flat pulley; it is used for quarter-turn drives and reversing, but the flat pulley gives less grip.

Sections, sizes and speed

V-belts come in standard sections. The letter gives the cross-section; the number gives the pitch length in millimetres. Thus B 1000 is a B-section belt with pitch length 1000 mm. Pitch length is measured on the pitch line, the line inside the belt that does not change length when the belt bends.

Larger sections carry more power but need larger pulleys. For a first choice, use a selection chart that plots kW against small-pulley speed for each section. The belt speed is v = π d N / 60, where d is the small pulley pitch diameter in metres, N is its speed in rev/min, and v is in m/s. Keep v ≤ 25 m/s. Above this, centrifugal tension Tc = m v² becomes large, where m is belt mass per metre; grip falls and the belt flexes too often.

A smaller section can run on a smaller pulley and at higher speed, but needs more belts. Do not mix sections on one pulley set. Tensioning is by sliding the motor on rails or by an idler; check deflection at mid-span.

Rating and number of belts

Design starts with the duty. The service factor Fa accounts for shock and running hours. Design power Pd = Fa P, where P is the motor power in kW and Pd is the power the belts must be rated for. A smooth electric motor driving a compressor for two shifts may need Fa = 1.2; a crusher may need 1.5 or more.

  1. Choose section from Pd and speed.
  2. Choose small pulley above the minimum for the section; find large pulley from ratio.
  3. Read rated power Pr per belt at the small-pulley speed and diameter.
  4. Find arc factor Kθ and length factor KL.
  5. N = Pd / (Pr Kθ KL); round up.
N = P<sub>d</sub> / (P<sub>r</sub> K<sub>θ</sub> K<sub>L</sub>)

N is number of belts; Pd is design power in kW; Pr is rated power per belt in kW; Kθ is arc of contact factor; KL is belt length factor.

Pr is from a chart for 180° arc and standard length. Kθ is below 1 when θ is less than 180°, as with a speed reduction. KL is below 1 for a belt shorter than the standard; a short belt bends more times per second. Round N up; keep N ≤ about 8. If N is larger, use a bigger section, larger pulleys or two drives. More belts share load unevenly unless they are matched and tensioned together.

Worked example

Question. A 7.5 kW, 1440 rpm motor drives a reciprocating compressor at 480 rpm for two shifts of 8 h a day. Select a B-section V-belt drive with pitch diameters 140 mm and 420 mm and centre distance 420 mm. The rating chart gives 2.6 kW per belt; arc factor is 0.87 and length factor 0.95. Take service factor 1.2. Find the number of belts.

  1. Design power Pd = Fa P = 1.2 × 7.5 = 9.0 kW.
  2. Belt speed v = π d N1 / 60 = π × 0.140 × 1440 / 60 = 10.56 m/s, below 25 m/s.
  3. Arc: sin α = (D − d) / (2C) = (0.420 − 0.140) / (2 × 0.420) = 0.3333, so α = 19.47°. Small-pulley arc θd = 180° − 2α = 141.06°.
  4. Corrected rating per belt = Pr Kθ KL = 2.6 × 0.87 × 0.95 = 2.149 kW.
  5. N = 9.0 / 2.149 = 4.19, round up to 5 belts.

Answer. Use 5 B-section belts.

Exam traps

  • Using the full groove angle 2β in sin β. If 2β = 38°, β = 19°, so sin β = sin 19° = 0.326, not sin 38° = 0.616. The wrong value roughly halves μ′ and oversizes the drive.
  • Forgetting the service factor. P is motor output, Pd = Fa P is what the belts must carry. Using P directly undersizes the drive.
  • Using millimetres in v = π d N / 60. Put d in metres; 140 mm is 0.140 m. Mixing units gives a speed 1000 times too large.
  • Rounding the number of belts down. 4.19 belts means 5 belts; 4 belts are overloaded.
  • Using the flat-belt tension ratio e(μθ) for a V-belt. The V-belt ratio uses μ / sin β, about three times larger.

6.4 Chain drives: types, failures, selection and lubrication

A chain drive pulls a roller chain over two sprockets. The rollers sit in tooth gaps, so the chain cannot slip and the velocity ratio is fixed by tooth counts. It carries more torque than a belt in the same space, works at high temperature and in oil, and is common on motorcycles, industrial conveyors and engine camshafts. The cost is chordal action, which makes the speed pulse and adds impact at engagement; separately, the pin-bush hinge slides under load, so the chain must be lubricated and it wears as pitch elongates.

You need: 6.1 for the place of a chain among flexible drives, and 6.3 for the service-factor habit.

Types and standard designation

A roller chain is a set of short links. Each link has two pins pressed into outer plates. Bushes on the pins carry rollers, and inner plates hold the bush ends. The roller touches the sprocket tooth. Pin, bush and roller make a hinge, so the chain bends around the sprocket without sliding.

Types: roller chain (bush roller chain) for power transmission; silent or inverted-tooth chain, where shaped plates engage the sprocket teeth and there are no rollers, used at high speed and low noise; block chain, simple cast blocks for low speed; detachable chain, hooked links for farm machinery.

Standard designation fixes pitch p (distance between pin centres), roller diameter and inner width. ISO 08B has p = 12.7 mm. ISO 10B has p = 15.875 mm. ANSI 40 and ANSI 50 are the US-series chains with the same two pitches. Give the standard number when ordering: two chains with the same pitch can have different roller sizes and will not fit the same sprocket.

Pitch geometry and chordal action

Let Z₁ be teeth on the small sprocket, Z₂ on the large, p pitch in metres, N₁ small-sprocket speed in rev/min. The exact mean chain speed from the polygon perimeter is v = Z₁ p N₁/60, while π d₁ N₁/60 is the speed of a point on the pitch circle; the two differ by less than 1% for Z ≥ 17. State which one you use.

v = Z₁ p N₁ / 60 ≈ π d₁ N₁ / 60

v is pitch-line speed in m/s; Z₁ is teeth on the small sprocket; p is pitch in m; N₁ is its speed in rev/min; d₁ is pitch diameter in m.

Pitch diameter is d = p/sin(180°/Z). This is the circle through roller centres, not the tip circle. A 19-tooth 08B sprocket has d = 12.7/sin 9.474° = 77.16 mm.

d = p / sin(180°/Z)

d is pitch diameter; p is pitch; Z is number of teeth. Use degrees in the calculator.

Chordal action: the chain leaves the sprocket as a polygon. Link speed rises and falls between v and v cos(180°/Z). The fractional fall is 1 − cos(180°/Z). At Z = 11 it is 4.0%; at Z = 19 it is 1.36%. Few teeth also mean a smaller pitch diameter, so the same torque gives a higher chain tension. Use at least 17 teeth; 19 or more for smooth running.

speed variation = 1 − cos(180°/Z)

fractional variation of chain speed for a sprocket with Z teeth; multiply by 100 for percent.

Chain length in pitches for centre distance C: L = 2C/p + (Z₁+Z₂)/2 + ((Z₂−Z₁)/(2π))² p/C. Round L up to an even number of links, because an odd count needs an offset link.

L = 2C/p + (Z₁ + Z₂)/2 + ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(2π))² p/C

L is chain length in pitches; C is centre distance; p is pitch; Z₁ and Z₂ are teeth. Round L up to an even number of links.

Failures, selection and lubrication

Failure modes: fatigue of the link plates under the fluctuating tension, and impact fatigue of rollers and bushes at each engagement with the sprocket; wear of pins and bushes, which increases pitch and elongates the chain; roller wear, which reduces roller diameter and damages tooth seating; roller breakage; galling when the oil film breaks down under high pressure; and jumping the tooth when elongation is too large. A common replacement limit is about 1.5% elongation; check the manufacturer's figure.

  1. Find service factor Ks from driver, driven machine and hours per day.
  2. Design power = Ks × motor power.
  3. Choose Z₁ ≥ 17 (19 for smooth running), Z₂ = i Z₁, where i is the velocity ratio.
  4. From the chain rating chart, read the power rating at N₁ for each chain number; pick the first chain with rating ≥ design power.
  5. Choose centre distance C between about 30p and 50p; compute L, round to even links, then find the exact C for those links.
  6. Check lubrication method and shaft loads.

Lubrication: brush or manual oiling for low speed; drip feed; bath or disc lubrication for enclosed drives; spray for high speed. Oil must reach the pin-bush hinge. Grease is for sealed motorcycle chains, not for high-speed industrial chains. Leave a small slack-side sag, roughly 2% of centre distance; too tight a chain overloads shafts and bearings. Use an idler or move the motor to adjust.

Worked example

Question. A 7.5 kW, 1000 rpm motor drives a compressor through a single-strand 08B roller chain. Pitch p = 12.7 mm, Z₁ = 19, Z₂ = 38, centre distance C = 500 mm. The catalogue breaking load is 18 kN. Find the pitch diameter of the small sprocket, the chain speed, the chain tension, the factor of safety on breaking load, the chain length in pitches and links, and the speed variation. Neglect centrifugal tension and sag.

  1. Pitch diameter: d₁ = p/sin(180°/Z₁) = 12.7/sin 9.474° = 12.7/0.1646 = 77.16 mm = 0.07716 m.
  2. Chain speed: v = π d₁ N₁/60 = π × 0.07716 × 1000/60 = 4.04 m/s.
  3. Chain tension: F = P/v = 7500/4.04 = 1856 N.
  4. Factor of safety: FoS = breaking load / F = 18000/1856 = 9.7.
  5. Length in pitches: L = 2C/p + (Z₁+Z₂)/2 + ((Z₂−Z₁)/(2π))² p/C. Substitute: 2×500/12.7 = 78.74; (19+38)/2 = 28.5; ((Z₂−Z₁)/(2π))² = ((38−19)/(2π))² = (3.024)² = 9.14; then 9.14 × p/C = 9.14 × 12.7/500 = 0.23. So L = 78.74 + 28.5 + 0.23 = 107.5 pitches.
  6. Links: round up to an even number, so use 108 links.
  7. Speed variation: 1 − cos(180°/19) = 1 − cos 9.474° = 1 − 0.9864 = 0.0136 = 1.36%.

Answer. d₁ = 77.16 mm, v = 4.04 m/s, F = 1856 N, FoS = 9.7, L = 107.5 pitches rounded to 108 links, and speed variation = 1.36%. This FoS only shows the chain will not break in tension; whether 08B is acceptable must be checked against its catalogue power rating at 1000 rpm with the service factor applied.

Exam traps

  • Rounding chain length to an odd number of links. An odd count needs an offset link, which is weaker; round up to an even number and recompute the exact centre distance for that link count (it comes out slightly larger, about 3 mm more here), or keep C and take up the small extra slack with an idler or by moving the motor.
  • Using the tip diameter instead of the pitch diameter in d = p/sin(180°/Z). The chain sits on the pitch circle; tip diameter gives a false speed and false tension.
  • Choosing a chain from motor kW alone. The catalogue rating must be compared with design power = service factor × motor power.
  • Writing speed variation as cos(180°/Z) instead of 1 − cos(180°/Z). At 19 teeth the variation is 1.36%, not 98.6%.
  • Setting a chain as tight as a V-belt. A chain needs a little slack for polygon action; too tight overloads shaft bearings.

6.5 Wire ropes: types, construction, lays and selection

A wire rope is a bundle of steel wires twisted into strands and then into a rope. It carries load only in tension: every wire shares the pull, so the rope is strong yet bends around a sheave. Cranes, lifts, mine hoists and bridge stays use it where a chain would be too heavy or a belt would slip. Selection fixes the construction and lay, sizes the diameter from the breaking load with a factor of safety, and keeps the sheave large enough to limit bending stress.

You need: 6.1 for the place of rope among flexible drives; Unit 1 for direct stress and factor of safety.

Strands, wires and cores

The first number in 6×19 is the number of strands; the second is the number of wires in each strand. A 6×19 strand usually has 12 outer wires, 6 inner wires and 1 core wire. The many small wires give flexibility, while the total steel area gives strength. A fibre core of sisal, manila or hemp holds lubricant and keeps the rope round. An independent wire rope core, written IWRC, is itself a small rope; it adds strength and resists crushing and heat.

ConstructionWires per strandCharacter
6×77stiff; standing ropes and guys
6×1919general hoisting; good balance

8×1919eight strands; smooth running, lifts

Spiral strand rope has layers of wires around a core, with no separate strands. Locked coil rope has shaped outer wires that interlock, giving a smooth, closed surface. Both are for standing duty such as bridge stays and guy ropes, not for running over small sheaves.

Regular lay and lang lay

In a regular lay, also called cross lay, wires in a strand run opposite to the direction of the strands around the rope. The rope is stable: a free load does not spin much. Right-hand regular lay is the usual default. In a lang lay, wires and strands run the same way. Lang lay is more flexible and puts more wire length on the sheave, so it wears longer, but it tends to untwist under load. Use it only with guided loads or fixed ends.

Right-hand and left-hand describe the helix direction. A right-hand lay advances away from you when turned clockwise. Lay length is the axial distance for one complete turn of a strand. A short lay length makes a more flexible rope; a long lay length gives better fatigue resistance but less flexibility. Record both direction and lay length when ordering, because two ropes of the same diameter can differ.

For a single-fall lift, choose regular lay. For a scraper rope or a rope with both ends fixed, lang lay can be considered. A free load on lang lay will rotate, which twists the falls and can damage the rope.

Stresses and rope selection

σt = W / A, A = k d²

σt is direct tensile stress in N/mm²; W is axial load in N; A is metallic area in mm²; d is rope diameter in mm; k is area coefficient, about 0.4 for 6×19. If your data sheet writes A = k′(π/4)d², then k′ is about 0.51 for the same rope.

σb = Ew δ / D

σb is bending stress in N/mm²; Ew is wire modulus, 2×10⁵ N/mm² for steel; δ is wire diameter in mm, about d/15 for 6×19; D is sheave or drum diameter in mm.

Bending stress rises when the sheave is small. Keep D/d at 30 or more; use 40 for lifts and higher for fast hoists. If D/d is too small, fit a more flexible rope such as 6×37.

  1. Fix the duty and the load W, including rope weight for long falls.
  2. Choose construction, core and lay; take factor of safety 5 to 8 by duty.
  3. Required breaking load Fbreak = factor of safety × W.
  4. Choose ultimate strength σu and area coefficient k; find d from Fbreak = σu k d².
  5. Round d to a standard size and check D/d ≥ 30, or ≥ 40 for a lift.
  6. If D/d is small, use a more flexible construction or a larger sheave.

In a multi-fall block, the drum tension is W/(n η), where n is the number of supporting falls and η is the overall block efficiency.

Worked example

Question. A lift cage and its load weigh 1000 kg and hang on a 6×19 wire rope with a fibre core. The rope ultimate strength is 1400 N/mm² and it runs over a sheave of 500 mm diameter. Use a factor of safety of 6. Find the minimum rope diameter, the diameter to adopt, the D/d ratio, the bending stress and the direct stress. Take k = 0.4 for area and Ew = 2×10⁵ N/mm².

  1. Load: W = 1000 × 9.81 = 9810 N.
  2. Breaking load needed: Fbreak = 6 × 9810 = 58 860 N.
  3. Required metallic area: A = 58 860 / 1400 = 42.04 mm².
  4. Diameter: d² = A / k = 42.04 / 0.4 = 105.1 mm², so d = 10.25 mm. Adopt 11 mm.
  5. Sheave check: D/d = 500 / 11 = 45.45, above 40 for a lift.
  6. Wire diameter: δ ≈ 11 / 15 = 0.733 mm. Bending stress: σb = 2×10⁵ × 0.733 / 500 = 293.33 N/mm².
  7. Actual area: A = 0.4 × 11² = 48.4 mm². Direct stress: σt = 9810 / 48.4 = 202.69 N/mm².

Answer. Minimum diameter 10.25 mm, so adopt 11 mm; D/d = 45.45, bending stress 293.33 N/mm² and direct stress 202.69 N/mm².

Exam traps

  • Applying the factor of safety to direct plus bending stress. The factor is applied to the tensile breaking load of the whole rope; bending is controlled by keeping D/d large.
  • Reading 6×19 as 6 wires and 19 strands. It means 6 strands, with 19 wires in each strand.
  • Treating lang lay as the default for a free load. Lang lay is more flexible and wears longer, but it tends to untwist; regular lay is the stable default.
  • Mixing area definitions. A = k d² with k ≈ 0.4 for 6×19 is not the same as A = k(π/4)d², where k is about 0.51 for the same rope.

Takeaways

  1. A flexible drive trades the rigid grip of gears for reach and tolerance of misalignment, and what it loses is either slip (belts) or a strict lubrication need (chains, ropes).
  2. The whole design of a flat belt comes from T₁/T₂ = e^(μθ) and P = (T₁ − T₂)v, so belt width is fixed by the strength limit and friction fixes the tension ratio.
  3. A V-belt multiplies friction by 1/sin β, roughly three times, so it can work with short centres and small pulleys; the price is that the drive is sold and rated in whole numbers of belts.
  4. Chain drives give an exact velocity ratio because there is no slip, but chordal action, wear at the joints, and the need for continuous lubrication are built into the design.
  5. Wire rope is a tension-only member; its strength is the sum of many small wires, and its life is decided as much by sheave diameter as by the breaking load.
  6. Selecting any flexible element means comparing a design load (after service, duty and life factors) with a catalogue rating, then checking the strength condition with a factor of safety.
  7. Misalignment, over-tensioning and under-tensioning cause most belt failures in the field; worn sprockets and dry pins cause most chain failures.
  8. In a wire rope, bending stress repeats every time the rope passes over a sheave, so fatigue — not simple tension — often limits the sheave size.

Fatigue from repeated bending as each wire passes over the sheave; bending stress varies as Er·dw/D, so a small sheave is the main culprit; Tension alone is checked by the factor of safety; combined tension plus bending is the real working condition; Sheave and drum diameter ratio D/d, groove radius, and fleet angle of the rope on the drum; Lay: lang lay is more flexible and wears better on the outside but is prone to unlaying; regular lay resists crushing; Also consider corrosion, incorrect lubrication, shock loading, rope being reversed over small sheaves, and end termination details.

Analyze Discuss the modes of failure of a roller chain drive and explain how lubrication and sprocket tooth count influence each mode. 12 marks

Failure modes: fatigue of the link plates, wear of pins and bushings (elongation), roller and bushing impact, and tooth wear on the sprocket; Elongation from pin wear changes the pitch line and causes the chain to ride high on the teeth, leading to jumping; Fewer teeth increase chordal action and the load per tooth, so the small sprocket should have at least 17 to 19 teeth; Lubrication: manual, drip, bath or slinger, and spray, chosen with chain speed and power; High speed demands continuous oil supply; dry running causes rapid pin and bushing wear and noise.

Evaluate A plant must choose between a chain drive and a V-belt drive for a crusher that experiences heavy starting torque and shock. Evaluate both options and recommend one with reasons. 12 marks

Crusher duty implies shock and overload, so the drive's ability to absorb shock matters; A V-belt has some elasticity and slips under overload, acting as a mechanical fuse; it also damps shocks; A chain is positive and transmits shock directly to the shafts and bearings, though it keeps exact ratio and needs less centre distance; Compare efficiency, maintenance, lubrication, cost and overload protection; A common practical answer: V-belt drive with a service factor well above 1, plus check of belt number and pulley size; state the trade-off clearly.

Glossary

Angle of contact
The arc of the pulley or sheave actually covered by the belt or rope, expressed in radians.
Belt
An endless flexible band that transmits power between two pulleys by friction.
Breaking load
The tensile load at which a chain, rope or belt actually fractures in a pull test.
Centrifugal tension
The tension induced in a moving belt or rope by its own mass as it travels round the pulley.
Chain
A series of linked rollers or pins that transmits power by engaging the teeth of two sprockets.
Chordal action
The periodic variation in chain speed caused by the chain wrapping a sprocket as a polygon rather than a circle.
Coefficient of friction μ
The ratio of the limiting friction force to the normal reaction between belt and pulley.
Core
The central member of a wire rope, of fibre or steel, that supports the strands and holds lubricant.
Creep
The small relative motion between belt and pulley caused by unequal elastic stretch on the two sides.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure load or stress to the working load or stress, applied to cover uncertainty and duty.
Flat belt
A belt of rectangular cross-section that runs on a flat or slightly crowned pulley rim.
Groove angle
The included angle of a V-belt pulley groove, usually 34° to 40°, which sets the wedging gain.
Idler pulley
A small extra pulley used to increase the angle of lap or to take up slack in a belt drive.
Lay
The helical pattern and direction in which wires and strands are twisted to form a rope.
Pitch (chain)
The centre-to-centre distance between two adjacent chain rollers, measured along the chain.
Pitch circle diameter
The diameter of the circle on which the chain rollers or belt centre line sit when wrapped on the sprocket or pulley.
Service factor
A multiplier applied to the transmitted power to account for the nature of the driven machine and its hours of duty.
Sheave
A grooved wheel over which a rope runs, used in hoists and rope drives.
Slip
Gross relative sliding between belt and pulley that occurs when the required friction exceeds the available friction.
Splice
The joint by which a flat belt is made endless, by lacing, metal fasteners, or cementing and vulcanising.
Sprocket
A toothed wheel whose teeth engage the chain rollers and so drive it without slip.
Strand
A group of wires twisted together in a helix, several of which are laid up to form a wire rope.
V-belt
A belt of trapezoidal cross-section that wedges into a grooved pulley to gain extra friction.
Wire rope
A tension member made by twisting steel wires into strands and strands into a rope around a core.
