6.1 Flexible drives: types, belt materials and construction

A flexible drive carries power from one shaft to another through a belt, chain or rope that bends around pulleys or sprockets. Because the member bends, the shafts can sit far apart and need only rough alignment, which a gear pair cannot do. Belts grip by friction and allow a little slip, which soaks up shock; chains and timing belts grip by teeth or rollers and hold the speed ratio exact. Belts, chains and wire ropes run mills, conveyors, motorcycles, lifts and generator sets across Indian plants.

You need: density, material properties and the factor of safety from the design foundations, and shaft design for the shaft that carries the pulley load. The tension and width calculation of the belt itself follows in design of flat belt drives.

Three families, one job

A flexible drive has three parts: a driving pulley or sprocket on the input shaft, a driven one on the output shaft, and one continuous member wrapped round both. Torque enters at the driving pulley, travels along the member as tension, and leaves as torque at the driven shaft. The small pulley is often, but not always, the driver. Nothing rigid connects the shafts, so they can sit a metre or ten metres apart and be out of line by a degree or two without stopping the drive.

The members divide by how they grip. Flat, V and round belts grip by friction on the pulley and slip a little when overloaded. Timing belts have moulded teeth that mesh with grooves in the pulley; chains have rollers that seat in sprocket teeth. Neither of those slips. Wire and fibre ropes carry load only in tension and run on drums or large sheaves.

Against a gear pair the trade is clear. Gears give an exact velocity ratio in a small rigid box, but need a short centre distance, accurate alignment, continuous lubrication and a sealed casing. A flexible drive is cheap and tolerant of misalignment; belt drives are also quiet and absorb shock, while chain and timing-belt drives are noisier and pass shock through more readily. In exchange you pay in slip (belts), extra shaft bending load and floor space.

Belt materials

Leather is the oldest belt material and still the textbook reference. Hides are oak-tanned — tanned with tannin from bark — which makes them tough and slightly stiff. Standard design values: density about 1000 kg/m³, and coefficient of friction μ = 0.25 to 0.4 for leather on a cast-iron pulley. Leather takes high tensile stress and runs quietly, but it stretches with use, loses strength when wet, and needs occasional dressing with a friction compound.

Cotton canvas, woven into a heavy cloth called duck and bonded with rubber, makes the common rubber flat belt. Several plies of canvas carry the load; the rubber holds them together and gives the friction face. Balata, a gum from a tropical tree, impregnates cotton belting to make it waterproof with a high, steady grip; it softens above about 40 °C, so it suits cool, damp places.

High-strength belts use synthetic cords. Nylon is strong and absorbs shock but stretches; polyester is stiffer and holds its length; aramid cord gives very high strength with low stretch where the belt must be narrow and fast. The cover is rubber or polyurethane, chosen for grip and resistance to oil and grit.

How a belt is built

A flat belt is a stack of layers. Tension cords carry the pull and sit as close as possible to the neutral axis — the plane inside the belt that neither stretches nor shortens when the belt bends. Above and below the cords sit compression rubber and fabric plies, with a cover on each face for friction and to keep oil out. Cords near the neutral axis see little bending stress, so the belt runs on a small pulley without fatigue.

A flat belt must be joined into a loop. A skived and cemented joint (ends cut to a taper, glued and pressed) runs smoothly and keeps most of the belt strength. Metal lacing threaded through punched holes is quick to fit but heavier and weaker, and slaps at every pulley; hinged wire hooks serve for temporary repairs. The joint is the weakest section, so the allowable tension is reduced by a joint efficiency.

V-belts are moulded endless, with a trapezoidal cross-section and a layer of tension cord just under the top face. Standard sections run A, B, C, D and E, with top widths from about 13 mm (A) up to 38 mm (E); each section covers a range of pulley diameters and powers. The belt itself has an included angle of 40°, and the pulley groove is cut a little narrower, 34° to 38°, so the belt always bears on its two sides. Small pulleys get the narrowest groove: when the belt bends round a small radius, its stretched top narrows and its squeezed bottom widens, so its side angle drops below 40°.

Tc = m v², where m = ρ b t

Tc is the centrifugal tension in N, which pulls on the belt even when it carries no load; m is the mass per metre length in kg/m; v is belt speed in m/s; ρ is belt material density in kg/m³; b is belt width in m; t is belt thickness in m.

Worked example

Question. A leather belt is 100 mm wide and 6 mm thick and runs at 15 m/s over a cast-iron pulley. Take the density of leather as 1000 kg/m³. Find the mass per metre length of the belt and the centrifugal tension set up in it.

  1. Convert to SI: width b = 100 mm = 0.1 m; thickness t = 6 mm = 0.006 m; speed v = 15 m/s; density ρ = 1000 kg/m³.
  2. Volume of one metre of belt = b × t × 1 m = 0.1 × 0.006 × 1 = 6 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  3. Mass per metre length m = ρ b t = 1000 × 0.1 × 0.006 = 0.6 kg per metre.
  4. Centrifugal tension Tc = m v² = 0.6 × (15)² = 0.6 × 225 = 135 N.
  5. The belt carries this 135 N on both strands whether or not it transmits power. In the flat-belt design that follows, it is added to the tight-side tension before the strength check, and it plays no part in the friction ratio eμθ, so it never helps transmit torque.

Answer. Mass per metre length = 0.6 kg/m and centrifugal tension = 135 N.

Exam traps

  • Assuming a V-belt grips on its flat bottom face. It grips on the two sloping sides; the bottom must clear the groove bottom, or the wedge action is lost.
  • Forgetting that centrifugal tension grows as the square of speed: 15 m/s to 30 m/s takes Tc from 135 N to 540 N, not 270 N.
  • Using a friction value without stating it. Leather on cast iron is taken as μ = 0.25 to 0.4; write down the value you use and keep it for the whole problem.
  • Applying the belt-friction ratio eμθ to a timing belt. It drives by tooth engagement, and its rating comes from the teeth, not from friction.
  • Ignoring the joint. The allowable tension is multiplied by a joint efficiency, and a laced joint is weaker and heavier than a cemented one.

The belt sinks until its bottom touches the groove bottom. Contact moves from the two sloping sides to the flat bottom, the wedge factor 1/sin β is lost, and the effective friction drops to about μ. The belt slips, heats and wears fast, and the power it can carry falls sharply.

6.2 Design of flat belt drives

A flat belt is a loop of flexible material that grips a pulley by friction over part of its rim. The driver pulls the tight side; the slack side returns. The two tensions are tied by T₁/T₂ = eμθ, so the belt can pull only as hard as friction allows. Design means choosing a width b so that the tight-side tension plus the centrifugal tension stays below the safe strength σbt. Flat belts drive line shafts, conveyors and small machines where the shafts are far apart and some slip is acceptable.

You need: belt materials, thickness and density from flexible drives and belt materials, and direct stress and the factor of safety from the design foundations.

Grip and the arc of contact

A flat belt transmits power only where it touches the pulley. The driver pulls the tight side and feeds the slack side. The arc of contact (also called the angle of lap) is the angle over which the belt touches a pulley. In an open drive the smaller pulley has the smaller arc, so with the same μ on both pulleys it slips first and sets the friction limit. In a crossed drive the two arcs are equal.

Use d and D for the small and large pulley diameters in metres, C for the centre distance in metres, and N₁, N₂ for speeds in rpm. Ignoring slip, N₁d = N₂D. With slip fraction s, N₂ = N₁(d/D)(1 − s); s is the fraction of belt speed lost.

θ = 180° − 2 sin⁻¹((D − d)/(2C))

θ is the arc of contact on the smaller pulley of an open belt, in degrees; d and D are the pulley diameters and C the centre distance, all in the same unit. The larger pulley gets 180° + 2 sin⁻¹((D − d)/(2C)).

θ = 180° + 2 sin⁻¹((D + d)/(2C))

θ is the arc of contact of a crossed belt, the same on both pulleys; the belt crosses between them and the pulleys turn in opposite directions.

Convert θ to radians before using eμθ. Belt length for an open drive is L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)²/(4C). For a crossed drive, L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D + d)²/(4C).

Tensions, centrifugal force and power

Let T₁ and T₂ be the tight-side and slack-side tensions that produce friction, in N. The belt also carries the centrifugal tension Tc = m v² on both sides, where m is the belt mass per metre in kg/m and v is belt speed in m/s; for width b and thickness t in metres and density ρ in kg/m³, m = ρbt. So the total pull in the tight side is T₁ + Tc.

T₁/T₂ = eμθ

μ is the coefficient of friction; θ is the arc of contact on the smaller pulley in radians; e = 2.71828…, the base of natural logarithms.

The ratio comes from the balance of one small piece of belt on the rim:

Take a small piece of belt on the rim, subtending dθ.
Tensions: T on the slack end, T + dT on the tight end.
Radial balance: dN = T dθ   (the two pulls each lean in by dθ/2)
Limiting friction: dT = μ dN = μ T dθ
So dT/T = μ dθ. Integrate from T₂ to T₁ over 0 to θ:
ln(T₁/T₂) = μθ,  that is  T₁/T₂ = exp(μθ)

Assumptions: friction is at its limit over the whole arc, μ is constant, and the belt is thin and does not stretch. If T₁ and T₂ are total tensions that include Tc, the result reads (T₁ − Tc)/(T₂ − Tc) = eμθ, because centrifugal force carries Tc without pressing the belt on the rim.

The safe tension is T = σbt, where σ is the safe stress (already reduced for the joint). The tight side carries T₁ plus Tc, so T₁ + Tc ≤ T. Power is P = (T₁ − T₂)v. The initial tension set when the belt is fitted is T₀ = (T₁ + T₂)/2 + Tc.

At fixed T and μθ, P = (1 − 1/eμθ)(T − m v²)v. Setting dP/dv = 0 gives T = 3m v²: maximum power comes when Tc = T/3, at v = √(T/(3m)). For the 80 mm belt of the worked example, T = 2000 N and m = 0.8 kg/m, so v = 28.9 m/s and the peak is 22.6 kW.

Design steps, creep and slip

  1. v = πdN₁/60 from the smaller pulley.
  2. θ of the smaller pulley, in radians.
  3. μθ and eμθ.
  4. T₁ − T₂ = P/v, then T₁ and T₂ from the ratio.
  5. m = ρbt and Tc = m v².
  6. Solve T₁ + Tc = σbt for b.
  7. Round b up to a standard width; check the stress and T₀. Make the pulley face about a quarter wider than the belt.

Creep is the stretch on the tight side and contraction on the slack side. It makes the driven pulley lag slightly even with good friction. Slip is gross sliding when T₁/T₂ exceeds eμθ. Both reduce the velocity ratio. A flat belt is chosen when some slip is acceptable and the shafts are far apart.

Worked example

Question. A 15 kW motor at 1440 rpm drives a line shaft at 480 rpm through an open flat belt. The pulley diameters are 200 mm and 600 mm, the centre distance is 2 m, μ = 0.3, belt thickness is 10 mm, density is 1000 kg/m³, and the safe stress is 2.5 N/mm². Find the belt width and the initial tension.

  1. Check the velocity ratio without slip: N₁d = 1440 × 0.200 = 288 and N₂D = 480 × 0.600 = 288. The speeds match the diameters.
  2. Belt speed: v = πdN₁/60 = π × 0.200 × 1440/60 = 15.08 m/s.
  3. Arc on the small pulley: (D − d)/(2C) = 0.400/4.0 = 0.1; sin⁻¹(0.1) = 5.74°; θ = 180° − 2 × 5.74° = 168.52° = 168.52 × π/180 = 2.941 rad.
  4. Friction factor: μθ = 0.3 × 2.941 = 0.8824; eμθ = e0.8824 = 2.4166.
  5. Driving tension difference: T₁ − T₂ = P/v = 15 000/15.08 = 994.72 N.
  6. With T₁ = 2.4166 T₂: T₂ = 994.72/(2.4166 − 1) = 994.72/1.4166 = 702.2 N, and T₁ = 2.4166 × 702.2 = 1696.9 N.
  7. With b in mm: m = 1000 × (b/1000) × 0.010 = 0.010b kg/m, so Tc = 0.010b × 15.08² = 2.274b N.
  8. Safe tension: T = σbt = 2.5 × b × 10 = 25b N (σ in N/mm², b and t in mm).
  9. Strength condition: T₁ + Tc = T, so 1696.9 + 2.274b = 25b, 22.726b = 1696.9 and b = 74.67 mm.
  10. Take the standard width b = 80 mm. Check: m = 0.8 kg/m, Tc = 181.9 N, pull = 1696.9 + 181.9 = 1878.8 N, stress = 1878.8/800 = 2.35 N/mm² < 2.5 N/mm².
  11. Initial tension: T₀ = (T₁ + T₂)/2 + Tc = (1696.9 + 702.2)/2 + 181.9 = 1199.5 + 181.9 = 1381.4 N.

Answer. The belt must be at least 74.67 mm wide; use a standard 80 mm belt, which runs at 2.35 N/mm². Fit it with an initial tension of about 1381 N.

Exam traps

  • Using 180° as the arc of contact. Use the smaller pulley’s arc: 168.52° in the worked example.
  • Feeding degrees into eμθ. Convert θ to radians first; 0.3 × 168.52 would give e50.6.
  • Forgetting Tc. Without it the worked example gives b = 1696.9/25 = 67.9 mm, a belt that would really run at 2.73 N/mm², above the safe 2.5 N/mm².
  • Mixing millimetres and metres in m = ρbt. Use metres, or keep b in mm and write m = 0.010b as the worked example does.
  • Treating T₁ alone as the safe tension. The strength limit is T₁ + Tc = σbt.

6.3 Design of V-belt drives

A V-belt sits in a grooved pulley and wedges into it. The wedge raises the effective friction from μ to about μ / sin β, roughly three times, so the drive grips with a short centre distance and small pulleys. The price is that one belt carries only a few kilowatts; the drive is rated as a number of standard belts. V-belts run pumps, compressors, fans and machine tools, including many Indian workshop drives.

You need: the tension ratio and power relation from design of flat belt drives, and belt construction from flexible drives and belt materials.

The wedge that multiplies friction

A flat belt grips only on the pulley rim. A V-belt grips on its two side faces, which press against the sides of a groove. If the belt is pushed into the groove with a radial force R, each side face pushes back with a normal force Rn, and only the radial parts of those two forces balance R: 2Rn sin β = R. The friction available is 2μRn = μR/sin β, larger than the μR a flat rim would give. So the flat-belt tension ratio applies with μ replaced by the effective friction μ′.

μ′ = μ / sin β

β is the half groove angle; μ is the real coefficient of friction between belt and pulley; μ′ is the effective friction.

T₁/T₂ = eμθ/sin β

T₁ and T₂ are the tight-side and slack-side tensions that produce friction, in N, as for flat belts; the total pull in the tight side is T₁ + Tc. θ is the arc of contact on the small pulley in radians and β is the half groove angle.

For 2β = 38°, β = 19° and sin 19° = 0.326, so μ′ ≈ 3.07μ. This is why a V-drive works on short centres and small pulleys. The belt’s own angle is 40°; the groove is cut at 34° to 38°, narrowest on small pulleys, so the bent belt still bears on both sides. A V-flat drive runs V-belts in a grooved small pulley and on a plain large one, often a flywheel, for large ratios on short centres.

Sections, sizes and speed

V-belts come in standard sections. The letter gives the cross-section and the number gives the belt length in millimetres, usually the pitch length. Thus B 1000 is a B-section belt 1000 mm long. Pitch length is measured on the pitch line, the line inside the belt that does not change length when the belt bends.

Larger sections carry more power but need larger pulleys. For a first choice, use a selection chart that plots design power against small-pulley speed for each section. The belt speed is v = πdN/60, where d is the small-pulley pitch diameter in metres, N its speed in rev/min and v is in m/s. Classical V-belts are kept below about 25 to 30 m/s: above that, the centrifugal tension Tc = m v² (m is belt mass per metre) eats the grip and the belt flexes too often.

A smaller section can run on a smaller pulley and at higher speed, but needs more belts. Do not mix sections on one pulley set. Tensioning is by sliding the motor on rails or by an idler; check deflection at mid-span.

Rating and number of belts

Design starts with the duty. The service factor Fa accounts for shock and running hours. Design power Pd = Fa P, where P is the motor power in kW and Pd is the power the belts must be rated for. A smooth electric motor driving a compressor for two shifts may need Fa = 1.2; a crusher may need 1.5 or more.

  1. Choose the section from Pd and the small-pulley speed.
  2. Choose a small pulley above the minimum for the section; find the large pulley from the speed ratio.
  3. Find the arc of contact and the pitch length; take the nearest standard length.
  4. Read the rated power Pr per belt at the small-pulley speed and diameter.
  5. Find the arc factor Kθ and the length factor KL.
  6. z = Pd / (Pr Kθ KL); round up.

z = Pd / (Pr Kθ KL), where z is the number of belts, Pd the design power in kW, Pr the rated power per belt in kW, Kθ the arc-of-contact factor and KL the belt-length factor.

Pr is read for a 180° arc and a standard length. Kθ is below 1 when θ is under 180°. KL is below 1 for a shorter belt, which passes round the pulleys more often each second and fatigues sooner, and above 1 for a longer one. Keep z at about 8 or fewer, or move to a bigger section or larger pulleys. Several belts share the load evenly only as a matched set, tensioned together.

Worked example

Question. A 7.5 kW, 1440 rpm motor drives a reciprocating compressor at 480 rpm for two shifts of 8 h a day. Select a B-section V-belt drive with pitch diameters 140 mm and 420 mm and a centre distance of 420 mm. The rating chart gives 2.6 kW per belt; the arc factor is 0.87 and the length factor 0.95. Take a service factor of 1.2. Find the pitch length and the number of belts.

  1. Design power Pd = FaP = 1.2 × 7.5 = 9.0 kW.
  2. Speed ratio check: 1440/480 = 3 = 420/140.
  3. Belt speed v = πdN₁/60 = π × 0.140 × 1440/60 = 10.56 m/s, below 25 m/s.
  4. Arc: sin α = (D − d)/(2C) = (420 − 140)/(2 × 420) = 0.3333, so α = 19.47°. Small-pulley arc θ = 180° − 2α = 141.06° = 2.462 rad.
  5. Pitch length: L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)²/(4C) = 840 + π × 560/2 + 280²/1680 = 840 + 879.6 + 46.7 = 1766.3 mm. Order the nearest standard pitch length from the maker’s list and reset the centre distance to suit; the length factor 0.95 applies to that belt.
  6. Corrected rating per belt = PrKθKL = 2.6 × 0.87 × 0.95 = 2.149 kW.
  7. Number of belts z = 9.0/2.149 = 4.19. Four belts give only 4 × 2.149 = 8.60 kW, short of 9.0 kW, so use 5 belts (10.74 kW).

Answer. Pitch length about 1766 mm (nearest standard length); use 5 B-section belts.

Worked example 2

Question. For the same drive, check the belts by tension instead of by chart. Each B-section belt has a mass of 0.19 kg/m and a safe working tension of 300 N; μ = 0.2 between belt and pulley and the groove angle is 38°. Find the power one belt can carry and the number of belts for 9.0 kW.

  1. Half groove angle β = 19°, sin β = 0.3256. Effective friction μ′ = 0.2/0.3256 = 0.614.
  2. Arc on the small pulley from worked example 1: θ = 2.462 rad. So μ′θ = 0.614 × 2.462 = 1.512 and e1.512 = 4.54. (A flat belt with μ = 0.2 would have only e0.492 = 1.64.)
  3. Centrifugal tension: Tc = m v² = 0.19 × 10.56² = 21.2 N.
  4. Friction tension on the tight side: T₁ = 300 − 21.2 = 278.8 N. Slack side: T₂ = 278.8/4.54 = 61.4 N.
  5. Power per belt: P = (T₁ − T₂)v = (278.8 − 61.4) × 10.56 = 2295 W = 2.29 kW.
  6. Belts: z = 9.0/2.29 = 3.92, so 4 belts.

Answer. One belt carries 2.29 kW, so the tension check asks for 4 belts. The chart method asked for 5 because its rating also allows for fatigue life; when both are worked, the design takes the larger count, 5 belts.

  • Using the full groove angle in sin β. For 2β = 38°, sin 19° = 0.326, not sin 38° = 0.616; the wrong value roughly halves μ′.
  • Forgetting the service factor. The belts must carry Pd = FaP, not the motor power P.
  • Using millimetres in v = πdN/60. Put d in metres, or the speed comes out 1000 times too large.
  • Rounding the number of belts down. 4.19 means 5 belts; 4 belts carry only 8.60 kW against 9.0 kW.
  • Using the flat-belt ratio eμθ for a V-belt. The V-belt exponent is μθ/sin β: 4.54 instead of 1.64 in worked example 2.

6.4 Chain drives: types, failures, selection and lubrication

A chain drive pulls a roller chain over two sprockets. The rollers sit in the tooth gaps, so the chain cannot slip and the velocity ratio is fixed by the tooth counts. It carries more torque than a belt in the same space, works hot and in oil, and runs motorcycles, conveyors and camshafts. Its costs are chordal action, which makes the speed pulse, and the sliding pin-bush hinge, which needs oil and slowly wears the chain longer.

You need: the place of a chain among flexible drives from flexible drives and belt materials, and the service-factor habit from design of V-belt drives.

Types and standard designation

A roller chain is a set of short links. Pins are pressed into the outer plates; bushes are pressed into the inner plates and turn on the pins; rollers turn freely on the bushes. As the chain bends onto a sprocket, the pin turns inside its bush: this hinge is where sliding, and so wear, happens. The roller rolls onto the tooth, so the tooth sees little rubbing.

Types: roller chain (bush roller chain) for power transmission; silent or inverted-tooth chain, where shaped plates engage the sprocket teeth and there are no rollers, used at high speed and low noise; block chain, simple cast blocks for low speed; detachable chain, hooked links for farm machinery.

The standard number fixes the pitch p (distance between pin centres), the roller diameter and the inner width. ISO 08B has p = 12.7 mm and ISO 10B has p = 15.875 mm; a suffix gives the strands, so 08B-1 is single and 08B-2 duplex. ANSI 40 and ANSI 50 are the US-series chains with the same two pitches. Give the full number when ordering: two chains with the same pitch can have different rollers and will not fit the same sprocket.

Pitch geometry and chordal action

Let Z₁ be the teeth on the small sprocket, Z₂ on the large, p the pitch and N₁ the small-sprocket speed in rev/min. The mean chain speed is exactly v = Z₁pN₁/60, one pitch per tooth passed. πd₁N₁/60 is the speed of a point on the pitch circle, the largest link speed; the two differ by less than 1 % for Z ≥ 17. The worked example uses the mean speed for the chain pull.

v = Z₁ p N₁ / 60

v is the mean chain speed in m/s; Z₁ is the teeth on the small sprocket; p is the pitch in m; N₁ is its speed in rev/min.

The pitch diameter is the circle through the roller centres, not the tip circle. A 19-tooth 08B sprocket has d = 12.7/sin 9.474° = 77.16 mm.

d = p / sin(180°/Z)

d is the pitch diameter; p is the pitch; Z is the number of teeth. Work the angle 180°/Z in degrees.

Chordal action: the chain wraps the sprocket as a polygon, so the link speed rises and falls between πdN/60 and πdN/60 × cos(180°/Z). The fractional fall is 1 − cos(180°/Z): 4.0 % at Z = 11 and 1.36 % at Z = 19. Few teeth also mean a smaller pitch diameter, so the same torque gives a higher chain pull. Use at least 17 teeth, and 19 or more for smooth running.

Chain length is counted in pitches (links). Round it up to an even number, because an odd count needs an offset link; then find the exact centre distance for that count.

L = 2C/p + (Z₁ + Z₂)/2 + ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(2π))² p/C

L is the chain length in pitches; C is the centre distance; p is the pitch. For a chosen even L, the exact centre distance is C = (p/4)[A + √(A² − 8B²)], with A = L − (Z₁ + Z₂)/2 and B = (Z₂ − Z₁)/(2π).

Failures, selection and lubrication

Failure modes: fatigue of the link plates under the fluctuating tension; impact fatigue of rollers and bushes at each engagement with the sprocket; wear of pins and bushes, which lengthens the pitch and the chain; roller wear, which damages the tooth seating; galling of pin and bush when the oil film breaks down at high speed and load; and the chain riding up and jumping teeth once it has stretched. Makers usually set the replacement limit between about 1.5 % and 3 % elongation, the smaller figure for large sprockets.

  1. Service factor Ks from the driver, the driven machine and the hours per day; design power = Ks × motor power.
  2. Z₁ ≥ 17 (19 for smooth running) and Z₂ = iZ₁, where i is the speed ratio.
  3. From the maker’s chart, the first chain whose rating at N₁ is at least the design power.
  4. C of about 30p to 50p; L rounded up to even links; the exact C for those links.
  5. Factor of safety on breaking load, lubrication method and shaft loads.

Lubrication: brush or manual oiling for low speed; drip feed; bath or disc lubrication for enclosed drives; spray for high speed. Oil must reach the pin-bush hinge. Grease is for sealed motorcycle chains, not for high-speed industrial chains. Leave a small slack-side sag, roughly 2% of centre distance; too tight a chain overloads shafts and bearings. Use an idler or move the motor to adjust.

Worked example

Question. A 3 kW, 1000 rpm electric motor drives a compressor at 500 rpm through a single-strand 08B roller chain (p = 12.7 mm, breaking load 17.8 kN). Z₁ = 19, Z₂ = 38 and the centre distance is about 500 mm. The service factor is 1.3, and the maker’s chart rates one strand of 08B at 4.5 kW at 1000 rpm on a 19-tooth sprocket. Check the chain, and find the pitch diameters, the chain speed, the chain pull, the factor of safety, the number of links with the exact centre distance, and the speed variation. Neglect centrifugal tension and sag.

  1. Speed ratio: Z₂/Z₁ = 38/19 = 2, so the compressor turns at 1000/2 = 500 rpm.
  2. Design power: 1.3 × 3 = 3.9 kW. The 08B rating of 4.5 kW is above it, so a single strand of 08B is acceptable.

  6. Factor of safety on breaking load: 17 800/746 = 23.9. The chain is far from breaking; the power rating, which allows for plate fatigue and wear, is the real limit.
  7. Length: 2C/p = 1000/12.7 = 78.74; (Z₁ + Z₂)/2 = 28.5; ((Z₂ − Z₁)/(2π))² = (19/6.283)² = 3.024² = 9.144, and 9.144 × 12.7/500 = 0.23. L = 78.74 + 28.5 + 0.23 = 107.47 pitches.

  • Rounding to an odd number of links. An odd count needs a weaker offset link; round up to even and recompute C: 108 links put the sprockets 503.4 mm apart, not 500 mm.
  • Using the tip diameter instead of the pitch diameter d = p/sin(180°/Z). The chain sits on the pitch circle.
  • Choosing a chain from the motor power alone. Compare the rating with the design power: 3.9 kW, not 3 kW, in the worked example.
  • Writing the speed variation as cos(180°/Z) instead of 1 − cos(180°/Z): 1.36 %, not 98.6 %, at 19 teeth.
  • Setting a chain as tight as a V-belt. It needs a little slack; too tight overloads the shaft bearings.

6.5 Wire ropes: types, construction, lays and selection

A wire rope is a bundle of steel wires twisted into strands and then into a rope. It carries load only in tension: every wire shares the pull, so the rope is strong yet bends round a sheave. Cranes, lifts, mine hoists and bridge stays use it where a chain would be too heavy or a belt would slip. Selection fixes the construction and lay, sizes the diameter from the breaking load, and then checks the factor of safety once the bending over the sheave and the starting acceleration are counted.

You need: the place of rope among flexible drives from flexible drives and belt materials, and direct stress and the factor of safety from the design foundations.

Strands, wires and cores

The first number in 6×19 is the number of strands; the second is the number of wires in each strand. A standard 6×19 strand has 12 outer wires, 6 inner wires and 1 centre wire. Many small wires give flexibility, while the total steel area gives strength. A fibre core of sisal, manila or polypropylene holds lubricant and keeps the rope round. An independent wire rope core, written IWRC, is itself a small rope; it adds strength and resists crushing on the drum and heat.

Ropes such as 6×61 go further for very severe bending. Spiral strand rope has layers of wires round a centre with no separate strands; locked coil rope has shaped outer wires that interlock into a smooth, closed surface. Both are for standing duty such as bridge stays and guys, not for running over small sheaves.

Regular lay and lang lay

In a regular lay, also called cross lay, wires in a strand run opposite to the direction of the strands around the rope. The rope is stable: a free load does not spin much. Right-hand regular lay is the usual default. In a lang lay, wires and strands run the same way. Lang lay is more flexible and puts more wire length on the sheave, so it wears longer, but it tends to untwist under load. Use it only with guided loads or fixed ends.

Right-hand and left-hand describe the helix of the strands. Hold the rope vertical: if the strands climb to the right like the thread of an ordinary screw, the lay is right-hand. Lay length is the length of rope in which one strand makes one full turn, about six to seven rope diameters in ordinary ropes. The lay direction is matched to the drum grooving so that winding does not unlay the rope, so state both lay type and direction when ordering.

For a single-fall lift, choose regular lay. For a scraper rope or a rope with both ends fixed, lang lay can be considered. A free load on lang lay will rotate, which twists the falls and can damage the rope.

Stresses and rope selection

A hoist rope carries more than the payload. The pull at the top of the rope is the load W plus the rope’s own weight wl (w in N/m, l the hanging length in m), and while the load speeds up at a m/s² both are multiplied by (1 + a/g). A rope that is slack when the drive starts takes an impact on top of this, so hoists are started with the rope taut.

Ft = (W + wl)(1 + a/g)

Ft is the largest steady pull in the rope in N; g = 9.81 m/s². The direct stress is σt = Ft/A, where A is the metallic area, about 0.4d² for 6×19 (d in mm, A in mm²).

σb = Er dw / D, where σb is the bending stress in the outer wires in N/mm²; Er is the modulus of the rope, about 0.8 × 10⁵ N/mm² for steel ropes, lower than the 2.1 × 10⁵ N/mm² of the wire because the wires lie in helices; dw is the outer wire diameter, about d/15 for 6×19; D is the sheave or drum diameter in mm. Multiplying by the area gives the equivalent bending load Fb = σbA.

n = (Fu − Fb) / Ft, where n is the factor of safety with bending counted; Fu is the breaking load of the rope (from the maker’s table, or σuA with σu the ultimate stress on the metallic area). Required values run from about 5 for a plain hoist to well above 10 for fast passenger lifts; the code for the machine sets the figure.

  1. Fix the duty, the load W, the lift l and the starting acceleration a.
  2. Choose the construction, core and lay.
  3. Make a first estimate of d from Fu = n × W with A = 0.4d², and round up to a standard size.
  4. Check D/d against the smallest sheave for the construction.
  5. Find Ft, σb and Fb, then n = (Fu − Fb)/Ft.
  6. If n is too low, take the next rope size, a larger sheave or more falls.

In a block with nf supporting falls and overall efficiency η, the drum-side rope pull is W/(nfη), so more falls allow a thinner rope.

Worked example

Question. A goods hoist lifts a cage and load of 1000 kg through 30 m and starts with an acceleration of 1 m/s². It uses a 6×19 fibre-core rope over a 500 mm sheave. The rope’s ultimate stress on its metallic area is 1400 N/mm², A = 0.4d², dw = d/15, Er = 0.8 × 10⁵ N/mm² and the rope weighs 0.45 kg/m. Take a first size from a factor of safety of 6 on the load alone, then find the factor of safety with the rope weight, the acceleration and the bending counted.

  1. Load: W = 1000 × 9.81 = 9810 N.
  2. First size: Fu = 6 × 9810 = 58 860 N, so A = 58 860/1400 = 42.04 mm² and d = √(42.04/0.4) = √105.1 = 10.25 mm. Adopt 11 mm.
  3. Rope area and breaking load: A = 0.4 × 11² = 48.4 mm²; Fu = 1400 × 48.4 = 67 760 N.
  4. Sheave check: D/d = 500/11 = 45.5, above the 30d minimum for 6×19.
  5. Rope weight: wl = 0.45 × 9.81 × 30 = 132.4 N. Static pull = 9810 + 132.4 = 9942.4 N.
  6. Starting pull: Ft = 9942.4 × (1 + 1/9.81) = 9942.4 × 1.1019 = 10 956 N. Direct stress σt = 10 956/48.4 = 226.4 N/mm².
  7. Bending: dw = 11/15 = 0.733 mm; σb = 0.8 × 10⁵ × 0.733/500 = 117.3 N/mm²; Fb = 117.3 × 48.4 = 5679 N.
  8. Factor of safety: n = (67 760 − 5679)/10 956 = 62 081/10 956 = 5.67.

Answer. Adopt an 11 mm 6×19 rope (first estimate 10.25 mm). On the load alone its factor of safety is 67 760/9810 = 6.91; with the rope weight, starting acceleration and bending it falls to 5.67, which is acceptable for a goods hoist. A passenger lift would need a much higher factor and several ropes.

Exam traps

  • Stopping at the static factor of safety. In the worked example 67 760/9810 = 6.91 looks safe, but counting the rope weight, the starting acceleration and the bending load brings it to 5.67.
  • Reading 6×19 as 6 wires and 19 strands. It means 6 strands, with 19 wires in each strand.
  • Using the modulus of steel wire, 2.1 × 10⁵ N/mm², in σb = Erdw/D. The rope modulus, about 0.8 × 10⁵ N/mm², is the one used; the wire value would give 308 N/mm² instead of 117.3 N/mm² in the worked example.
  • Treating lang lay as the default for a free load. Lang lay is more flexible and wears longer, but it tends to untwist; regular lay is the stable default.
  • Mixing area definitions. A = 0.4d² for 6×19 is not the same as A = k(π/4)d²; in that second form k is about 0.51 for the same rope.

Takeaways

  1. A flexible drive trades the rigid grip of gears for reach and tolerance of misalignment; what it costs is slip (belts), a lubrication need (chains) or bending fatigue (ropes).
  2. The whole design of a flat belt comes from T₁/T₂ = eμθ, P = (T₁ − T₂)v and T₁ + Tc ≤ σbt: friction fixes the tension ratio and strength fixes the width.
  3. A V-belt multiplies friction by 1/sin β, roughly three times, so it can work with short centres and small pulleys; the price is that the drive is sold and rated in whole numbers of belts.
  4. Chain drives give an exact velocity ratio because there is no slip, but chordal action, wear at the pin-bush hinge and the need for lubrication are built into the design.
  5. Wire rope is a tension-only member; its strength is the sum of many small wires, and its life is decided as much by sheave diameter as by the breaking load.
  6. Selecting any flexible element means comparing a design load (after service, duty and life factors) with a catalogue rating, then checking the strength condition with a factor of safety.
  7. Misalignment, over-tensioning and under-tensioning cause most belt failures in the field; worn sprockets and dry pins cause most chain failures.
  8. In a wire rope, bending stress repeats every time the rope passes over a sheave, so the factor of safety is counted after the bending load, the rope weight and the starting acceleration, not on the payload alone.

Understand Classify the flexible drives used for power transmission. Compare belts, chains and wire ropes on slip, velocity ratio, centre distance, lubrication and typical applications. 12 marks

Points to cover. Belts: flat, V, round and timing; friction drives (except timing belts) with creep and some slip, no lubrication; Chains: roller, silent, block and detachable; positive drive, exact mean ratio, need lubrication and alignment; Wire ropes: tension members wound on drums or run over sheaves, for hoisting over long lifts and spans; A comparison table on grip, slip, ratio, centre distance, shock, lubrication and use; All three allow distant shafts and some misalignment, which gears do not.

Model answer.

  1. Definition: a flexible drive carries power between shafts through a member that bends round wheels on both shafts: a belt over pulleys, a chain over sprockets, or a rope over drums and sheaves. Nothing rigid joins the shafts, so they can be far apart and slightly out of line.
  2. Belts. Flat belts grip one face by friction and need long centres to get enough arc of contact. V-belts wedge into grooves and grip on two sides, so they work on short centres with small pulleys. Round belts serve light drives such as sewing machines. Timing belts have teeth and do not slip. Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Set-up: a belt on the driving pulley is about to slip. The tension rises from T₂ where the belt arrives to T₁ where it leaves, over the arc θ. Take a small piece of belt PQ that subtends the angle dθ at the pulley centre. The tension at P is T and at Q is T + dT. The rim pushes on the piece with a normal force dN and a friction force μ dN along the belt.
  2. Radial balance (along the line to the centre). Each end tension leans inward by dθ/2:
    dN = T sin(dθ/2) + (T + dT) sin(dθ/2)
   ≈ T dθ        (sin(dθ/2) ≈ dθ/2, and dT × dθ is negligible)
  3. Tangential balance (along the belt). The tension difference is carried by friction:
    (T + dT) cos(dθ/2) − T cos(dθ/2) = μ dN
dT = μ T dθ      (cos(dθ/2) ≈ 1)
  4. Separate and integrate over the arc of contact:
    dT / T = μ dθ
∫ from T₂ to T₁ of dT/T = μ ∫ from 0 to θ of dθ
ln(T₁/T₂) = μθ
T₁/T₂ = exp(μθ)

Model answer.

Apply Explain step by step how the length of an open belt between two pulleys of diameters d and D at centre distance C is found, and how the same geometry gives the angle of lap. Work it for d = 200 mm, D = 600 mm and C = 2 m, and compare a crossed belt. 10 marks

Points to cover. Geometry: the straight spans are tangents; sin α = (D − d)/(2C); Arc on the small pulley π − 2α, on the large pulley π + 2α; Exact length = 2C cos α + (D/2)(π + 2α) + (d/2)(π − 2α); approximation L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D − d)²/(4C); Crossed belt: sin α = (D + d)/(2C), lap π + 2α on both, L = 2C + π(D + d)/2 + (D + d)²/(4C); The smaller lap sets eμθ; a small lap makes the drive slip.

Model answer.

  1. Geometry: draw the two pulleys with centres O₁ (small, radius r = d/2) and O₂ (large, radius R = D/2), a distance C apart. Each straight span of belt is a common tangent.   3. Length: two straight spans plus the two arcs.
    each span = C cos α

Analyze For a given duty, compare flat belt, V-belt and chain drives and justify which one you would choose where the centre distance is short, the speed ratio is high and no slip can be tolerated. 12 marks

Points to cover. Flat belt: needs long centres and a large arc; slips; poor for high ratios; V-belt: wedge gives μ/sin β, works on short centres, but still a friction drive with creep; Chain: positive drive, exact mean ratio, short centres, high ratios with enough teeth; Recommend the chain (or a timing belt at light load); state the costs: lubrication, alignment, chordal action; Support with numbers for the arc and the tension ratio.

Model answer.

  1. The duty sets three tests: short centres, a high ratio, and no slip. A high ratio on short centres makes the arc of contact on the small pulley small, so any friction drive loses grip just where it needs it most.
  2. Effect of a high ratio on short centres. Take d = 100 mm, D = 500 mm (ratio 5) and C = 500 mm:
    sin α = (500 − 100)/(2 × 500) = 0.4      α = 23.58°
θ (small pulley) = 180° − 47.16° = 132.84° = 2.318 rad
  3. Grip that this arc gives with μ = 0.25:
    DriveExponentT₁/T₂Share of T₁ that drives, 1 − T₂/T₁
    Flat beltμθ = 0.5801.7944 %
    V-belt, 2β = 38°μθ/sin β = 1.7805.9383 %
    Chainno friction limitpositive100 % of the rated pull
  4. Flat belt: on 132.84° of arc it can turn only 44 % of its tight-side tension into driving force, so it needs a wide belt and high shaft loads; it also creeps and slips. It fails the short-centre and no-slip tests.
  5. V-belt: the wedge raises the ratio to 5.93, so it works well on short centres and high ratios; this is why V-drives dominate compact industrial drives. But it still creeps and slips on overload, so it fails the no-slip test.
  6. Chain: the rollers seat in the teeth, so the mean ratio is exactly Z₂/Z₁ with no creep or slip. A ratio of 5 needs, for example, Z₁ = 19 and Z₂ = 95; the short centre distance is fine because a chain needs only enough wrap to engage a few teeth. It passes all three tests.
  7. Costs of the chain: it needs lubrication of the pin-bush hinge and a closed case at speed, accurate alignment of the sprockets, and it pulsates by 1 − cos(180°/Z₁), 1.36 % at 19 teeth. Very high ratios with a small sprocket also wrap few teeth, so ratios above about 6 to 7 in one stage are usually split into two.
  8. Alternative: a timing belt is also positive, clean and quiet, and suits light to moderate power at high speed; a chain is preferred at high torque, in heat or in dirt.
  9. Recommendation: a roller chain drive (or a timing belt if the load is light and the place must stay clean), with Z₁ of at least 17 to 19, an even number of links and proper lubrication.
  10. Check: the table’s numbers follow from 0.25 × 2.318 = 0.580 and 0.25/0.3256 × 2.318 = 1.780; e0.580 = 1.79 and e1.780 = 5.93. The V-belt’s advantage is real, but only the chain removes slip entirely.
Evaluate Explain why replacing a flat pulley by a V-grooved pulley greatly increases the power that can be carried for the same belt tension, and discuss the side effects of that change on the drive. 12 marks

Points to cover. Wedge: 2Rn sin β = R, friction μR/sin β, so the exponent becomes μθ/sin β; Numbers: β = 19°, 1/sin β = 3.07; the ratio rises from 2.09 to 9.57 for μ = 0.25, θ = 2.94 rad; Same T₁ carries more power, or the same power needs a smaller T₂ and lower shaft load; Side effects: thicker belt needs larger minimum pulleys; heat and loss from wedging; groove wear; matched sets; Conclusion on when the V-groove is worth it.

Model answer.

  1. Mechanism: on a flat rim the radial push R of the belt equals the normal force, and friction is μR. In a groove of half angle β, the belt touches two sloping flanks. Each pushes back with Rn, and only their radial parts balance R: 2Rn sin β = R. Friction acts on both flanks, so it is 2μRn = μR/sin β. The derivation of the tension ratio is unchanged except that μ becomes μ′ = μ/sin β, giving T₁/T₂ = eμθ/sin β.
  2. Size of the gain: for the usual 38° groove, β = 19° and 1/sin β = 3.07, so the exponent is three times larger. Because the ratio is an exponential, the tension ratio grows far more than three times.
  3. Numbers, μ = 0.25 and θ = 168.52° = 2.941 rad:
    flat:   T₁/T₂ = exp(0.25 × 2.941) = exp(0.735) = 2.09
V:      T₁/T₂ = exp(0.768 × 2.941) = exp(2.258) = 9.57
share of T₁ that drives, 1 − T₂/T₁:   flat 0.521,  V 0.895
  4. Same tension, more power: power is P = T₁(1 − T₂/T₁)v, so for the same safe T₁ and speed the V-groove carries 0.895/0.521 = 1.72 times the power of the flat rim.
  5. Same power, lower shaft load. For 10 kW at 15 m/s, T₁ − T₂ = 666.7 N:
    PulleyT₂ (N)T₁ (N)T₁ + T₂ (N), roughly the shaft load
    Flat613.81280.51894.3
    V-groove77.8744.5822.3
  6. So the V-drive does not load the bearings more for the same power; it needs less initial tension and gives a smaller shaft load. The large wedge forces Rn act inside the pulley rim, not on the shaft.
  7. Side effects that do matter: (1) the V-belt is thick, so it bends harder and needs larger minimum pulley diameters for each section; (2) the belt is wedged in and pulled out of the groove at every turn, which costs some efficiency and heats the belt; (3) the flanks wear, and a worn groove lets the belt bottom, which kills the wedge; (4) one belt carries only a few kilowatts, so drives use several belts, which must be a matched set tensioned together; (5) the groove angle must suit the pulley size (34° to 38°) so the bent belt still bears on both sides.
  8. Conclusion: the V-groove is worth it whenever centres are short or the ratio is high, because it multiplies the usable grip at the cost of a thicker, lossier belt and grooves that must be kept true.
  9. Check: 1/sin 19° = 1/0.3256 = 3.07, 0.25 × 3.07 = 0.768, and 666.7 × 2.086/(2.086 − 1) = 1280.5 N for the flat rim, as in the table (2.086 is the unrounded ratio).
Evaluate A wire rope fails repeatedly in service at the same place near a sheave. Analyse the likely causes and explain how tension, bending, sheave size and lay each affect rope life. 12 marks

Points to cover. Bending fatigue at the sheave: σb = Erdw/D; a small sheave is the first suspect; Tension: rope weight and starting acceleration raise Ft; n = (Fu − Fb)/Ft; Sheave and groove: D/d, groove radius, reverse bends, fleet angle; Lay and construction: regular vs lang lay, 6×19 vs 6×37, core crushing; Other causes: dry or corroded rope, shock from slack rope, damaged sheave; remedies.

Model answer.

  1. Reading the symptom: failure at the same place near a sheave points to the length of rope that bends over that sheave most often, under the highest tension, for example the part that sits on the sheave when the load starts to lift. Broken outer wires there are the classic sign of bending fatigue.
  2. Bending: each pass over a sheave bends the outer wires to the sheave radius, with σb = Erdw/D, and straightens them again: one stress cycle per pass. Take the goods-hoist rope of the worked example (11 mm, 6×19, dw = 0.733 mm, A = 48.4 mm², Fu = 67 760 N, Ft = 10 956 N).
  3. Effect of the sheave diameter on that rope:
    Sheave D (mm)D/dσb (N/mm²)Fb (N)n = (Fu − Fb)/Ft
    50045.5117.356795.67
    30027.3195.694655.32
  4. A 300 mm guide sheave on the same rope raises the bending stress by two thirds and drops below the 30d minimum for 6×19. The static factor barely changes, but fatigue life falls steeply, so a small sheave is the first suspect. A sheave that bends the rope the opposite way (a reverse bend) does even more harm, because each pass gives a full reversal.
  5. Tension: the steady tension adds to the bending stress in every wire. Starting acceleration, rope weight on a long lift and any shock when slack rope is snatched tight raise the peak pull Ft. In the example the starting pull is 10 956 N against 9810 N of payload, an extra 11.7 %.
  6. Sheave groove: a groove that is too narrow pinches and flattens the rope; one that is too wide lets it flatten under load; a worn, corrugated groove scores the wires. A large fleet angle (the rope running onto the sheave at a slant) rubs the rope against the flange. All of these concentrate wear on the length that runs over that sheave.
  7. Lay and construction: lang lay puts a longer length of each outer wire on the sheave and resists bending fatigue and wear better, but it must have both ends fixed. A more flexible construction (6×37, with wires about d/22) lowers σb for the same sheave. A fibre core can be crushed on a small sheave or a drum, letting the strands bite into each other; an IWRC resists that.
  8. Other causes: dry rope (the wires rub and fret inside), corrosion from water or acid fumes, kinks from bad handling, and a seized sheave bearing that drags the rope.
  9. Remedies, in order of effect: (1) enlarge the sheave to at least 30d for 6×19, better 45d; (2) remove reverse bends; (3) correct the groove size and fleet angle; (4) consider a more flexible construction or lang lay where both ends are fixed; (5) start smoothly with the rope taut; (6) keep the rope dressed and inspect that length for broken wires.
  10. Check: σb = 0.8 × 10⁵ × 0.733/300 = 195.6 N/mm² and Fb = 195.6 × 48.4 = 9465 N, so n = (67 760 − 9465)/10 956 = 5.32, as in the table.
Analyze Discuss the modes of failure of a roller chain drive and explain how lubrication and the number of sprocket teeth influence each mode. 12 marks

Points to cover. Link plate fatigue under fluctuating tension; Roller and bush impact fatigue at engagement; Pin and bush wear: pitch elongation, riding up and jumping teeth; Galling of the pin-bush hinge; sprocket tooth wear; Lubrication methods by speed, and the effect of tooth count through chordal action and chain pull.

Model answer.

  1. A chain is a string of hinges loaded in tension. Its failures come from the fluctuating pull, from the impact of each roller on the tooth, and from sliding in the hinges.
  2. Link plate fatigue: the pull rises from near zero on the slack side to full tension on the tight side once per circuit of the chain, so the plates see a fluctuating tensile stress and crack at the holes. This mode limits the rating at low and medium speed.
  3. Roller and bush impact fatigue: each roller strikes a tooth as it enters the sprocket. The impact grows with speed and pitch and with fewer teeth, so this mode limits the rating at high speed.
  4. Pin and bush wear: the pin turns inside the bush under load each time the link enters and leaves a sprocket. Wear there lengthens every pitch, so the chain rides higher on the teeth and finally jumps them. Makers set the replacement limit at about 1.5 % to 3 % elongation.
  5. Galling: at high speed and load the oil film in the hinge breaks down, the pin and bush weld and tear, and the chain seizes quickly.
  6. Sprocket tooth wear: a stretched chain bears on the tip side of the teeth and hooks them, and a worn sprocket in turn wears a new chain fast, so chain and sprockets are replaced together.
  7. Effect of lubrication:
    ModeWhat oil does
    Pin and bush wearSeparates the sliding surfaces; the main defence, wear rises steeply when dry
    GallingKeeps the film unbroken; needs a continuous supply at high speed
    Impact fatigueCushions the roller on the tooth and reduces noise
    Plate fatigueLittle direct effect, but prevents fretting and corrosion that start cracks
  8. Lubrication methods rise with speed: manual or brush oiling for slow drives, drip feed, an oil bath or slinger disc in a closed case, and a pressure spray for high speed. Oil must reach the pin-bush gap, so it is fed onto the inner edges of the link plates on the slack side.
  9. Effect of the number of teeth on the small sprocket (08B chain, 3 kW at 1000 rpm):
    Z₁d₁ (mm)Speed variationChain pull F = 2T/d₁ (N)
    1145.084.05 %1271
    1977.161.36 %743
  10. Few teeth raise both the impact and the chain pull: the 11-tooth sprocket gives three times the speed pulsation and 71 % more pull than the 19-tooth one, and each link also turns through a larger angle (360°/Z) in the hinge, so wear per engagement rises. Hence at least 17 teeth, and 19 or more for smooth running.
  11. Check: the torque of 3 kW at 1000 rpm is 3000/(2π × 1000/60) = 28.65 N·m, and 2 × 28.65/0.04508 = 1271 N and 2 × 28.65/0.07716 = 743 N; 1271/743 = 1.71.
Evaluate A plant must choose between a chain drive and a V-belt drive for a crusher that has heavy starting torque and shock. Evaluate both options and recommend one with reasons. 12 marks

Points to cover. Crusher duty: shock, overload, jamming, dust; the drive should protect the machine and motor; V-belt: elastic, damps shock, slips on a jam (a mechanical fuse), no lubrication, tolerant of dust; Chain: positive, passes shock to shafts and bearings, needs oil and a sealed case in dust, exact ratio not needed; Design consequences: service factor about 1.5 or more, number of belts, shaft loads; Recommend the V-belt drive and state when the chain would be chosen instead.

Model answer.

  1. What the crusher duty demands: a jaw or cone crusher starts under heavy load, takes repeated shocks as rock breaks, and can jam outright. The drive should start the machine, survive the shocks, and protect the motor and the shafts when it jams. Dust is everywhere. An exact speed ratio is not needed.
  2. V-belt drive: rubber belts are elastic, so each shock is softened before it reaches the motor. On a jam the belts slip and squeal instead of breaking a shaft, which is the cheapest overload protection there is. They need no lubrication, tolerate dust, and are quick to replace. Their costs: some creep, a large shaft load from belt tension, and several belts of a matched set.
  3. Chain drive: a chain is positive and stiff, so every shock goes straight into the shafts, bearings and motor, and a jam stalls the motor or breaks the weakest part unless a shear pin or slip clutch is added. In dust it needs a sealed case and an oil bath, or it wears fast. Its exact ratio and compactness are no advantage here.
  4. Comparison for this duty:
    CriterionV-beltChain
    Shock absorptionGood (elastic belts)Poor (positive and stiff)
    Overload or jamSlips; acts as a fuseNeeds a shear pin or slip clutch
    DustTolerantNeeds a sealed case and oil
    MaintenanceRe-tension, replace setsLubricate, re-tension, replace with sprockets
    Exact ratioNo, not neededYes, not needed
    EfficiencySlightly lowerSlightly higher
  5. Design consequence for the V-belt drive. With the B-section rating of the worked example (2.149 kW per belt after the arc and length factors), a 7.5 kW motor gives:
    smooth compressor, service factor 1.2:  Pd = 9.0 kW   z = 9.0/2.149 = 4.19 → 5 belts
crusher, service factor 1.5:          Pd = 11.25 kW  z = 11.25/2.149 = 5.24 → 6 belts
  6. So the shock duty costs one extra belt, a small price. If the count went above about 8, the designer would move to a larger section or larger pulleys.
  7. Recommendation: a V-belt drive with a heavy-duty service factor (1.5 or more, from the maker’s table), a matched set of belts, an adjustable motor base for tensioning, and a belt guard. A flywheel on the crusher shaft, which crushers often carry, can serve as the large pulley.
  8. When the chain would win: if the crusher were inside a hot, oily enclosure where rubber would perish, or if the shafts had to keep exact timing with another machine; then a chain with a shear-pin sprocket or a slip clutch would be used.
  9. Check: 1.5 × 7.5 = 11.25 kW and 11.25/2.149 = 5.24, so 6 belts carry 6 × 2.149 = 12.9 kW ≥ 11.25 kW, while 5 belts would carry only 10.7 kW.

Glossary

Angle of contact
The arc of the pulley or sheave actually covered by the belt or rope; it is used in radians in the tension ratio.
Belt
An endless flexible band that transmits power between two pulleys by friction.
Breaking load
The tensile load at which a chain, rope or belt actually fractures in a pull test.
Centrifugal tension
The tension induced in a moving belt or rope by its own mass as it travels round the pulley.
Chain
A series of pin-jointed links, usually carrying rollers, that transmits power by engaging the teeth of two sprockets.
Chordal action
The periodic variation in chain speed caused by the chain wrapping a sprocket as a polygon rather than a circle.
Coefficient of friction μ
The ratio of the limiting friction force to the normal reaction between belt and pulley.
Core
The central member of a wire rope, of fibre or steel, that supports the strands and holds lubricant.
Creep
The small relative motion between belt and pulley caused by unequal elastic stretch on the two sides.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure load or stress to the working load or stress, applied to cover uncertainty and duty.
Flat belt
A belt of rectangular cross-section that runs on a flat or slightly crowned pulley rim.
Groove angle
The included angle of a V-belt pulley groove, 34° to 38°, a little less than the 40° of the belt, which sets the wedging gain.
Idler pulley
A small extra pulley used to increase the angle of lap or to take up slack in a belt drive.
IWRC
Independent wire rope core: a small wire rope used as the core of a larger one, for strength and resistance to crushing.
Lang lay
A rope lay in which the wires and the strands are twisted the same way; flexible and hard-wearing, but it tends to untwist under a free load.
Lay
The helical pattern and direction in which wires and strands are twisted to form a rope.
Pitch (chain)
The centre-to-centre distance between two adjacent chain rollers, measured along the chain.
Pitch circle diameter
The diameter of the circle on which the chain rollers or belt centre line sit when wrapped on the sprocket or pulley.
Service factor
A multiplier applied to the transmitted power to account for the nature of the driven machine and its hours of duty.
Sheave
A grooved wheel over which a rope runs, used in hoists and rope drives.
Slip
Gross relative sliding between belt and pulley that occurs when the required friction exceeds the available friction.
Splice
The joint by which a flat belt is made endless, by lacing, metal fasteners, or cementing and vulcanising.
Sprocket
A toothed wheel whose teeth engage the chain rollers and so drive it without slip.
Strand
A group of wires twisted together in a helix, several of which are laid up to form a wire rope.
V-belt
A belt of trapezoidal cross-section that wedges into a grooved pulley to gain extra friction.
Wire rope
A tension member made by twisting steel wires into strands and strands into a rope around a core.
