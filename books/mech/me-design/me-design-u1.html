1.1 The design process and factors influencing machine design

Machine design is the route from a need to a drawing that a workshop can make, inspect and sell. A need appears (a workshop must hang a ceiling fan from a brick wall, a pump maker needs a shaft that survives a restart after a Chennai power cut) and the designer turns it into sizes, materials, tolerances and a cost. The route is a loop, not a line: every stage can send the team back a step, and a fault found on paper costs a pencil stroke while the same fault found in the field costs a recall. Every stage balances function, strength, cost and ease of making.

The general design procedure

Design starts with a need, not a formula. Someone wants a part that does a job at a price: transmit 5 kW at 900 rpm for ten years, for under ₹4,000. Some syllabi call this topic the introduction to the design process; most list the same stages.

  1. Recognition of need: a customer, a market survey or a breakdown report shows a job done badly or at high cost.
  2. Specification: the need becomes numbers: function, load, speed, life, size limits, cost ceiling and the codes to follow.
  3. Synthesis (concept): two or three working principles or mechanisms are sketched and compared. Most of the final cost is fixed here.
  4. Analysis of forces: the force, moment or torque on each element is found from the mechanism and its duty.
  5. Selection of material and design of elements: each part gets a material, then its sizes from the stress and deflection checks.
  6. Modification: sizes are rounded to standard values and changed for manufacture, assembly, appearance and cost.
  7. Detailed drawing and prototype test: every dimension, tolerance and surface finish is frozen; one or a few pieces are built and measured.
  8. Production: drawings, process sheets and inspection plans go to the shop floor.

Figure 1.1.1 draws this order with the feedback arrows that make it a loop. A prototype that cracks, a cost that overshoots the ceiling or a welder who cannot make the chosen joint sends the team back, sometimes to the concept, sometimes only to the detail. Returning early is cheap; returning after the tooling is cut is very costly. Good teams therefore test the risky idea first.

C is the cost per piece (₹). Cmat, Cmach, Cweld and Cover are the material, machining, welding and overhead (rent, power, supervision, inspection) charges per piece (₹). Csetup is the one-time cost of a pattern, die or jig (₹), and N is the number of pieces made. Only the last term is divided by N.

The division by N is why the batch size changes the answer. A ₹60,000 casting pattern adds ₹300 per piece when N = 200 and only ₹12 when N = 5,000. So casting wins in large batches, while fabrication from bar or rolled sections, which needs only a cheap jig, wins in small ones.

The specification is the exit from the loop

A loop with no exit is churn. The specification is the exit: every revision is judged against the same written targets, so the team knows when to stop. It states the function in numbers (carry 8 kg, deflect less than 2 mm, last 5 years), the limits (envelope, mass, cost, codes), and how each target will be verified: a calculation, a bench test or inspection of the first piece.

The later chapters of this unit supply the tools for the analysis and detail stages: how loads vary with time (static, dynamic and impact loads), the stress equations, and the strength and stiffness checks.

Worked example

Question. A small workshop in Chennai must supply wall brackets for an 8 kg ceiling fan. The bracket must last 5 years, the order is 200 pieces, the shop has manual milling and arc welding, and the selling price is ₹250 per bracket. (a) List the design stages for this bracket. (b) Find the dead load in newtons. (c) Per bracket, material costs ₹105, machining ₹55, welding ₹40 and overhead ₹30, and a drilling jig costs ₹2,000 once. Find the cost per bracket, the margin per bracket and the profit on the order.

  1. Need: a fan must hang safely from a brick wall for 5 years. Specification: mass 8 kg, life 5 years, 200 pieces, ₹250 each, mild vibration, coastal air.
  2. Concept: compare a bent flat bar, a rolled angle and a welded T. Analysis and detail: pick the angle, fix its section, the bolt holes, the weld size and the paint. Prototype: hang one bracket with 8 kg, measure the deflection and shake it. Production: drawing, jig, first-piece inspection.
  3. Dead load: W = m × g = 8 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 78.48 N. Here m is the fan mass and g = 9.81 m/s². This force, not the 8 kg, goes into every later stress formula.
  4. Direct cost per bracket: 105 + 55 + 40 + 30 = ₹230.
  5. Jig share per bracket: Csetup/N = 2000/200 = ₹10. Cost per bracket C = 230 + 10 = ₹240.
  6. Margin per bracket: 250 − 240 = ₹10. Order value: 200 × 250 = ₹50,000; profit: 200 × 10 = ₹2,000.

Answer. Dead load 78.48 N; cost ₹240 per bracket (₹230 direct plus ₹10 jig share); margin ₹10 per bracket, ₹2,000 on an order worth ₹50,000.

Worked example 2

Question. The same bracket could be cast instead. A pattern costs ₹60,000 once, and a cast bracket then costs ₹120 to cast, machine and finish. The fabricated bracket of the first example costs ₹230 per piece plus a ₹2,000 jig. Find the batch size above which casting is cheaper, and the cost per piece of each route at 200 and 5,000 pieces.

  1. Total cost of N cast brackets: 60,000 + 120N. Total cost of N fabricated brackets: 2,000 + 230N.
  2. Break-even when the totals are equal: 60,000 + 120N = 2,000 + 230N, so 110N = 58,000 and N = 527.3. Casting is cheaper from 528 pieces up.
  3. At N = 200: cast 120 + 60,000/200 = ₹420; fabricated 230 + 2,000/200 = ₹240. Fabrication wins.
  4. At N = 5,000: cast 120 + 60,000/5,000 = ₹132; fabricated 230 + 2,000/5,000 = ₹230.40. Casting wins by about ₹98 a piece.

Answer. Break-even at about 528 pieces. For the 200-piece order fabrication (₹240) beats casting (₹420); at 5,000 pieces casting (₹132) beats fabrication (₹230.40).

Exam traps

  • Drawing the stages as a straight line. An answer that shows the feedback arrows (test back to concept, cost back to detail) scores more.
  • Assuming the stronger material is always better. Stiffness, mass, weldability and cost usually decide, not ultimate strength.
  • Calling the stress calculation "the design". It is one step, the design of elements, inside a longer procedure.
  • Dividing the whole cost by the batch size. Only the one-time setup cost is shared over N pieces; material and machining are paid on every piece.
  • Mixing mass and weight: 8 kg is a mass; the load is 78.48 N.

1.2 Selection of materials based on mechanical properties

Choosing a material means matching what the part must do to the numbers a material offers. A tie that must not stretch permanently needs yield strength, a lever that must not bend out of line needs stiffness, a bush that slides needs hardness, and a hook that may be jerked needs toughness. No single number decides: the designer screens candidates on the properties the part needs, sizes the part in each, and then compares mass and cost. The same reasoning picks mild steel for a fan bracket, cast iron for a lathe bed and aluminium alloy for a bicycle crank.

You need the factors from the design process.

Mechanical properties a designer reads

A tensile test, pulling a bar until it breaks, gives most of them.

  • Yield strength Syt (MPa): the stress at which permanent deformation starts. A ductile part is designed against it.
  • Ultimate tensile strength Sut (MPa): the highest stress the bar carries. A brittle part, which breaks without yielding, is designed against it.
  • Modulus of elasticity E (GPa): stress divided by strain in the elastic range. It sets stiffness, not strength. All steels have E of about 200 to 210 GPa, so heat treatment that triples the strength leaves the deflection unchanged.
  • Ductility: the percentage elongation at fracture. Above about 5 % the material is called ductile (mild steel, about 20 to 25 %); below it, brittle (grey cast iron, under 1 %).
  • Toughness (J): the energy absorbed up to fracture, the area under the whole stress-strain curve; needed for shock loads. Resilience is the energy stored elastically, Syt²/(2E) per unit volume.
  • Hardness (Brinell HB, Vickers HV, Rockwell): resistance to indentation, a guide to wear resistance.
  • Fatigue strength for repeated loads and creep strength for long service at high temperature; see failure theories and fatigue design.

Beside these the designer checks density ρ (kg/m³) for mass, corrosion resistance, machinability, weldability, castability and cost (₹/kg).

Indian standards name steels by composition. In 40C8 the 40 means 0.40 % carbon (in hundredths of a per cent) and the 8 means about 0.8 % manganese (ten times the manganese content); an alloy steel such as 40Cr4 has 0.40 % carbon and about 1 % chromium. Grey cast iron FG 200 has a minimum tensile strength of 200 MPa, and spheroidal graphite iron SG 500/7 has 500 MPa with 7 % elongation. Look up the property values for a grade in your design data book; the numbers in this chapter are typical values for teaching.

Selecting: screen, size, rank

  1. Write the load, the failure mode (yield, fracture, wear, deflection), the environment and the number to be made.
  2. Screen out materials that cannot work: a brittle material for a shock-loaded hook, plain steel in sea water without a coating.
  3. Size the part in each remaining material, once for strength and once for stiffness; the larger section governs.
  4. Compare the sized parts on mass and cost, then on making and finishing.

A light tie of given strength needs the largest Syt/ρ (specific strength); one of given stiffness needs the largest E/ρ (specific stiffness). Steel and aluminium have almost the same E/ρ (25.5 and 25.6 GPa per tonne/m³), so a stiffness-limited tie is no lighter in aluminium. Only a few syllabi teach these material indices.

The weighted-point method scores each candidate on each property out of 100: for a larger-is-better property, 100 × value/best value; for cost, 100 × lowest cost/its cost. Each score is multiplied by a weight (the weights add up to 1) and the products are summed. The highest total wins, provided no single property failed the screen.

Worked example

Question. A tie rod 500 mm long carries a steady pull of 20 kN. It must have a factor of safety of 2 on yield and must not stretch more than 0.6 mm. Three materials are offered: mild steel (Syt = 250 MPa, E = 200 GPa, ρ = 7850 kg/m³, ₹60/kg), aluminium alloy 6061-T6 (276 MPa, 69 GPa, 2700 kg/m³, ₹300/kg) and a quenched and tempered alloy steel (650 MPa, 200 GPa, 7850 kg/m³, ₹120/kg). Size the rod in each material, and choose the material for the lowest mass and for the lowest cost.

  1. Strength: the allowable stress is Syt/2, so the area needed is A = 2F/Syt. With F = 20,000 N: mild steel 40,000/250 = 160.0 mm²; aluminium 40,000/276 = 144.9 mm²; alloy steel 40,000/650 = 61.5 mm².
  2. Stiffness: the extension is δ = FL/(AE) ≤ 0.6 mm, so A ≥ FL/(0.6E) = 20,000 × 500/(0.6E). Steels: 10,000,000/(0.6 × 200,000) = 83.3 mm²; aluminium: 10,000,000/(0.6 × 69,000) = 241.5 mm².
  3. Governing area (the larger of the two): mild steel 160.0 mm² (strength); aluminium 241.5 mm² (stiffness); alloy steel 83.3 mm² (stiffness).
  4. Mass m = ρAL with A in m² and L = 0.5 m. Mild steel: 7850 × 160.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.5 = 0.628 kg. Aluminium: 2700 × 241.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.5 = 0.326 kg. Alloy steel: 7850 × 83.3 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.5 = 0.327 kg.
  5. Cost: mild steel 0.628 × 60 = ₹37.7; aluminium 0.326 × 300 = ₹97.8; alloy steel 0.327 × 120 = ₹39.2.
  6. Check: at its strength size of 144.9 mm², aluminium would stretch 20,000 × 500/(144.9 × 69,000) = 1.00 mm, which is why stiffness governs there; mild steel at 160 mm² stretches only 0.31 mm.

Answer. Mild steel needs 160 mm² (0.628 kg, ₹37.7), aluminium 241.5 mm² (0.326 kg, ₹97.8) and alloy steel 83.3 mm² (0.327 kg, ₹39.2). Aluminium is lightest by only 1 g; alloy steel is almost as light at about a third of its cost, so alloy steel is the sensible choice, with mild steel if mass does not matter.

Worked example 2

Question. A bracket for a coastal pump house can be mild steel, aluminium alloy 6061-T6 or stainless steel 304. Data: yield strength 250, 276 and 215 MPa; E 200, 69 and 193 GPa; cost ₹60, ₹300 and ₹250 per kg; corrosion rating out of 5: 1, 4 and 5. Weights: strength 0.25, stiffness 0.15, cost 0.35, corrosion 0.25. Rank the materials by the weighted-point method.

  1. Scaled strength (best 276): mild steel 100 × 250/276 = 90.6; aluminium 100; stainless 100 × 215/276 = 77.9.
  2. Scaled stiffness (best 200): 100; 100 × 69/200 = 34.5; 100 × 193/200 = 96.5.
  3. Scaled cost (lowest ₹60): 100; 100 × 60/300 = 20.0; 100 × 60/250 = 24.0.
  4. Scaled corrosion (best 5): 100 × 1/5 = 20; 80; 100.
  5. Mild steel: 0.25 × 90.6 + 0.15 × 100 + 0.35 × 100 + 0.25 × 20 = 22.65 + 15 + 35 + 5 = 77.6.

Exam traps

  • Raising strength to cut deflection. Heat treatment raises Syt but not E, so a stiffness-limited steel part does not get stiffer.
  • Designing a ductile part on Sut, or a brittle part on a yield strength it does not have. Grey cast iron has no clear yield point.
  • Confusing hardness with toughness. A hardened file is hard but brittle; a mild steel hook is soft but tough.
  • Comparing materials by strength alone. Size the part in each, then compare mass and cost, as in the tie-rod example.
  • Reading 40C8 as 40 % carbon. It is 0.40 % carbon and about 0.8 % manganese.

1.3 Preferred numbers, fits and tolerances

Interchangeable parts need agreed sizes. Preferred numbers fix the standard sizes and ratings (shaft diameters, motor powers, press capacities) so that factories make a few well-chosen variants instead of hundreds. Fits and tolerances fix how much a size may vary and how a shaft sits in its hole, with a planned clearance or grip. A 25 mm shaft made in Coimbatore must enter a 25 mm hole bored in Pune. This chapter gives the series, the vocabulary, the ISO code such as H7/g6 and how its limits are calculated.

Preferred numbers set standard sizes

Preferred numbers, first proposed by Charles Renard and standardised as ISO 3, are geometric series: each term is the previous one times a fixed ratio. The basic series are R5, R10, R20 and R40, with 5, 10, 20 and 40 terms in each decade (1 to 10, 10 to 100, and so on).

q = ¹⁰√10 for R10; in general q = 101/n, and the terms are 10i/n rounded

q is the step ratio; n = 5, 10, 20 or 40; i = 0, 1, 2, …, n. R5: q = 1.585. R10: q = 1.259. R20: q = 1.122. R40: q = 1.059.

Geometric spacing gives equal relative steps: about 26 % between R10 terms whether the size is 10 mm or 100 mm. Small sizes get fine choices, large sizes coarse ones, and each coarser series is part of the finer one, so R5 sizes are also R10 sizes. A derived series takes every p-th term of a basic series: R10/3 takes every third term of R10, giving 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 (ratio about 2). A 25 mm shaft is a preferred size; 24.8 mm is not.

Basic size, limits, tolerance and deviation

The basic size D is the size named on the drawing, 25 mm in the example. No machinist can hit D exactly at an affordable cost, so the drawing gives an upper limit and a lower limit, the largest and smallest acceptable sizes.

Tolerance = upper limit − lower limit

The tolerance is the full permitted band, in mm or μm (1 μm = 0.001 mm). A size 25 ± 0.010 mm has limits 25.010 and 24.990 mm and a tolerance of 0.020 mm, not 0.010 mm.

A deviation is a limit minus the basic size, with its sign. For a hole the upper and lower deviations are written ES and EI; for a shaft, es and ei (capitals for holes, small letters for shafts). The fundamental deviation is the one nearer the basic size; its letter (H, g, p…) places the tolerance zone, and the IT grade number (IT01 to IT18) sets the width of the zone. A smaller IT number is a tighter tolerance.

i = 0.45 ∛D + 0.001D (μm); IT6 = 10i, IT7 = 16i, IT8 = 25i

i is the standard tolerance unit in μm; D (mm) is the geometric mean of the diameter step that contains the basic size (for 18 to 30 mm, D = √(18 × 30) = 23.24 mm). For the shaft letters the standard gives fundamental deviations such as es = −2.5D0.34 μm for g and es = −5.5D0.41 μm for f; letter h has es = 0 and hole H has EI = 0.

Fits and the hole-basis system

A fit is the relation between a hole and the shaft in it. In a clearance fit the smallest hole is never smaller than the largest shaft, so the clearance is never negative (H7/h6 has a minimum clearance of exactly zero). In an interference fit the smallest shaft is always larger than the largest hole, so the parts must be pressed or shrunk together. In a transition fit the zones overlap and one pair may have a small clearance and the next a small interference.

Allowance is the planned difference at the tightest assembly: smallest hole minus largest shaft. It is the minimum clearance, or (if negative) the maximum interference. Clearance itself is the actual gap in one assembled pair and lies between the minimum and maximum clearance.

In the hole-basis system every hole is H (EI = 0, so the smallest hole is the basic size) and the shaft letter sets the fit; it is preferred because a few standard reamers and plug gauges make every hole. In the shaft-basis system every shaft is h (es = 0) and the hole letter is varied; it suits long bright bars with many parts on them. A fit is always written hole first: H7/g6 is hole-basis, G7/h6 is shaft-basis.

Worked example

Question. A firm makes six sizes of hydraulic press from 10 kN to 100 kN. Find the capacities from a preferred-number series.

  1. Six sizes from 10 to 100 kN means five equal ratio steps: q⁵ = 100/10 = 10, so q = 101/5 = 1.585. This is the R5 ratio.
  2. Terms: 10, 10 × 1.585 = 15.85, × 1.585 = 25.12, then 39.81, 63.10, 100.
  3. Round to the R5 values: 10, 16, 25, 40, 63, 100 kN.

Answer. 10, 16, 25, 40, 63 and 100 kN (the R5 series).

Worked example 2

Question. Calculate the limits of the fit 25 H7/g6. The diameter step is 18 to 30 mm; use i = 0.45∛D + 0.001D, IT7 = 16i, IT6 = 10i and, for g, es = −2.5D0.34 (all in μm). Find the maximum and minimum clearance and name the type of fit.

  1. Geometric mean of the step: D = √(18 × 30) = 23.24 mm.
  2. Tolerance unit: ∛23.24 = 2.854, so i = 0.45 × 2.854 + 0.001 × 23.24 = 1.284 + 0.023 = 1.307 μm.
  3. Hole tolerance IT7 = 16 × 1.307 = 20.9 ≈ 21 μm. Shaft tolerance IT6 = 10 × 1.307 = 13.1 ≈ 13 μm.
  4. Hole H: EI = 0, ES = +21 μm. Limits 25.000 mm and 25.021 mm.
  5. Shaft g: es = −2.5 × 23.240.34 = −2.5 × 2.914 = −7.3 ≈ −7 μm, and ei = es − IT6 = −7 − 13 = −20 μm. Limits 24.993 mm and 24.980 mm.
  6. Maximum clearance = largest hole − smallest shaft = 25.021 − 24.980 = 0.041 mm. Minimum clearance (the allowance) = smallest hole − largest shaft = 25.000 − 24.993 = 0.007 mm.

Exam traps

  • Tolerance is the full band, not the ± value: 25 ± 0.010 mm has a tolerance of 0.020 mm.
  • Writing the shaft first. ISO always writes the hole first: H7/g6, G7/h6. A capital letter is always the hole.

1.4 Factor of safety and design codes

A factor of safety is the number that turns a measured strength into the stress a designer allows. It covers loads that are only estimated, strengths that scatter from batch to batch, simplified stress formulas and the cost of a failure. Design codes and standards are the published rulebooks that fix minimum factors, methods and materials, so that two engineers sizing the same part reach the same defensible answer. Before either is chosen, the designer must know how the part can fail.

You need the properties from material selection.

Modes of failure

A part has failed when it can no longer do its job safely, which can happen long before it breaks.

  • Yielding: a ductile part deforms permanently when stress reaches Syt; a bent lever is a failure.
  • Fracture: a brittle part (cast iron) breaks suddenly near Sut without warning.
  • Excessive deflection: a shaft or frame bends or twists enough to misalign gears or spoil accuracy, while still strong.
  • Fatigue: a crack grows under repeated stress far below Sut; see failure theories and fatigue design.
  • Buckling, wear, corrosion and creep: a slender strut collapses sideways; surfaces wear away; rust thins a section; a hot part stretches slowly with time.

The mode fixes which strength goes on top of the factor of safety.

Factor of safety and allowable stress

n = failure stress/allowable stress; σallow = Syt/n (ductile) or Sut/n (brittle)

n is the factor of safety (no unit, n > 1); Syt and Sut are the yield and ultimate tensile strengths (MPa); σallow is the allowable or permissible stress (MPa). The part is safe when the working stress σ ≤ σallow, that is, when the actual factor of safety S/σ is at least the required n.

  1. Choose the strength for the failure mode: Syt for a ductile part, Sut for a brittle one.
  2. Choose the required n from a code or from judgement (below).
  3. Find σallow = S/n.
  4. Find the working stress from the load and section (see direct, bending and torsional stresses).
  5. Compare; if σ > σallow, enlarge the section or change the material.

The factor grows with uncertainty. Typical textbook values: steady load on ductile steel, 1.5 to 2 on yield; brittle materials, 3 to 4 on ultimate (the strength scatters and failure gives no warning); shock loads, 3 or more; lifting equipment and pressure vessels, fixed by their codes. A larger n is not free: it means a heavier and costlier part. The margin of safety is n − 1, so n = 2.5 is a margin of 1.5.

Design codes and standards

A standard fixes agreed sizes, materials, tests and dimensions so that parts from different makers fit and behave alike; a code is a standard that sets design rules for safety: load cases, stress methods, minimum factors, tests and marking. A code is followed because law, a contract or an inspector requires it. Examples: the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code; ISO 286 for limits and fits and ISO 3 for preferred numbers; and the Indian Standards of the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), such as IS 1570 for the designation of steels, IS 919 for limits and fits and IS 2062 for structural steel. Some syllabi call this exposure to standards.

Codes may replace one factor of safety with partial factors: the nominal resistance R is reduced by a resistance factor φ ≤ 1 and each load effect Q is raised by a load factor γ ≥ 1, and the check becomes φR ≥ ΣγQ. Loads and strengths scatter differently (a dead weight is better known than wind), so splitting the margin keeps the reliability even. Codes are the minimum, they lag new materials, and they cover only the failure modes they list: a code-compliant part can still fail by fatigue, corrosion or misuse.

Worked example

Question. A ductile steel tie of cross-section 250 mm² carries a steady pull of 45 kN. Syt = 280 MPa and the required factor of safety is 2.5 on yield. Find the allowable stress and the working stress, decide whether the tie is safe, and find the smallest standard round bar (20, 22, 25 mm) that is.

  1. Units: 1 MPa = 1 N/mm²; F = 45 kN = 45,000 N.
  2. Allowable stress: σallow = 280/2.5 = 112 MPa.
  3. Working stress: σ = F/A = 45,000/250 = 180 MPa.

  5. Area needed: A = F/σallow = 45,000/112 = 401.8 mm², so d = √(4A/π) = √(4 × 401.8/π) = 22.6 mm.
  6. Bars: 20 mm gives 314.2 mm² and 22 mm gives 380.1 mm², both too small; 25 mm gives 490.9 mm², stress 45,000/490.9 = 91.7 MPa, actual n = 280/91.7 = 3.05.

Answer. σallow = 112 MPa, working stress 180 MPa, so the 250 mm² tie is unsafe; at least 402 mm² is needed, and a 25 mm bar (91.7 MPa, n = 3.05) is the smallest that passes.

  • Mixing kN and MPa: 45 kN on 250 mm² is 45,000/250 = 180 MPa, not 0.18 MPa.
  • Confusing the factor of safety with the margin: n = 2.5 is a margin of 1.5 (150 %).
  • Treating a code as a factor of safety. A code is a document; it may fix n, or replace it by partial factors.
  • Assuming a large n makes any design safe. If the wrong failure mode was checked (yield instead of deflection or fatigue), the factor protects nothing.

1.5 Static, dynamic and impact loads

Every load on a machine part changes with time in its own way. It may be steady, it may repeat or reverse thousands of times, or it may arrive as a sudden blow. The time history decides the analysis: a static stress check for a steady load, a fatigue check for a repeated one, and an energy balance for an impact. This chapter sorts loads into the three kinds and shows why a load applied suddenly doubles the stress, while a weight dropped from a small height can multiply it hundreds of times.

You need σ = P/A and δ = PL/(AE) from direct stresses.

Three load histories

  • Static load: its size and direction stay constant, or change so slowly that inertia is negligible. A water tank on a roof, a lift hanging at rest on its rope.
  • Dynamic (variable) load: its size or direction changes with time, often in a repeating cycle. A gear tooth loaded once per turn, a connecting rod pushed and pulled every stroke, a rotating shaft whose bending stress reverses each revolution. Repeated stress causes fatigue: see failure theories and fatigue design.
  • Impact (shock) load: a large force for a very short time, usually because one body strikes another. A hammer blow, a railway buffer, a car wheel hitting a pothole, a rope catching a falling load.

The same part may see all three. A crane hook carries a static load while the load hangs still, a dynamic load while the crane travels, and an impact when a slack rope suddenly takes the load. Figure 1.5.1 draws the three histories side by side.

Suddenly applied load: twice the stress

Hang a weight W on a bar gradually, so that the force grows from 0 to W. The work done, ½Wδ, is stored as strain energy and the stress is σ = W/A, where A is the area of the bar.

Now let the same weight act suddenly at full value with no drop (it is held just touching the collar and released). The weight does work Wδmax as the bar stretches, but the bar stores only ½Pmaxδmax, where Pmax is the largest force in the bar. Equating, Pmax = 2W, so σmax = 2W/A. A suddenly applied load gives twice the stress and twice the extension of the same load applied gradually; the bar then oscillates about the static position.

Falling weight: the energy method

Let W fall through a height h onto a collar at the end of a bar of length L, area A and modulus E. At the lowest point the weight has lost potential energy W(h + δmax), and the bar stores σmax²AL/(2E). With δmax = σmaxL/E, the energy balance is a quadratic in σmax whose positive root gives the impact factor.

k = σmax/σst = 1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)

k is the impact factor (no unit); σst = W/A is the static stress and δst = WL/(AE) the static extension, both for the weight applied gradually; h is the height of fall (same unit as δst). For h = 0, k = 2, the sudden-load result. When h is much larger than δst, k ≈ √(2h/δst), and then σmax ≈ √(2EWh/(AL)).

  1. Convert everything to N and mm (stress in MPa).
  2. Find σst = W/A and δst = WL/(AE).
  3. Find k = 1 + √(1 + 2h/δst), then σmax = kσst and δmax = kδst.
  4. Check the energy: W(h + δmax) should equal σmax²AL/(2E).

The last formula shows two surprises. A longer bar lowers the impact stress, because it stores the same energy at a lower stress, and a larger area lowers it only as 1/√A, not 1/A: what counts is the volume AL that works at full stress. That is why a bolt that takes shock has its shank turned down to the root diameter of the thread: the whole length then works at the same stress and stores more energy before the threads reach their limit. Impact design with springs and energy absorbers continues in the chapter on impact and shock loading.

Worked example

Question. A 10 kg weight falls 100 mm onto a collar at the lower end of a vertical steel bar 1 m long, 500 mm² in area, E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum stress and extension, and compare them with the gradually and suddenly applied cases. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

1.6 Direct, bending and torsional stresses

Cut a loaded machine member anywhere and the section must carry an internal axial force P, shear force V, bending moment M and torque T. Each makes its own stress pattern: P a uniform normal stress, V a shear stress, M a normal stress that grows linearly from the neutral axis, and T a shear stress that grows from the shaft axis to the surface. The four formulas σ = P/A, τ = V/A, σ = My/I and τ = Tr/J size a first trial section for a shaft, pin, lever or bolt, and every later check in machine design builds on them.

Direct stresses: tension, compression and shear

σ = P/A and τ = V/A

σ is the direct normal stress (MPa = N/mm²); P is the axial force along the centroidal axis (N); A is the area resisting it (mm²). Tension is positive, compression negative. τ is the average shear stress (MPa); V is the shear force (N) and A the area that would slide.

The normal stress is uniform only away from load points, holes and grooves; near them the stress rises (stress concentration, see failure theories and fatigue design). Use the net area for a plate with a hole or the core area for a threaded rod. A pin that passes through a fork is in double shear: it must shear across two sections at once, so A = 2 × πd²/4.

Bending stress

A bending moment M (N·mm) stretches the fibres on one side of a beam and shortens those on the other. Between them lies the neutral axis, where the bending stress is zero; for pure bending it passes through the centroid of the section.

σb = My/I; σb,max = Mc/I = M/Z

y is the distance of a fibre from the neutral axis (mm); I = ∫y² dA is the second moment of area (mm⁴); c is the distance to the outermost fibre (mm); Z = I/c is the section modulus (mm³). Rectangle b wide and h deep: I = bh³/12, Z = bh²/6. Solid circle of diameter d: I = πd⁴/64, Z = πd³/32.

The formula assumes a straight beam of linear elastic material, small deflections and plane sections that remain plane. A transverse shear force V also gives a shear stress across the section that is zero at the outer fibres and largest at the neutral axis: 1.5V/A for a rectangle and (4/3)V/A for a circle. It is usually small beside the bending stress except in short, deep members. In a deeply curved member the neutral axis shifts and the stress is no longer linear; see curved beams.

Torsional shear stress

A torque T (N·mm) twists a circular shaft. Each cross-section rotates as a rigid disc, and the shear stress grows linearly from zero on the axis to its largest value at the surface.

τ = Tr/J; τmax = T(d/2)/J = 16T/(πd³)

r is the radial distance from the axis (mm), the same r over which J = ∫r² dA is taken; J is the polar second moment of area (mm⁴): πd⁴/32 for a solid shaft, π(do⁴ − di⁴)/32 for a hollow one with outer and inner diameters do and di. J/(d/2) = πd³/16 is the polar section modulus Zp. For a solid circle J = 2I.

A shaft transmitting power Pw (W) at N rpm carries T = 60Pw/(2πN) in N·m. Torsional stress is shear and bending stress is normal, so they cannot be added; they are combined through principal stresses in combined loading. 1.7 Combined and eccentric loading; principal stresses

Real machine parts rarely carry one kind of stress. A shaft with a pulley is bent and twisted at the same point; a bracket with an offset load is pulled and bent at once. At a point, the stresses from each load add (superposition) into one stress state, and that state is then turned to find the principal stresses: the largest and smallest normal stresses at the point, on planes that carry no shear. They tell where a brittle part will crack and feed the failure theories for ductile parts. Some syllabi call this the calculation of principal stresses for various load combinations.

You need the stress formulas of direct, bending and torsional stresses.

Adding stresses at a point

Superposition holds while the material stays linear elastic and the deflections are small. Take a small square element at the chosen point. The face whose normal is the x-axis carries a normal stress σx and a shear stress τxy; the y face carries σy and the same τxy. Tension is positive.

σx = ±P/A ± My/I; τxy = Tr/J (+ shear from V)

P/A, My/I and Tr/J are the direct, bending and torsional stresses at that point, each with the sign it has there. For a solid shaft at its surface, σx = 32M/(πd³) and τxy = 16T/(πd³); σy = 0, so the element is in plane stress.

Pick the point where the stresses add: the outer fibre on the side where bending and direct stress have the same sign. Transverse shear from V is zero at that outer fibre and largest at the neutral axis, so it is checked separately in short, deep members.

Eccentric loading: a force plus a moment

An eccentric load is a force whose line of action misses the centroid of the section by a distance e. Moving it to the centroid keeps the force and adds a couple M = Pe. The section then carries a uniform direct stress P/A and a linear bending stress Pe·c/I; they add on one side and subtract on the other.

σmax, min = P/A ± Pe·c/I

P is the load (N), e its offset from the centroidal axis (mm), c the distance from the neutral axis to the outer fibre (mm) and I the second moment of area about the axis of bending (mm⁴). If the load is offset in two directions, add the two bending stresses at the corner where they have the same sign.

The rule applies to brackets, clamps, columns with offset loads and the straight parts of C-frames; never use P/A alone when the force is offset. Bolted and welded joints under eccentric loads are treated in their own chapters.

Principal stresses and Mohr's circle

Rotate the element by an angle θ and the stresses on its faces change. On two perpendicular planes the shear stress is zero; these are the principal planes and their normal stresses are the principal stresses σ₁ (algebraically larger) and σ₂.

σ1,2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √{[(σx − σy)/2]² + τxy²}

The square root is also the maximum in-plane shear stress τmax = (σ₁ − σ₂)/2, which acts on planes at 45° to the principal planes. The principal planes are at θp from the x face, where tan 2θp = 2τxy/(σx − σy). Check: σ₁ + σ₂ = σx + σy.

Mohr's circle draws the same result. Plot the x face as the point (σx, τxy) and the y face as (σy, −τxy); the line joining them is a diameter. The centre is at ((σx + σy)/2, 0) and the radius is τmax. The circle cuts the σ axis at σ₁ and σ₂, and an angle 2θ on the circle is θ on the element. Figure 1.7.1 draws the circle of the first worked example.

In plane stress the third principal stress, normal to the surface, is σ₃ = 0. When σ₁ and σ₂ have the same sign, the largest shear stress at the point is σ₁/2 (or |σ₂|/2), larger than the in-plane value. A brittle part is checked by comparing σ₁ with its allowable tensile stress; a ductile part needs a theory of failure (maximum shear stress or distortion energy), covered with failure theories and fatigue design.

Worked example

Question. A 50 mm diameter solid shaft transmits 15 kW at 300 rpm and carries a bending moment of 800 N·m at the section considered. Find the bending and torsional stresses at the surface, the principal stresses, the maximum shear stress and the angle of the principal planes.

  1. Torque: T = 60P/(2πN) = 60 × 15,000/(2π × 300) = 477.5 N·m = 477,465 N·mm.
  2. Bending stress: σx = 32M/(πd³) = 32 × 800,000/(π × 50³) = 65.19 MPa; σy = 0.
  3. Torsional shear: τxy = 16T/(πd³) = 16 × 477,465/(π × 50³) = 19.45 MPa.
  4. Centre and radius: (65.19 + 0)/2 = 32.60 MPa; τmax = √(32.60² + 19.45²) = 37.96 MPa.
  5. Principal stresses: σ₁ = 32.60 + 37.96 = 70.55 MPa; σ₂ = 32.60 − 37.96 = −5.36 MPa. Check: 70.55 − 5.36 = 65.19 = σx + σy.
  6. Angle: tan 2θp = 2 × 19.45/65.19 = 0.597, so 2θp = 30.8° and θp = 15.4° from the shaft axis.

Answer. σx = 65.19 MPa, τxy = 19.45 MPa; σ₁ = 70.55 MPa, σ₂ = −5.36 MPa, τmax = 37.96 MPa; principal planes at 15.4° (and 105.4°) from the shaft axis.

Worked example 2

Question. A tie bar 40 mm wide and 10 mm thick carries a pull of 20 kN whose line of action is 15 mm from the centroidal axis, in the plane of the width. Find the largest and smallest normal stresses.

  1. Area A = 40 × 10 = 400 mm². Direct stress P/A = 20,000/400 = 50.0 MPa (tension).
  2. Moment M = Pe = 20,000 × 15 = 300,000 N·mm, bending about the axis normal to the width.
  3. I = 10 × 40³/12 = 53,333 mm⁴; c = 20 mm. Bending stress = 300,000 × 20/53,333 = 112.5 MPa.
  4. Edge nearer the load: 50.0 + 112.5 = 162.5 MPa tension. Far edge: 50.0 − 112.5 = −62.5 MPa, compression.
  5. There is no shear at the edges, so these are principal stresses there.

Answer. σmax = 162.5 MPa (tension) on the edge nearer the load line and σmin = −62.5 MPa (compression) on the far edge: the offset raises the stress 3.25 times above P/A.

Exam traps

  • Adding a shear stress to a normal stress. Combine them only through the principal-stress formula or Mohr's circle.
  • Forgetting the moment Pe of an offset load: P/A alone can be several times too small (162.5 against 50 MPa in the tie bar).
  • Dropping the sign of σ₂. A negative principal stress is compression and matters for brittle parts and for the maximum shear stress.
  • Reading 2θ off Mohr's circle as θ on the element. The angle on the circle is twice the angle on the element.
  • Checking a ductile part by σ₁ alone. Ductile materials yield in shear; use a theory of failure.

1.8 Design based on strength and stiffness

A machine part must pass two different tests. Strength asks whether it can carry its loads without yielding or breaking: the largest stress must stay below the allowable stress. Stiffness asks whether it deforms too much to do its job: the deflection or angle of twist must stay below a limit set by the machine, such as gear alignment or the accuracy of a cutting tool. Both tests use the same loads and the same trial section but different section properties, and the larger of the two sizes is the answer. Long shafts, slender beams and precision frames are usually sized by stiffness.

You need factor of safety and the stress formulas.

Two checks, two section properties

Strength: σmax ≤ σallow = Syt/n; stiffness: δmax ≤ δallow, θ ≤ θallow

σmax is the largest working stress (MPa) and n the factor of safety; δmax is the largest deflection (mm) and θ the angle of twist (rad or degrees); the limits come from the function of the machine or from a design guide.

Bending strength depends on the section modulus Z = I/c, bending stiffness on the second moment I. Torsional strength depends on J/r, torsional stiffness on J. Stiffness also depends on the material through E or G, while strength depends on Syt. Since every steel has about the same E (200 GPa) and G (80 GPa), a stronger steel does nothing for a stiffness-limited part; only a larger section does.

Deflection formulas for simple parts

δ = PL/(AE); θ = TL/(GJ)

δ is the extension of a bar of length L (mm) and area A (mm²) under an axial load P (N); θ is the angle of twist (rad) of a shaft of length L under torque T (N·mm); E and G are the moduli of elasticity and rigidity (N/mm²); J is the polar second moment of area (mm⁴). Multiply θ in rad by 180/π for degrees.

In the table, P is a point load (N), w a load per unit length (N/mm), L the span (mm), E the modulus of elasticity (N/mm²) and I the second moment of area of the section about the bending axis (mm⁴). Bending deflection grows with L³ and torsional twist only with L, which is why slender beams are almost always stiffness-limited, and long shafts are when a twist limit is set. A common limit for transmission shafts is 0.25° of twist per metre of length.

The design loop: trial, strength, stiffness, resize

  1. Choose the material and the allowable stress Syt/n.
  2. Express the section by one unknown (diameter d, or width b with depth h = 2b).
  3. Size for strength: set σmax = σallow and solve for the unknown.
  4. Size for stiffness: set δ or θ equal to its limit and solve again.
  5. Take the larger size, round it up to a standard (preferred) size, and recheck both stress and deflection at that size.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported steel beam of span 1.2 m carries a central load of 10 kN. The allowable bending stress is 120 MPa, the allowable deflection 3 mm and E = 200 GPa. The section is rectangular with depth h = 2b. Find the smallest width b and the deflection at the chosen size.

  1. Data: P = 10,000 N; L = 1200 mm; σallow = 120 MPa; δallow = 3 mm; E = 200,000 N/mm².
  2. Moment: M = PL/4 = 10,000 × 1200/4 = 3.0 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  3. Section: I = b(2b)³/12 = (2/3)b⁴; Z = I/(h/2) = I/b = (2/3)b³.
  4. Strength: σ = M/Z = 3.0 × 10⁶/((2/3)b³) = 4.5 × 10⁶/b³ ≤ 120, so b³ ≥ 37,500 and b ≥ 33.47 mm.
  5. Stiffness: δ = PL³/(48EI) = 10,000 × 1200³/(48 × 200,000 × (2/3)b⁴) = 2.70 × 10⁶/b⁴ ≤ 3, so b⁴ ≥ 900,000 and b ≥ 30.80 mm.
  6. Strength needs the larger width. Round up to b = 34 mm, h = 68 mm.
  7. Recheck at 34 mm: σ = 4.5 × 10⁶/34³ = 114.5 MPa ≤ 120; δ = 2.70 × 10⁶/34⁴ = 2.02 mm ≤ 3.

Answer. Strength governs: b ≥ 33.47 mm against 30.80 mm for stiffness. Use 34 mm × 68 mm, with σ = 114.5 MPa and δ = 2.02 mm.

Exam traps

  • Checking strength only. A part that is strong enough can still twist or sag too much.
  • Using Z (or J/r) in the deflection check, or I (or J) in the strength check.
  • Leaving the twist limit in degrees inside θ = TL/(GJ), which gives radians.
  • Not rechecking after rounding up. The deflection at 34 mm (2.02 mm) is not the one at 33.47 mm (2.15 mm).
  • Choosing a higher-strength steel to cure excessive deflection. E and G hardly change between steels.

1.9 Curved beams

A curved beam is a member whose axis is already curved before it is loaded: a crane hook, the frame of a C-clamp or punch press, a chain link, a ring. When the depth of the section is not small compared with its radius of curvature, the straight-beam formula My/I is no longer right. The inner fibres are shorter than the outer ones, so the same rotation of the section strains them more: the stress varies hyperbolically, the neutral axis moves toward the centre of curvature, and the inner fibre carries the highest stress. This chapter gives the formulas and applies them to the crane hook and the C-frame.

You need bending stress and eccentric loading from combined loading.

Why the neutral axis shifts

In a straight beam all fibres between two sections have the same length, so the change in length, the strain and the stress all grow linearly with the distance from the neutral axis. In a curved beam a fibre at radius r between two sections at angle dφ has length r·dφ. When the sections rotate relative to each other, every fibre changes length by an amount proportional to its distance from the neutral axis, but the inner fibres started shorter. Dividing by the original length gives a strain, and so a stress, that is largest at the inner fibre and falls off as 1/r: a hyperbola.

With no net axial force from bending, the tension and compression must balance, which pulls the neutral axis toward the centre of curvature. It lies at radius Rn, a distance e inside the centroidal radius R.

Rn = A/∫(dA/r); rectangle: Rn = h/ln(Ro/Ri); e = R − Rn

Ri and Ro are the inner and outer radii (mm); h = Ro − Ri is the depth and b the width of the rectangle (mm); A = bh is the area (mm²); R = Ri + h/2 is the radius of the centroidal axis (mm). For a solid circle of diameter d, Rn = (√Ro + √Ri)²/4. For a trapezium with width bi at the inner face and bo at the outer, Rn = ½(bi + bo)h / {[(biRo − boRi)/h] ln(Ro/Ri) − (bi − bo)}; its centroid lies h(bi + 2bo)/[3(bi + bo)] from the inner face.

The curved-beam stress formula

σb = M(Rn − r)/(A·e·r)

σb is the bending stress (MPa) at a fibre of radius r (mm); M is the bending moment about the centroidal axis (N·mm), taken positive when it tends to straighten (open) the beam, as a load does on a hook. Positive σb is tension.

At the inner fibre r = Ri: σbi = M(Rn − Ri)/(A·e·Ri), tension. At the outer fibre r = Ro: σbo = −M(Ro − Rn)/(A·e·Ro), compression. The small Ri in the denominator is what raises the inner stress. A hook or frame section also carries the load as a direct pull, so the resultant stress is σ = P/A + σb at each fibre. Some textbooks call this the Winkler–Bach theory. When h is small compared with R (h/R below about 0.2), e becomes tiny and the result approaches My/I.

Crane hooks and C-frames

In a crane hook the critical section is the horizontal section through the centre of curvature, the deepest part of the bend. The load line passes through the centre of curvature, so the moment about the centroid of that section is M = P·R. Hook sections are made trapezoidal, wide on the inside and narrow on the outside, to put more material where the tensile stress is high; a rectangular section is used here because its formula is short.

In a C-frame (a press, a riveter, a G-clamp) the load acts in the jaw at a distance a from the centre of curvature of the curved back, so M = P(a + R) about the centroidal axis of the curved section. The load tends to open the C, so the inner fibre at the throat is in tension, as in the hook. Figure 1.9.1 plots the stress across the hook section of the first example; the curved-beam curve is 36 % higher than the straight-beam line at the inner fibre.

  1. Find Ro = Ri + h, A and R.
  2. Find Rn from the section formula and e = R − Rn.
  3. Find M about the centroidal axis: PR for a hook, P(a + R) for a C-frame.
  4. Find σbi and σbo, add P/A to both, and check the larger against the allowable stress.

Worked example

Question. A crane hook carries 20 kN. Its critical section is rectangular, 30 mm wide and 60 mm deep, with an inner radius of 40 mm, and the load line passes through the centre of curvature. Find the resultant stresses at the inner and outer fibres, and compare with the straight-beam formula.

  1. Ro = 40 + 60 = 100 mm; A = 30 × 60 = 1800 mm²; R = 40 + 30 = 70 mm.
  2. Rn = 60/ln(100/40) = 60/0.9163 = 65.48 mm; e = 70 − 65.48 = 4.52 mm.
  3. M = PR = 20,000 × 70 = 1.40 × 10⁶ N·mm. Direct stress P/A = 20,000/1800 = 11.11 MPa.
  4. Inner fibre: σbi = 1.40 × 10⁶ × (65.48 − 40)/(1800 × 4.52 × 40) = 109.6 MPa; resultant 11.11 + 109.6 = 120.8 MPa tension.
  5. Outer fibre: σbo = −1.40 × 10⁶ × (100 − 65.48)/(1800 × 4.52 × 100) = −59.4 MPa; resultant 11.11 − 59.4 = −48.3 MPa (compression).
  6. Straight beam: Z = 30 × 60²/6 = 18,000 mm³, M/Z = 77.8 MPa, resultant 88.9 MPa at both faces. It under-estimates the inner stress by 120.8/88.9 = 1.36 times.

Answer. Inner fibre 120.8 MPa tension, outer fibre 48.3 MPa compression. The straight-beam formula would give 88.9 MPa, 26 % too low at the critical fibre.

Exam traps

  • Using My/I for a deeply curved member: it under-estimates the inner-fibre stress.
  • Putting R instead of Rn in the stress formula, or forgetting e = R − Rn; e is small, so round it to at least two decimals.
  • Forgetting the direct stress P/A, or taking M = Pa instead of P(a + R) for a C-frame.
  • Taking the inner fibre of a hook as compressed. The load opens the hook, so the inner fibre is in tension.
  • Using diameters instead of radii, or mixing N·mm with m.

Takeaways

  1. Design is a loop from need to production; the specification is the test that ends it, and the concept stage fixes most of the cost.
  2. Choose a material by sizing the part in each candidate for strength and stiffness, then comparing mass and cost; heat treatment raises strength but never E.
  3. Preferred numbers are geometric series (R5 to R40); limits and fits make parts interchangeable, and the hole-basis system keeps the tools few.
  4. The failure mode picks the strength: Syt for ductile parts, Sut for brittle ones; the factor of safety covers uncertainty and codes set its minimum.
  5. A suddenly applied load doubles the stress; a falling weight multiplies it by 1 + √(1 + 2h/δst), often by hundreds.
  6. P/A, My/I and Tr/J give the three basic stresses; bending and direct stresses add, but shear combines with them only through principal stresses.
  7. An offset load adds a moment Pe; the principal stresses and Mohr's circle turn any plane stress state into its largest and smallest normal stresses.
  8. Every part must pass both a strength check and a stiffness check; long shafts and slender beams are usually sized by stiffness.
  9. In hooks and C-frames the neutral axis moves inward and the inner fibre carries the peak stress, a third or more above the straight-beam value.

The hook steel has Syt = 360 MPa and the factor of safety is 3. Find b, then compare with the size a designer would get from the straight-beam formula. 16 marks

Points to cover. allowable stress from n; Rn, e and stresses as functions of b; trial solution for b; round up and recheck both fibres; straight-beam size and why it is unsafe.

Model answer.

  1. Allowable stress: σallow = Syt/n = 360/3 = 120 MPa. The hook is ductile steel and the load is steady, so yield governs; the factor of 3 also covers the shock of lifting.
  2. Section in terms of b: h = 2b, Ro = 50 + 2b, A = 2b², R = 50 + b, Rn = 2b/ln[(50 + 2b)/50], e = R − Rn, M = P·R = 50,000(50 + b). The inner fibre carries σ = P/A + M(Rn − 50)/(A·e·50).
  3. Trial values (because Rn depends on b, solve by trial):
    b (mm)Rn (mm)e (mm)σ inner (MPa)
    3882.235.77187.4
    4083.726.28166.8
    46.388.377.95120.0
    4889.598.41110.9
  4. So b ≥ 46.3 mm. Round up to b = 48 mm, h = 96 mm.
  5. Recheck at b = 48 mm:
    Ro = 146 mm, A = 4608 mm², R = 98 mm
ln(146/50) = 1.0716, Rn = 96/1.0716 = 89.59 mm, e = 8.41 mm
M = 50,000 × 98 = 4.90 × 10⁶ N·mm, P/A = 10.85 MPa
inner: 4.90 × 10⁶ × 39.59/(4608 × 8.413 × 50) + 10.85 = 100.07 + 10.85 = 110.92 MPa  ✓
outer: 4.90 × 10⁶ × (89.59 − 146)/(4608 × 8.413 × 146) + 10.85 = −48.84 + 10.85 = −37.99 MPa
actual n = 360/110.92 = 3.25
  6. Straight-beam design for comparison: σ = P/A + M/Z with Z = b(2b)²/6 = (2/3)b³. At b = 40 mm: 50,000/3200 + 4.5 × 10⁶/42,667 = 15.6 + 105.5 = 121.1 MPa; at b = 41 mm: 14.87 + 99.03 = 113.9 MPa. A straight-beam designer would choose b of about 41 mm.
  7. Evaluation. At b = 41 mm the true curved-beam inner stress is 157.7 MPa, so the real factor of safety would be 360/157.7 = 2.28 instead of 3. The straight-beam formula is unsafe for a hook because h/R is large (96/98 ≈ 1); the curved-beam formula raises the size from 41 to 48 mm.
  8. Shank check. The straight shank above the bend carries only the direct pull: a round shank of 40 mm (1257 mm²) would work at 50,000/1257 = 39.8 MPa, far below 120 MPa, so the bend, not the shank, sets the size.
  9. Answer: b = 48 mm, h = 96 mm; inner fibre 110.9 MPa tension, outer fibre 38.0 MPa compression, actual factor of safety 3.25. In practice a trapezoidal section, wider at the inside, would save mass.
Evaluate A cast-iron punch-press frame has a rectangular curved section 50 mm wide and 120 mm deep with an inner radius of 80 mm. The punching force of 25 kN acts 200 mm from the centre of curvature. The cast iron has Sut = 240 MPa and the required factor of safety is 3. A checker used the straight-beam formula and passed the frame. Evaluate the frame and, if needed, find a new width. 15 marks

Points to cover. straight-beam check the checker did; curved-beam Rn, e, M = P(a + R); inner and outer resultant stresses; true factor of safety and verdict; new width with the same depth.

Model answer.

  1. Allowable stress: cast iron is brittle, so σallow = Sut/n = 240/3 = 80 MPa in tension.
  2. Section and moment:
    A = 50 × 120 = 6000 mm², Ro = 200 mm, R = 80 + 60 = 140 mm
M = P(a + R) = 25,000 × (200 + 140) = 8.5 × 10⁶ N·mm
P/A = 25,000/6000 = 4.17 MPa
  3. The checker's straight-beam check:
    Z = 50 × 120²/6 = 120,000 mm³
σ = P/A + M/Z = 4.17 + 70.83 = 75.0 MPa ≤ 80 → "safe", n = 240/75.0 = 3.20
  4. Curved-beam check:
    ln(200/80) = 0.9163, Rn = 120/0.9163 = 130.96 mm, e = 140 − 130.96 = 9.04 mm
inner: 8.5 × 10⁶ × (130.96 − 80)/(6000 × 9.037 × 80) = 99.86 MPa
outer: 8.5 × 10⁶ × (130.96 − 200)/(6000 × 9.037 × 200) = −54.11 MPa
  5. Resultant stresses:
    FibreStraight beam (MPa)Curved beam (MPa)
    Inner (tension)75.099.86 + 4.17 = 104.03
    Outer (compression)−66.67−54.11 + 4.17 = −49.94
  6. Verdict. The true tensile stress at the throat is 104.0 MPa, 39 % above the straight-beam value, and the factor of safety is 240/104.0 = 2.31, below the required 3. The frame fails the check. The outer compression (49.9 MPa) is no problem: cast iron is about three to four times stronger in compression than in tension. The error came from using a straight-beam formula on a section whose depth (120 mm) is almost the centroidal radius (140 mm).
  7. New width. With h and the radii unchanged, Rn and e do not depend on b, and every stress varies as 1/b. The width that brings the inner stress to 80 MPa is:
    b = 50 × 104.03/80 = 65.0 mm → take 66 mm
at b = 66 mm: σinner = 104.03 × 50/66 = 78.8 MPa, n = 240/78.8 = 3.05  ✓
  8. Other remedies. Increasing the inner radius also helps, because it lowers the curvature: a larger Ri moves the result toward the straight-beam value. Increasing the depth helps most, since the bending stress falls roughly as 1/h², but it changes the frame's outline and the throat clearance of the press.
  9. Answer: the frame is unsafe (n = 2.31); widening the section to 66 mm restores n = 3.05. A T or I section with the flange at the throat would do the same with less metal.
Evaluate A hand lever is a cantilever 300 mm long with a square section of side a and an end load of 500 N. It must have a factor of safety of 2 on yield and an end deflection of at most 1 mm. Compare mild steel (Syt 250 MPa, E 200 GPa, 7850 kg/m³, ₹60/kg), aluminium alloy 6061-T6 (276 MPa, 69 GPa, 2700 kg/m³, ₹300/kg) and alloy steel (650 MPa, 200 GPa, 7850 kg/m³, ₹120/kg), and recommend one. 13 marks

Points to cover. bending moment and Z, I of a square; side a for strength in each material; side a for stiffness in each material; governing size, mass and cost; recommendation and why alloy steel does not help.

Model answer.

  1. Loads and section: root moment M = FL = 500 × 300 = 150,000 N·mm; for a square of side a, Z = a³/6 and I = a⁴/12.
  2. Strength: σ = M/Z = 6M/a³ ≤ Syt/2, so a ≥ (12M/Syt)1/3.
    mild steel:  (1.8 × 10⁶/250)1/3 = 19.31 mm
aluminium:   (1.8 × 10⁶/276)1/3 = 18.68 mm
alloy steel: (1.8 × 10⁶/650)1/3 = 14.04 mm
  3. Stiffness: δ = FL³/(3EI) = 4FL³/(Ea⁴) ≤ 1 mm, so a ≥ (4FL³/E)1/4.
    steels (E = 200,000):  (4 × 500 × 300³/200,000)1/4 = (270,000)1/4 = 22.80 mm
aluminium (E = 69,000): (5.4 × 10¹⁰/69,000)1/4 = 29.74 mm
  4. Governing size, mass and cost (mass = ρa²L):
    Materiala strengtha stiffnessa usedMass (kg)Cost (₹)
    Mild steel19.3122.8022.801.22473.4
    Aluminium 6061-T618.6829.7429.740.717215.0
    Alloy steel14.0422.8022.801.224146.8
  5. Analysis. Stiffness governs in all three. Alloy steel is far stronger, but its E is the same as mild steel's, so it needs the same 22.80 mm section and simply costs twice as much: strength bought and not used. Aluminium is 41 % lighter, because for a stiff beam the merit index is √E/ρ (√200/7.85 = 1.80 against √69/2.70 = 3.08), but it costs nearly three times as much as mild steel.
  6. Check of the stresses at the chosen size: steel at 22.80 mm: σ = 6 × 150,000/22.80³ = 75.9 MPa, actual n = 250/75.9 = 3.29; aluminium at 29.74 mm: σ = 34.2 MPa, n = 8.1. Both are well inside the strength limit, confirming that stiffness sets the size.
  7. Check of the deflection at a = 23 mm for mild steel: I = 23⁴/12 = 23,320 mm⁴; δ = 500 × 300³/(3 × 200,000 × 23,320) = 0.965 mm ≤ 1 mm ✓; stress = 6 × 150,000/23³ = 74.0 MPa, n = 3.38.
  8. Rounding. The governing 22.80 mm is rounded up to the next whole size, 23 mm for steel and 30 mm for aluminium; rounding down would break the deflection limit, because the deflection grows as 1/a⁴.
  9. Recommendation. For a hand lever on a machine, mass hardly matters and cost does: choose mild steel, 23 mm square (1.22 kg, about ₹73). Choose aluminium only where mass matters, as on a vehicle or a portable tool. Never choose alloy steel for a stiffness-limited part.

Glossary

Allowable stress
The highest working stress permitted: the material strength divided by the factor of safety.
Allowance
The planned difference between the smallest hole and the largest shaft: the minimum clearance, or if negative the maximum interference.
Basic size
The size named on the drawing, from which the limits are set by the deviations.
Brittle material
A material that breaks with little plastic deformation (elongation below about 5 %), such as grey cast iron.
Clearance fit
A fit in which the hole is never smaller than the shaft, so there is always a gap or zero clearance.
Curved beam
A member curved before loading, whose bending stress varies hyperbolically across the depth.
Design code
A published, enforceable set of design rules for safety: load cases, methods, minimum factors and tests.
Ductile material
A material that deforms plastically before breaking (elongation above about 5 %), such as mild steel.
Eccentric load
A load whose line of action does not pass through the centroid of the section, giving direct stress plus bending.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the failure stress (yield or ultimate strength) to the allowable stress.
Fundamental deviation
The deviation of a tolerance zone nearer the basic size; its letter places the zone.
Hole-basis system
A system of fits in which every hole has zero lower deviation (H) and the shaft is varied.
Impact load
A large force acting for a very short time, as when one body strikes another.
Interference fit
A fit in which the shaft is always larger than the hole, so the parts must be pressed or shrunk together.
IT grade
The number of an international tolerance grade (IT01 to IT18) that sets the width of a tolerance zone.
Mohr's circle
A graphical construction giving the normal and shear stresses on every plane at a point, and the principal stresses.
Neutral axis
The line in a bent section where the bending stress is zero.
Polar section modulus
Zp = J/(d/2), equal to πd³/16 for a solid shaft.
Preferred numbers
Standard values in geometric series (R5, R10, R20, R40) used for sizes and ratings.
Principal stress
A normal stress on a plane that carries no shear stress; the largest and smallest normal stresses at a point.
Resilience
The strain energy a material stores elastically, Syt²/(2E) per unit volume at yield.
Section modulus
Z = I/c, the ratio of the second moment of area to the distance of the outer fibre.
Tolerance
The difference between the upper and lower limits of a size.
Toughness
The energy a material absorbs before fracture.
Transition fit
A fit whose tolerance zones overlap, so a pair may have a small clearance or a small interference.
Weighted-point method
A way of ranking materials by scaling each property out of 100, weighting it and adding the scores.
