2.1 Theories of failure for ductile and brittle materials

A point inside a machine part rarely feels a single stress: bending, torsion and axial load combine into three principal stresses, but a tensile test gives only one strength. A theory of failure turns the combined state into one equivalent stress that can be compared with the yield strength (ductile material) or the ultimate strength (brittle material). Shafts, brackets, pressure vessels and crank pins are all sized this way.

You need: principal stresses from combined loading and the factor of safety (see Combined and eccentric loading; principal stresses, and Factor of safety and design codes).

One equivalent stress

At a point, choose axes so that the shear stresses vanish. The normal stresses left are the principal stresses, ordered σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3, with tension positive and compression negative. The equivalent stress σeq is the single uniaxial stress that would bring the tensile test bar to the same state of failure as the real combined stresses.

A ductile material fails by yielding, so it is checked against Syt. A brittle material fails by fracture, so it is checked against Sut in tension and Suc in compression. With a factor of safety n:

σeq ≤ Syt/n (ductile) σeq ≤ Sut/n (brittle)

σeq is the equivalent stress in MPa; Syt is the tensile yield strength; Sut is the ultimate tensile strength; Suc is the ultimate compressive strength (all in MPa); n is the factor of safety, a pure number.

The six standard theories

Each theory names a different cause of failure. ν is Poisson's ratio (about 0.3 for steel); Ssy is the shear yield strength the theory predicts.

Hydrostatic stress (equal on all three planes) changes volume but not shape and does not make a ductile metal yield; only the distortion energy theory leaves it out completely, which is why it fits ductile tests best. Coulomb–Mohr with equal strengths S becomes σ1 − σ3 ≤ S/n, which is Tresca, not Rankine.

In plane stress the Tresca safe region is a hexagon inside the von Mises ellipse, which passes through the same six corners, so Tresca is the safer of the two. The gap is largest in pure shear: 0.5Syt against 0.577Syt, about 15 %. The figure draws both, to scale, for the first worked example.

Choice rule: ductile steel, von Mises (most codes) or Tresca for a quick, safe answer; brittle cast iron, Rankine for mainly tensile stresses and Coulomb–Mohr when tension and compression both act. Repeated loading needs a separate check (see fatigue and the S–N curve).

Working forms for plane stress and shafts

σ1,2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √(((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²)

σx, σy and τxy are the plane stresses in MPa (Mohr's circle).

Put into von Mises, the roots cancel:

σeq = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²)

A solid shaft of diameter d under moment M and torque T has σx = 32M/(πd³), σy = 0 and τxy = 16T/(πd³), which gives:

Tresca: τmax = 16√(M² + T²)/(πd³) ≤ 0.5Syt/n von Mises: σeq = 16√(4M² + 3T²)/(πd³) ≤ Syt/n

M and T in N·mm, d in mm. √(M² + T²) is the equivalent twisting moment Te, used again in Shafts under bending, torsion and axial loads.

Worked example

Question. A steel shaft has Syt = 400 MPa. At its critical point σx = 100 MPa, σy = 40 MPa and τxy = 60 MPa; the third principal stress is zero. Find the principal stresses, then the factor of safety by the maximum shear stress (Tresca) and distortion energy (von Mises) theories.

  1. Centre of Mohr's circle: (σx + σy)/2 = (100 + 40)/2 = 70 MPa.
  2. Radius: R = √(((100 − 40)/2)² + 60²) = √(900 + 3600) = √4500 = 67.08 MPa.
  3. In-plane principal stresses: 70 + 67.08 = 137.08 MPa and 70 − 67.08 = 2.92 MPa. With the zero stress, σ1 = 137.08 MPa, σ2 = 2.92 MPa, σ3 = 0.
  4. Tresca: σ1 − σ3 = 137.08 − 0 = 137.08 MPa, so n = 400/137.08 = 2.92.
  5. Von Mises from the loads: σeq = √(100² − 100 × 40 + 40² + 3 × 60²) = √(10 000 − 4000 + 1600 + 10 800) = √18 400 = 135.65 MPa.
  6. Von Mises: n = 400/135.65 = 2.95.

Answer. σ1 = 137.08 MPa, σ2 = 2.92 MPa, σ3 = 0; n = 2.92 by Tresca and 2.95 by von Mises. They agree closely because the point sits near a corner the hexagon and ellipse share.

Worked example 2

Question. A solid shaft of plain carbon steel (Syt = 380 MPa) carries a bending moment M = 1000 N·m and a torque T = 1500 N·m at the same section. Using a factor of safety of 2.5, find the shaft diameter by the maximum shear stress theory and by the distortion energy theory, and choose a size.

  1. Units: M = 1.0 × 10⁶ N·mm and T = 1.5 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  2. Allowable stresses: shear 0.5 × 380/2.5 = 76 MPa (Tresca); normal 380/2.5 = 152 MPa (von Mises).
  3. Tresca: √(M² + T²) = √(1.0² + 1.5²) × 10⁶ = 1.8028 × 10⁶ N·mm. Then d³ = 16 × 1.8028 × 10⁶/(π × 76) = 1.2081 × 10⁵ mm³, so d = 49.43 mm.
  4. Von Mises: √(4M² + 3T²) = √(4 × 1.0² + 3 × 1.5²) × 10⁶ = √10.75 × 10⁶ = 3.2787 × 10⁶ N·mm. Then d³ = 16 × 3.2787 × 10⁶/(π × 152) = 1.0986 × 10⁵ mm³, so d = 47.89 mm.
  5. Choose the next standard size above both: d = 50 mm.
  6. Check at 50 mm: σx = 81.49 MPa, τxy = 61.12 MPa; Tresca n = 190/√(40.74² + 61.12²) = 2.59, von Mises n = 380/√(81.49² + 3 × 61.12²) = 2.84.

Answer. d = 49.43 mm (Tresca) and 47.89 mm (von Mises); a 50 mm shaft is safe by both. Tresca, the conservative theory, asks for the larger shaft.

Exam traps

  • Setting σ3 = 0 when an in-plane principal stress is negative: for 73 and −8 MPa, Tresca uses 73 − (−8) = 81 MPa, not 73.
  • Using Sut for a ductile material; it hides yielding.
  • Taking the shear yield strength as 0.5Syt in a von Mises answer. Von Mises gives Ssy = 0.577Syt; 0.5Syt belongs to Tresca.
  • Using Rankine for ductile steel: it ignores shear yielding, most of all in torsion.
  • Entering a compressive σ3 as positive in Coulomb–Mohr; keep it negative.

2.2 Stress concentration and notch sensitivity

A hole, a groove or a sharp shoulder makes the lines of stress crowd together, and the local stress can be two or three times the stress the simple formulas give. The theoretical stress concentration factor Kt measures this rise for the geometry alone. Under repeated load a real material feels only part of it: the notch sensitivity q turns Kt into the fatigue notch factor Kf. Shaft shoulders, keyways, oil holes, bolt threads and crane hooks all need this correction, and a few millimetres of fillet radius can decide whether a part survives a million cycles.

You need: the bending and torsion stress formulas (see Direct, bending and torsional stresses) and the chapter on theories of failure for comparing a local stress with a strength.

The stress concentration factor Kt

Far from a notch the stress follows the simple formula; at the notch root it rises to a sharp peak. The theoretical (geometric) stress concentration factor is the ratio of the two:

Kt = σmax/σnom

σmax is the highest local elastic stress at the notch and σnom is the nominal stress on the net section at the notch for the same load, both in MPa. Kt is a pure number and depends only on the shape and the type of load, not on the material.

Take σnom on the net section, because the charts were drawn that way. For a stepped shaft in bending σnom = 32M/(πd³) with d the smaller diameter; in torsion σnom = 16T/(πd³); for an axial load on a plate with a hole σnom = P/Anet with Anet = (w − d)t, where w is the width, d the hole diameter and t the thickness.

An elliptical hole in a wide plate has Kt = 1 + 2a/b (a the half-axis across the load, b along it): a circle gives 3 and a thin slit a very large value, which is why cracks grow. A central hole in a plate of width w has Kt = 2 + (1 − d/w)³ on the net section (Heywood). For fillets, keyways and grooves read Kt from Peterson-type charts using D/d and r/d, or find it by photoelasticity or finite elements.

Notch sensitivity and the fatigue notch factor

Under repeated load the fatigue strength of a notched bar falls by a factor Kf that is usually smaller than Kt, because the steep stress gradient at a small notch leaves the material around it to help. The notch sensitivity q says what fraction of the theoretical rise is felt:

q = (Kf − 1)/(Kt − 1), so Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1)

q runs from 0 (the material ignores the notch, Kf = 1) to 1 (full sensitivity, Kf = Kt), so Kf always lies between 1 and Kt.

Data books give q against notch radius r for each class of steel. Neuber's estimate q = 1/(1 + √a/√r) shows the trend: √a is a material constant in √mm and r is the notch radius in mm. A larger r raises q, and a stronger steel has a smaller √a, which also raises q. For a 600 MPa steel in bending √a ≈ 0.38 √mm, so q = 0.72 at r = 1 mm and 0.84 at r = 4 mm. Grey cast iron is the exception: its graphite flakes already act as internal notches, so an external notch adds little and q is low (about 0.2).

Under a steady load a ductile part yields locally at the notch, the peak spreads out, and design on the nominal stress is usually enough. A brittle material cannot yield, so its static check uses Kt σnom. Every fatigue check uses Kf, for ductile and brittle materials alike.

Design fixes and checks

A gentler stress path lowers Kt: a larger fillet radius, a taper instead of a step, a sled-runner keyway instead of an end-milled one, or small extra holes beside a loaded hole so the stress lines bend gradually. A larger r lowers Kt and raises q; the fall in Kt usually wins, so Kf falls.

When a shoulder must stay square for a bearing, a relief groove (a shallow undercut) gives room for a bigger radius. Thread roots and keyway corners, with their tiny radii, are the usual crack starters.

  • Use Kf in fatigue and Kt for a brittle part under static load.
  • Take σnom on the net section, as the chart does.
  • Pick the chart for the load: axial, bending and torsion curves differ.
  • Apply the notch factor once: if a data book already divides Se by Kf, do not multiply the stress by Kf too.

Worked example

Question. A stepped shaft has d = 40 mm, D = 50 mm and a fillet of radius r = 4 mm. It carries a fully reversed bending moment of amplitude M = 300 N·m. For D/d = 1.25 and r/d = 0.10 the bending chart gives Kt = 1.6, and the steel has √a = 0.38 √mm. Find the nominal stress, the elastic peak stress, q, Kf and the fatigue-effective stress at the fillet.

  1. M = 300 N·m = 300 000 N·mm; d³ = 40³ = 64 000 mm³.
  2. σnom = 32M/(πd³) = 32 × 300 000/(π × 64 000) = 47.75 MPa.
  3. Elastic peak: σmax = Kt σnom = 1.6 × 47.75 = 76.39 MPa.
  4. Notch sensitivity: q = 1/(1 + 0.38/√4) = 1/(1 + 0.19) = 0.840.
  5. Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1) = 1 + 0.840 × 0.6 = 1.504.
  6. Fatigue-effective stress: Kf σnom = 1.504 × 47.75 = 71.82 MPa.

Answer. σnom = 47.75 MPa, σmax = 76.39 MPa, q = 0.84, Kf = 1.50 and the fatigue check uses 71.8 MPa. For comparison, the larger section carries only 32 × 300 000/(π × 50³) = 24.4 MPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A steel plate 60 mm wide and 10 mm thick has a central hole of 12 mm diameter. It carries an axial load that alternates between +36 kN and −36 kN, and the steel has q = 0.9 at this hole. Find the nominal stress, Kt by Heywood's formula, the peak stress and the fatigue-effective stress.

  1. Net area: Anet = (60 − 12) × 10 = 480 mm².
  2. σnom = 36 000/480 = 75.0 MPa (the gross section would give 36 000/600 = 60 MPa, which is not what the formula uses).
  3. d/w = 12/60 = 0.2, so Kt = 2 + (1 − 0.2)³ = 2 + 0.512 = 2.512.
  4. Peak elastic stress: 2.512 × 75.0 = 188.4 MPa.
  5. Kf = 1 + 0.9 × (2.512 − 1) = 2.361.
  6. Fatigue-effective stress amplitude: 2.361 × 75.0 = 177.1 MPa.

Answer. σnom = 75 MPa, Kt = 2.51, peak 188.4 MPa, Kf = 2.36 and a fatigue-effective amplitude of 177.1 MPa: the small hole more than doubles the stress the plate's fatigue check must carry.

Exam traps

  • Using Kt in a fatigue calculation. Fatigue uses Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1), which is smaller unless q = 1.
  • Taking σnom on the gross area: in the plate example 60 MPa instead of 75 MPa, a peak 20 % too low.
  • Reading the bending chart for a torsion load. The curves differ for axial, bending and torsion.
  • Calling Kt a material property. Kt depends only on geometry and load type; q (and so Kf) depends on the material and the radius.
  • Applying the notch twice: multiplying the stress by Kf after the data book has already divided Se by Kf.

2.3 Fatigue: mechanism, S-N curve and endurance limit

A shaft that has run for years can break at a stress well below yield. The load repeats, so a tiny crack starts at a notch, grows a little each cycle, and finally the remaining metal snaps. Fatigue design asks one question: at a given stress amplitude, how many cycles will the part survive? The S–N curve answers it, and for steel the endurance limit gives the amplitude below which the part lasts indefinitely. Shafts, axles, springs, bolts and connecting rods are all designed this way, for infinite life or for a stated number of cycles.

You need: Kf and notch sensitivity from the chapter on stress concentration, and fully reversed bending stress (see Direct, bending and torsional stresses).

How fatigue starts and grows

Fatigue is failure under a stress that repeats. For a cycle between σmax and σmin the stress amplitude is σa = (σmax − σmin)/2 and the mean stress is σm = (σmax + σmin)/2. Fully reversed loading has σm = 0, so σa = σmax. A part whose peak stress is below yield can still fail after enough cycles, in three stages.

Stage 1, crack initiation. Cyclic slip in surface grains forms persistent slip bands with tiny intrusions and extrusions. A notch, scratch or corrosion pit raises the local stress to Kt σnom, so the crack starts there; the notched part's fatigue strength is lower by the factor Kf (see stress concentration).

Stage 2, crack growth. The crack runs across the section, normal to the largest tensile stress, a little on every cycle. Under a microscope the face shows striations, one per cycle. Beach marks are larger curved lines, visible to the eye, where the load changed or the crack paused.

Stage 3, final fracture. The uncracked area becomes too small and breaks in one cycle, leaving a rough, fibrous zone beside the smooth, beach-marked fatigue zone.

The S–N curve and the endurance limit

The R. R. Moore rotating-beam test measures fatigue. A polished round specimen is turned under a constant bending moment, so every surface point sees a fully reversed sinusoidal stress. The machine counts the cycles N until fracture; the test is repeated at several amplitudes S.

Plotted on log–log axes, steel results lie close to a straight line from about (10³, 0.9Sut) to (10⁶, Se), where Se is the endurance limit. Below 10³ cycles is low-cycle fatigue, designed as a static case; from 10³ to 10⁶ is finite (high-cycle) life. The line is S = A Nb, with b negative:

b = (1/3) log₁₀(Se/0.9Sut) log₁₀ Sf = log₁₀(0.9Sut) + b(log₁₀ N − 3)

Sf is the fatigue strength (the amplitude that gives a life of N cycles) in MPa; b is the slope per decade of N. Turned round, the life at an amplitude σa is log₁₀ N = 3 + log₁₀(σa/0.9Sut)/b.

Ferrous metals show a knee at about 10⁶ cycles (some books use 10⁷); below Se they survive indefinitely. Aluminium, copper and most non-ferrous metals have no knee, so they are designed with a fatigue strength at a stated life, never with an endurance limit.

From the test bar to the real part

Se′ is the endurance limit of the small polished test bar. For steels Se′ ≈ 0.5Sut up to Sut ≈ 1400 MPa, and about 700 MPa above that. The real part is rougher, larger and differently loaded, so Marin's factors reduce it: Se = ka kb kc kd ke Se′.

Finite life and cumulative damage

A part that sees several stress levels uses up its life in pieces. Miner's rule says each block of ni cycles at a level whose life is Ni uses the fraction ni/Ni, and the part fails when the fractions add up to 1:

n1/N1 + n2/N2 + … = 1 at failure

ni is the number of cycles applied at stress level i, and Ni the life at that level from the S–N line. Levels below Se do no damage.

Mean stress (a cycle that does not swing equally about zero) needs the Soderberg, Goodman or Gerber lines in the chapter on fluctuating stresses.

Worked example

Question. A machined steel shaft has Sut = 600 MPa. Take ka = 0.85, kb = 0.90 and kc = kd = ke = 1.00. The shaft carries a fully reversed bending stress amplitude of 300 MPa. Find (a) Se′, (b) Se, (c) the fatigue life.

  1. Sut = 600 MPa is below 1400 MPa, so Se′ = 0.5 × 600 = 300 MPa.
  2. Se = 0.85 × 0.90 × 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 × 300 = 229.5 MPa; take Se = 230 MPa.
  3. The S–N line runs from (10³, 0.9 × 600 = 540 MPa) to (10⁶, 230 MPa): b = log₁₀(230/540)/3 = (−0.3707)/3 = −0.1236.
  4. At 300 MPa: log₁₀ N = 3 + log₁₀(300/540)/b = 3 + (−0.2553)/(−0.1236) = 3 + 2.066 = 5.066.
  5. N = 105.066 = 1.16 × 10⁵ cycles.

Answer. Se′ = 300 MPa, Se = 230 MPa, and at 300 MPa the shaft lasts about 1.16 × 10⁵ cycles (116 000). At 230 MPa or less it would last indefinitely.

Worked example 2

Question. The shaft of the first example runs 40 000 cycles at 300 MPa and then 150 000 cycles at 260 MPa. Find the life at 260 MPa, the damage used by Miner's rule, and how many more cycles at 260 MPa it can take.

  1. Life at 300 MPa (first example): N1 = 1.164 × 10⁵ cycles.
  2. Life at 260 MPa: log₁₀ N2 = 3 + log₁₀(260/540)/(−0.1236) = 3 + (−0.3174)/(−0.1236) = 5.569, so N2 = 3.71 × 10⁵ cycles.
  3. Damage: n1/N1 = 40 000/116 400 = 0.344 and n2/N2 = 150 000/370 700 = 0.405.
  4. Total so far: 0.344 + 0.405 = 0.748, below 1, so the shaft has not failed.
  5. Life left: (1 − 0.748) × 370 700 = 0.252 × 370 700 ≈ 93 400 cycles at 260 MPa.

Answer. N2 = 3.71 × 10⁵ cycles; 74.8 % of the life is used, and about 93 400 more cycles at 260 MPa remain.

Exam traps

  • Using Se′ = 0.5Sut as the design endurance limit. It belongs to the polished test bar; multiply by the Marin factors for the real part.
  • Using an endurance limit for aluminium. Non-ferrous metals have no knee; use the fatigue strength at the required life.
  • Mixing logarithms. b is the slope of log₁₀ S against log₁₀ N; use log₁₀ in every step, never ln in one step and log₁₀ in the next.
  • Calling beach marks striations. Beach marks are visible to the eye and mark load changes; striations need a microscope and mark single cycles.
  • Counting damage from levels below Se. In Miner's rule a level at or below the endurance limit adds nothing.

Not for the exam. Real load histories are cut into cycles by rainflow counting before Miner's rule is applied. Very-high-cycle tests past 10⁹ cycles show that even some steels keep failing slowly from inside the part. Search for 'rainflow counting', 'Paris law' and 'very high cycle fatigue'.

2.4 Fluctuating stresses: Soderberg, Goodman and Gerber criteria

Most machine parts carry a steady load with a vibration on top: a bolt tightened and then pulled by gas pressure, a spring preloaded and then cycled. The stress moves between a maximum and a minimum that are not equal and opposite. Splitting the cycle into a mean and an alternating stress turns it into one point on a diagram, and failure lines drawn from the endurance limit and the static strengths give the factor of safety.

You need: Se and the S–N line from fatigue and the S–N curve, equivalent stresses from theories of failure, and Kf from stress concentration.

From a stress cycle to one point

Let σmax and σmin be the largest and smallest stresses in the cycle, in MPa, with tension positive. Then:

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2 σa = (σmax − σmin)/2 R = σmin/σmax

σm is the mean stress, σa the alternating stress (half the range), both in MPa, and R the stress ratio. Put σmin in with its sign: for σmax = 150 MPa and σmin = −50 MPa, σm = (150 + (−50))/2 = 50 MPa and σa = (150 − (−50))/2 = 100 MPa.

Three cycles have names. Fully reversed: σm = 0, R = −1 (a rotating shaft in bending). Repeated: σmin = 0, R = 0, σm = σa (a gear tooth that loads and unloads). Fluctuating: any other pair (a preloaded bolt).

Plot σm along the horizontal axis and σa up the vertical one (the Haigh diagram). The part is safe while its point stays inside the failure line.

The four lines

Each fatigue line starts at Se on the σa axis and ends at a static strength on the σm axis; none is drawn past that intercept.

LineEquation at failureEnds at
Soderbergσa/Se + σm/Syt = 1Syt
Goodman (modified Goodman)σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1Sut
Gerber (parabola)σa/Se + (σm/Sut)² = 1Sut
Langer (first-cycle yield)σa + σm = SytSyt on both axes

Soderberg is the lowest line because Syt < Sut, so it is the safest and also guards against yield. Goodman is the usual design line. Gerber bows outward and passes closest to test points for ductile steels, so it must be paired with the Langer line, which stops the peak stress σa + σm from reaching yield in the first cycle. A compressive mean stress does not shorten fatigue life; for σm < 0 the usual rule is simply σa ≤ Se/n.

Factor of safety, notches and finite life

Scale the working point by n until it reaches the chosen line:

Goodman: 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sut Soderberg: 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Syt Gerber: (σm/Sut)² n² + (σa/Se) n − 1 = 0

n is the factor of safety; for Gerber take the positive root. Then check Langer: ny = Syt/(σa + σm), and the smaller n governs.

Notches: multiply σa by Kf (many textbooks apply Kf to the alternating part only for ductile materials, others to σm as well; follow your prescribed book). Combined bending and torsion: build von Mises equivalents for each part, σa,eq = √(σa² + 3τa²) and σm,eq = √(σm² + 3τm²), and use them on the diagram.

Finite life: a part that need last only N cycles uses Sf at N (from the S–N line) in place of Se in the same equations. Shaft codes such as ASME use an elliptic line in place of Goodman.

Worked example

Question. A shaft section sees σmax = 120 MPa and σmin = 40 MPa. The steel has Sut = 600 MPa, Syt = 400 MPa and Se = 230 MPa (already corrected). Find σm, σa and the factor of safety by Goodman, Soderberg and Gerber, and check first-cycle yield.

  1. σm = (120 + 40)/2 = 80 MPa; σa = (120 − 40)/2 = 40 MPa.

  3. Soderberg: 1/n = 40/230 + 80/400 = 0.1739 + 0.2000 = 0.3739, so n = 2.67.

  5. Langer: ny = 400/(40 + 80) = 3.33.
  6. Gerber's 4.06 is above 3.33, so with Gerber the part would yield first: the Gerber design value is n = 3.33. Goodman's 3.25 is below 3.33, so Goodman governs on its own.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the axes. σm goes along the horizontal axis and σa up the vertical one; swapped, every intercept reads wrongly.
  • Pairing the wrong strength: Goodman with Syt or Soderberg with Sut. Goodman ends at Sut, Soderberg at Syt.
  • Scaling only one stress. The factor of safety multiplies both σa and σm; scaling σa alone overstates n.
  • Quoting the Gerber n without the Langer check. In the first example Gerber gives 4.06 but the part yields at 3.33.
  • Using a Goodman-type line for a compressive mean stress. For σm < 0 use σa ≤ Se/n; the line would wrongly credit the compression.

2.5 Impact and shock loading

A load that arrives in a very short time stresses a part far more than the same load applied slowly. A weight dropped on a collar stretches the rod much further than the same weight hung gently, because at the lowest point the weight has stopped and the rod has taken up the whole loss of potential energy, W(h + δ), as strain energy. The impact factor compares the dynamic stress with the static one. Crane hooks, lift cages, hammers, railway buffers and machine mounts are designed with it. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; most treat it as an application of strain energy.

You need: axial extension δ = PL/(AE) and beam deflection (see Direct, bending and torsional stresses, and Static, dynamic and impact loads), and the chapter on theories of failure for comparing the result with a strength.

Three ways a load arrives

Take a vertical rod of length L, area A and Young's modulus E, so its stiffness is k = AE/L. A weight W hung slowly gives the static extension δst = W/k = WL/(AE) and the static stress σst = W/A. Units: W in N, L and δ in mm, A in mm², E in N/mm² (MPa).

Applied suddenly (released from touching the collar, with no drop), the weight does work Wδ while the rod stores only ½kδ², so δ = 2δst and σ = 2σst. The rod overshoots to twice the static stretch and then oscillates.

Dropped through a height h, the weight does work W(h + δ) by the time it stops. Equating this to ½kδ² gives a quadratic in δ whose positive root is:

δ = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)) σ = σst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst))

The bracket is the impact factor. At h = 0 it is 2, the sudden load. When h is much larger than δst it is close to √(2h/δst), so a stiff part (small δst) feels a large factor.

The formula assumes the rod stays elastic, the rod's own mass is neglected, the stress is uniform along the rod at every instant (no stress waves), the weight stays in contact and no energy is lost as heat or sound. Leaving out the rod's mass errs slightly on the safe side when the weight is much heavier than the rod; for very fast impacts on long bars, stress waves make the real peak higher than the formula gives.

Energy, resilience and shock

The strain energy stored in the rod at stress σ is U = σ²AL/(2E). The modulus of resilience is the energy per unit volume up to yield, ur = Syt²/(2E); the modulus of toughness is the energy per unit volume up to fracture, the whole area under the stress–strain curve. A part that must absorb shock should have a large stressed volume and a low working stress, which is why bolts for shock loads are turned down to the root diameter of the thread along their shank.

Shock loading means any load applied in a very short time, once or many times: a hammer blow, a cam follower hitting its cam, a lorry crossing a joint. Design multiplies the static load by an impact or service factor and then checks strength and, if the shocks repeat, fatigue. A spring or rubber pad adds deflection for the same force, so δst grows and the impact factor falls; the second worked example shows how much.

Worked example

Question. A 500 N weight falls 50 mm onto a collar at the lower end of a vertical steel rod 20 mm in diameter and 1 m long. E = 200 GPa. Find the static extension, the impact factor, the maximum extension and the maximum stress.

  1. A = π × 20²/4 = 314.16 mm²; L = 1000 mm; E = 200 000 N/mm².
  2. δst = WL/(AE) = 500 × 1000/(314.16 × 200 000) = 0.007958 mm.
  3. 2h/δst = 2 × 50/0.007958 = 12 566; √(1 + 12 566) = 112.10.
  4. Impact factor = 1 + 112.10 = 113.10.
  5. δ = 113.10 × 0.007958 = 0.900 mm.
  6. σ = Eδ/L = 200 000 × 0.900/1000 = 180.0 MPa, against a static stress of 500/314.16 = 1.59 MPa.
  7. Energy check: W(h + δ) = 500 × 50.90 = 25 450 N·mm; σ²AL/(2E) = 180² × 314 160/400 000 = 25 450 N·mm.

Answer. δ = 0.90 mm and σ = 180 MPa, 113 times the static stress. A steel with Syt = 250 MPa stays elastic, but only just with a factor of 1.4.

Worked example 2

Question. To protect the rod of the first example, a helical spring of stiffness 2000 N/mm is fitted on the collar, so the weight now lands on the spring. Find the new maximum stress in the rod.

  1. Rod stiffness: krod = AE/L = 314.16 × 200 000/1000 = 62 832 N/mm.
  2. Spring and rod act in series: 1/keq = 1/62 832 + 1/2000, so keq = 1938.3 N/mm.
  3. δst = 500/1938.3 = 0.2580 mm.
  4. Impact factor = 1 + √(1 + 2 × 50/0.2580) = 1 + √388.6 = 1 + 19.71 = 20.71.
  5. Peak force = 20.71 × 500 = 10 357 N; rod stress = 10 357/314.16 = 32.97 MPa.
  6. The spring shortens by 10 357/2000 = 5.18 mm and the rod stretches by 10 357/62 832 = 0.165 mm.

Answer. The rod stress falls from 180 MPa to about 33 MPa: the soft spring takes the energy over a 5.2 mm stroke instead of 0.9 mm.

Exam traps

  • Taking the impact factor as 1 for a suddenly applied load. It is 2.
  • Writing the work as Wh only. The weight keeps falling while the rod stretches, so the work is W(h + δ).
  • Mixing units in 2h/δst: h in metres and δst in millimetres throws the root off by a factor of about 30.
  • Thinking a stronger, stiffer rod always helps. A smaller δst raises the impact factor; extra flexibility often helps more than extra strength.

Takeaways

  1. A theory of failure replaces the whole combined stress state by one equivalent stress; ductile parts are checked against Syt, brittle parts against Sut and Suc.
  2. Tresca's hexagon lies inside the von Mises ellipse, so Tresca is the safer of the two; they differ most in pure shear, 0.5Syt against 0.577Syt.
  3. A hole or fillet raises the local stress by Kt, but under repeated load the material feels only Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1).
  4. Fatigue cracks start at a surface stress raiser at stresses well below yield, so a generous fillet and a smooth or peened surface add more life than extra material.
  5. For steel the S–N line runs from 0.9Sut at 10³ cycles to Se at 10⁶ cycles; the same line gives the life at any amplitude in between, and Miner's rule adds up mixed levels.
  6. Marin's factors turn the test-bar endurance limit 0.5Sut into the endurance limit of the real part.
  7. A mean stress lowers the allowable amplitude: Soderberg is the most conservative line, Goodman the usual design line, Gerber the closest to tests, and Gerber and Goodman need the Langer yield check.
  8. A weight dropped through a height must be stopped by strain energy, W(h + δ) = ½kδ², so a stiff part feels a very large impact factor and a soft cushion lowers it.

Model answer.

  1. Set up. At the critical point find the principal stresses and order them σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3 (tension positive). Each theory says which quantity, when it reaches its value in the tensile test, causes failure. With a factor of safety n the limit is divided by n.
  2. Maximum principal stress theory (Rankine). Failure when the largest principal stress reaches the strength: σ1 = Sut in tension or σ3 = −Suc in compression. It ignores shear, so it suits brittle materials, which fail by separation.
  3. Maximum shear stress theory (Tresca, Guest). Failure when τmax = (σ1 − σ3)/2 reaches the shear stress at yield in the tensile test, Syt/2. So σ1 − σ3 = Syt, and the shear yield strength is Ssy = 0.5Syt.

  5. Maximum strain energy theory (Haigh). Failure when the strain energy per unit volume reaches its value at yield, Syt²/(2E): σ1² + σ2² + σ3² − 2ν(σ1σ2 + σ2σ3 + σ3σ1) = Syt². It wrongly predicts that a large hydrostatic pressure alone causes yield.

Understand Explain why one failure theory cannot serve both ductile and brittle materials. Use the shape of the tensile test curve, the type of fracture and the effect of hydrostatic stress in your answer. 10 marks

Points to cover. ductile: yielding by shear, large plastic strain before fracture; brittle: separation with little or no plasticity; hydrostatic stress changes volume but not shape, so it does not yield a ductile metal; hydrostatic compression strengthens a brittle material; Coulomb–Mohr needed when Suc differs from Sut; design comparison: against Syt or against Sut.

Model answer.

  1. The tensile test shows the difference. A ductile steel yields at Syt, then stretches plastically (often 20 % or more) before it fractures at a necked, cup-and-cone surface. A brittle cast iron shows almost no plastic strain and breaks at Sut on a flat face normal to the load.
  2. So the two materials fail by different mechanisms. Ductile failure begins with yielding, which is slip on planes of maximum shear; the useful limit is Syt and the right theories are shear or distortion theories (Tresca, von Mises). Brittle failure is separation by normal stress at flaws; the limit is Sut and the right theories are normal-stress theories (Rankine, Coulomb–Mohr).
  3. Hydrostatic stress. Equal stresses on three planes change volume but not shape, so they cannot drive slip: a ductile metal under high hydrostatic pressure does not yield, and von Mises (which ignores the hydrostatic part) predicts exactly this. A brittle material, by contrast, is much stronger when its flaws are squeezed shut: hydrostatic compression strengthens it, and its compressive strength Suc is often three to four times Sut.
  4. Unequal tension and compression strengths. Because Suc ≠ Sut for cast iron, a theory with one strength cannot fit both sides; Coulomb–Mohr uses both: σ1/Sut − σ3/Suc = 1/n.

Points to cover. Kt = σmax/σnom from charts, photoelasticity or finite elements; Kt depends on geometry and load type, not on material; q = (Kf − 1)/(Kt − 1); rearrange to Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1); q rises with notch radius and with tensile strength (finer-grained, stronger steels); q about 0.2 for grey cast iron, close to 1 for hardened steel unless the radius is very small.

Model answer.

  1. Stress concentration factor. At a hole, groove or fillet the elastic stress rises to a local peak σmax above the nominal stress σnom computed on the net section. Kt = σmax/σnom. It is found from Peterson-type charts (against D/d and r/d), photoelasticity or finite element analysis. Being an elastic result, it depends only on the shape and on whether the load is axial, bending or torsion.
  2. Fatigue notch factor. Fatigue tests on notched and smooth bars of the same material give Kf = (endurance limit of the smooth bar)/(endurance limit of the notched bar). Tests show Kf ≤ Kt, because at a small notch the stress falls steeply away from the root and the material around helps the stressed grains.
  3. Notch sensitivity. q measures what fraction of the theoretical rise above 1 is felt in fatigue:
    q = (Kf − 1)/(Kt − 1)
  4. Rearranging: q(Kt − 1) = Kf − 1, so Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1). With q = 0, Kf = 1 (notch has no effect); with q = 1, Kf = Kt (full effect).
  5. What q depends on. Material: stronger, finer-grained steels have a smaller Neuber constant √a and so a higher q; grey cast iron already contains graphite flakes that act as internal notches, so an external notch adds little and q ≈ 0.2. Geometry: q rises with the notch radius r. Neuber's estimate q = 1/(1 + √a/√r) shows both trends.

    r (mm)qKf if Kt = 1.6

    41/(1 + 0.19) = 0.8401 + 0.840 × 0.6 = 1.504
  7. Use in design. Kf multiplies the alternating nominal stress (or divides the endurance limit, but not both); Kt is used for a brittle part under static load; a ductile part under static load is normally designed on the nominal stress because local yielding relieves the peak.

Remember Describe the mechanism of fatigue failure in a metal part, covering crack initiation, stage I and stage II growth, and final fracture, and state where the crack usually starts. 12 marks

Points to cover. cyclic slip in persistent slip bands on the surface; initiation at a stress raiser, inclusion or machining mark; stage I growth along slip planes, slow and shallow; stage II growth normal to the load, one striation per cycle; beach marks show the crack front position; final fast fracture when the remaining ligament cannot carry the load; most cracks start at a free surface.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. Fatigue is the progressive growth of a crack under repeated or fluctuating stress, ending in sudden fracture, at stresses that may be well below the yield strength. It accounts for most service failures of shafts, axles, springs and bolts.
  2. Crack initiation. Even below yield, a few favourably oriented surface grains slip back and forth each cycle. The slip concentrates in persistent slip bands, which push out tiny extrusions and intrusions on the surface; an intrusion is a micro-notch from which a crack starts. Initiation is fastest where the local stress is highest: at fillets, keyways, thread roots, holes, machining marks, inclusions, corrosion pits and decarburised skins. Most cracks start at a free surface, because the bending and torsion stresses are largest there and the surface carries the flaws.
  3. Stage I growth. The new crack grows a few grains deep along the active slip planes, at about 45° to the load, very slowly. It is shallow and hard to see.
  4. Stage II growth. The crack turns to run normal to the largest tensile stress and grows by a small step each cycle: the tip opens and blunts on loading and resharpens on unloading. Each step leaves a striation, visible only under an electron microscope. Changes in load or pauses leave beach marks (clamshell marks), curved lines visible to the eye that show the successive positions of the crack front and point back to the origin.
  5. Final fracture. The uncracked ligament shrinks until it can no longer carry the peak load; it then breaks in one cycle by ductile overload or brittle fracture. The fracture face therefore has two zones.
    ZoneAppearanceTells you
    Fatigue zonesmooth, beach marks, origin at the surfacewhere and how slowly the crack grew
    Final fracturerough, fibrous or crystallinethe load at the end; a large zone means a high stress
  6. Design consequences. Because cracks start at surface stress raisers, life is improved by generous fillets, a fine finish, shot peening or surface rolling (residual compression), and by avoiding corrosion; extra cross-section helps less.
  7. Closing check. For the machined 600 MPa shaft of the fatigue chapter (Se = 230 MPa), an amplitude of 300 MPa, half the ultimate strength, still gives only 1.16 × 10⁵ cycles: well below yield, yet a finite life.

Model answer.

  3. Tresca in pure shear: σ1 − σ3 = τ − (−τ) = 2τ = Syt, so the shear yield strength is τy = 0.5Syt.

    Soderberg n = 2.67   Goodman n = 3.25   Gerber n = 4.06   Langer n = 3.33
  5. So Soderberg is the most conservative and needs no yield check; Goodman is moderate; Gerber is closest to test data but here would let the part yield (4.06 > 3.33), so Gerber must always be paired with the Langer line, and Goodman too near the σm axis.
  6. Gearbox shaft. Material data (Sut, Syt, Se with Marin factors) are known, the load is well defined and the shaft is easily inspected and replaced. Recommend Goodman (or the ASME elliptic line that shaft codes use) with a yield check, at n ≈ 1.5 to 2. Soderberg would oversize the shaft; Gerber is acceptable only with Langer.
  7. Highly stressed aircraft component. Weight matters, so Gerber or test-based curves are used to avoid needless mass, but failure is catastrophic, so the margin comes from high-reliability Se (ke at 99.9 %, 0.753), measured S–N data, a safety factor applied to life as well as stress, and inspection intervals from crack-growth analysis. If only handbook data are available, Goodman with the Langer check is the defensible choice; Soderberg would be too heavy.
  8. Conclusion: choose the line by the quality of the data and the cost of failure; always check yield; never use a straight-line criterion for a compressive mean stress (use σa ≤ Se/n).
Evaluate A shaft has failed in service at a keyway even though the calculated nominal stress was below yield. Analyse the possible reasons and explain how you would redesign the part. 14 marks

Points to cover. fatigue under repeated stress below yield; Kt at the keyway end, converted to Kf through q; rough surface, corrosion or fretting under the key; residual tensile stress from machining; bending plus torsion acting together at the same section; remedies: end-milled or sled-runner keyway, larger fillet, shot peening, induction hardening, better finish; recheck with the Goodman line and a suitable factor of safety.

Model answer.

  1. Diagnosis: fatigue. A failure below yield in a rotating shaft is almost always fatigue. The fracture face would show a smooth zone with beach marks starting at the keyway corner and a rough final-fracture zone; its size shows how high the stress was.
  2. Why at the keyway. The keyway end and its root corners are notches. An end-milled keyway has Kt of about 2 in bending and higher in torsion at sharp corners; with q ≈ 0.9 for a hardened shaft steel, Kf = 1 + 0.9(Kt − 1) ≈ 1.9 or more, so the effective stress amplitude was about twice the calculated nominal value. The designer probably compared the nominal stress with yield, a static check, and ignored the notch.
  3. Other contributors.
    CauseEffect
    Rotating bendingevery surface point sees a fully reversed stress each turn
    Torsion at the same sectionadds shear; use von Mises equivalents of σa and τa
    Rough or machined surfaceka about 0.8; tool marks start cracks
    Fretting under the keymicro-slip wears the surface and starts cracks
    Corrosionpits act as notches; no endurance limit in a wet environment
    Tensile residual stress from machining or weldingadds to the mean stress
    Shock loads, misalignmentpeaks above the design stress
  4. Check that should have been made: Se = ka kb kc kd ke × 0.5Sut; σa,eq = √((Kf σa)² + 3(Kfs τa)²) and σm,eq from the steady torque; Goodman 1/n = σa,eq/Se + σm,eq/Sut with n ≈ 1.5 to 2, plus a Langer yield check.
  5. Redesign. (1) Use a sled-runner keyway (cut by a disc cutter) instead of an end-milled one, and give the keyway root a generous radius. (2) Move the keyway away from the shoulder so the two stress raisers do not add. (3) Increase the shaft diameter at that section, or the fillet radius of any nearby shoulder. (4) Improve the surface: grind, shot peen or roll the keyway area to put it in compression; induction-harden the surface. (5) Fit the hub with an interference fit or use a spline or keyless locking to reduce fretting. (6) Protect against corrosion.
  6. Numerical illustration. A shaft with Se = 220 MPa and a nominal alternating bending stress of 120 MPa is safe as a smooth bar (n = 1.83). With Kt = 2.2 and q = 0.9 at a sharp corner, Kf = 2.08 and n = 220/(2.08 × 120) = 0.88: it fails. Reducing Kf to 1.22 by a milder geometry and finish restores n = 220/(1.22 × 120) = 1.50.
  7. Recheck the new design with the Goodman line and the yield line, then confirm by inspecting the first units in service.
Evaluate Explain why a load applied suddenly produces a larger stress than the same load applied gradually. Derive the impact factor for a suddenly applied load and for a weight dropped from a height, and state the assumptions made. 12 marks

Points to cover. work done by the load equals strain energy stored; gradual load: work ½Pδ, factor 1; sudden load: work Wδ = ½kδ², factor 2; dropped weight: W(h + δ) = ½kδ², δ = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)); assumptions: elastic, no energy lost, struck mass neglected, uniform stress; stress concentration still applies on top.

Model answer.

  1. Set up: a bar (or any elastic member) of stiffness k = AE/L carries a weight W. Static extension δst = W/k, static stress σst = W/A. Since the bar is linear, its force is kδ and its strain energy is U = ½kδ² = σ²AL/(2E).
  2. Gradual load. The load grows from 0 to W in step with the extension, so the work done is the triangle ½Wδ. Equating ½Wδ = ½kδ² gives δ = W/k = δst: impact factor 1.
  3. Suddenly applied load. The full W acts from the first instant (released from touching the collar). By the time the bar has stretched δ the weight has done work Wδ, a rectangle, but the bar stores only the triangle ½kδ²:
    Wδ = ½kδ²  →  δ = 2W/k = 2δst,   σ = 2σst
  4. At this instant the bar force is 2W, twice the load: the extra work has gone into speeding the weight up, and the bar overshoots to twice the static stretch and then oscillates about δst.
  5. Weight dropped through h. When the bar is at its maximum stretch the weight is momentarily at rest, so all the potential energy it has lost, over the fall h and the stretch δ, is stored:
    W(h + δ) = ½kδ²
with W = kδst:   δ² − 2δst δ − 2hδst = 0
δ = δst + √(δst² + 2hδst) = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst))
impact factor = 1 + √(1 + 2h/δst);  σ = σst × impact factor
  6. Checks on the result. At h = 0 it gives 2, the sudden load. When h ≫ δst it tends to √(2h/δst), so a stiff member (small δst) suffers a large factor.
  7. Assumptions: the material stays within its elastic limit; no energy is lost as heat, sound or local plastic flow at the point of contact; the mass of the struck member is neglected; the stress is uniform along the bar at every instant (no stress waves); the weight stays in contact after impact. Stress concentration at holes or threads still multiplies the result.
  8. Numbers: W = 500 N on a 20 mm steel rod 1 m long, h = 50 mm: δst = 0.007958 mm, impact factor = 1 + √(1 + 12 566) = 113.1, σ = 113.1 × 1.59 = 180 MPa. A 2000 N/mm spring on the collar raises δst to 0.258 mm and cuts the factor to 20.7 and the rod stress to 33 MPa.

Glossary

Beach mark
A larger curved mark on a fatigue fracture surface showing where the crack front stood after a block of cycles.
Brittle material
A material that fractures with little or no plastic deformation and is stronger in compression than in tension.
Ductile material
A material that shows large plastic strain before fracture, usually more than 5 % elongation in a tensile test.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the strength of the part to the stress it actually carries.
Fatigue crack growth
The steady extension of a crack by a small step each cycle in stage II, roughly following the Paris law.
Fatigue life
The number of cycles a part survives at a given stress amplitude before it fails.
Fillet
A rounded transition between two sections of different size, provided to reduce the stress concentration.
Haigh diagram
A plot of alternating stress against mean stress used to check fatigue safety against a failure line.
Hydrostatic stress
Equal normal stress on all three planes with no shear; it changes volume but does not make a ductile metal yield.
Marin factors
Correction factors for surface finish, size, load type, temperature and reliability that convert the specimen endurance limit into a component endurance limit.
Modulus of resilience
The strain energy stored per unit volume at the yield point, equal to Syt²/(2E).
Nominal stress
The stress found from the simple axial, bending or torsion formula on the net section at a notch, ignoring the local disturbance.
Octahedral shear stress
The shear stress on the octahedral planes, whose magnitude is proportional to the von Mises equivalent stress.
Persistent slip band
A narrow band on the surface of a cyclically loaded metal where slip concentrates and a fatigue crack starts.
Plane stress
A stress state in which one principal stress is zero, as on the free surface of a loaded shaft.
Repeated stress
A cycle that rises from zero to a maximum and falls back to zero, so the stress ratio R = 0.
Reversed stress
A cycle in which the stress swings equally on both sides of zero, so the stress ratio R = −1.
Strain energy
The energy stored in a deformed body, equal to the area under its load–deflection curve.
Stress raiser
Any geometric feature such as a hole, groove, keyway or shoulder that forces the stress lines to crowd together.
Stress ratio R
R = σmin/σmax, the number that fixes where a cycle sits on the Haigh diagram.
Striation
A fine ridge on a fatigue fracture surface that marks the advance of the crack in one load cycle.
Sudden load
A load that goes from zero to its full value in one step, producing twice the static deflection and stress.
Toughness
The energy absorbed per unit volume up to fracture, given by the whole area under the stress–strain curve.
Ultimate tensile strength Sut
The highest engineering stress reached in a tensile test, before necking begins.
Yield strength Syt
The stress at which a material begins to deform permanently, measured in MPa.
