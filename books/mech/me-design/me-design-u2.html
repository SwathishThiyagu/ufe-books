Machine Design Unit 2 · Failure Theories and Fatigue Design

Machine parts fail in different ways depending on whether the material is ductile or brittle, and on whether the load is steady, repeated or sudden. This unit turns a complicated three-dimensional stress state into one number that can be compared with a material strength, adds the effect of notches, and builds the fatigue criteria used to size shafts, springs and gears.

2.1 Theories of failure for ductile and brittle materials

A point inside a machine part rarely feels a single stress. Bending, torsion and axial load combine into three principal stresses. A failure theory replaces that complicated state with one equivalent stress, σeq. For a ductile material, compare σeq with the yield strength Syt; for a brittle material, compare it with the ultimate strength Sut. The same idea sizes shafts, pressure vessels and brackets. This chapter gives the standard theories, shows which one to use, and derives the von Mises form used in most design codes.

You need: principal stresses from combined loading and the factor of safety (Unit 1, Foundations).

One equivalent stress

At a point, choose axes so that shear stresses vanish. The remaining normal stresses are the principal stresses, ordered σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3. Tension is positive; compression is negative. A uniaxial tensile test gives one strength: Syt for yield, Sut for fracture. The equivalent stress σeq is the uniaxial stress that would cause the same failure as the real combined state. Design is safe when σeq ≤ Syt/n for a ductile material, or σeq ≤ Sut/n for a brittle material.

The factor n is the factor of safety. Use Syt with ductile materials because they fail by yielding. Use Sut with brittle materials because they fail by fracture. Using Sut for a ductile part is a common mistake: it hides yielding and gives an unsafe design.

σeq = f(σ1, σ2, σ3); n = Syt/σeq (ductile), n = Sut/σeq (brittle)

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses; Syt is tensile yield strength; Sut is ultimate tensile strength; n is factor of safety.

The five standard theories

Each theory assumes a different cause of failure. The table gives the equivalent stress. ν is Poisson's ratio; Suc is compressive ultimate strength.

TheoryEquivalent stressBest for
Rankine, maximum principal stressσeq = σ1 when σ1 ≥ 0 (tensile); σeq = |σ3| when σ3 < 0 (compressive). When Sut ≈ Suc, σeq = max(|σ1|, |σ3|)Brittle; Sut ≈ Suc
St Venant, maximum principal strainσeq = σ1 − ν(σ2 + σ3)Brittle; strain-controlled
Tresca, maximum shear stressσeq = σ1 − σ3Ductile; conservative
von Mises, maximum distortion energyσeq = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²)Ductile; plane stress
Coulomb–Mohrσ1/Sut − σ3/Suc = 1/nBrittle; different strengths

For Coulomb–Mohr, σ1 is tensile positive and σ3 is compressive negative. If Sut = Suc = S, the equation becomes σ1 − σ3 = S/n, so it reduces to Tresca (maximum shear stress), not Rankine. For Rankine, take the magnitude of the numerically largest principal stress, not its algebraic value.

σeq = √(½[(σ1−σ2)² + (σ2−σ3)² + (σ3−σ1)²])

general three-dimensional von Mises equivalent stress; σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses.

In plane stress, plot σ1 against σ2. The Tresca safe region is a hexagon. The von Mises safe region is an ellipse. The hexagon lies inside the ellipse. Tresca therefore predicts failure earlier and is conservative. The largest difference is at pure shear, where von Mises allows about 15.5% more stress.

Choice rule: ductile material, use von Mises; use Tresca for conservative design or when a code requires it. Brittle material, use Rankine when tension and compression strengths are close; use Coulomb–Mohr when they differ. Most design codes prefer von Mises for ductile metals because it matches test data for combined loading. Fatigue is a separate check (see <a href="#2.3">2.3).

Von Mises from σx, σy and τxy

Plane stress means σz = 0, but τxy may act. The in-plane principal stresses come from Mohr's circle:

σ1,2 = (σx + σy)/2 ± √(((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²)

σx, σy are normal stresses; τxy is shear stress; σ1 and σ2 are in-plane principal stresses.

Substitute these into σeq² = σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2². The square-root terms cancel. The result is the working formula:

σeq = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²)

σeq is von Mises equivalent stress; σx, σy are normal stresses; τxy is shear stress.

This form is convenient because it uses the loads directly, without first finding principal stresses. It is the formula used for shaft design in Unit 3 and for combined bending and torsion.

Worked example

Question. A steel shaft has Syt = 400 MPa. A combined load gives σx = 100 MPa, σy = 40 MPa and τxy = 60 MPa. The third principal stress is zero. Find σ1 and σ3, then the factor of safety by Tresca and by von Mises.

  1. Mean stress: σm = (σx + σy)/2 = (100 + 40)/2 = 70 MPa.
  2. Radius of Mohr's circle: R = √(((σx − σy)/2)² + τxy²) = √(30² + 60²) = 67.1 MPa.
  3. Principal stresses: σ1 = 70 + 67.1 = 137.1 MPa; σ2 = 70 − 67.1 = 2.9 MPa.
  4. Order them: σ1 = 137.1 MPa, σ2 = 2.9 MPa, σ3 = 0 MPa.
  5. Tresca equivalent stress: σeq = σ1 − σ3 = 137.1 − 0 = 137.1 MPa.
  6. Tresca factor: n = Syt/σeq = 400/137.1 = 2.92.
  7. Von Mises equivalent stress: σeq = √(137.1² − 137.1×2.9 + 2.9²) = 135.6 MPa.
  8. Von Mises factor: n = 400/135.6 = 2.95.

Answer. Tresca gives n = 2.92; von Mises gives n = 2.95. Von Mises is slightly less conservative for this stress state.

Exam traps

  • Setting σ3 = 0 when a compressive load exists. If σx and σy are both negative, σ3 is the most negative principal stress, not zero.
  • Using Sut instead of Syt for a ductile material. Ductile design checks yield, not ultimate fracture.
  • Forgetting to order the principal stresses. Tresca uses σ1 − σ3, the largest minus the smallest algebraic value.
  • Using Rankine for a ductile steel because it is simple. It is unsafe: it ignores shear yielding.
  • In Coulomb–Mohr, putting a compressive σ3 as positive. Keep tensile positive and compressive negative.

Ductile: yield strength Syt. Brittle: ultimate strength Sut.

2.2 Stress concentration and notch sensitivity

A hole, a groove or a sharp shoulder makes stress lines crowd together. The local stress can be three or more times the stress calculated on the net section. The theoretical stress concentration factor Kt records this rise for the geometry alone. Under fatigue, a real material feels only part of it; notch sensitivity q converts Kt into the fatigue notch factor Kf. Shaft shoulders, keyways, bolt threads and crane hooks all need this correction. The same idea warns you when a small change in radius decides whether a part survives a million cycles or cracks early.

You need Unit 1 bending and torsional stress formulas and Chapter 2.1 for comparing a local stress with strength.

The stress concentration factor Kt

A hole, groove, keyway or shoulder forces stress lines to crowd. The local peak stress is larger than the stress calculated away from the notch. The theoretical stress concentration factor is defined by Kt = σmax/σnom. σmax is the highest local elastic stress; σnom is the nominal stress on the net section at the notch, using the same load type. Kt is a pure number.

Use the net section every time. For a stepped shaft in bending, σnom = 32M/(πd³), where d is the smaller diameter and M is the bending moment. For axial load σnom = P/A_net, where P is axial load and A_net is net area. For torsion σnom = 16T/(πd³), where T is torque. The chart's Kt was drawn for that σnom, not for the gross area.

A small circular hole in a wide plate in tension gives Kt≈3. A shoulder fillet gives a higher Kt as r/d falls; a keyway or sharp thread root is severe. Charts are separate for axial, bending and torsion because the stress pattern differs. Charts exist because the elastic solution for each shape is a long calculation. Read a chart by forming D/d and r/d, choosing the correct curve, reading Kt, then multiplying your σnom. Do not memorize every curve.

Kt = σmax / σnom

σmax is the peak local elastic stress in MPa; σnom is the nominal stress on the net section in MPa for the same axial, bending or torsional load.

Notch sensitivity and fatigue notch factor

A sharp notch raises elastic stress, but a ductile metal can yield at the tip. Under fatigue, the useful factor is Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1). q is the notch sensitivity, between 0 and 1. q=0 means the material ignores the notch; q=1 means it feels the full Kt. Kf is always between 1 and Kt.

Neuber's estimate is q = 1/(1 + √a/√r). √a is a material constant in √mm; r is the notch radius in mm. A larger notch radius r raises q. A stronger, more notch-sensitive steel has a smaller √a, and a smaller √a also raises q at a given radius. Use Kf with σnom in fatigue: σ_fatigue = Kf σnom.

Under static load, a ductile material yields locally at the notch and redistributes stress, so Kt may be ignored for design on the net section. A brittle material does not yield; use Kt. Every fatigue calculation uses Kf, even for ductile steel. The figure shows Kf against Kt for three q values.

Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1)

Kf is the fatigue notch factor; q is notch sensitivity from 0 to 1; Kt is the theoretical stress concentration factor.

Design fixes and checks

Fix a concentration by making the stress path gentler. Increase fillet radius, use a taper instead of a step, cut a relief groove beside a shoulder, and round keyway corners. A large r lowers Kt directly and also raises q; the fall in Kt usually wins, so Kf usually falls.

A relief groove is a shallow undercut machined into the larger diameter next to the shoulder. It removes material where the stress lines crowd, so they spread over more length and the peak stress drops. A gradual taper spreads the load over more length. Thread roots and keyway corners are common crack starters because their radii are tiny.

The cheapest fix is often a larger radius at the drawing stage. After manufacture, polishing a sharp corner also helps, but it does not replace a proper radius.

  • Use Kf in fatigue and Kt for brittle static stress.
  • Take nominal stress on the net section, matching the chart.
  • Choose the axial, bending or torsion chart; Kt differs by load type.
  • Check a keyway or thread root as severe even if the drawing looks smooth.

Worked example

Question. A stepped shaft has d = 40 mm, D = 50 mm and fillet radius r = 4 mm. It carries a fully reversed bending moment of amplitude M = 300 N·m. A chart for this D/d and r/d gives Kt = 1.8, and the material has q = 0.85 at r = 4 mm. Find the nominal bending stress, the elastic peak stress, the fatigue notch factor, and the fatigue-effective stress.

  1. M = 300 N·m = 300,000 N·mm. d³ = 40³ = 64,000 mm³.
  2. σnom = 32M/(πd³) = 32 × 300,000/(π × 64,000) = 47.75 MPa.
  3. Elastic peak stress = Kt σnom = 1.8 × 47.75 = 85.94 MPa.
  4. Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1) = 1 + 0.85(1.8 − 1) = 1.68.
  5. Fatigue-effective stress = Kf σnom = 1.68 × 47.75 = 80.21 MPa.

Answer. The nominal stress is 47.75 MPa, the elastic peak is 85.94 MPa, Kf is 1.68, and the fatigue-effective stress is 80.21 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using Kt in a fatigue calculation instead of Kf.
  • Taking σnom on the gross area when the chart was drawn for the net area.
  • Assuming one Kt chart serves axial, bending and torsion; the curves differ.
  • Treating q as always 1, or as a property of geometry alone; q also depends on material and radius.
  • Using Kf for a ductile part under truly static load, where local yielding may make the notch harmless.

2.3 Fatigue: mechanism, S-N curve and endurance limit

A shaft that has run for years can break at a stress well below yield. The load repeats, so a tiny crack starts at a notch, grows a little each cycle, and finally snaps the remaining metal. Fatigue design asks one question: for a given stress amplitude, how many cycles will the part survive? The S–N curve answers it. The endurance limit gives the amplitude below which steel seems to last indefinitely. You meet this in shafts, springs, bolts, axles and any part under rotation or vibration.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for Kf and notch sensitivity, and Unit 1 for fully reversed bending stress and stress amplitude.

How fatigue starts and grows

Fatigue is failure under a stress that repeats. The stress amplitude is Sa = (σmax − σmin)/2. The mean stress is σm = (σmax + σmin)/2. Fully reversed loading has σm = 0, so Sa = σmax. A part can carry a static stress below yield and still fail after many cycles. Fatigue happens in three stages.

Stage 1, crack initiation. Cyclic slip inside grains forms persistent slip bands. The surface develops intrusions and extrusions. A notch, keyway, thread root or corrosion pit raises the local elastic stress by the theoretical stress concentration factor Kt times the nominal stress. The fatigue notch factor Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1), where q is the notch sensitivity, is the smaller factor by which the fatigue strength or endurance limit is reduced, so the crack starts earlier at the notch. Chapter 2.2 explains Kt, Kf and notch sensitivity.

Stage 2, stable crack growth. The crack moves across the section a little on every cycle. In some metals the fracture face shows striations, one stripe per cycle. Beach marks are larger lines where the load changed or the crack paused; they are not cycle-by-cycle stripes.

Stage 3, final fracture. The remaining section is too small, so one overload breaks it. The fracture face then has a rough fast-fracture zone. Fatigue design tries to keep the part in stage 1 or to count cycles before stage 2 becomes unsafe.

The S–N curve

The R. R. Moore rotating bending test measures fatigue. A polished specimen of circular section is rotated under a constant bending moment. Every surface point sees a fully reversed sinusoidal bending stress. The tester counts cycles N until the specimen breaks. The test repeats at several stress amplitudes S.

Plot S against N on log–log axes. For steel the points lie near a straight line from about (10³, 0.9Sut) to (10⁶, Se). Here Sut is the ultimate tensile strength in MPa and Se is the endurance limit in MPa. The line is S = A N^b. S is the stress amplitude in MPa, N is cycles, A is a constant in MPa, and b is the slope per decade, a negative number. From the two points, b = [log₁₀(Se) − log₁₀(0.9Sut)] / (6 − 3) = (1/3) log₁₀(Se/(0.9Sut)). Finite life follows from the same line: N = (S/A)^(1/b), or log₁₀ N = log₁₀(S/A)/b.

Ferrous metals show a knee at about 10⁶ cycles. Below Se they survive many more cycles, so Se is a true endurance limit. Aluminium, copper and most non-ferrous metals have no knee. Their S–N line keeps falling, so a fatigue strength at a stated life is used instead of an endurance limit. Some syllabi place the steel knee at 10⁷ cycles; use the value your data gives.

Marin factors

Se' is the endurance limit of a polished, small, bending specimen. For steels up to about Sut = 1400 MPa, Se' = 0.5Sut. Above that, use about 700 MPa. The real part is not the test specimen, so apply Marin factors: Se = ka kb kc kd ke Se'.

ka is the surface finish factor. A machined surface is lower than a polished one; a forged or corroded surface is lower still. kb is the size factor. In a larger section the stress gradient is flatter and a bigger volume of material is highly stressed, so a weak spot is more likely; kb therefore falls as the diameter rises. kc is the load factor. Bending is the reference, kc = 1.0. Axial loading and torsion give lower values. kd is the temperature factor. It is 1.0 at and below room temperature; the endurance limit falls as temperature rises well above room temperature (roughly above 200–250 °C for steel). ke is the reliability factor; a 90% reliability gives ke below 1.0, and higher reliability gives a smaller ke. Use the charts or tables in your data book.

Surface treatment changes fatigue strength more than bulk strength. Polishing removes scratches. Shot peening puts the surface in compression, so a crack must first overcome that compression; it raises fatigue strength. Corrosion pits and sharp notches act as small cracks and lower it. The S–N curve here is for fully reversed constant amplitude stress. Mean stress needs the Goodman, Soderberg or Gerber line in Chapter 2.4.

Worked example

Question. A machined steel shaft has Sut = 600 MPa. The surface factor is ka = 0.85, the size factor is kb = 0.90, and the load, temperature and reliability factors are all 1.00. The shaft carries a fully reversed bending stress amplitude of 300 MPa. (a) Find Se'. (b) Find Se. (c) Find the fatigue life N in cycles.

  1. Check the steel range: Sut = 600 MPa is below about 1400 MPa, so Se' = 0.5Sut = 0.5 × 600 = 300 MPa.
  2. Apply Marin factors: Se = ka kb kc kd ke Se' = 0.85 × 0.90 × 1.00 × 1.00 × 1.00 × 300 = 229.5 MPa. Take Se = 230 MPa for the S–N line.
  3. The line passes through (10³, 0.9Sut) = (10³, 540 MPa) and (10⁶, 230 MPa). Slope b = [log₁₀(230) − log₁₀(540)] / (6 − 3) = −0.1236 per decade.
  4. At S = 300 MPa, log₁₀ N = 3 + [log₁₀(300/540)] / b = 3 + (−0.2553)/(−0.1236) = 5.07.
  5. N = 10^5.07 = 1.16 × 10⁵ cycles, about 116000 cycles.

Answer. Se' = 300 MPa, Se = 229.5 MPa (taken as 230 MPa), and the life at 300 MPa is about 1.16 × 10⁵ cycles.

Exam traps

  • Se' = 0.5Sut is not the design endurance limit. It is the polished specimen value; multiply by all five Marin factors for the actual part.
  • Do not use an endurance limit for aluminium or most non-ferrous metals. They have no knee; use fatigue strength at the required life.
  • The S–N line from a rotating bending test is fully reversed bending. A mean stress moves the failure point; use Chapter 2.4.
  • Beach marks are not striations. Beach marks are macroscopic arrest lines; striations are one per cycle under a microscope.
  • Use log₁₀ consistently: b = Δlog₁₀S / Δlog₁₀N, so over 3 decades log₁₀S changes by 3b. Do not mix log₁₀ with ln, and do not read b as a change in S per cycle.

2.4 Fluctuating stresses: Soderberg, Goodman and Gerber criteria

Machine parts rarely see a steady stress. A shaft may bend one way then the other as it turns, so the stress at a point moves between a maximum and a minimum. Mean and alternating stresses turn that movement into one working point on a diagram. The diagram carries failure lines built from the endurance limit and the static strengths. Keeping the point below a chosen line gives a factor of safety. These lines are used for shafts, springs, bolted joints and any part that repeats a load.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for Se and the S–N curve, Chapter 2.1 for equivalent stresses, and Chapter 2.2 for Kf.

From a stress cycle to one point

Let σmax be the largest stress in a cycle and σmin the smallest, in MPa, with tension positive. The mean stress is σm = (σmax + σmin)/2. The alternating stress is σa = (σmax − σmin)/2. A fully reversed cycle has σm = 0 and R = −1; a pulsating tension cycle from zero to a peak has R = 0.

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2,   σa = (σmax − σmin)/2,   R = σmin/σmax

σmax and σmin are the maximum and minimum stresses in MPa; σm is the mean stress in MPa; σa is the alternating stress in MPa; R is the stress ratio, dimensionless.

Plot σm on the horizontal axis and σa on the vertical axis. One repeated stress becomes one point. A point on the vertical axis is a fully reversed cycle; a point on the horizontal axis is a steady load. Safe operation keeps the point below a failure boundary. The boundary uses Se, the endurance limit in MPa, Sut, the ultimate tensile strength in MPa, and Syt, the yield strength in MPa. For ductile steels Se is below Syt, so the boundary runs from Se on the vertical axis to a static strength on the horizontal axis.

The four failure lines

The Goodman or Haigh line joins Se on the vertical axis to Sut on the horizontal axis:

σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1

σa is alternating stress in MPa; σm is mean stress in MPa; Se is endurance limit in MPa; Sut is ultimate tensile strength in MPa.

The Soderberg line joins Se to Syt:

σa/Se + σm/Syt = 1

Syt is yield strength in MPa; the other symbols have the same meaning.

The Gerber line is a parabola through Se and Sut:

σa/Se + (σm/Sut)² = 1

The square makes the line bow outward between the two intercepts.

The Langer yield line is a straight yield check:

σa + σm = Syt

σa + σm equals the maximum stress in MPa; Syt is yield strength in MPa.

Only the first three are fatigue lines. Soderberg is the lowest and safest because Syt < Sut. Goodman is next. Gerber bows outward above Goodman and usually stays closest to test data for ductile steels. Langer stops plastic yielding at the maximum stress; it is not a fatigue limit.

Factor of safety and real stress raisers

At a working point (σm, σa), let n be the factor of safety. Multiply both stresses by n and put the scaled point on the chosen line. For Goodman and Soderberg:

1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sut   (Goodman)     1/n = σa/Se + σm/Syt   (Soderberg)

n is the factor of safety, dimensionless; the other symbols are as defined above.

Gerber gives a quadratic in n:

(σm/Sut)² n² + (σa/Se)n − 1 = 0

Take the positive root. For σm = 0, use n = Se/σa.

Fatigue notch factor Kf from Chapter 2.2 multiplies σa: replace σa by Kf σa. Some textbooks also multiply σm by Kf; follow the rule taught in your course. For combined bending and torsion, first build equivalent stresses at the same cycle: σa,eq = √(σa² + 3τa²) and σm,eq = √(σm² + 3τm²), where τa and τm are alternating and mean shear stresses in MPa. Then use the diagram. A code version for shafts is the ASME elliptic line, which replaces the straight Goodman line by an ellipse.

Worked example

Question. A shaft section sees σmax = 120 MPa and σmin = 40 MPa. The material has Sut = 600 MPa, Syt = 400 MPa and Se = 230 MPa. Find σm, σa and the factor of safety by Goodman, Soderberg and Gerber.

  1. Mean stress: σm = (120 + 40)/2 = 80 MPa.
  2. Alternating stress: σa = (120 − 40)/2 = 40 MPa.
  3. Goodman: 1/n = 40/230 + 80/600 = 0.1739 + 0.1333 = 0.3072; n = 1/0.3072 = 3.25.
  4. Soderberg: 1/n = 40/230 + 80/400 = 0.1739 + 0.2000 = 0.3739; n = 1/0.3739 = 2.67.
  5. Gerber: (80/600)² = 0.01778, so 0.01778 n² + 0.1739 n − 1 = 0.
  6. Discriminant = 0.1739² + 4 × 0.01778 = 0.1014; n = (−0.1739 + √0.1014)/(2 × 0.01778) = (−0.1739 + 0.3184)/0.03556 = 4.06.

Answer. σm = 80 MPa, σa = 40 MPa; n_Goodman = 3.25, n_Soderberg = 2.67, n_Gerber = 4.06.

Exam traps

  • Putting σm on the vertical axis and σa on the horizontal axis. Standard diagrams put σm on x and σa on y; swapping makes every intercept read wrongly.
  • Using Sut with Soderberg or Syt with Goodman. Goodman pairs with Sut; Soderberg pairs with Syt.
  • Forgetting that n multiplies both σa and σm. Scaling only one coordinate gives a wrong, usually unsafe, n.
  • Applying Kf to Se instead of to σa, or mixing Kf with Kt. Kf multiplies the alternating stress; follow your textbook on whether it also multiplies σm.
  • Putting a signed mean stress into Gerber without squaring it. The square removes sign, but the physical cycle still needs correct tension and compression signs.

Check yourself

Remember Write σm and σa in terms of σmax and σmin. 2 marks

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2 and σa = (σmax − σmin)/2, with stresses in MPa.

Understand Why is the Soderberg line below the Goodman line for a ductile steel? 3 marks

Soderberg uses Syt on the horizontal intercept; Goodman uses Sut. Since Syt < Sut, the Soderberg line is lower and gives a smaller n.

Apply A part has σm = 100 MPa, σa = 50 MPa, Se = 200 MPa, Sut = 500 MPa and Syt = 350 MPa. Find n by Goodman and Soderberg. 4 marks

Goodman: 1/n = 50/200 + 100/500 = 0.45, n = 2.22. Soderberg: 1/n = 50/200 + 100/350 = 0.5357, n = 1.87.

Analyze A designer raises Sut by heat treatment while Se and Syt stay fixed. At σm = 100 MPa and σa = 80 MPa, which n changes more: Goodman or Soderberg? 4 marks

Soderberg does not change because Syt is fixed. Goodman n rises because its mean-stress term 100/Sut falls. Goodman changes more.

Apply For σm = 120 MPa, σa = 60 MPa, Se = 240 MPa and Sut = 600 MPa, find n by Gerber. 4 marks

0.04 n² + 0.25 n − 1 = 0, so n = (−0.25 + √0.2225)/0.08 = 2.77.

Remember Which line on the σa–σm diagram is a yield check rather than a fatigue line? 2 marks

The Langer yield line, σa + σm = Syt. It prevents yielding; it is not a fatigue limit.

Analyze Why is Gerber often closer to test data than Goodman for ductile steels? 4 marks

Test points for ductile steels bow outward between Se and Sut. The Gerber parabola follows that bow; the Goodman straight line cuts below it and is more conservative.

Apply A cycle has σmax = 200 MPa and σmin = −100 MPa. Find σm, σa and R. 3 marks

σm = (200 − 100)/2 = 50 MPa, σa = (200 + 100)/2 = 150 MPa, R = −100/200 = −0.5.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Commercial shaft design software often switches between Goodman, Gerber and ASME elliptic checks and reports the governing line. Standards for shafts, springs and fasteners use these diagrams with code factors. Research extends them to variable-amplitude loading, multiaxial stress and probabilistic scatter in Se. Search for modified Goodman diagram, Gerber fatigue criterion and ASME shaft elliptic.

2.5 Impact and shock loading

Impact and shock loading is about force that arrives in a short time. A weight dropped on a rod stretches it more than the same weight hung slowly. The extra stretch comes from the kinetic energy that must be stored as strain energy. The dynamic factor compares the impact extension or stress with the static value. Designers use it for crane hooks, lift chains, railway drawbars and mounting pads. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; most treat it as a short application of strain energy.

You need: Unit 1 for static axial and bending deflection and strain energy, and Chapter 2.1 for comparing the computed stress with yield or ultimate strength.

Three ways a load arrives

Take a vertical rod of length L, area A and Young's modulus E. A weight W hung slowly gives static extension δst = WL/(AE) and static stress σst = W/A. Units: W in N, L and δst in mm, A in mm², E in N/mm². For a beam use the static deflection from Unit 1, for example δst = WL³/(48EI) for a simply supported beam with central load; I is the second moment of area.

A suddenly applied weight gives δ = 2δst and σ = 2σst. The dynamic factor is 2. The load is not held at its static value while the rod strains; the rod overshoots.

A weight falling through height h gives δ = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)). Stress follows the same factor. At h = 0 the factor is 2, matching a suddenly applied load. The figure plots this factor against h/δst. A stiff rod has small δst, so the same drop gives a large factor; a soft pad increases δst and lowers it.

Energy, resilience and shock

Equate external work to strain energy. For a slowly loaded rod U = ½Pδ = σ²V/(2E), where V = AL is volume. For impact the falling weight does work W(h + δ); this becomes strain energy. Both views give the same δ.

Modulus of resilience is strain energy per unit volume at yield, u_r = σ_y²/(2E) for linear elastic behaviour. Modulus of toughness is energy per unit volume to fracture, the area under the stress-strain curve. Resilience is elastic spring-back; toughness is total absorption.

Shock loading is a repeated sudden load, as in a cam follower or a chute. Design applies a service factor to the static load, then checks stress and fatigue. A spring or rubber pad increases deflection for the same force, reducing stiffness and spreading the impact over more time. Check the pad's compression and heating.

Worked example

Question. A 500 N weight falls 50 mm onto a collar at the lower end of a vertical steel rod of diameter 20 mm and length 1 m. E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum extension and maximum stress.

  1. 1. A = πd²/4 = π(20)²/4 = 314.2 mm².
  2. 2. δst = WL/(AE) = 500 × 1000/(314.2 × 200000) = 0.00796 mm.
  3. 3. 2h/δst = 2 × 50/0.00796 = 12563; √(1 + 12563) = 112.1.
  4. 4. δ = 0.00796(1 + 112.1) = 0.90 mm.
  5. 5. σ = Eδ/L = 200000 × 0.90/1000 = 180 N/mm² = 180 MPa. Static stress = 500/314.2 = 1.59 MPa.

Answer. Maximum extension is 0.90 mm and maximum stress is 180 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using dynamic factor 1 for a suddenly applied load; it is 2.
  • Mixing millimetres and metres inside δst = WL/(AE), which throws the square-root term far off.
  • Putting h in metres while δst is in millimetres in 2h/δst.
  • Forgetting that stress follows the same dynamic factor as extension for the same rod.
  • Treating modulus of resilience as modulus of toughness; one stops at yield, the other reaches fracture.

Check yourself

Remember Define dynamic factor. 2 marks

Ratio of impact extension or stress to the static value under the same load.

Understand Why does a suddenly applied load give factor 2? 3 marks

The load does work over increasing deflection; at maximum, the bar force is twice the applied load and strain energy equals the work done.

Apply A rod of area 200 mm² carries a 1 kN load applied suddenly. Static stress is 5 MPa. Find maximum stress. 4 marks

σst = 1000/200 = 5 MPa; factor = 2; σ = 2 × 5 = 10 MPa.

Analyze Why does a rubber pad reduce impact stress? 4 marks

It increases static deflection δst for the same load, so h/δst falls and the dynamic factor falls; it also absorbs energy.

Remember Define modulus of resilience and modulus of toughness. 3 marks

Resilience: energy per volume at yield, σy²/(2E). Toughness: energy per volume to fracture, area under the stress-strain curve.

Apply In the falling-weight formula, what is the dynamic factor when h = 0? 3 marks

1 + √1 = 2; this is the suddenly applied load case.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Practical impact design uses transient finite element analysis and strain-rate-dependent material data. Cushioning pads are selected by energy absorption per unit volume and by heat build-up under repeated shocks. Search terms: drop test, dynamic factor, strain energy impact, shock isolator, modulus of toughness.

Takeaways

  1. In static design one equivalent stress replaces the whole combined stress state, and the theory you choose depends on whether the material is ductile or brittle.
  2. Tresca and von Mises predictions differ by about 15 % in pure shear, and Tresca is always the safer of the two for a ductile metal.
  3. A hole or fillet raises the local stress by Kt, but the material actually feels only Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1) of that rise.
  4. Fatigue cracks start at a surface stress raiser under a stress well below yield, so a smooth finish and a generous fillet add more life than extra material.
  5. The S–N curve turns the question how strong into strong enough for how many cycles, and for steels it flattens at the endurance limit.
  6. Mean stress eats into fatigue life, so any part carrying steady load plus vibration must be checked on a Haigh or Goodman diagram.
  7. Soderberg is the conservative choice, Goodman the traditional one, and Gerber sits closest to test data for ductile metals.
  8. A load dropped from a height causes several times the stress of the same load applied slowly, because the part must absorb the kinetic energy as strain energy.
  9. Every design decision in this unit ends with a factor of safety measured on a failure line, never on a single stress number alone.

Exam question bank

Long answers in this unit almost always pair a derivation or comparison of failure criteria with a numerical check, so expect marks for the correct equivalent stress, the correct failure line and a clear comment on which criterion is conservative and why.

Remember and understand

Understand State the four static failure theories used for ductile materials, write the failure equation of each in terms of principal stresses, and explain why the distortion energy theory is usually preferred for ductile metals. 13 marks

Rankine: σ1 = Syt; St Venant: σ1 − ν(σ2 + σ3) = Syt; Tresca: σ1 − σ3 = Syt; von Mises: σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2² = Syt²; von Mises matches ductile test data and ignores hydrostatic stress; Tresca used where simplicity and conservatism matter.

Understand Explain why one failure theory cannot serve both ductile and brittle materials. Use the shape of the tensile test curve, the type of fracture and the effect of hydrostatic stress in your answer. 10 marks

ductile: yielding by shear, large plastic strain before fracture; brittle: separation with little or no plasticity; hydrostatic stress changes volume but not shape, so it does not yield a ductile metal; hydrostatic compression strengthens a brittle material; Coulomb–Mohr needed when Suc differs from Sut; design comparison: against Syt or against Sut.

Understand Define stress concentration factor Kt and notch sensitivity q. Show that the fatigue notch factor is Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1), and state the material and geometric factors on which q depends. 12 marks

Kt = σmax/σnom, obtained from charts, photoelasticity or finite elements; Kt depends on geometry and loading, not on material; definition q = (Kf − 1)/(Kt − 1); rearrange to Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1); q rises with notch radius, tensile strength and grain size; q near 0 for cast iron, near 1 for strong steel with a sharp notch.

Remember Describe the mechanism of fatigue failure in a metal part, covering crack initiation, stage I and stage II growth, and final fracture, and state where the crack usually starts. 12 marks

cyclic slip in persistent slip bands on the surface; initiation at a stress raiser, inclusion or machining mark; stage I growth along slip planes, slow and shallow; stage II growth normal to the load, one striation per cycle; beach marks show the crack front position; final fast fracture when the remaining ligament cannot carry the load; most cracks start at a free surface.

Understand Draw a typical S–N curve for steel and explain its shape. Define endurance limit and fatigue strength, and list the factors that make the endurance limit of a real component lower than that of a polished laboratory specimen. 13 marks

log S against log N, low-cycle and high-cycle regions; knee at about 10⁶ to 10⁷ cycles for steel; Se is the plateau value, Sf is the value at a stated life; non-ferrous metals show no true plateau, so design for a stated life; Marin factors: surface finish, size, load type, temperature, reliability; stress concentration added through Kf.

Apply

Apply A solid circular shaft of 50 mm diameter carries a bending moment of 800 N·m and a torque of 600 N·m. The material has yield strength 350 MPa. Find the factor of safety by the maximum shear stress theory and by the distortion energy theory, and comment on the difference. 13 marks

σ = 32M/(πd³) and τ = 16T/(πd³); principal stresses σ1,2 = σ/2 ± √((σ/2)² + τ²), σ3 = 0; Tresca: FS = Syt/(σ1 − σ3); von Mises: σ′ = √(σ² + 3τ²), FS = Syt/σ′; Tresca gives the smaller factor of safety; difference of the order of 15 % in pure shear.

Apply A flat steel plate 10 mm thick and 100 mm wide has a central circular hole of 20 mm diameter and carries an axial pull of 60 kN. Taking Kt = 2.4 from the chart and q = 0.85, find the nominal stress, the peak stress and the fatigue notch factor. 10 marks

nominal stress on the net section: σnom = P/((w − d)t); peak stress σmax = Ktσnom; Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1); state that the net section is used for σnom in tension; comment that peak stress is local and dies out within one hole diameter.

Apply A steel component has Sut = 650 MPa, Syt = 520 MPa and a completely reversed endurance limit Se = 240 MPa. It works at σm = 120 MPa and σa = 150 MPa. Check the safety by the Soderberg, Goodman and Gerber criteria and state which one is most conservative. 13 marks

Soderberg: σa/Se + σm/Syt = 1/FS; Goodman: σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1/FS; Gerber: σa/Se + (σm/Sut)² = 1/FS; Soderberg gives the lowest factor of safety; check the yield line σa + σm ≤ Syt; Gerber closest to ductile test data.

Apply A weight of 500 N is dropped through a height of 40 mm on to a collar at the lower end of a vertical steel rod 20 mm diameter and 1.5 m long, with E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum stress in the rod and the impact factor. 12 marks

static extension δst = WL/(AE); energy balance W(h + δ) = ½Pδ; dynamic extension δ = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)); impact factor = δ/δst; σmax = impact factor × σst; state assumptions: elastic impact, no energy lost, mass of rod neglected.

Apply A stepped shaft has a fillet with Kt = 2.2, and the material has q = 0.9, Se = 220 MPa, Sut = 700 MPa and Syt = 550 MPa. Bending at the fillet alternates between +120 MPa and −120 MPa. Find the fatigue factor of safety by the Goodman criterion and comment on the result. 14 marks

Kf = 1 + q(Kt − 1); the cycle is fully reversed, so σm = 0 and σa = 120 MPa; apply Kf to the alternating stress; FS = Se/(Kfσa) for zero mean stress; if a mean stress existed, use σa/Se + σm/Sut = 1/FS; local yielding at the notch may relieve the peak, so the estimate is conservative.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the maximum shear stress theory and the distortion energy theory. Show how much their yield predictions differ in pure shear, and explain why the distortion energy theory agrees better with test data for ductile metals. 13 marks

in pure shear σ1 = τ, σ2 = −τ, σ3 = 0; Tresca gives τy = 0.5 Syt; von Mises gives τy = Syt/√3 = 0.577 Syt; difference of about 15 %; hydrostatic stress does not cause yield, and only the distortion energy excludes it; Tresca is simple and safe for design; von Mises is more accurate for analysis.

Evaluate Discuss the effect of mean stress on fatigue failure. Compare the Soderberg, Goodman, Gerber and yield-line criteria on a Haigh diagram, and recommend a criterion for a gearbox shaft and for a highly stressed aircraft component, giving reasons. 14 marks

Haigh diagram: σa against σm, intercepts at Se and Sut; Soderberg line uses Syt, Goodman uses Sut, Gerber is a parabola; yield line σa + σm = Syt must never be crossed; Gerber fits ductile data, Goodman is slightly conservative, Soderberg most conservative; choice depends on data available and consequence of failure; state the factor of safety you would use in each case.

Evaluate A shaft has failed in service at a keyway even though the calculated nominal stress was below yield. Analyse the possible reasons and explain how you would redesign the part. 14 marks

fatigue under repeated stress below yield; Kt at the keyway end, converted to Kf through q; rough surface, corrosion or fretting under the key; residual tensile stress from machining; bending plus torsion acting together at the same section; remedies: end-milled or sled-runner keyway, larger fillet, shot peening, induction hardening, better finish; recheck with the Goodman line and a suitable factor of safety.

Evaluate Explain why a load applied suddenly produces a larger stress than the same load applied gradually. Derive the impact factor for a suddenly applied load and for a weight dropped from a height, and state the assumptions made. 12 marks

work done by the load equals strain energy stored; gradual load: work = ½Pδ; suddenly applied load: work = Pδ, so impact factor = 2; dropped weight: W(h + δ) = ½Pδ, leading to δ = δst(1 + √(1 + 2h/δst)); assumptions: stress remains elastic, no energy lost as heat or sound, struck mass neglected, impact concentrated at one point; real parts add stress concentration on top of the impact factor.

Glossary

Beach mark
A larger curved mark on a fatigue fracture surface showing where the crack front stood after a block of cycles.
Brittle material
A material that fractures with little or no plastic deformation and is stronger in compression than in tension.
Ductile material
A material that shows large plastic strain before fracture, usually more than 5 % elongation in a tensile test.
Factor of safety
The ratio of the strength of the part to the stress it actually carries.
Fatigue crack growth
The steady extension of a crack by a small step each cycle in stage II, roughly following the Paris law.
Fatigue life
The number of cycles a part survives at a given stress amplitude before it fails.
Fillet
A rounded transition between two sections of different size, provided to reduce the stress concentration.
Haigh diagram
A plot of alternating stress against mean stress used to check fatigue safety against a failure line.
Hydrostatic stress
Equal normal stress on all three planes with no shear; it changes volume but does not make a ductile metal yield.
Marin factors
Correction factors for surface finish, size, load type, temperature and reliability that convert the specimen endurance limit into a component endurance limit.
Modulus of resilience
The strain energy stored per unit volume at the yield point, equal to Syt²/(2E).
Nominal stress
The stress found from simple bending, torsion or axial formulas on the net or gross section, ignoring the local disturbance.
Octahedral shear stress
The shear stress on the octahedral planes, whose magnitude is proportional to the von Mises equivalent stress.
Persistent slip band
A narrow band on the surface of a cyclically loaded metal where slip concentrates and a fatigue crack starts.
Plane stress
A stress state in which one principal stress is zero, as on the free surface of a loaded shaft.
Repeated stress
A cycle that rises from zero to a maximum and falls back to zero, so the stress ratio R = 0.
Reversed stress
A cycle in which the stress swings equally on both sides of zero, so the stress ratio R = −1.
Strain energy
The energy stored in a deformed body, equal to the area under its load–deflection curve.
Stress raiser
Any geometric feature such as a hole, groove, keyway or shoulder that forces the stress lines to crowd together.
Stress ratio R
R = σmin/σmax, the number that fixes where a cycle sits on the Haigh diagram.
Striation
A fine ridge on a fatigue fracture surface that marks the advance of the crack in one load cycle.
Sudden load
A load that goes from zero to its full value in one step, producing twice the static deflection and stress.
Toughness
The energy absorbed per unit volume up to fracture, given by the whole area under the stress–strain curve.
Ultimate tensile strength Sut
The highest engineering stress reached in a tensile test, before necking begins.
Yield strength Syt
The stress at which a material begins to deform permanently, measured in MPa.
