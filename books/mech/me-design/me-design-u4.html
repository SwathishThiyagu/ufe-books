Machine Design Unit 4 · Joints, Screws, Springs and Flywheels

This unit sizes the parts that hold machines together and the parts that store energy: bolts, welds, pins and cotters, rivets and adhesives, power screws, springs and flywheels. Each is designed the same way: find every way it can fail, write one stress for each, and make the weakest one strong enough. About this unit

The first four chapters are joints. Threaded fasteners and welds share one method for eccentric loads: move the load to the centroid, split it into a direct part and a moment part, and add the two at the worst point. Knuckle and cotter joints and riveted joints are then designed failure mode by failure mode, and bonded joints show why adhesives are loaded in shear. Power screws turn torque into a large axial force through an inclined plane. The next four chapters store energy in springs: helical springs in torsion, nested springs and fatigue, leaf springs in bending and rubber springs in shear and compression. The unit ends with flywheels, which store energy in rotation and must survive their own centrifugal stress.

4.1 Threaded fasteners and bolted joints under eccentric loading

A bolted bracket rarely pulls through the centre of its bolt group. The load arrives off-centre, so each bolt feels a direct share plus a turning share. The method is always the same: move the load to the centroid of the bolts, split it into a force and a moment, find each bolt's share of both, and add them at the worst bolt. Machine frames, motor mounts, wall cranes and pipe brackets are sized this way.

You need direct shear, tension and the maximum principal and maximum shear stress theories from the chapters on stresses and theories of failure.

Threads, fasteners and the stress area

A screw thread has three diameters. The major diameter d is the largest and is the size in the name. The minor (core) diameter dc is at the thread root. The pitch diameter lies between them. Pitch p is the axial distance between neighbouring threads; lead is the axial advance in one turn (lead = p for a single-start thread).

M20 × 2.5 means a 20 mm major diameter and a 2.5 mm pitch (plain M20 means the coarse pitch). A through bolt takes a nut, a cap screw goes into a tapped hole and a stud is threaded at both ends.

A bolt in tension breaks across the threaded part, so its stress is taken on the tensile stress area:

As = (π/4)(d − 0.9382p)²

As is in mm², d is the major diameter in mm and p the pitch in mm. For M20 × 2.5, As = (π/4)(17.65)² = 245 mm²; for M12 × 1.75, 84.3 mm²; for M16 × 2, 157 mm². Tables give the same values.

A bolt in shear is checked on the shank area πd²/4, or on the core area when threads cross the shear plane.

Initial tension and the bolt under an external load

A bolt is tightened before the service load arrives. The clamp force is the initial tension (preload) Pi. Many Indian textbooks take Pi = 2840d N (d in mm) for a fluid-tight joint: 56.8 kN for M20. The tightening torque is about T = 0.2 d Pi for dry steel.

When an external tensile load P is applied, the bolt stretches a little more and the clamped plates relax a little. Only a fraction C of P reaches the bolt:

Fb = Pi + C·P

C = kb/(kb + kc), where kb is the bolt stiffness and kc the stiffness of the clamped parts. A soft gasket makes C near 1; metal-to-metal joints give about 0.2 to 0.3. The joint opens when P reaches Pi/(1 − C).

Eccentric load in the plane of the bolts

When the load lies in the plane of the joint, every bolt is in shear. The plate is taken as rigid and turns slightly about the bolt centroid G, so a bolt's moment share grows with its distance from G.

  1. Find the centroid G of the bolt group (the centre of the bolt areas), not of the bracket.
  2. Move the load P to G and add the moment M = P·e, where e is the perpendicular distance from G to the line of action.
  3. Primary shear on every bolt: P1 = P/n, parallel to P (n is the number of bolts).
  4. Secondary shear on bolt i: P2 = M·ri/Σr², perpendicular to the line from G to that bolt, in the sense of the moment.
  5. Add P1 and P2 as vectors at each bolt: R = √(P1² + P2² + 2P1P2 cos θ), with θ the angle between them. The largest R sizes the bolts.

Eccentric load in the plane of the bolt axes

A bracket bolted to a column face, with a load P hanging at distance e from the face, tries to tilt about its bottom edge. Each bolt stretches in proportion to its distance l from that edge, and every bolt also carries direct shear.

Wi = P·e·li / Σl²

Wi is the tensile load on bolt i in N; li its distance from the tilting edge in mm; Σl² the sum over all bolts. Direct shear per bolt is Ps = P/n.

The top bolt carries tension W and shear Ps together. By the maximum principal stress theory the equivalent tensile load is Pe = ½[W + √(W² + 4Ps²)]; by the maximum shear stress theory the equivalent shear load is ½√(W² + 4Ps²). Then As ≥ Pe/σallow.

Worked example

Question. A bracket is held by four bolts at the corners of a 100 mm × 200 mm rectangle (100 mm across, 200 mm high), with bolt centres at (±50, ±100) mm from the centroid G. A vertical load P = 12 kN acts downwards, 250 mm to the right of G, in the plane of the bracket. Find the largest bolt load and the bolt size for an allowable shear stress of 60 MPa on the shank.

  1. Primary shear: P1 = 12 000/4 = 3000 N per bolt, downwards.
  2. Moment about G: M = 12 000 × 250 = 3.0 × 10⁶ N·mm, clockwise.
  3. Every bolt has r = √(50² + 100²) = 111.8 mm, so Σr² = 4 × 111.8² = 50 000 mm².
  4. Secondary shear: P2 = 3.0 × 10⁶ × 111.8/50 000 = 6708 N per bolt, square to its radius, clockwise.
  5. At bolt (50, 100) the clockwise direction is (100, −50)/111.8, so P2 = (6000, −3000) N; adding P1 = (0, −3000) N gives (6000, −6000) N.
  6. R = √(6000² + 6000²) = 8485 N. Bolt (50, −100) also gives 8485 N; the two left bolts give only 6000 N.
  7. Shank area: A = 8485/60 = 141.4 mm², d = √(4 × 141.4/π) = 13.4 mm. Choose M16, the preferred size above it.

Answer. The two bolts on the load side carry 8.49 kN each; M16 bolts (shank 16 mm) are used, with a shear stress of 8485/201 = 42.2 MPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A bracket is bolted to a steel column by four bolts in two rows. The bottom row is 50 mm and the top row 250 mm above the tilting edge, two bolts in each row. A load P = 10 kN hangs 300 mm from the column face. The allowable tensile stress of the bolt material is 84 MPa. Find the bolt size by the maximum principal stress theory.

  1. Σl² = 2(50² + 250²) = 130 000 mm².
  2. Top-bolt tension: W = 10 000 × 300 × 250/130 000 = 5769 N.
  3. Direct shear: Ps = 10 000/4 = 2500 N per bolt.
  4. Pe = ½[5769 + √(5769² + 4 × 2500²)] = ½[5769 + 7634] = 6702 N.
  5. As ≥ 6702/84 = 79.8 mm². M12 has As = 84.3 mm²; σ = 6702/84.3 = 79.5 MPa ≤ 84 MPa.

Answer. M12 bolts; the top bolts carry 5.77 kN tension and 2.5 kN shear, an equivalent 6.70 kN.

Exam traps

  • Adding primary and secondary shear as numbers: 3000 + 6708 = 9708 N overstates the true 8485 N by 14 %. Add them as vectors.
  • Measuring r from the load point. In-plane shear uses r from the bolt centroid; the tilting bracket uses l from the tilting edge.
  • Using πd²/4 for a bolt in tension. Use As: 245 mm² for M20, not 314 mm².
  • Forgetting the direct shear P/n on a tilting bracket. The top bolt carries tension and shear, combined by a failure theory.

Analyze In the first worked example, why do the two left bolts carry only 6.0 kN while the right bolts carry 8.49 kN? 4 marks

On the right the secondary shear has a 3000 N downward part that adds to the primary shear. On the left its vertical part is upwards and cancels the primary shear, leaving only the horizontal 6000 N.

4.2 Welded joints under axial and eccentric loads

A weld joins parts by fusing metal, so the joint is permanent and needs no holes. Every weld is sized on the area that actually carries the load, the throat. Once the throat area and the second moments of the weld group are known, an eccentric load splits into a direct part and a moment part, exactly as for a bolt group. Brackets, machine frames, pressure-vessel nozzles and truss gussets are sized this way.

You need the eccentric bolt-group method of threaded fasteners and bolted joints.

Butt welds, fillet welds and the throat

A butt weld joins plates edge to edge and fills the full thickness (single-V, double-V, U or J edge preparation). Its throat equals the plate thickness t, so under a load P across it σ = P/(tL).

A fillet weld joins two surfaces at a right angle and has a triangular section with leg (size) h. It breaks on its throat, the shortest section through the triangle, at 45°: throat = h cos 45° = 0.707h. A fillet weld is parallel when it runs along the load and transverse when it runs across it.

P = 0.707·h·L·τ

P is the load one fillet weld carries in N; h the leg in mm; L the effective length in mm (actual length minus about 2h for the craters at start and stop); τ the permissible shear stress in MPa. Two welds carry twice this.

Parallel fillet welds are always checked in shear on the throat. For transverse fillet welds most designers also use shear on the throat; some textbooks use the tensile stress instead (P = 0.707hLσt), so follow your prescribed book. A joint with both kinds is checked as P = 0.707h·ΣL·τ.

Eccentric load in the plane of the welds

When the load lies in the plane of the weld group but misses its centroid G, move it to G and add a torque T = P·e. Treat each weld as a line of zero width (the weld-line method) and multiply by the throat at the end.

  1. Find G of the weld lines. For two equal parallel welds it lies midway.
  2. Throat area A = 0.707h·ΣL.
  3. Polar moment J = 0.707h·Σ(L³/12 + L·d²), where d is the distance of each weld's centre from G.
  4. Primary shear τ₁ = P/A, parallel to P and equal everywhere.
  5. Secondary shear τ₂ = T·r/J, square to the line from G, largest at the farthest points.
  6. Add τ₁ and τ₂ as vectors at the corner points: τ = √(τ₁² + τ₂² + 2τ₁τ₂ cos θ). The largest value must not exceed the permissible shear.

Eccentric load causing bending of the welds

A bracket welded to the face of a column, with the load P at distance e from the face, puts the welds in bending about a horizontal axis through G. The load gives a direct shear on the throats and the moment M = P·e gives a bending stress that is largest at the top and bottom of the welds.

τ₁ = P/A, σb = M·y/I

A is the throat area in mm²; I the second moment of the throat areas about the bending axis in mm⁴ (two vertical welds of length L: I = 2 × 0.707h·L³/12); y the distance from that axis to the weld end in mm.

The two stresses act at right angles on the throat, so they are combined as a resultant τ = √(σb² + τ₁²), which is the convention of Shigley and Bhandari. Some textbooks use the maximum shear stress instead, ½√(σb² + 4τ₁²); it gives a smaller leg, so state which one you use.

The permissible shear stress comes from the electrode, the code and the inspection level, and examiners state it. Welds under fatigue loading need much lower values, because the weld toe is a notch.

Worked example

Question. Two vertical fillet welds, each with effective length 100 mm and leg h = 6 mm, are 100 mm apart. A vertical load P = 10 kN acts downwards in the plane of the welds, 150 mm to the right of the centroid G. Find the largest resultant shear stress on the throat.

  1. Throat: 0.707 × 6 = 4.24 mm. Area A = 2 × 4.24 × 100 = 848.4 mm².
  2. Primary shear: τ₁ = 10 000/848.4 = 11.8 MPa, downwards everywhere.
  3. Torque: T = 10 000 × 150 = 1.5 × 10⁶ N·mm, clockwise.
  4. J = 4.24 × 2(100³/12 + 100 × 50²) = 4.24 × 666 667 = 2.83 × 10⁶ mm⁴.
  5. The weld ends are farthest from G: r = √(50² + 50²) = 70.7 mm, so τ₂ = 1.5 × 10⁶ × 70.7/2.83 × 10⁶ = 37.5 MPa at 45°.
  6. At the ends of the right weld, τ₂ has parts 26.5 MPa across and 26.5 MPa down; with τ₁ the down part is 38.3 MPa, so τ = √(26.5² + 38.3²) = 46.6 MPa.
  7. At the ends of the left weld the down part of τ₂ opposes τ₁: 26.5 − 11.8 = 14.7 MPa, so τ = √(26.5² + 14.7²) = 30.3 MPa.

Answer. The largest throat shear is 46.6 MPa, at the top and bottom of the weld nearer the load; the far weld reaches only 30.3 MPa.

Worked example 2

Question. A bracket plate is welded to a column face by two vertical fillet welds, each 150 mm long, one on each side of the plate. A load P = 15 kN acts downwards 150 mm from the column face. The permissible shear stress in the weld is 80 MPa. Find the leg size h.

  1. Work per mm of leg h. Throat area A = 2 × 0.707h × 150 = 212.1h mm².
  2. Direct shear: τ₁ = 15 000/(212.1h) = 70.7/h MPa.
  3. I = 2 × 0.707h × 150³/12 = 397 700h mm⁴; y = 75 mm; M = 15 000 × 150 = 2.25 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  4. Bending stress: σb = 2.25 × 10⁶ × 75/(397 700h) = 424.3/h MPa at the weld ends.
  5. Resultant: τ = √(424.3² + 70.7²)/h = 430.2/h MPa.
  6. 430.2/h ≤ 80 gives h ≥ 5.38 mm. Use h = 6 mm: τ = 430.2/6 = 71.7 MPa.

Answer. Leg size 6 mm (the maximum shear stress convention would accept 3 mm, so say which convention you use).

Exam traps

  • Using the leg h as the throat. The throat is 0.707h; using h gives a stress only 0.707 of the true value, an understatement of 29 %.
  • Adding τ₁ and τ₂ as numbers. They act at an angle: in the first example 11.8 + 37.5 = 49.3 MPa, against the true 46.6 MPa.
  • Checking only one corner. The weld nearer the load is worse (46.6 MPa) than the far weld (30.3 MPa).
  • Using J or I of the bracket plate instead of the weld lines times the throat.
  • Mixing kN·m with mm. Work in N and mm, so stresses come out in MPa.

Check yourself

Remember Why is the throat of an equal-leg fillet weld 0.707h? 2 marks

The throat is the shortest distance from the root to the face of the 45° triangle: h cos 45° = 0.707h.

Remember Distinguish parallel and transverse fillet welds. 2 marks

A parallel fillet weld runs along the direction of the load and is loaded in shear along its length. A transverse fillet weld runs across the load.

Understand Why is the primary shear the same everywhere in a weld group while the secondary shear is not? 3 marks

The direct load is shared equally by the whole throat area. The torque turns the group about G, so the shear grows with the distance r from G.

Throat 5.66 mm; area 848.4 mm²; τ = 30 000/848.4 = 35.4 MPa.

Apply Two parallel fillet welds, each 80 mm long with leg 5 mm, are 60 mm apart. A 6 kN load parallel to them acts 100 mm from G. Find the largest throat shear. 5 marks

Throat 3.54 mm; A = 565.6 mm²; τ₁ = 10.6 MPa. J = 3.54 × 2(80³/12 + 80 × 30²) = 8.11 × 10⁵ mm⁴. r = √(40² + 30²) = 50 mm; τ₂ = 6 × 10⁵ × 50/8.11 × 10⁵ = 37.0 MPa, with parts 29.6 and 22.2 MPa. Near weld: √(29.6² + (22.2 + 10.6)²) = 44.2 MPa.

Analyze In the second worked example, why does the bending stress dominate the direct shear? 4 marks

σb/τ₁ = (M·y/I)/(P/A) = 6e/L for these welds = 6 × 150/150 = 6. Any lever arm comparable with the weld length makes bending about six times the direct shear, so longer welds help more than a larger leg.

4.3 Knuckle and cotter joints

Knuckle and cotter joints connect two rods that pull apart. A knuckle joint passes a pin through an eye and a fork, so it allows a little angular movement; a cotter joint drives a tapered flat cotter through a socket and a spigot, so it holds the rods rigidly in line. Each part fails in its own way, by tension, shear, crushing or bending, so the design is a chain of simple checks, one per failure mode. They are used in tie rods, valve rods, roof trusses, crane links and the piston rod to crosshead of steam engines.

You need direct tension, shear, crushing (bearing) and bending stress from the chapters on simple stresses.

Knuckle joint: parts and load path

One rod ends in an eye; the other ends in a fork with two tines. The eye sits between the tines and a pin passes through all three; a collar and a taper pin keep it in place. The pull P goes from the eye into the middle of the pin and out through the two tines, so the pin has two shear planes: double shear.

Common starting proportions for rod diameter d are: pin diameter d₁ = d, outer diameter of eye d₂ = 2d, collar diameter 1.5d, eye thickness b = 1.25d and each fork tine a = 0.75d. Proportions only propose sizes; the stress checks decide them.

Knuckle joint: the checks

  • Rod in tension: σt = 4P/(πd²).
  • Pin in double shear: τ = 2P/(πd₁²).
  • Pin in bending: M = (P/2)(b/4 + a/3) and σb = 32M/(πd₁³). This assumes the eye load spread evenly over b and the tine loads varying over a. The simpler point-load model M = P(a + b)/4 is more cautious.
  • Eye in tension and in double shear across the hole: σt = τ = P/[b(d₂ − d₁)].
  • Eye crushing on the pin: σc = P/(b·d₁).
  • Fork in tension and shear: P/[2a(d₂ − d₁)]; fork crushing: P/(2a·d₁).

The pin is long compared with its diameter, so bending often decides it, not shear. A pin that passes the shear check can still bend.

Cotter joint: socket, spigot and cotter

In the socket-and-spigot cotter joint, one rod ends in a socket (a hollow end) and the other in a spigot with a collar. A flat cotter, tapered about 1 in 30 so it wedges tight and can be knocked out, passes through slots in both. With rod diameter d, spigot diameter d₂, socket outer diameter d₁, cotter thickness t and width b:

  • Rod tension: P = (π/4)d²σt.
  • Spigot tension across the slot: P = [(π/4)d₂² − d₂t]σt, usually with t = 0.31d₂.
  • Socket tension across the slot: P = [(π/4)(d₁² − d₂²) − (d₁ − d₂)t]σt.
  • Cotter in double shear: P = 2btτ.
  • Spigot crushing on the cotter: P = d₂tσc.
  • Socket collar crushing: P = (d₄ − d₂)tσc, with d₄ its outer diameter.
  • Spigot collar crushing on the socket: P = (π/4)(d₃² − d₂²)σc.

Ends beyond the slot also shear out: the spigot end needs a length a with P = 2a·d₂·τ.

Worked example

Question. Design a knuckle joint for a tie rod pull of 50 kN. Permissible stresses: tension and bending 80 MPa, shear 50 MPa, crushing 90 MPa. Use the standard proportions, then check every part.

  1. Rod: d = √(4 × 50 000/(π × 80)) = 28.2 mm; take d = 30 mm.
  2. Proportions: d₁ = 30 mm, d₂ = 60 mm, b = 1.25 × 30 = 37.5 mm, a = 0.75 × 30 = 22.5 mm.
  3. Pin shear: τ = 2 × 50 000/(π × 30²) = 35.4 MPa ≤ 50. Passes.
  4. Pin bending: M = 25 000(37.5/4 + 22.5/3) = 421 900 N·mm; σb = 32 × 421 900/(π × 30³) = 159 MPa > 80. Fails.
  5. Pin needed for bending: d₁ = (32 × 421 900/(π × 80))1/3 = 37.7 mm; take d₁ = 38 mm (σb = 78.3 MPa).
  6. Eye with d₂ = 60 mm: P/[b(d₂ − d₁)] = 50 000/(37.5 × 22) = 60.6 MPa, above τ = 50. Raise d₂ to 66 mm: 50 000/(37.5 × 28) = 47.6 MPa, below both 50 and 80.
  7. Eye crushing: 50 000/(37.5 × 38) = 35.1 MPa. Fork: tension and shear 50 000/(2 × 22.5 × 28) = 39.7 MPa; crushing 50 000/(2 × 22.5 × 38) = 29.2 MPa. All pass.

Answer. Rod 30 mm, pin 38 mm (set by bending, not shear), eye outer diameter 66 mm, eye 37.5 mm thick, tines 22.5 mm thick.

Worked example 2

Question. A socket-and-spigot cotter joint carries 50 kN. Permissible stresses: tension 75 MPa, shear 60 MPa, crushing 100 MPa. Find d, d₂, t, d₁ and b.

  1. Rod: d = √(4 × 50 000/(π × 75)) = 29.1 mm; take 30 mm.
  2. Spigot with t = 0.31d₂: 50 000 = (0.785 − 0.31)d₂² × 75 gives d₂ = 37.4 mm; take d₂ = 40 mm, t = 12.4 mm.
  3. Spigot crushing with t = 12: 50 000/(40 × 12) = 104 MPa > 100. Take t = 13 mm: 96.2 MPa. Spigot tension: 50 000/(1256.6 − 520) = 67.9 MPa.
  4. Socket: [(π/4)(d₁² − 1600) − 13(d₁ − 40)] × 75 = 50 000 gives d₁ = 51.3 mm; take 52 mm.
  5. Cotter shear: b = 50 000/(2 × 13 × 60) = 32.1 mm; take b = 34 mm (τ = 56.6 MPa).
  6. Spigot collar: d₃ = √(4 × 50 000/(π × 100) + 40²) = 47.3 mm, say 48 mm. Socket collar: d₄ = 40 + 50 000/(13 × 100) = 78.5 mm, say 80 mm.

Answer. d = 30 mm, d₂ = 40 mm, t = 13 mm, d₁ = 52 mm, b = 34 mm, d₃ = 48 mm, d₄ = 80 mm.

Exam traps

  • Single shear for the knuckle pin. It has two shear planes: τ = 2P/(πd₁²).
  • Stopping after the shear check. In the worked example the 30 mm pin passes shear at 35 MPa but bends at 159 MPa.
  • Forgetting that the eye's net section is b(d₂ − d₁), not b·d₂.
  • Taking the cotter shear area as b × t once. The cotter has two shear planes: P = 2btτ.
  • Using the rod diameter for spigot crushing. Crushing is on d₂ × t, the projected contact of the cotter on the spigot.

Check yourself

Remember Name the parts of a knuckle joint and of a cotter joint. 2 marks

Knuckle: eye, fork, pin, collar and taper pin. Cotter joint: socket, spigot with collar, and cotter.

Understand Why is a cotter given a taper, and why on one side only? 3 marks

The taper (about 1 in 30) lets the cotter be driven in to pull the joint tight and knocked out to dismantle it. One taper is enough to wedge it; a small taper keeps it self-locking so it does not work loose.

Apply A cotter 20 mm wide and 8 mm thick carries 30 kN. Find its shear stress. 3 marks

τ = 30 000/(2 × 20 × 8) = 93.8 MPa.

Apply A knuckle pin of 25 mm carries 60 kN. Find the shear stress. 3 marks

τ = 2 × 60 000/(π × 25²) = 61.1 MPa.

Analyze If the knuckle pin diameter is doubled at the same load, how do its shear and bending stresses change? 4 marks

Shear stress ∝ 1/d₁², so it becomes one quarter. Bending stress ∝ 1/d₁³, so it becomes one eighth (the moment arm b/4 + a/3 is unchanged if a and b stay the same).

Analyze Why is a knuckle joint used where the rods must swing, and a cotter joint where they must stay in line? 4 marks

The knuckle pin acts as a hinge, so the rods can turn about it in one plane. The cotter wedges the spigot hard into the socket, so the rods cannot turn and the joint can also take compression.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern knuckle and clevis joints are bought as standard clevis pins and rod ends sized from catalogues. Their life is often set by fretting and wear at the pin, so bushes and grease nipples matter more than the static checks. Search for clevis pin standards and fretting fatigue of pinned joints.

4.4 Riveted joints for structures and bonded joints

A rivet is a soft-metal pin with one head, put through matching holes and hammered so that a second head forms on the other side. Riveted joints are permanent, do not need welding skill and do not crack through a heat-affected zone, so they survive in bridges, truss gussets, aircraft skins and older boilers. Bonded joints use a layer of adhesive instead of fasteners: they spread the load over a whole area, join thin or unlike materials and add no holes. Both are designed by finding every way the joint can fail and making the weakest one strong enough.

You need tension, shear and crushing stress, and the bolt-group method of threaded fasteners and bolted joints for eccentric loads.

Riveted joints: types and terms

In a lap joint one plate overlaps the other. In a butt joint the plates meet edge to edge and one or two cover plates (straps) are riveted over the gap. Rows may be chain riveted (rivets in line) or zig-zag riveted (staggered).

  • Pitch p: the distance between rivet centres along a row.
  • Back pitch: the distance between rows.
  • Margin m: from a rivet centre to the plate edge, usually 1.5d.
  • Rivet hole diameter d: for plates over 8 mm, Unwin's formula d = 6√t (t in mm) gives a first size; for t = 10 mm, d ≈ 19 mm.

For structures, rivets of a tie bar are often arranged in a lozenge (diamond) pattern: one rivet in the first row, then two, then three. The plate loses only one hole at the first section, where it carries the full load, so the joint is efficient.

How a riveted joint fails, and its efficiency

Take one pitch length of the joint as a repeating strip. It can fail in four ways:

  • Tearing of the plate between the holes: Pt = (p − d)tσt.
  • Shearing of the rivets: Ps = n(π/4)d²τ in single shear; in double shear (butt joint with two straps) about twice this.
  • Crushing of the plate or rivet: Pc = n·d·t·σc.
  • Tearing or shearing of the margin: prevented by keeping m ≥ 1.5d.

Here n is the number of rivets in one pitch length and σt, τ, σc are the permissible stresses. The joint is as strong as the least of these. Its efficiency compares that with the solid plate:

η = min(Pt, Ps, Pc) / (p·t·σt)

η is the efficiency (a fraction); p·t·σt is the strength of an unpunched plate one pitch wide.

The best design makes Pt equal to the smaller of Ps and Pc, which fixes the pitch. A rivet group under an eccentric load is analysed like a bolt group: direct shear P/n plus moment shear Pe·r/Σr², added as vectors.

Bonded (adhesive) joints

An adhesive joint carries load through a thin layer of epoxy, acrylic, polyurethane or cyanoacrylate. Adhesives are strong in shear and weak in peel and cleavage, so joints are laid out as lap, strap or scarf joints that load the layer in shear.

τavg = P/(b·l)

τavg is the average shear stress in the adhesive in MPa; P the load in N; b the joint width and l the overlap length in mm.

The shear is not uniform. The adherends stretch, so most of the load transfers near the two ends of the overlap, where the shear peaks (the shear-lag theory of Volkersen). A single lap joint also bends, adding peel stress at the ends. So doubling the overlap does not double the strength; widening the joint does.

  • Advantages: uniform load spread, no holes, joins thin and unlike materials, seals and damps.
  • Limits: weak in peel, creep and temperature limits, surface preparation decides strength, cannot be inspected easily.
  • Design rules: load in shear, avoid peel, taper the ends, keep bond lines thin and even, and use a rivet or bolt where peel cannot be avoided.

Worked example

Question. A double-riveted zig-zag lap joint joins 10 mm plates. Rivet holes are 20 mm in diameter. Permissible stresses: tension 80 MPa, shear 60 MPa, crushing 120 MPa. Find the pitch and the efficiency.

  1. In one pitch length there are n = 2 rivets, each in single shear.
  2. Shearing: Ps = 2 × (π/4) × 20² × 60 = 37 700 N.
  3. Crushing: Pc = 2 × 20 × 10 × 120 = 48 000 N.
  4. Shearing is the weaker, so make tearing equal to it: (p − 20) × 10 × 80 = 37 700 gives p = 67.1 mm; take p = 67 mm.
  5. Tearing at p = 67: Pt = 47 × 10 × 80 = 37 600 N.
  6. Solid plate: 67 × 10 × 80 = 53 600 N. Efficiency η = 37 600/53 600 = 0.701.
  7. Margin m = 1.5 × 20 = 30 mm.

Answer. Pitch 67 mm, margin 30 mm, efficiency 70.1 %; the joint fails first by tearing of the plate (37.6 kN per pitch), just below rivet shear (37.7 kN).

Worked example 2

Question. Two steel strips 25 mm wide are bonded in a single lap joint with 20 mm overlap. The epoxy's lap-shear strength is 20 MPa. With a factor of safety of 2, what load can the joint carry? Why would a 40 mm overlap not carry twice as much?

  1. Permissible average shear: 20/2 = 10 MPa.
  2. Bonded area: b × l = 25 × 20 = 500 mm².
  3. Load: P = 10 × 500 = 5000 N = 5 kN.
  4. With 40 mm overlap the average-stress formula would give 10 kN, but the shear peaks at the ends of the overlap and the middle carries little; the real gain is much smaller. Widening the joint to 50 mm doubles the strength reliably.

Answer. 5 kN at 20 mm overlap; for more load widen the joint or use a scarf joint rather than lengthening the overlap.

Exam traps

  • Using p instead of p − d for tearing. The plate tears through the holes, so only p − d of each pitch is left.
  • Taking every rivet in double shear. Lap joints put rivets in single shear; only butt joints with two straps give double shear.
  • Calling the strongest mode the joint strength. The least of tearing, shearing and crushing decides it.
  • Treating adhesive shear as uniform. τ = P/(bl) is an average; the ends carry far more, so overlap length has diminishing returns.

4.5 Design of power screws

A power screw turns rotation into straight-line motion: turning the screw pushes a nut, or the screw itself, along its axis. The thread is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder, so the torque must lift the load up that slope and beat friction on it. A small lead angle gives a large force for a small torque and makes the screw hold its load when let go. Screw jacks, vices, lathe lead screws, presses and valve spindles all work this way.

You need friction on an inclined plane, and combined direct and torsional stress from the chapters on stresses.

Thread forms and the inclined plane

Square threads have the highest efficiency but are hard to cut. ISO trapezoidal threads (30° included angle) and Acme threads (29°) are easier to make and can take up wear with a split nut. Buttress threads carry heavy load in one direction only.

For a square thread of nominal diameter d and pitch p, the core diameter is dc = d − p and the mean diameter dm = d − p/2. Unwrap one turn at the mean diameter: it becomes a slope of base πdm and height l, the lead (l = p for one start, 2p for two starts).

tan α = l/(π·dm)

α is the lead (helix) angle. The friction angle φ is given by tan φ = μ, the thread friction coefficient.

Torque, self-locking and efficiency

Tr = (W·dm/2)·tan(φ + α)

Tr is the torque to raise load W in N·mm. In terms of μ: Tr = (Wdm/2)(μ + tan α)/(1 − μ tan α).

Tl = (W·dm/2)·tan(φ − α)

Tl is the torque to lower the load, (Wdm/2)(μ − tan α)/(1 + μ tan α).

If φ > α, Tl is positive: a torque must be applied to lower the load, so the screw holds the load by itself. It is self-locking. If φ < α, Tl is negative and the load runs the screw down: it overhauls.

A thrust collar between the rotating screw and the load cup adds friction torque Tc = μc·W·Rm, where Rm = (R₁ + R₂)/2 is the mean collar radius (uniform wear). Tc is added both when raising and when lowering, so a collar makes a screw harder to overhaul.

η = W·l / (2π·T)

η is the efficiency; W·l is the work done on the load in one turn; 2πT the work put in. For a square thread without a collar, η = tan α/tan(α + φ).

A self-locking square-thread screw has an efficiency below 50 %: putting φ = α in tan α/tan(2α) gives (1 − tan²α)/2, which is under ½. For a trapezoidal or Acme thread, use μ/cos 15° (or μ/cos 14.5°) in place of μ, because the inclined flanks press harder on the nut.

Stresses in the screw and the nut

  • Direct stress on the core: σc = W/(πdc²/4).
  • Torsional shear on the core: τ = 16T/(πdc³).
  • Combined: τmax = √((σc/2)² + τ²) ≤ permissible shear.
  • Bearing pressure on the threads: pb = W/(πdm·h·z), with h = p/2 the thread depth and z the number of threads in the nut. Tables give the permissible pressure for each pair of materials; it falls as the rubbing speed rises.
  • Thread shear: τ = W/(πdc·(p/2)·z) on the screw and W/(πd·(p/2)·z) on the nut.
  • A long screw in compression must also be checked for buckling (Euler or Johnson).

Worked example

Question. A single-start square-thread screw has dm = 24 mm and lead 6 mm. It raises W = 20 kN with μ = 0.15. Find α, the raising and lowering torques and the efficiency, and say whether it self-locks. Then add a collar with μc = 0.12 and Rm = 20 mm.

  4. η = 20 000 × 6/(2π × 55 800) = 0.342.
  5. Collar: Tc = 0.12 × 20 000 × 20 = 48 000 N·mm. Total raising torque 103.8 N·m; η = 20 000 × 6/(2π × 103 800) = 0.184.

Answer. α = 4.55°, Tr = 55.8 N·m, Tl = 16.7 N·m, η = 34.2 %, self-locking; with the collar, 103.8 N·m and 18.4 %.

  • Using μ for a trapezoidal thread. Use μ/cos 15°.

4.6 Helical compression springs: stresses, deflection and surge

A helical compression spring is wire coiled into a helix. Push on it and each coil twists, so the wire is a torsion bar wound into a small space. Springs store energy, absorb shock, keep parts in contact and measure force: valve springs, clutch springs, suspension coils, spring balances and railway buffers. The design links four numbers, wire diameter d, mean coil diameter D, active coils and the load, through one stress formula and one deflection formula.

You need torsion of a round bar and strain energy from the chapters on stresses.

Types of springs and the terms of a helical spring

Springs come as helical compression, helical extension and torsion springs, spiral springs (clocks), leaf springs (vehicles), disc or Belleville springs (high force in little space) and rubber springs. This chapter covers the helical compression spring.

  • Spring index C = D/d, usually 4 to 12. A small C is hard to wind and highly stressed; a large C is floppy and buckles.
  • Active coils Na deflect; end coils only seat the spring.
  • Total coils Nt: plain ends Nt = Na; plain ground Na + 1; squared, or squared and ground, Na + 2.
  • Solid length Ls = Nt·d (squared and ground ends) with all coils touching.
  • Free length Lf = Ls + δmax + gap. A common gap allowance is 15 % of δmax, so the coils never close in service.
  • Pitch = Lf/(Nt − 1) for squared and ground ends.

Stress and deflection

A load W makes each section of wire carry a torque WD/2 and a direct shear W. The torsion gives 8WD/(πd³). The curvature of the coil raises the stress on the inside of the coil, and the direct shear adds to it there. The Wahl factor K corrects for both:

τ = K·8WD/(πd³) = K·8WC/(πd²)

τ is the largest shear stress, at the inside of the coil, in MPa; W in N; D and d in mm.

K = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C

K is the Wahl factor (1.25 at C = 6, 1.18 at C = 8). The direct-shear factor alone is Ks = 1 + 0.5/C.

δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴)

δ is the axial deflection in mm; G the shear modulus (about 81 000 MPa for spring steel). The rate is k = W/δ = Gd⁴/(8D³Na) and the energy stored U = ½Wδ.

A spring much longer than its diameter buckles sideways like a column. If Lf/D is above about 4, guide it on a rod or inside a tube.

Surge in springs

A spring has mass as well as stiffness, so it has its own natural frequency. When one end is struck, as by a cam, a compression wave runs along the coils. If the cam's frequency, or one of its harmonics, matches the spring's natural frequency, the coils resonate: this is surge. Some coils close up while others open, the stress rises far above the static value and the valve may float off the cam.

fn = (d/(2πD²Na))·√(G/2ρ)

fn is the natural frequency in Hz of a spring held between two plates; d and D in m; G in Pa; ρ the wire density in kg/m³ (7850 for steel).

Keep fn at least 15 to 20 times the operating frequency. Cures: raise fn (thicker wire, smaller D, fewer active coils), use friction dampers on the middle coils, use two concentric springs of different frequencies, or use a variable-pitch spring whose coils close one by one.

Worked example

Question. A spring of D = 50 mm, d = 6 mm and 10 active coils carries 500 N. G = 80 000 MPa. Find the Wahl factor, largest shear stress, deflection and rate.

  3. τ = 1.176 × 8 × 500 × 50/(π × 6³) = 1.176 × 294.7 = 346.6 MPa.
  4. δ = 8 × 500 × 50³ × 10/(80 000 × 6⁴) = 5.0 × 10⁹/1.037 × 10⁸ = 48.2 mm.
  5. k = 500/48.2 = 10.4 N/mm.

Answer. K = 1.176, τ = 347 MPa, δ = 48.2 mm, k = 10.4 N/mm.

4.7 Concentric springs and springs under fatigue loading

Two springs can be nested, one inside the other, so that a large load fits in a small space and the set still works if one spring breaks. Railway buffers, heavy-duty valve trains and governors use such concentric springs. Many springs also work under a load that rises and falls millions of times, so they fail by fatigue long before the static stress is reached. This chapter shows how nested springs share a load and how a spring is checked for a fluctuating load.

You need the stress and deflection formulas of helical compression springs, and the Goodman and Soderberg lines from the chapter on fluctuating stresses.

Concentric springs: sharing the load

Two springs of the same free length placed one inside the other are compressed by the same amount: they act in parallel. So their rates add and the load divides in the ratio of the rates:

W = P₁ + P₂, P₁/P₂ = k₁/k₂

P₁ and P₂ are the loads on the outer and inner spring in N; k₁ and k₂ their rates in N/mm; the set's rate is k₁ + k₂.

The two springs are wound in opposite hands, one right-hand and one left-hand, so that a coil of one cannot slip between the coils of the other and jam the set.

The standard design assumes both springs are of the same material, have the same free length, the same solid length, the same spring index C and the same maximum stress. Since τ = K·8PC/(πd²) with the same K and C, the load each carries is proportional to d²:

P₁/P₂ = d₁²/d₂²

d₁ and d₂ are the wire diameters of the outer and inner springs.

The radial clearance between the springs is usually taken as half the difference of the wire diameters, c = (d₁ − d₂)/2. Writing the clearance as (D₁ − d₁)/2 − (D₂ + d₂)/2 with D = Cd gives d₁/d₂ = C/(C − 2). Equal solid lengths of the active coils need N₁d₁ = N₂d₂.

Springs under fluctuating load

A spring in a valve train or clutch is loaded between Pmin and Pmax again and again. Split the load into a mean part and an alternating part:

Pm = (Pmax + Pmin)/2

Pm is the mean load; the alternating load is Pa = (Pmax − Pmin)/2.

Indian textbooks such as Bhandari apply only the direct-shear factor Ks = 1 + 0.5/C to the mean stress and the full Wahl factor K to the alternating stress, because the curvature effect behaves like a stress concentration, which matters only for the fluctuating part. Shigley applies the curvature factor to both, which is a little more cautious. Follow your prescribed book.

τm = Ks·8PmD/(πd³)

τm is the mean shear stress; the alternating shear stress is τa = K·8PaD/(πd³).

τa/Sse + τm/Ssu = 1/n

Sse is the torsional endurance limit of the wire (from data or given); Ssu the torsional ultimate strength, about 0.67Sut; n the factor of safety. Using the torsional yield strength Ssy (about 0.45Sut for cold-drawn wire) in place of Ssu gives the safer Soderberg line.

Some textbooks build the line from the spring's pulsating test, through (Sse′/2, Sse′/2) to (Ssy, 0); use it when your book does. Shot peening leaves the wire surface in compression and raises Sse; presetting (closing the new spring to solid once) lets it carry higher stress without taking a set.

  3. Inner: d₂ = 15/1.5 = 10 mm, D₂ = 60 mm. Both springs: τ = 1.2525 × 8 × 6923 × 90/(π × 15³) = 588.8 MPa ≤ 600.
  4. Clearance: (90 − 15)/2 − (60 + 10)/2 = 37.5 − 35 = 2.5 mm = (15 − 10)/2.
  5. Outer coils: N₁ = Gd₁⁴δ/(8P₁D₁³) = 81 370 × 15⁴ × 80/(8 × 6923 × 90³) = 8.16; take 8. Inner: N₂ = 12.24; take 12, so N₁d₁ = N₂d₂ = 120 mm.

4.8 Leaf (laminated) springs

A leaf (laminated) spring is a stack of flat steel strips of graded length, clamped at the centre and fixed to the vehicle frame at the ends. It works as a beam in bending, and because the number of leaves falls towards the ends roughly as the bending moment falls, every leaf is stressed about equally. The spring carries the load, locates the axle and damps vibration by friction between the leaves. Trucks, buses, trailers and railway wagons use it.

You need bending stress M/Z and beam deflection from the chapters on stresses, and spring energy from helical compression springs.

Construction and the constant-strength idea

The longest leaf, the master leaf, is rolled into an eye at each end for the shackle pins. Some springs have one or two more full-length leaves under it. The shorter graduated leaves lie below. A centre bolt and U-bolts clamp the stack to the axle, and rebound clips keep the leaves together when the spring springs back.

A semi-elliptic spring of eye-to-eye span 2L₁ with central load 2W acts as two cantilevers of length L, each loaded by W at its eye. Bending moment grows linearly from the eye to the centre. A triangular plate of constant thickness has a section modulus that grows the same way, so its stress is the same everywhere: a beam of uniform strength. Cut that triangle into strips and stack them, and you have the graduated leaves.

The clamped centre does not bend, so the effective length is 2L = 2L₁ − l, where l is the width of the centre band (about two-thirds of the U-bolt spacing when U-bolts are used).

Stress and deflection

With nf full-length leaves (including the master leaf), ng graduated leaves, n = nf + ng, leaf width b and thickness t, the full-length leaves are stressed 50 % more than the graduated ones:

σf = 18WL/[bt²(3nf + 2ng)]

σf is the stress in the full-length leaves; the graduated leaves carry σg = 12WL/[bt²(3nf + 2ng)].

δ = 12WL³/[Ebt³(3nf + 2ng)]

δ is the deflection at the eye relative to the centre, in mm; E the Young's modulus, about 207 000 MPa for spring steel. With all leaves graduated (nf = 0) this becomes 6WL³/(Enbt³); with all full-length, 4WL³/(Enbt³).

Nipping: equalising the stresses

To stress all leaves equally, the full-length leaves are given a slightly larger radius of curvature than the graduated leaves. Before assembly a gap C, the nip, appears between them at the ends. Tightening the centre bolt closes it, which pre-stresses the full-length leaves in the opposite sense to the load and the graduated leaves in the same sense. Under the full load all leaves then reach the same stress:

σ = 6WL/(nbt²)

σ is the equal stress in every leaf after nipping. The nip is C = 2WL³/(Enbt³), and the initial load on the centre bolt is Pi = 2nfngW/[n(3nf + 2ng)].

Nipping lowers the peak stress in the master leaf, which carries the eyes and is the most important leaf. The deflection formula is unchanged. Design steps: choose b and t from standard strip sizes (b is often 6 to 12 times t), find n for the permissible stress, check deflection, find the nip and bolt load, and check the eye and shackle pin.

Worked example

Question. A semi-elliptic leaf spring has an eye-to-eye span of 1000 mm and a central band 100 mm wide. It carries a central load of 9 kN. It has 2 full-length leaves and 6 graduated leaves, each 75 mm wide and 10 mm thick; E = 207 000 MPa. Find the stresses without and with nipping, the deflection, the nip and the bolt load.

  1. Effective length 2L = 1000 − 100 = 900 mm, so L = 450 mm. Load at each eye W = 9000/2 = 4500 N.
  2. 3nf + 2ng = 6 + 12 = 18; bt² = 75 × 100 = 7500 mm³.
  3. Without nipping: σf = 18 × 4500 × 450/(7500 × 18) = 270 MPa; σg = 12 × 4500 × 450/(7500 × 18) = 180 MPa.
  4. With nipping: σ = 6 × 4500 × 450/(8 × 7500) = 202.5 MPa in every leaf.
  5. δ = 12 × 4500 × 450³/(207 000 × 75 × 1000 × 18) = 17.6 mm.
  6. Nip: C = 2 × 4500 × 450³/(207 000 × 8 × 75 × 1000) = 6.6 mm.
  7. Bolt load: Pi = 2 × 2 × 6 × 4500/(8 × 18) = 750 N.

Answer. 270 MPa (full-length) and 180 MPa (graduated) without nip; 202.5 MPa in all leaves with a 6.6 mm nip and a 750 N bolt load; deflection 17.6 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the whole eye-to-eye span as L. L is half the effective length, after subtracting the clamped band: 450 mm here, not 1000 or 500 mm.
  • Using the central load 2W as W. Every formula uses the load at one eye.
  • Leaving out the master leaf from nf. The master leaf is a full-length leaf.
  • Thinking nipping changes the deflection. It only redistributes the stress between leaves.
  • Mixing GPa and MPa: with N and mm, E must be in MPa (207 000).

4.9 Rubber springs

A rubber spring is a block of rubber, usually bonded to metal plates, loaded in shear or compression. Rubber stretches many times more than steel before it is damaged, damps vibration by turning part of each cycle into heat, needs no lubrication and is quiet. It is the usual choice for engine and machine mounts, suspension bushes, bridge bearings, flexible couplings and buffers. Its design rests on two ideas: shear stiffness from the shear modulus, and compression stiffness that depends strongly on the shape of the block.

You need shear stress and strain, and the spring rate and natural frequency ideas of helical compression springs.

Rubber as a spring material

  • The shear modulus G of rubber is small, roughly 0.3 to 1.5 MPa, and rises with hardness (measured on the Shore A scale).
  • Rubber is nearly incompressible (Poisson's ratio close to 0.5), so its Young's modulus is about E = 3G and its volume hardly changes when it is squeezed.
  • Stiffness depends on the speed and temperature of loading: the dynamic stiffness is higher than the static stiffness.
  • Rubber creeps under steady load and loses stiffness with heat, ozone and oil, so designs keep stresses and strains low.

Rubber is used mostly in shear (sandwich mounts, bushes) and in compression (pads, buffers). It is not loaded in tension, because bonded rubber in tension tears from the edges.

Shear springs and compression springs

In a shear sandwich, a rubber layer of area A and thickness h is bonded between two plates that slide past each other:

ks = G·A/h

ks is the shear stiffness in N/mm; G in MPa; A in mm²; h in mm. The shear strain is γ = x/h for a sideways movement x.

In compression, a bonded block cannot bulge at its bonded faces, only at its free sides. The more bonded area there is compared with free area, the stiffer the block. This is measured by the shape factor:

S = loaded area / free (bulge) area

For a disc of diameter D and thickness h, S = D/(4h); for a block a × b × h, S = ab/[2h(a + b)].

Ec = E(1 + 2kS²), kc = EcA/h

Ec is the effective compression modulus; k is a material constant close to 1 for soft rubbers and smaller for hard ones; kc the compression stiffness in N/mm.

So a thin bonded pad is far stiffer in compression than in shear. Laminated bridge bearings use this: thin rubber layers between steel plates are very stiff vertically yet let the deck slide and rotate.

Mounts for vibration isolation

A machine of mass m on mounts of total stiffness k vibrates at its natural frequency fn = (1/2π)√(k/m), or fn = (1/2π)√(g/δs) from the static deflection δs. A forcing frequency f is passed on to the floor reduced only when the frequency ratio r = f/fn is above √2. With little damping the fraction passed on is the transmissibility T = 1/(r² − 1).

So isolation needs soft mounts: a low natural frequency and a large static deflection. Rubber's damping keeps the vibration limited when the machine runs up through resonance at start-up.

Worked example

Question. A 200 kg compressor running at 1440 rpm stands on four rubber sandwich mounts loaded in shear. Each mount has a rubber layer 60 mm × 60 mm, 20 mm thick, with G = 0.8 MPa. Find the static deflection, the natural frequency and the transmissibility.

  1. Stiffness of one mount: ks = 0.8 × 3600/20 = 144 N/mm. Four mounts in parallel: k = 576 N/mm = 576 000 N/m.
  2. Weight: 200 × 9.81 = 1962 N; static deflection δs = 1962/576 = 3.41 mm; shear strain 3.41/20 = 0.17.
  3. Natural frequency: fn = (1/2π)√(576 000/200) = 53.7/2π = 8.54 Hz.
  4. Forcing frequency: 1440/60 = 24 Hz, so r = 24/8.54 = 2.81, above √2.
  5. Transmissibility: T = 1/(2.81² − 1) = 1/6.90 = 0.145.

Answer. δs = 3.41 mm, fn = 8.54 Hz, and only about 14.5 % of the shaking force reaches the floor.

Worked example 2

Question. A rubber disc 80 mm in diameter and 10 mm thick (G = 0.8 MPa, k ≈ 1) is bonded to steel plates on both faces. Compare its stiffness in compression and in shear.

  1. Shape factor: S = 80/(4 × 10) = 2.

  3. Area: A = π × 80²/4 = 5027 mm².

Exam traps

  • Taking the total surface as the free area. Only the unbonded sides that can bulge count.
  • Choosing a stiff mount to cut vibration. Isolation needs f/fn above √2, which means a soft mount.
  • Designing rubber in tension. Bonded rubber is used in shear and compression.

4.10 Flywheels: solid and rimmed

A flywheel is a heavy rotating wheel that stores kinetic energy. An engine delivers torque in pulses and a punching press needs a large burst of energy for a moment in each stroke; the flywheel absorbs the surplus when supply exceeds demand and gives it back when demand exceeds supply, so the speed stays within limits. Its size is set by the energy it must swing and the speed variation allowed; its rim and arms must then survive the centrifugal and bending stresses of spinning.

You need kinetic energy of rotation, centrifugal force and bending stress from the chapters on stresses.

Energy fluctuation and the coefficient of speed fluctuation

Plot the engine torque against crank angle: this is the turning moment diagram. The mean torque line cuts it into loops; each loop's area is energy gained (above the line) or lost (below). Add the loop areas in turn: the difference between the largest and smallest running totals is the maximum fluctuation of energy ΔE.

Cs = (ω₁ − ω₂)/ω

Cs is the coefficient of fluctuation of speed; ω₁ and ω₂ the largest and smallest speeds; ω = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 the mean speed in rad/s. Engines need about 0.002 to 0.03; punching presses accept 0.05 to 0.2.

ΔE = I·ω²·Cs = m·v²·Cs

ΔE in J; I the mass moment of inertia in kg·m²; for a thin rim I ≈ mR², so ΔE = mv²Cs with v = ωR the rim speed in m/s.

The ratio ΔE / (work done per cycle) is the coefficient of fluctuation of energy Ce.

Solid disc flywheels

A solid disc of radius R and thickness b has I = ½mR² with m = ρπR²b. It is compact and cheap for small engines and presses. Its largest stress is at the centre, where the radial and hoop stresses are equal:

σmax = (3 + ν)ρω²R²/8

σmax in Pa; ν is Poisson's ratio (about 0.25 for cast iron); ρ in kg/m³. A cast-iron disc of R = 0.4 m at 600 rpm reaches only 1.85 MPa.

Rimmed flywheels: rim and arm stresses

A rimmed flywheel puts most of its mass in a heavy rim joined to the hub by arms, so it gives about twice the inertia of a disc of the same mass and radius. The arms and hub add only a small part, so they are usually neglected in finding the rim mass.

  • Hoop stress in a free rim: σt = ρv². It depends only on rim speed, so the material sets a safe speed: cast iron is usually kept below about 30 to 40 m/s.
  • Bending of the rim between arms: each segment acts as a beam fixed at the arms and loaded by its own centrifugal force, giving σb = 19.74ρv²R/(n²t), where n is the number of arms and t the radial thickness of the rim.
  • Combined rim stress (the usual textbook rule): σ = 0.75σt + 0.25σb.
  • Arms act as cantilevers from the hub carrying the torque T: the force at the rim is T/(nR), so the moment at the hub end is M = T(R − r)/(nR), with r the hub radius. Arms are usually elliptical, major axis a = 2 × minor axis, with Z = πa³/64 in the plane of rotation.

Worked example

Question. A press punches 20 mm holes in 15 mm plate of ultimate shear strength 350 MPa, 30 holes a minute. Punching takes one-tenth of each cycle. The flywheel has a rim of mean radius 0.5 m, runs at 300 rpm, and its speed may fall by 10 % (Cs = 0.1). Cast iron, ρ = 7200 kg/m³. Find the energy per hole, the flywheel's ΔE, the rim mass and cross-section, and the motor power.

  1. Peak force: F = π × 20 × 15 × 350 = 329 900 N.
  2. Energy per hole (force falling linearly to zero over the thickness): E = ½ × 329 900 × 0.015 = 2474 J.
  3. The motor supplies this evenly over the cycle, so during the punch it gives only 1/10 of it. The flywheel must supply ΔE = 2474 × (1 − 1/10) = 2227 J.
  4. Rim speed: v = 2π × 0.5 × 300/60 = 15.71 m/s.
  5. Rim mass: m = ΔE/(v²Cs) = 2227/(15.71² × 0.1) = 90.2 kg.
  6. Rim area: A = m/(ρ·2πR) = 90.2/(7200 × 3.142) = 0.00399 m² = 3990 mm², for example 80 mm × 50 mm.
  7. Hoop stress: ρv² = 7200 × 15.71² = 1.78 MPa. Motor power: 2474 × 30/60 = 1237 W.

Answer. 2474 J per hole, ΔE = 2227 J, rim 90 kg of 3990 mm² section, hoop stress 1.78 MPa, motor about 1.24 kW (before losses).

Worked example 2

Question. An engine flywheel must handle ΔE = 15 kJ at 600 rpm with Cs = 0.02. Rim mean radius 0.6 m, radial thickness 120 mm, 6 elliptical arms, hub radius 0.1 m, cast iron ρ = 7200 kg/m³. Takeaways

  1. An eccentric load on a bolt, rivet or weld group becomes a direct share P/n (or P/A) plus a moment share proportional to distance from the centroid; the two are added as vectors at the worst point.
  2. A bracket bolted to a column face tilts about its edge: bolt tension grows with distance from that edge and is combined with direct shear by a failure theory.
  3. A fillet weld is checked on its throat, 0.707 times the leg; bending stress and direct shear on the throat are combined as a resultant.
  4. Pins, cotters and rivets are designed one failure mode at a time: tension, shear, crushing and bending; the weakest mode sets the strength and the joint efficiency.
  5. Adhesives are strong in shear and weak in peel, and their shear peaks at the ends of an overlap, so wider joints help more than longer ones.
  6. A power screw is an inclined plane: φ > α makes it self-locking but limits its efficiency to below 50 %, and collar friction can take half the torque.
  7. A helical spring's wire is in torsion: τ = K·8WD/(πd³), δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴), and its natural frequency must stay far above the load frequency to avoid surge.
  8. Nested springs share deflection, so load divides as their rates; fluctuating loads are checked with mean and alternating shear against a fatigue line.
  9. A leaf spring is a beam of uniform strength; nipping makes all leaves equally stressed without changing the deflection.
  10. Rubber springs are soft in shear and, when bonded, stiff in compression; soft mounts isolate vibration once the forcing frequency exceeds √2 times the natural frequency.
  11. A flywheel's inertia comes from ΔE = Iω²Cs, and its rim must survive hoop stress ρv² plus bending between the arms.

Parts of the joint: rod with eye, rod with fork, pin, collar and taper pin; Failure modes: rod in tension, pin in double shear, pin in bending, eye and fork in tension, shear and crushing; Standard proportions d₁ = d, d₂ = 2d, a = 0.75d, b = 1.25d; Pin bending moment M = (P/2)(b/4 + a/3) with load spread over the eye and fork; Numbers: rod 26 mm, pin 34 mm, eye 60 mm, b = 32.5 mm, a = 19.5 mm; Closing check of every stress against its allowable value.

Model answer.

  1. A knuckle joint links two rods that carry a pull and must swing a little, as in a valve rod or a tie bar of a roof truss. One rod ends in a single eye of thickness b; the other ends in a fork of two eyes, each of thickness a. A pin of diameter d₁ passes through all three and is held by a collar and a taper pin. The joint is designed by writing one stress for each way it can fail and making each stress no larger than the allowable value.
  2. The failure modes and their equations, with P the pull, d the rod diameter and d₂ the outside diameter of the eye:
    PartFailureStress
    RodTensionσt = 4P/(πd²)
    PinDouble shearτ = 2P/(πd₁²)
    PinBendingσb = 32M/(πd₁³), M = (P/2)(b/4 + a/3)
    EyeTension or shear across the holeP/[b(d₂ − d₁)]
    EyeCrushing on the pinP/(b·d₁)
    ForkTension or shear across the holesP/[2a(d₂ − d₁)]
    ForkCrushing on the pinP/(2a·d₁)
  3. The bending moment comes from how the load spreads along the pin. The eye presses on the middle of the pin with a load spread uniformly over b, and each fork leg reacts P/2 with a load that grows linearly towards the inside face, so its centroid lies a/3 from that face. The moment at the middle of the pin is then M = (P/2)(b/4 + a/3). Taking the load as point loads would give a larger moment and an oversized pin.
  4. Rod diameter from tension:
    d = √(4P/(πσt)) = √(4 × 40 000/(π × 80)) = 25.2 mm
Take d = 26 mm.
  5. Standard proportions based on the rod: d₁ = d = 26 mm, d₂ = 2d = 52 mm, a = 0.75d = 19.5 mm, b = 1.25d = 32.5 mm. Check the pin at 26 mm:
    Shear:   τ = 2 × 40 000/(π × 26²) = 37.7 MPa ≤ 50 MPa
M = (40 000/2)(32.5/4 + 19.5/3) = 20 000 × 14.625 = 292 500 N·mm
Bending: σb = 32 × 292 500/(π × 26³) = 169.5 MPa > 80 MPa
  6. The pin fails in bending, not shear, so size it for bending:
    d₁ = ∛(32M/(πσb)) = ∛(32 × 292 500/(π × 80)) = 33.4 mm
Take d₁ = 34 mm.
σb = 32 × 292 500/(π × 34³) = 75.8 MPa ≤ 80 MPa
τ = 2 × 40 000/(π × 34²) = 22.0 MPa ≤ 50 MPa
  7. Eye diameter. With the larger pin the standard d₂ = 52 mm leaves only 18 mm of metal across the eye: 40 000/(32.5 × 18) = 68.4 MPa, above the 50 MPa allowed in shear. Find d₂ from shear of the eye:
    d₂ = d₁ + P/(bτ) = 34 + 40 000/(32.5 × 50) = 58.6 mm
Take d₂ = 60 mm.
  8. Check the eye and fork at d₁ = 34 mm and d₂ = 60 mm:
    CheckWorkingStress (MPa)Allowed (MPa)
    Eye, tension and shear40 000/(32.5 × 26)47.350
    Fork, tension and shear40 000/(2 × 19.5 × 26)39.450
    Eye, crushing40 000/(32.5 × 34)36.290
    Fork, crushing40 000/(2 × 19.5 × 34)30.290
  9. The remaining parts follow proportion: the collar is about 1.5d₁ = 51 mm across, and the taper pin is about 0.25d₁ = 8.5 mm, say 8 mm. The rod is often thickened near the eye to about 1.1d = 29 mm so that the change in section does not sit at the highest stress.
  10. Result: rod 26 mm, pin 34 mm, eye outside diameter 60 mm, eye thickness 32.5 mm, fork legs 19.5 mm each. Every stress is within its limit: rod 75.3 MPa in tension (40 000/530.9), pin 75.8 MPa in bending and 22.0 MPa in shear, eye 47.3 MPa, fork 39.4 MPa, crushing at most 36.2 MPa. The lesson of the example is that the pin's bending check decides the joint; a design that stops at shear would use a 26 mm pin stressed to 170 MPa.

Points to cover. Free body of half a coil: the wire carries torque WD/2 and direct shear W; Torsion stress 8WD/(πd³) plus direct shear, giving Ks = 1 + 0.5/C; Curvature raises the inner-fibre stress: Wahl factor K; Deflection from strain energy or the twist of the wire: δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴); Surge: natural frequency of the coils, fn = (d/(2πD²Na))√(G/2ρ); Numbers: C = 8, K = 1.184, τ = 193 MPa, δ = 20.5 mm, fn = 112 Hz.

Model answer.

  1. Cut the spring across the wire and keep one half. The axial load W acts along the spring axis, a distance D/2 from the wire centre. To keep the cut piece in balance the wire section must carry a torque T = WD/2 and a direct shear force W. In a close-coiled spring the helix angle is small, so the wire behaves as a straight bar in torsion.
  2. Torsion of a round bar gives the shear at the surface; the direct shear adds roughly uniformly at W/(πd²/4):
    τtorsion = 16T/(πd³) = 16(WD/2)/(πd³) = 8WD/(πd³)
τdirect = 4W/(πd²) = (8WD/(πd³)) × (d/2D) = (8WD/(πd³)) × 0.5/C
τ = Ks × 8WD/(πd³),  Ks = 1 + 0.5/C,  C = D/d
  3. The two stresses add on the inside of the coil, where the torsion shear and the direct shear point the same way. The wire is also curved: fibres on the inside of the coil are shorter than those on the outside, so the same twist strains them more. Wahl combined the curvature effect and the direct shear into one factor: K = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C, and the design stress is τ = K × 8WD/(πd³). K rises sharply for small C, which is why C is kept between about 4 and 12.

    L = πDNa
θ = (WD/2)(πDNa)/(G × πd⁴/32) = 16WD²Na/(Gd⁴)
δ = θ × D/2 = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴)
k = W/δ = Gd⁴/(8D³Na)
  5. The same result follows from strain energy: the work done ½Wδ equals the torsion energy T²L/(2GJ) of the wire, which again gives δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴). Direct shear adds very little to the deflection and is ignored.
  6. Surge. A spring has mass spread along its coils, so it can vibrate on its own like a string. When a cam opens and closes a valve at a frequency near the spring's natural frequency, a wave of compression runs up and down the coils, some coils close up while others open, and the stress rises far above the static value. For a spring held between two flat plates the lowest natural frequency is fn = (d/(2πD²Na))√(G/2ρ), in SI units. It is kept 15 to 20 times the operating frequency; otherwise the designer adds a damper, uses nested springs or picks a stiffer, lighter spring.

Model answer.

  1. A riveted joint holds plates together with rivets that are closed hot or cold in drilled holes. It is designed on one pitch length p, the repeating strip of plate that contains one rivet in each row. In a lap joint the plates overlap and each rivet is in single shear; in a butt joint with two cover plates each rivet is in double shear.
  2. There are four ways a joint can fail, each with its strength over one pitch (t the plate thickness, d the hole diameter, n the rivets per pitch):
    ModeWhat happensStrength per pitch
    Tearing of the plateThe plate breaks across the line of holesPt = (p − d)tσt
    Shearing of the rivetsThe rivets shear at the faying surfacePs = n(π/4)d²τ (× 2 in double shear)
    CrushingRivet or plate yields where they bearPc = n·d·t·σc
    Tearing at the marginThe plate splits in front of the rivetAvoided by a margin of 1.5d
  3. The margin is not calculated in the usual design: it is made large enough by rule, at least 1.5d from the hole centre to the plate edge, so the first three modes decide. The strength of the solid plate over one pitch is S = p·t·σt, and the efficiency is η = least of (Pt, Ps, Pc)/S. It says how much of the plate's strength the holes and rivets leave.
  4. Rivet size. Unwin's formula for plates over 8 mm gives a first size:
    d = 6√t = 6√12 = 20.8 mm, so a 21 mm hole is consistent with the rule.
  5. Strengths for p = 50 mm, one rivet per pitch:
    Pt = (50 − 21) × 12 × 80 = 27 840 N
Ps = (π/4) × 21² × 60 = 20 782 N
Pc = 21 × 12 × 120 = 30 240 N
S = 50 × 12 × 80 = 48 000 N
η = 20 782/48 000 = 0.433 = 43.3 %
  6. The joint fails by shearing the rivets. Tearing would need 27 840 N and crushing 30 240 N, so the plate between the holes is stronger than it needs to be: the pitch is too large for this rivet.
  7. A better pitch makes tearing and shearing equal, so neither mode wastes material:
    (p − 21) × 12 × 80 = 20 782
p = 21 + 20 782/960 = 42.6 mm; take p = 43 mm
Pt = (43 − 21) × 12 × 80 = 21 120 N
S = 43 × 12 × 80 = 41 280 N
η = 20 782/41 280 = 50.3 %
  8. The efficiency rises from 43.3 % to 50.3 % with no change of rivet: the joint still fails at 20 782 N per rivet, but that is now compared with a narrower strip of plate. The pitch must also stay above about 2d (42 mm) so the rivet can be closed and the plate between holes is not too narrow. A single-riveted lap joint rarely does better than about 50 to 60 %; double or triple rows, or butt joints whose rivets are in double shear, are used where higher efficiency is needed, and a diamond (lozenge) pattern on a tie bar loses only one hole at the first section.
  9. Closing check: least strength 20 782 N against crushing 30 240 N and tearing 21 120 N at p = 43 mm, efficiency 50.3 %, and the 1.5d margin of 31.5 mm rules out margin tearing.

   = (50 000 × 23)(0.10 − 0.1107)/(1 + 0.0111) = −12 190 N·mm = −12.2 N·m
  3. A negative lowering torque means the load would turn the screw and run down on its own if only the thread friction acted. In this jack the collar friction, Tc = 0.10 × 50 000 × 30 = 150 N·m, is large enough to hold it while all is still. But vibration lowers the effective friction at the collar (the surfaces slip in small steps), and then the 12.2 N·m tendency of the thread wins. This matches the complaint: the jack is self-locking only by its collar, and that cannot be relied on.
  4. Nut bearing pressure with 3 threads (thread depth p/2 = 4 mm):
    pb = W/(πdm·(p/2)·z) = 50 000/(π × 46 × 4 × 3) = 28.8 MPa
  5. Permissible bearing pressures for a steel screw in a bronze nut at low rubbing speed are usually 15 to 18 MPa. At 28.8 MPa the oil film is squeezed out and the nut wears fast: the second complaint.
  6. Compare the two-start thread with a single-start thread of the same size (lead 8 mm):

  8. The two-start thread is more efficient, and its screw efficiency of 52 % is itself the warning sign: a screw that is more than 50 % efficient cannot be self-locking. The single-start thread needs twice as many turns, but each turn needs less torque, 330 N·m instead of 395 N·m, so with a 600 mm handle the effort drops from 658 N to 549 N.
  9. Nut length for the single-start thread:
    z = 50 000/(π × 46 × 4 × 15) = 5.8; take z = 6
pb = 50 000/(π × 46 × 4 × 6) = 14.4 MPa ≤ 15 MPa
Nut height = 6 × 8 = 48 mm (z = 5 would give 17.3 MPa, at the top of the range)
  10. Recommendations: (1) use a single-start thread, so the jack holds its load by the thread itself, with Tl = +51.1 N·m in hand; (2) lengthen the nut to 6 threads, 48 mm, which halves the bearing pressure to 14.4 MPa; (3) keep the collar plain rather than fitting a thrust bearing: in a lifting device holding the load matters more than a lower effort, and a ball thrust bearing would remove the collar's extra holding torque. If faster lifting is truly needed, a two-start thread could be kept only with a separate brake or ratchet that holds the load, which adds cost and a part that can fail.
  11. Judgement: the two-start design traded safety for speed, and in a lifting device that trade is wrong. The single-start thread with a 48 mm nut is slower and less efficient, 19.3 % against 32.2 %, but it cannot let the load down, and its nut will last.

Glossary

Active coils
The coils that deflect; end coils only seat the spring.
Coefficient of fluctuation of speed
Cs = (ω₁ − ω₂)/ω.
Crushing
Failure by compressive bearing on a projected area, such as a pin on its hole.
Double shear
Shear across two sections at once, as in a knuckle pin or a cotter.
Friction angle
φ = tan⁻¹μ; a screw self-locks when φ > α.
Lead
The axial advance of a screw in one turn: pitch times the number of starts.
Lead angle
The angle α of the unwrapped thread: tan α = l/(πdm).
Lozenge joint
A diamond rivet pattern on a tie bar that loses only one hole at the first section.
Margin
The distance from a rivet centre to the plate edge, usually 1.5d.
Master leaf
The longest leaf of a leaf spring, rolled into eyes at both ends.
Maximum fluctuation of energy
The largest change in the flywheel's stored energy in one cycle, ΔE.
Mean diameter
For a square thread dm = d − p/2; used in the lead angle and the torque.
Nipping
Pre-bending the full-length leaves so that all leaves reach equal stress under load.
Overhauling
A screw lowers its load by itself because φ < α.
Pitch (thread)
The axial distance between neighbouring threads.
Preload
The clamping tension put into a bolt when it is tightened.
Shape factor
For a rubber block, one loaded area divided by the free area that can bulge.
Shear lag
The concentration of shear at the ends of an adhesive overlap.
Shot peening
Blasting the wire with small shot to leave compressive stress at the surface and raise fatigue strength.
Solid length
The length of a spring with every coil touching.
Spring index
C = D/d, mean coil diameter over wire diameter.
Surge
Resonance of a spring's coils when the load frequency matches its natural frequency.
Throat
The weakest section of a fillet weld, 0.707 times the leg.
Transmissibility
The fraction of a vibrating force that a mount passes to the floor.
