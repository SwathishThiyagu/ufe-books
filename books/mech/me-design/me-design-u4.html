4.1 Threaded fasteners and bolted joints under eccentric loading

A bolted bracket rarely pulls at the centre of its bolt group. The load arrives off-centre, so each bolt feels a direct share plus a turning share. Move the load to the group centroid, split it into a force and a moment, calculate each bolt's share, then add the two as vectors. The worst bolt sizes the joint. Machine frames, motor mounts, pipe flanges and payment-terminal housings use this idea.

You need Unit 1's direct shear, tension, eccentric loading and principal stresses.

Thread terms and the stress area

A screw thread has three diameters. The major diameter d is the largest. The minor diameter d₁ is the smallest, at the thread root. The pitch diameter lies between them, where thread thickness equals gap. Pitch p is the axial distance between adjacent threads. Lead l is the axial advance in one turn. For a single-start thread l = p; for a double-start thread l = 2p.

The thread root reduces the tension area. Design uses the tensile stress area As for ISO metric threads:

As = (π/4)(d − 0.9382 p)²

As is tensile stress area in mm²; d is nominal major diameter in mm; p is pitch in mm.

Use standard table values of As. The minor-area circle πd₁²/4 is smaller and is not the standard design area for a bolt in tension.

Preload, torque and joint loading

A bolt is tightened before external load arrives. This clamp force is preload Fi. Tightening torque includes thread friction and friction under the nut or bolt head.

T = K d Fi

T is tightening torque in N·mm; K is torque coefficient, about 0.2 for dry steel; d is nominal diameter in mm; Fi is preload in N.

In a shear joint, preload clamps the plates. External shear is carried by friction until slip. After slip, bolts carry shear by bearing and dowel action. In a tension joint, external load adds to bolt tension; check using As. A joint with shear and tension is checked for each effect and their interaction.

Eccentric load in the plane

An eccentric shear load is handled in five steps, working in the plane of the joint.

  1. Find the centroid of the bolt group, not the centroid of the bracket.
  2. Replace load F by the same force at the centroid plus moment M = F e, where e is the perpendicular distance from the centroid to the load line.
  3. Give every bolt primary shear Fp = F/n, parallel to F. Here n is the number of bolts.
  4. Give bolt i secondary shear Fsi = M ri / Σri², perpendicular to the radius from the centroid. Here ri is the bolt distance from the centroid in mm, and Σri² is the sum of r² over all bolts in mm².
  5. Add primary and secondary shears as vectors at each bolt. The largest resultant magnitude is the design shear.

Fs = M r / Σr²

Fs is secondary shear on one bolt in N; M is moment about the centroid in N·mm; r is that bolt's distance from the centroid in mm; Σr² is the sum of squares of all bolt distances in mm².

The direction of Fs follows the moment. For a clockwise moment, secondary shear on a bolt at (x, y) has direction (y, −x). Resolve Fp and Fs into x and y components, add, then take the square root of the sum of squares. The worst bolt is often not the farthest bolt, because the angle between vectors matters. The same centroid-and-moment method applies to welded joints under axial and eccentric loads.

Eccentric tension out of plane

When the moment bends the joint about an axis in its plane, bolts on one side stretch more than those on the other. For a symmetric bolt group the neutral axis passes through the centroid. The total external tensile force on bolt i has a direct part and a moment part:

F<sub>b,i</sub> = F/n + M y<sub>i</sub> / Σy<sub>i</sub>²

Fb,i is total external tensile force in bolt i in N; F is eccentric tensile load in N; n is number of bolts; M = F e is bending moment in N·mm; yi is signed distance from the neutral axis in mm; Σyi² is the sum over all bolts in mm².

Add this total external tensile force to preload when checking the tension-side bolt. Use As, not the nominal shank area. If the bolt group is not symmetric, the neutral axis shifts; then y must be measured from the shifted axis, not from the centroid.

Worked example

Question. A bracket is held by four bolts at the corners of a 100 mm × 200 mm rectangle. A vertical load F = 12 kN acts at a horizontal distance e = 250 mm from the bolt-group centroid. Take the bolt coordinates as (±50, ±100) mm. Find the maximum shear force in a bolt. The load lies in the plane of the bracket.

  1. Number of bolts n = 4. Centroid at (0, 0). Load F = 12 000 N. Eccentricity e = 250 mm.
  2. Primary shear on every bolt: F_p = F/n = 12 000/4 = 3 000 N = 3.0 kN, downward.
  3. Moment about the centroid: M = F e = 12 000 × 250 = 3 000 000 N·mm = 3.0 kN·m, clockwise.
  4. Sum of squares of bolt distances: Σr² = 4(50² + 100²) = 4(2 500 + 10 000) = 50 000 mm².
  5. All four corner bolts have r = √(50² + 100²) = 111.8 mm and equal secondary shear magnitude. After vector addition, the largest resultant occurs at the two bolts at x = +50 for the assumed clockwise moment.
  6. Secondary shear at (50, 100): F_s = M r/Σr² = 3 000 000 × 111.8/50 000 = 6 708 N = 6.71 kN. Its direction is perpendicular to the radius, clockwise: components (6.0, −3.0) kN.
  7. Add primary shear (0, −3.0) kN: resultant at (50, 100) is (6.0, −6.0) kN. Magnitude = √(6.0² + 6.0²) = 8.49 kN. The bolt at (50, −100) gives the same magnitude.
  8. The maximum bolt shear is therefore 8.49 kN.

Answer. The maximum shear force in a bolt is 8.49 kN, at the two bolts at x = +50 mm on the loaded side of the group.

Exam traps

  • Adding primary and secondary shears as scalars. They are vectors at an angle, so resolve into components at the worst bolt.
  • Measuring r from the load point instead of the bolt-group centroid. The moment is about the centroid.
  • Using the nominal bolt diameter or shank area for tensile stress. Use the tensile stress area As.
  • Assuming the farthest bolt is always worst. The vector angle can make a nearer bolt carry more.
  • In eccentric tension, using y from the centroid when the bolt group is not symmetric. The neutral axis shifts.

Analyze Why can the worst bolt in an eccentric shear joint be nearer than the farthest bolt? 4 marks

The total bolt force is the vector sum of primary and secondary shears. Primary shear is the same on all bolts. Secondary shear grows with r, but its direction changes with position. At a nearer bolt the two vectors may be more closely aligned, giving a larger resultant than at the farthest bolt.

Understand What does the coefficient K ≈ 0.2 in T = K d Fi include? 3 marks

It lumps thread friction, friction under the nut or bolt head, and thread geometry. It is not a pure friction coefficient.

Analyze A preloaded bolted joint in shear slips. What changes in the load path? 4 marks

Before slip, shear is carried mainly by friction between clamped plates. After slip, plates move and bolts carry shear by bearing and dowel action. The bolts then see shear and bending, and the joint may lose clamp load.

Remember Write the torque-tension relation for tightening a bolt. 2 marks

T = K d Fi, where T is tightening torque, K is torque coefficient, d is nominal diameter and Fi is preload.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Industry checks bolted joints with detailed preload and slip calculations, often using VDI 2230-style methods. Finite element models are used for stiff brackets where the centroid method is too simple. Fatigue of bolts, relaxation of preload and vibration loosening are major research topics.

4.2 Welded joints under axial and eccentric loads

Welding joins metal parts by melting metal at the joint. A weld is sized by the area that carries load: the throat. Once throat area and the second moment of the weld lines about the group centroid are known, an eccentric load splits into a direct part and a moment part, as in a bolted joint. Brackets, frames, trusses and nozzles are sized this way.

You need Unit 1 for direct, bending and torsion stress, and chapter 4.1 for the direct-plus-moment split of an eccentric load.

Butt welds, fillet welds and the throat

Two shapes carry most loads. A butt weld joins plates edge to edge and fills the thickness; load crosses the weld, so throat equals plate thickness t. A fillet weld joins surfaces at a corner and has triangular section. Its weakest plane is the throat at 45°: t = 0.707h, where h is leg size, the dimension beside the weld symbol.

The 0.707 is geometry. An equal-leg fillet has legs h and h, so throat is h·cos 45° = 0.707h. Stress is uniform over throat. Use effective length L = actual length − 2h, allowing for start and stop craters.

τ = P / (0.707 h L)

τ is shear stress on the throat in N/mm² (MPa); P is the axial load in N; h is the leg size in mm; L is the effective length in mm. For a butt weld, replace 0.707h by the plate thickness t.

Drawing carries h and L through the symbol: triangle on reference line means fillet, size left, length right. Read symbol first. Leg size, not plate thickness, enters the formula.

Eccentric load in the plane of the welds

An eccentric load does not pass through the weld-group centroid. Replace it by the same P at centroid plus torque T = P·e, where e is perpendicular distance in mm from centroid to line of action.

Direct part: τ_d = P/A_w with A_w = t·ΣL. It has direction of P and same value everywhere. Torque part: τ_t = T·r/J_w; r is distance in mm from centroid to point; J_w is polar second moment of throat area about centroid. It acts perpendicular to r.

J_w for a thin shape by the weld-line method: weld as zero-thickness lines. A_w = t·A_line and J_w = t·J_line, where A_line = ΣL in mm, J_line = Σ(L³/12 + L·d²) in mm³, and d is perpendicular distance from the weld line to the parallel axis through the group centroid. A throat thickness t multiplies every area element, so it multiplies both quantities.

  1. Mark the weld group and find the centroid. For two equal parallel welds it lies midway between them.
  2. Compute A_line = ΣL in mm and J_line = Σ(L³/12 + L·d²) in mm³.
  3. Direct shear τ_d = P/(t·A_line), same everywhere, along P.
  4. Torsional shear τ_t = T·r/(t·J_line) at the trial point, perpendicular to r.
  5. Add the two as vectors at each trial point; keep the largest resultant.

τ_max = √(τ_d² + τ_t² + 2 τ_d τ_t cos θ)

τ_d is the direct shear in MPa; τ_t is the torsional shear at that point in MPa; θ is the angle between their directions at that point. Plain addition of the two numbers is wrong.

Out-of-plane load and the allowable stress

When the load is perpendicular to the plane of the weld group it produces bending about an axis through the centroid, not torsion. M = P·e bends the group; stress at distance y from the neutral axis is M·y/I_w, with I_w = t·I_line and I_line about that axis. Add direct stress P/A_w algebraically, since both act on the same throat plane in the same sense.

For a fillet weld both stresses are taken on the throat; they act in the same direction, so add them algebraically: τ_total = P/A_w + M·y/I_w. Vector addition is needed only for the in-plane case of Section 2. For a butt weld bending stress is normal to the throat, so it adds directly to the axial stress and the total is compared with the permissible tensile value.

Permissible stress comes from yield and factor of safety. Distortion-energy theory gives shear yield σ_y/√3 ≈ 0.577σ_y, so τ_perm = 0.577σ_y/FOS. Codes publish lower values for weld metal, electrodes and service conditions; use code value when a code applies. Weld fatigue is separate (Unit 2).

Worked example

Question. Two vertical parallel fillet welds, each with effective length L = 100 mm and leg h = 6 mm, are 100 mm apart centre to centre. A vertical load P = 10 kN acts in the plane of the welds at e = 150 mm from the centroid of the group. Find the maximum resultant shear stress on the throat.

  1. Throat thickness t = 0.707h = 0.707 × 6 = 4.24 mm.
  2. Throat area A_w = 2 × t × L = 2 × 4.24 × 100 = 848.4 mm².
  3. Direct shear τ_d = P/A_w = 10 000/848.4 = 11.8 MPa, vertical, same at every point.
  4. Torque T = P·e = 10 000 × 150 = 1.5 × 10⁶ N·mm = 1.5 kN·m.
  5. Weld-line polar moment J_line = 2(100³/12 + 100 × 50²) = 2(83 333 + 250 000) = 666 667 mm³. Then J_w = t·J_line = 4.24 × 666 667 = 2.83 × 10⁶ mm⁴.
  6. Trial points: the two ends of the weld nearer the line of action, r = √(50² + 50²) = 70.7 mm.
  7. Torsional shear τ_t = T·r/J_w = 1.5 × 10⁶ × 70.7 / 2.83 × 10⁶ = 37.5 MPa, perpendicular to r, that is at 45° to the vertical.
  8. At a near-weld end, vertical component = 11.8 + 37.5 × 0.707 = 38.3 MPa. Horizontal component = 37.5 × 0.707 = 26.5 MPa.
  9. τ_max = √(38.3² + 26.5²) = 46.6 MPa.

Answer. Maximum resultant shear stress is 46.6 MPa at the two ends of the weld nearer the line of action. The ends of the far weld give about 30.3 MPa. Compare with τ_perm = 0.577σ_y/FOS.

Exam traps

  • Using leg size h as the throat. For a fillet weld the throat is 0.707h, so the wrong choice gives τ = P/(hL) = 0.707 × true stress: stress is understated by about 29%; true stress is about 41% higher than the wrong answer.
  • Adding direct and torsional shear as plain numbers. They act at an angle and must be added as vectors.
  • Using J of a solid rectangle instead of the weld line, or using the line value without multiplying by the throat thickness.
  • Assuming the farthest point is always critical. At a nearer point the two shears can line up and give a larger resultant.
  • Mixing units: torque in kN·m with r in mm. Work throughout in N and mm.

Check yourself

Remember Why is the throat of an equal-leg fillet weld 0.707h? 2 marks

The throat is the shortest distance from the corner to the hypotenuse of the triangular section; for legs h and h it is h·cos 45° = 0.707h.

Remember What is the weld-line method? 2 marks

Treating the weld as zero-thickness lines to get A_line and J_line, then multiplying both by the throat thickness t to get the real throat area and polar moment.

Understand Why is the direct shear the same at every point of the weld group while the torsional shear is not? 3 marks

The direct load is shared by the whole throat area equally. The torque produces a shear proportional to r, so points farther from the centroid carry more.

Apply A single fillet weld of leg 8 mm and effective length 150 mm carries an axial load of 30 kN. Find the shear stress on the throat. 4 marks

t = 0.707 × 8 = 5.66 mm; A = 5.66 × 150 = 848.4 mm²; τ = 30 000/848.4 = 35.4 MPa.

Apply Two parallel fillet welds, each with effective length L = 80 mm and leg 5 mm, are 60 mm apart. A load of 6 kN acts in the plane, parallel to the welds, at 100 mm from the centroid. Find the maximum throat shear. 5 marks

t = 3.54 mm; A = 565.6 mm²; τ_d = 10.6 MPa; J_line = 2(80³/12 + 80 × 30²) = 229 333 mm³; J_w = 810 700 mm⁴; r = √(40² + 30²) = 50 mm; τ_t = 6 × 10⁵ × 50/810 700 = 37.0 MPa; components give τ_max = √(32.8² + 29.6²) = 44.2 MPa.

Analyze For a single long weld, why does torsional shear fall faster than direct shear as L increases? 3 marks

Area grows as L, so direct shear falls as 1/L. For one long weld J_line ≈ L³/12 grows as L³ and r also grows with L, so torsional shear falls as about 1/L². For two well-separated welds J_line grows mostly as L·d², so both fall at similar rates.

Analyze An out-of-plane load acts on a bracket with two fillet welds. Why can the bending stress and the direct stress be added algebraically here, while an in-plane load needs a vector sum? 4 marks

Out of plane, both stresses act on the same throat plane in the same sense, so they add as numbers. In plane, the direct shear and the torsional shear point in different directions and must be combined as vectors.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real welds carry residual stress, undercut and slag inclusions that act as stress raisers, and codes such as AWS D1.1 or ISO 2553 fix both symbol grammar and allowable values. Finite element models resolve the throat stress gradient that the weld-line method averages away. Welding robots and laser-hybrid processes change the heat-affected zone, so weldability of the base metal matters as much as the arithmetic. Useful search terms: weld throat effective length, weld group polar moment, AWS D1.1 allowable shear, heat-affected zone softening.

4.3 Knuckle and cotter joints

Knuckle and cotter joints connect two rods that pull apart. A knuckle joint uses a pin through an eye and a fork; the pin works in double shear and bearing. A cotter joint uses a tapered rectangular cotter through a socket and spigot; the cotter works in double shear and bearing. Each part is sized by its own failure mode, so rod, pin or cotter, eye, fork, socket and collar can have different critical sections. They appear in tie rods, turnbuckles, railway couplers and link mechanisms.

You need: tensile stress σt = P/A, shear stress τ = P/A, bearing stress σc = P/(projected area), and factor of safety from Unit 1.

Knuckle joint: four parts and one load path

A knuckle joint joins two rods on one axis and allows small angular movement. Rod 1 ends in an eye: a ring with a hole. Rod 2 ends in a fork: a U-shaped end with two eyes, called tines. The eye sits between the tines. A pin passes through all three holes. A head on one end and a split pin or collar on the other keeps the pin in place. Some syllabi call this pin a hinge pin.

When the rods pull with force P, the eye pulls the pin at its middle. The two fork tines pull the pin at two outer regions. The pin therefore has two shear planes, one between the eye and each tine. That is double shear. The pin also presses on the eye and tines over small contact areas; that is bearing. The eye is in tension across its net section beside the pin hole. The fork tines are in tension and bearing.

Tie rods of pandal frames, turnbuckles and link mechanisms use this joint.

Sizing the knuckle joint by failure mode

Size the rod first, then the pin, then check bearing and bending. Use P in newtons, diameters in millimetres, and stresses in N/mm², which is the same as MPa.

σt = 4P/(πd²) ≤ σt,allow

P is the tensile load in N; d is the rod diameter in mm; σt is the rod tensile stress in N/mm².

τ = P/(2 × πd<sub>p</sub>²/4) = 2P/(πd<sub>p</sub>²) ≤ τ<sub>allow</sub>

dp is the pin diameter in mm; τ is the pin shear stress in N/mm²; the 2 is for two shear planes.

σ<sub>t</sub> = P/[t<sub>e</sub>(D<sub>e</sub> − d<sub>p</sub>)] ≤ σ<sub>t,allow</sub>

te is eye thickness in mm; De is eye outside diameter in mm; dp is pin diameter in mm.

Bearing (crushing) stress on the eye is σc = P/(dpte). Bearing on each fork tine is σc = P/(2dptf), where tf is one tine thickness in mm. Bending stress in the pin is σb = M/Z. Treat the pin as a beam with P at the centre and P/2 at each tine. The lever arm is half the eye thickness plus half one tine thickness, so M = (P/2)(te/2 + tf/2) = P(te + tf)/4, with te and tf in mm. Z = πdp³/32, where dp is pin diameter in mm.

Standard proportions are a starting point only. The stress checks decide the final sizes. If a syllabus gives proportions, use them to propose De, te, tf and then verify shear, bearing and bending.

Cotter joint: socket, spigot, cotter

A cotter joint also joins two rods in tension. Rod 1 ends in a socket: a hollow end with a slot. Rod 2 ends in a spigot: a smaller cylinder that fits into the socket. A flat rectangular cotter passes through slots in both parts. A collar on the spigot bears against the socket and stops the spigot from pulling out. The cotter is slightly tapered so it can be driven tight and later removed.

The load P pulls the socket left and the spigot right. The cotter bears on the socket slot and on the spigot slot. The cotter has two shear planes, one on each side of the spigot. The rod is in tension; the spigot is in tension across the cotter hole; the socket wall is in tension beside the slot; the collar is in bearing against the socket.

  • Cotter double shear: τ = P/(2bt) ≤ τallow, where b is cotter width in mm and t is cotter thickness in mm.
  • Cotter bearing on spigot: σc = P/(dst) ≤ σc,allow, where ds is spigot diameter in mm.
  • Rod tension: σt = 4P/(πd²) ≤ σt,allow, where d is rod diameter in mm.
  • Socket tearing: σt = P/Anet ≤ σt,allow, where Anet = (π/4)(D² − ds²) − (D − ds)t, with D the socket outside diameter in mm, ds the spigot diameter in mm and t the cotter thickness in mm.
  • Collar crushing: σc = P/[(π/4)(Dc² − ds²)] ≤ σc,allow, where Dc is collar outside diameter in mm.

Choose b and t from shear and bearing, then check the spigot, socket and collar. A cotter that passes shear can still crush the spigot, so both checks are needed.

Worked example

Question. A knuckle joint carries a rod tension P = 50 kN. The allowable tensile stress is 60 MPa and the allowable shear stress is 40 MPa. Find the rod diameter and the pin diameter for double shear. Round each up to the next even millimetre.

  1. P = 50 kN = 50 000 N; 60 MPa = 60 N/mm²; 40 MPa = 40 N/mm².
  2. Rod tension: σt = 4P/(πd²). Rearrange d = √(4P/(πσt)).
  3. d = √(4 × 50 000/(π × 60)) = √(200 000/188.50) = √1061.0 = 32.6 mm. The next even size is 34 mm.
  4. Pin double shear: shear area = 2 × πdp²/4 = πdp²/2. τ = P/(πdp²/2) = 2P/(πdp²).
  5. dp = √(2P/(πτ)) = √(2 × 50 000/(π × 40)) = √(100 000/125.66) = √795.8 = 28.2 mm. The next even size is 30 mm.
  6. Check bearing on the eye and fork after these sizes are fixed.

Answer. Rod diameter = 34 mm; pin diameter = 30 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using single shear for the knuckle pin. The pin has two shear planes, so the shear area is πdp²/2, not πdp²/4.
  • Taking the rod diameter as the pin diameter. The rod is sized by tension; the pin is sized by double shear, so the two sizes differ.
  • Skipping the bearing check after choosing a pin. A pin that passes in shear can still crush the eye or fork.
  • Using the cotter width b in the shear formula when the thickness t is meant, or forgetting the factor 2. The cotter has two shear planes, τ = P/(2bt).

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Production knuckle joints are sized with standard tables, then checked by finite element contact analysis because the pin-eye contact is not uniform. Fretting and corrosion at the pin hole often decide the life of railway couplers and suspension links. Cotter joints survive in older tie rods and turnbuckles because they are easy to assemble without threads. Search for knuckle joint fillet stress concentration, cotter joint fretting fatigue, and pin bearing contact pressure.

4.4 Design of power screws

A power screw turns rotary motion into straight-line motion by pushing a nut along a threaded shaft. The thread is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder, so the torque you apply must beat thread friction and lift the load. Screws raise car jacks, move machine slides, clamp work in a vice, and drive slow linear actuators. Some syllabi call this a translational screw.

You need: friction, torsion and combined stress (Unit 1, Foundations: Design Process and Stresses), and the thread terms from chapter 4.1.

The thread as an inclined plane

Unwrap one turn of the thread from the mean cylinder. The turn becomes a right triangle. The base is the circumference π dm; the height is the lead l. The angle between the slope and the base is the lead angle α.

dm is the mean diameter, the average of the major and minor diameters. l is the lead, the axial distance the nut moves in one turn. p is the pitch, the distance between adjacent threads. For a single-start thread l = p. For a double-start thread l = 2p, and so on. The mean diameter is not the nominal outside diameter.

α = tan⁻¹( l / (π dm) )

α is the lead (helix) angle; l is the lead; dm is the mean diameter. Use the same units for l and dm.

Square, trapezoidal and Acme threads are common. A square thread has flat flanks and gives the simplest friction formula. A trapezoidal thread has angled flanks, usually 30° included. The Acme thread is the inch version with 29°. Angled flanks carry side loads better and are easier to cut.

Torque, efficiency and self-locking

For a square thread, the torque to raise the load is:

T<sub>r</sub> = (W d<sub>m</sub> / 2) × (μ + tan α) / (1 − μ tan α)

Tr is raising torque in N·m; W is axial load in N; dm is mean diameter in m; μ is thread friction coefficient; α is lead angle.

To lower the load, the torque is:

T<sub>l</sub> = (W d<sub>m</sub> / 2) × (μ − tan α) / (1 + μ tan α)

Tl is lowering torque in N·m; the other symbols are as above. If Tl > 0, a torque must still be applied to lower the load: the screw is self-locking. If Tl < 0 (that is, μ < tan α), the load runs the screw down by itself and the screw is overhauling; a brake or extra friction is needed to hold the load. Tl = 0 at μ = tan α is the boundary.

That condition is self-locking. For a square thread it is μ ≥ tan α. A large friction coefficient or a small lead angle both help.

Efficiency is useful work out divided by work in: η = W l / (2π Tr). With thread friction only, it becomes:

η = tan α (1 − μ tan α) / (tan α + μ)

η is efficiency (no unit); W l is useful work per turn; 2π Tr is input work per turn. At small α the factor (1 − μ tan α) is near 1, but keep it in exams.

Collar friction adds Tc = μc W rc, where μc is collar friction coefficient and rc is mean collar radius. Add Tc to Tr; efficiency falls because the same load needs more input work.

Stresses, bearing and buckling

The screw body carries axial compression W and torsion T. At core diameter dc, direct stress is σ = W/Ac, with Ac = π dc²/4. Torsional shear is τ = 16T/(π dc³). For a ductile screw use σeq = √(σ² + 3τ²) and compare with yield strength divided by factor of safety.

The nut threads share the load over z engaged threads. Bearing pressure is p = W/(π dm h z), where h is thread depth. Keep p below the allowable for the nut material.

A long screw in compression can buckle. Check slenderness and use Euler or Johnson formula. Short screws usually fail by thread shear or bearing before buckling.

Worked example

Question. A square-thread power screw has mean diameter dm = 24 mm, lead l = 6 mm, and raises a load W = 20 kN. The thread friction coefficient is μ = 0.15. Collar friction is neglected. Find the lead angle, the raising torque, the efficiency, and state whether the screw self-locks.

  1. Convert units: dm = 0.024 m, l = 0.006 m, W = 20 000 N.
  2. tan α = l/(π dm) = 0.006/(π × 0.024) = 0.07958. α = tan⁻¹(0.07958) = 4.55°.
  3. Tr = (20 000 × 0.012) × (0.15 + 0.07958)/(1 − 0.15 × 0.07958). The first factor is 240 N·m. The bracket is 0.22958/0.98806 = 0.23235. So Tr = 240 × 0.23235 = 55.8 N·m.
  4. η = 0.07958 × (1 − 0.15 × 0.07958)/(0.07958 + 0.15) = 0.07958 × 0.98806/0.22958 = 0.3425 = 34.2%.
  5. μ = 0.15 and tan α = 0.07958, so μ > tan α. The screw is self-locking.

Answer. Lead angle α = 4.55°, raising torque Tr = 55.8 N·m, efficiency η = 0.342 (34.2%), and the screw is self-locking.

Exam traps

  • Using nominal diameter d instead of mean diameter dm in tan α and torque. The mean is dm = (d + dc)/2, not the outside diameter.
  • Using pitch p in place of lead l. For a single-start thread they are equal; for a double-start thread l = 2p, so α and torque change.
  • Dropping the (1 − μ tan α) factor in efficiency when the lead angle is not small. At α = 4.55° it changes the third digit; in exams it can change the mark.
  • Forgetting collar friction. A collar adds Tc = μc W rc to the raising torque, so more input torque is needed and efficiency falls. Collar friction also resists the load running down, so a screw that overhauls with thread friction alone may become self-locking once the collar is included: the load runs down only if (W dm/2)(tan α − μ)/(1 + μ tan α) > μc W rc.
  • Reading self-locking as 'small lead' only. For a square thread the test is μ ≥ tan α. A large μ or a small α both help.

4.5 Helical compression springs: stresses, deflection and surge

A helical compression spring is a wire coiled into a helix. An axial load W makes the wire twist, so the spring stores energy like a torsion bar. The wire diameter d, mean coil diameter D and spring index C = D/d set the stress and stiffness. Curvature raises the stress on the inner side, so a Wahl factor corrects it. Deflection follows from strain energy. A long spring can buckle. A fast-changing load can make the coils surge at resonance. Springs appear in vehicle suspension, engine valves, clutches and punches.

You need torsion of a circular wire and strain energy from Unit 1, Foundations: Design Process and Stresses, plus the fatigue idea from Unit 2, Failure Theories and Fatigue Design; fatigue design of springs itself is in 4.6.

Wire in torsion, corrected by the Wahl factor

A helical compression spring is a wire coiled into a helix. An axial load W makes each coil twist; the wire carries torsion T = W D / 2. The wire diameter is d. The mean coil diameter D is measured from wire centre to wire centre. The spring index is C = D/d. The active coils Na are the coils that deflect; end coils only seat the spring. Pitch is the axial distance between centres of adjacent active coils. Solid length is the length when all coils touch. Free length is the unloaded length.

Small C means a tightly curved spring: high stress and difficult winding. Large C means a floppy spring that may buckle. Many designs use C = 4 to 12.

τ0 = 8WD/(πd³)

τ0 is the uncorrected shear stress in Pa; W is axial load in N; D is mean coil diameter in m; d is wire diameter in m.

The inner side of a coil is more stressed than the outer side. The Wahl factor Kw corrects for curvature and direct shear.

Kw = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C

Kw is the Wahl factor; C = D/d is the spring index.

τ = Kw × 8WD/(πd³)

τ is the maximum shear stress in Pa; other terms are as above.

Deflection, rate, energy and buckling

The wire length is about π D Na. Torsion energy in that length gives the axial deflection. The result is:

δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴)

δ is axial deflection in m; G is shear modulus in Pa; Na is active coils; W, D, d as above.

k = W/δ = Gd⁴/(8D³Na)

k is spring rate in N/m.

Energy stored is U = ½ W δ = ½ k δ². U is in joules when W is in N and δ in m. This energy is why a spring can absorb shock. The rate is linear only while the coils do not touch. At solid length the rate becomes very high.

Solid length Ls = Nt d, where Nt is total coils including inactive end coils. Free length Lf = Ls + δmax + clearance, where clearance is the gap left between coils at maximum load. A common value is about 15% of δmax, so Lf = Ls + 1.15 δmax. A spring that is too long for its diameter can buckle sideways. If Lf/D is large, guide the spring on a rod or inside a sleeve. Design so the spring does not reach solid height in service; otherwise stress rises sharply.

Surge: resonance of the coils

A sudden load sends a compression wave along the coils. If the exciting frequency matches the spring's natural frequency, the coils surge. Surge is resonance of the spring coils. Stress and noise rise, and a spring can fail even when static stress is safe.

f<sub>n</sub> = (1/2)(d/(πD²N<sub>a</sub>))√(G/(2ρ))

fn is natural frequency in Hz for both ends fixed; ρ is wire density in kg/m³; other terms as above. One end free halves this value.

Keep the operating frequency fop and its harmonics away from fn. A common rule is fn ≥ 15 fop. Changing Na, d or D moves fn. Avoid cam lobes or valves that strike at fn. Fatigue under repeated load is separate; see 4.6.

Surge is worst when one end is fixed and the other is struck, as in a valve train. A damper or friction between coils reduces the amplitude. Two concentric springs can also change the natural frequency; their load sharing is in 4.6.

Worked example

Question. A helical compression spring has mean coil diameter D = 50 mm, wire diameter d = 6 mm, Na = 10 active coils, G = 80 GPa, and carries W = 500 N. Find the Wahl factor, maximum shear stress, deflection and spring rate.

  1. C = D/d = 50 mm / 6 mm = 8.33.
  2. Kw = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C = (33.33 − 1)/(33.33 − 4) + 0.615/8.33 = 32.33/29.33 + 0.0738 = 1.102 + 0.0738 = 1.176.
  3. τ = Kw × 8WD/(πd³). Use metres: W = 500 N, D = 0.050 m, d = 0.006 m. 8WD = 200 N·m. πd³ = π(0.006)³ = 6.786×10⁻⁷ m³. τ = 1.176 × 200 / 6.786×10⁻⁷ = 346.6×10⁶ Pa = 347 MPa.
  4. δ = 8WD³Na/(Gd⁴). D³ = 1.25×10⁻⁴ m³; d⁴ = 1.296×10⁻⁹ m⁴; G = 80×10⁹ Pa. Numerator = 8×500×1.25×10⁻⁴×10 = 5.000. Denominator = 80×10⁹×1.296×10⁻⁹ = 103.68. δ = 0.04822 m = 48.2 mm.
  5. k = W/δ = 500/0.04822 = 10370 N/m = 10.4 N/mm. Check formula k = Gd⁴/(8D³Na) gives the same.

Answer. Kw = 1.176; τmax = 347 MPa; δ = 48.2 mm; k = 10.4 N/mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the uncorrected stress 8WD/(πd³) and omitting Kw; at small C the error is large.
  • Using outside diameter Do instead of mean coil diameter D; C and δ both become wrong.
  • Using total coils Nt instead of active coils Na in the deflection and rate formulas; end coils do not deflect.
  • Mixing units: D and d in mm with G in GPa; convert to metres or use N/mm² consistently.
  • Treating surge as a static problem: if fop equals fn or a harmonic, stress rises even when static τ is safe.

4.6 Concentric springs and springs under fatigue loading

Two springs can sit one inside the other and share load. This concentric spring set packs high rate into small space and keeps working if one spring breaks. Railway buffers, clutch release mechanisms and valve trains use them. Fatigue matters: a spring in service is loaded millions of times, so wire must survive fluctuating shear stress, not just peak load. This chapter shows how load divides between nested springs, which clearances and solid heights to check, and how mean and alternating shear compare with a torsional endurance limit.

You need: Chapter 4.5 for spring rate, Wahl factor and solid length, and Unit 2 for the S-N curve and Goodman line.

Nested springs share deflection, not load

Two helical compression springs of same free length can be nested, one inside other. They act in parallel: under total load W both compress by same δ.

P₁ = k₁δ, P₂ = k₂δ, W = P₁ + P₂ = (k₁ + k₂)δ

k₁ and k₂ are rates of outer and inner spring (N/mm); P₁ and P₂ are loads they carry (N); δ is common deflection (mm); W is total load (N).

k_total = k₁ + k₂, P₁/P₂ = k₁/k₂

k_total is rate of whole set (N/mm). Load splits in same ratio as rates, so stiffer spring carries bigger share.

  • High rate in small space: one 80 N/mm spring may not fit; 50 and 30 N/mm do.
  • Fail-safe action: if outer cracks, inner keeps carrying load at reduced rate.
  • Two-stage rate: if inner reaches solid height partway, further load goes to outer alone and rate drops to k₁.
  • Wound in opposite hands, outer right and inner left, so a broken coil cannot screw into the other and jam the set.

Clearances, solid height and clash

Inner spring must fit inside outer with radial gap about 1 mm or more each side. Touching coils rub, wear and lose coating.

Solid length L_s = N_t d, where N_t is total coils and d is wire diameter (mm). At solid height coils touch, deflection stops and rate jumps. Check each spring separately at maximum load.

  1. δ_max = W_max/(k₁ + k₂).
  2. P₁ = k₁δ_max, P₂ = k₂δ_max.
  3. Solid load P_s = k(L_f − L_s), with k its own rate and L_f free length (mm).
  4. Require P₁ < P_s1 and P₂ < P_s2 with margin. Whichever goes solid first stops deflecting; the other takes further load.
  5. Pass guide rod through inner spring to stop sideways buckling; rod must clear inner bore.

Fatigue: mean and alternating shear

A spring in clutch or valve train sees load swinging between P_min and P_max, cycle after cycle. Fatigue cracks start inside coil, where shear stress is highest.

P_m = (P_max + P_min)/2,   P_a = (P_max − P_min)/2

P_m is mean load and P_a alternating load, both in N; P_max and P_min are extreme loads of cycle, in N.

τ_m = K_w·8P_m·D/(πd³),   τ_a = K_w·8P_a·D/(πd³)

τ_m and τ_a are mean and alternating shear stress (MPa = N/mm²); K_w is Wahl factor (dimensionless); D is mean coil diameter and d wire diameter, both in mm.

Wahl factor multiplies both parts. Curvature and direct shear act at every instant, at mean load as well as peak, so K_w cannot apply to one only.

1/n = τ_a/S_se + τ_m/S_su

n is fatigue factor of safety; S_se is torsional endurance limit (MPa); S_su is torsional ultimate strength (MPa). Replace S_su by torsional yield strength S_sy for Soderberg line, which is more conservative.

  • S_su ≈ 0.67 S_ut and S_sy ≈ 0.45 S_ut, where S_ut is tensile ultimate strength of wire.
  • S_se ≈ 0.22 S_ut unpeened, ≈ 0.35 S_ut shot peened.
  • Shot peening leaves surface in compression, so surface crack must first overcome it and S_se rises.
  • Presetting (scragging) presses new spring to solid once. Residual stress lets it carry higher stress without permanent set.

These fractions are starting values for steel spring wire. Use endurance data of wire actually bought.

Worked example

Question. Two concentric helical compression springs have the same free length. The outer spring has rate k₁ = 50 N/mm and the inner spring k₂ = 30 N/mm. The set carries a total load W = 12 kN. (a) Find the common deflection. (b) Find the load in each spring. (c) The inner spring has a solid load of 5000 N. Does it clash at this deflection?

  1. Nested springs deflect together, so their rates add: k_total = k₁ + k₂ = 50 + 30 = 80 N/mm.
  2. Convert the load: W = 12 kN = 12 000 N.
  3. Common deflection: δ = W/k_total = 12 000/80 = 150 mm.
  4. Outer spring load: P₁ = k₁δ = 50 × 150 = 7500 N.
  5. Inner spring load: P₂ = k₂δ = 30 × 150 = 4500 N.
  6. Check: P₁ + P₂ = 7500 + 4500 = 12 000 N, which matches W.
  7. Clash check on the inner spring: P₂ = 4500 N against a solid load of 5000 N. Margin = 5000 − 4500 = 500 N, so the inner spring still has travel and does not go solid.

Answer. δ = 150 mm, the outer spring carries 7500 N and the inner 4500 N; the inner spring stays 500 N below its solid load.

Question. A helical compression spring is made of unpeened music wire with wire diameter d = 10 mm and mean coil diameter D = 80 mm. The tensile ultimate strength of the wire is S_ut = 1400 MPa. The working load fluctuates between P_min = 500 N and P_max = 1500 N. Find the Wahl factor, the mean and alternating shear stress, and the Goodman factor of safety.

  4. Wire section factor: πd³ = π × 1000 = 3141.6 mm³.
  5. Mean shear stress: τ_m = K_w × 8P_mD/(πd³) = 1.184 × (8 × 1000 × 80)/3141.6 = 1.184 × 203.7 = 241.2 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Halving total load between concentric springs. They share deflection, not load: P₁/P₂ = k₁/k₂, so stiffer spring carries more.
  • Adding deflections of nested springs. Nested springs share δ, so rates add. Deflections add only in series.
  • Applying Wahl factor to τ_m only or τ_a only. K_w multiplies both.
  • Comparing shear stress with tensile strength. Use S_se for τ_a and S_su (or S_sy for Soderberg) for τ_m.
  • Skipping clash check on inner spring. Smaller spring can go solid while outer still travels, changing rate.

4.7 Leaf (laminated) springs

A leaf spring is a stack of curved steel strips clamped at the centre. The strips are called leaves. The longest leaf, the master leaf, has eyes at both ends. Shorter graduated leaves sit above or below it. The stack bends as one beam. A laminated leaf spring is used on trucks, buses, trailers and railway wagons because it both supports load and damps vibration through inter-leaf friction. Its shape gives near-constant strength, so it saves weight compared with a solid beam of the same span.

You need bending of beams and strain energy from Unit 1, and the spring energy comparison in chapter 4.5.

Form and the constant-strength idea

A leaf spring is a stack of flat spring-steel strips of different lengths. The longest strip is the master leaf. It has an eye at each end for the shackle or bracket pin. The shorter strips are graduated leaves. The master leaf forms the top (or bottom) face of the stack and the graduated leaves lie on one side of it, usually below. The stack is symmetric about the centre bolt along its length, with equal leaf lengths on each side of the centre. A central clamp or centre bolt holds the stack. Rebound clips near the ends stop the leaves separating when the spring rebounds.

Semi-elliptic springs have two eyes and look like half an ellipse. Quarter-elliptic springs are clamped at one end and have one eye. Full-elliptic springs are two semi-elliptic springs back to back. Some syllabi call the semi-elliptic form a carriage spring.

In a cantilever of constant width and depth, bending moment is largest at the fixed end and zero at the free end. A constant-depth beam therefore has extra material near the tip. A leaf spring splits the beam into strips. Long strips reach the centre; short strips stop earlier. The number of strips carrying moment falls towards the ends, roughly with the bending moment. Stress becomes more uniform and weight is saved. The clamp transfers load between leaves by friction and bearing.

Stress and deflection

Take a semi-elliptic spring with eye-to-eye span 2L. L is half the span. Let the central load be 2W. Each eye then carries W. The spring acts as two cantilevers of length L back to back. The maximum bending moment at the centre is M = W L. For n leaves of width b and thickness t, the total section modulus is Z = n b t²/6.

σ = 6WL/(nbt²)

σ is maximum bending stress in Pa; W is the load at one eye in N; L is half the eye-to-eye span in m; n is the total number of leaves; b is leaf width in m; t is leaf thickness in m. The central load is 2W.

δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³)

δ is central deflection in m; E is Young's modulus in Pa; W, L, n, b and t are as above.

The factor 6 comes from the uniform-strength assumption. The number of leaves, and so the second moment of area, grows from zero at the eye to nbt³/12 at the centre, roughly in step with the bending moment. Integrating the curvature over that varying second moment gives δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³). A stack of constant second moment nbt³/12 would give only 4WL³/(Enbt³). It ignores inter-leaf friction and nip. Use total leaves n. Do not use only the graduated leaves. For a quarter-elliptic spring, replace L by the cantilever length and W by the end load.

Nipping, clips and checks

Shorter leaves are made with slightly more curvature than the master leaf. When the stack is clamped, they are flattened and push on the master leaf. This is nipping. It leaves residual stress in the assembled spring. The residual stress opposes the working stress, so the peak stress in the master leaf under load is lower. The stress range for a given load cycle is unchanged; the mean stress shifts. Nipping also keeps leaves in contact.

Rebound clips hold the leaves together when the spring rebounds and tries to straighten. The central clamp transmits shear between leaves and locates the spring on the axle.

  1. Choose material and allowable stress.
  2. Choose b and t from standard strip sizes.
  3. Choose n so σ is within the allowable.
  4. Compute δ and compare with the required ride height.
  5. Adjust n or t if needed.
  6. Check the eye, pin and clamp.

The static load-deflection rate is k = 2W/δ = E n b t³/(3L³). Fatigue is checked separately with mean and alternating stress (Unit 2).

Worked example

Question. A semi-elliptic leaf spring has span 1000 mm between eye centres, central load 5 kN, n = 8 leaves, b = 75 mm, t = 8 mm, E = 200 GPa. Find the maximum bending stress and the central deflection.

  1. Span = 1000 mm = 1 m, so half-span L = 0.5 m.
  2. Central load = 5 kN = 5000 N. End load W = 5000/2 = 2500 N.
  3. b = 75 mm = 0.075 m; t = 8 mm = 0.008 m; E = 200 GPa = 200×10⁹ Pa; n = 8.
  4. σ = 6WL/(nbt²) = 6×2500×0.5/(8×0.075×0.008²) = 195.3×10⁶ Pa ≈ 195 MPa.
  5. δ = 6WL³/(Enbt³) = 6×2500×0.5³/(200×10⁹×8×0.075×0.008³) = 0.0305 m = 30.5 mm.

Answer. Maximum bending stress is about 195 MPa; central deflection is 30.5 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the full eye-to-eye span as L. The formula uses half the span, and W is the load at one eye. A central load of 5 kN means W = 2.5 kN.
  • Using the central load 2W in place of W. Every formula above is written for the end load at one eye.
  • Using only graduated leaves in n. n is the total number of leaves, including the master leaf.
  • Mixing millimetres and metres. Put b and t in metres before using E in Pa; otherwise stress and deflection are wrong by powers of 1000.
  • Forgetting that nipping is a pre-stress, not an extra external load. It changes residual stress, not the basic stress and deflection equations.

The longest leaf. It carries an eye at each end and is the top or bottom leaf of the stack.

Bending moment falls towards the ends. Shorter leaves stop where moment is small, so material is removed from low-stress regions and stress is more uniform.

The number of leaves carrying moment decreases towards the ends roughly as the bending moment decreases. Stress stays nearly uniform and excess material is avoided.

W = 10/2 = 5 kN.

4.8 Flywheels: solid and rimmed

A flywheel is a rotating store of kinetic energy. An engine or press delivers torque in pulses; the flywheel gives energy back between pulses and keeps speed nearly steady. A solid disk uses its whole mass; a rimmed wheel puts most mass in a thin rim at large radius, so it stores more energy for the same mass. Flywheels smooth speed in punch presses, IC engines, steam engines and rolling mills.

You need Unit 1 for centrifugal force and thin-ring stress, and chapter 4.7 for another energy-storing spring.

Speed fluctuation fixes inertia

Let ω be the mean angular speed in rad/s. The wheel runs between ω₁ and ω₂. The coefficient of fluctuation of speed is Cs = (ω₁ − ω₂)/ω. It is dimensionless. A small Cs means tight speed control.

Cs = (ω₁ − ω₂)/ω

ω₁ is maximum angular speed in rad/s; ω₂ is minimum angular speed in rad/s; ω = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 is mean angular speed in rad/s.

ΔE = I ω² Cs

ΔE is energy fluctuation per cycle in J; I is mass moment of inertia in kg·m²; ω is mean angular speed in rad/s; Cs is the speed-fluctuation coefficient.

This follows from ΔE = ½I(ω₁² − ω₂²) = Iω(ω₁ − ω₂) = Iω²Cs. To size a wheel: find ω; choose Cs for the duty; compute I; choose geometry; compute mass; check stress. Presses use a large ΔE for one stroke, so I is large.

Solid disk: I = ½ m r². Rimmed thin rim: I ≈ m r².

m is mass in kg; r is outer radius for a solid disk and mean rim radius for a rimmed wheel, in m. Hub and arm inertia are usually neglected.

  • A solid disk is compact but gives less I for the same mass.
  • A rimmed wheel gives high I per kg because the rim sits at large r. Some syllabi call it a spoked flywheel.
  • Do not use ½mr² for a rimmed wheel unless the wheel really is a solid disk.

Rim and arm stresses

At rim speed v = ωr, every rim element has outward centrifugal force. For a free thin ring this gives hoop stress σ = ρv². It does not depend on rim thickness. In engines the rim also sees torque pulses; in presses it releases a large energy burst.

σ = ρ v², with v = ω r

σ is hoop stress in Pa; ρ is density in kg/m³; v is rim speed in m/s; r is mean rim radius in m; ω is angular speed in rad/s.

Cast iron has ρ ≈ 7200 kg/m³. A rim at 40 m/s reaches σ ≈ 11.5 MPa; at 80 m/s it reaches σ ≈ 46 MPa. Stress rises with speed squared, so the safe speed limit is set by the rim material, not by torque alone.

Arms carry torque T from hub to rim. With n arms, the tangential force per arm at the rim is F = T/(n r). Treat an arm as a cantilever of length r. Its bending moment at the hub is M = F r = T/n, and bending stress is σb = M c / Iarm. Arms also feel centrifugal tension; check σt = Fc/A.

M = T/n,  σ<sub>b</sub> = M c / I<sub>arm</sub>,  F<sub>c</sub> = m<sub>arm</sub> ω² r<sub>g</sub>

T is torque in N·m; n is number of arms; c is distance from arm neutral axis to outer fibre in m; Iarm is second moment of the arm section in m⁴; marm is mass of one arm in kg; rg is its centroid radius in m; A is arm cross-section area in m².

Worked example

Question. A rimmed flywheel absorbs ΔE = 15 kJ per cycle at mean speed N = 600 rpm with Cs = 0.05. The rim mean radius is r = 0.6 m and cast-iron density is ρ = 7200 kg/m³. Find the inertia, rim mass, rim speed and hoop stress.

  1. ω = 2πN/60 = 2π × 600/60 = 62.83 rad/s.
  2. I = ΔE/(ω²Cs) = 15 000/(62.83² × 0.05) = 76.0 kg·m².
  3. m = I/r² = 76.0/0.6² = 211.1 kg.
  4. v = ωr = 62.83 × 0.6 = 37.7 m/s.
  5. σ = ρv² = 7200 × 37.7² = 10.2 × 10⁶ Pa = 10.2 MPa.

Answer. I = 76.0 kg·m², m = 211.1 kg, v = 37.7 m/s and σ = 10.2 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using diameter in place of radius in m = I/r² and v = ωr. Use the mean radius.
  • Using the full speed change ω₁ − ω₂ as Cs. Cs is already divided by mean speed ω.
  • Using I = ½mr² for a rimmed flywheel. That is the solid-disk value; use I ≈ mr² for a thin rim.
  • Leaving ΔE in kJ or N in rpm. Convert to J and rad/s before substitution.
  • Treating σ = ρv² as a complete arm-and-rim design. It is the free thin-ring hoop stress; arms need their own bending and centrifugal checks.

Check yourself

Remember Define the coefficient of fluctuation of speed. 2 marks

Cs = (ω₁ − ω₂)/ω, where ω₁ and ω₂ are maximum and minimum angular speeds and ω is the mean angular speed.

Understand Why does a rimmed wheel store more energy per kg than a solid disk of the same outer radius? 3 marks

Most mass sits at the mean rim radius, so I ≈ mr². A solid disk has I = ½mr², with much mass near the axis; for the same mass and radius the rim gives about twice the inertia.

Apply A flywheel absorbs ΔE = 8 kJ per cycle at ω = 50 rad/s with Cs = 0.04. Find I. 3 marks

I = ΔE/(ω²Cs) = 8000/(50² × 0.04) = 80 kg·m².

Apply A cast-iron rim has mean radius 0.5 m and runs at 900 rpm. Take ρ = 7200 kg/m³. Find rim speed and hoop stress. 4 marks

ω = 2π × 900/60 = 94.25 rad/s; v = 94.25 × 0.5 = 47.1 m/s; σ = 7200 × 47.1² = 16.0 MPa.

Analyze Why can a rimmed flywheel fail at high speed even when it transmits little torque? 4 marks

The rim's own centrifugal force causes hoop stress σ = ρv². This grows with speed squared and does not depend on the transmitted torque; at high v the rim can burst even at light load.

Analyze A flywheel has n arms carrying torque T at rim radius r. What happens to arm bending stress if n is doubled, all else unchanged? 3 marks

Force per arm is F = T/(nr), so M = Fr = T/n. Doubling n halves M and halves bending stress, if the arm section is unchanged.

Remember State two duties of a flywheel in an engine or press. 2 marks

Store kinetic energy between power pulses and reduce speed fluctuation during each cycle.

Not for the exam. Production flywheels are checked by finite element analysis for rim, arm and hub stress, and burst containment is tested for safety. Composite rims can reach higher tip speed than cast iron because ρ is lower while strength is high. Search terms: flywheel energy storage, burst speed, composite flywheel, rim hoop stress.

Takeaways

  1. An eccentric load is handled by splitting it into a direct share for every fastener plus a moment share proportional to distance from the centroid, then adding the two as vectors at the worst point.
  2. A weld carries load only through its throat; bending and torsion of the weld group are found from its second moment about the group centroid.
  3. Knuckle and cotter joints are designed part by part, each part checked for its own failure mode: tension, shear, bearing or crushing.
  4. A power screw is an inclined plane on a cylinder: torque must beat thread friction, and the same lead angle and friction decide both efficiency and self-locking.
  5. Spring wire carries torsion, not tension; the curvature correction gives the real maximum shear at the inside of the coil.
  6. Surge is a resonance problem, cured by changing the spring's natural frequency, not by adding material.
  7. Concentric springs share load in the ratio of their stiffnesses, because they must deflect equally.
  8. Fatigue design of a spring replaces one static stress by a mean and an alternating shear compared against an endurance limit.
  9. A laminated leaf spring is a constant-strength beam, so stress and deflection both fall as the number of leaves rises.
  10. A flywheel stores energy as rotating inertia: the allowable speed fluctuation fixes the inertia, and rim centrifugal stress fixes the top speed.

Glossary

Active coils
The coils of a spring that deflect; end coils that only seat do not count.
Bearing pressure
Compressive stress on a projected area, such as p = P/(d·t) on a pin or p = W/(π d_m·n_t·h) on a thread.
Butt weld
A weld across the full thickness of the joined plates; the throat equals the plate thickness.
Coefficient of steadiness
The reciprocal of the coefficient of speed fluctuation, m = 1/C_s.
Collar friction
Torque lost at the thrust collar, T_c = μ_c·W·(r_o + r_i)/2, where r_o and r_i are the collar radii in mm.
Cushing
Local compressive failure where two parts press against each other, checked on the projected contact area.
Endurance limit
The stress amplitude a material survives for a very large number of cycles without fatigue failure.
Fillet weld
A triangular weld joining two surfaces at right angles; load is carried on its throat, not on the leg.
Free length
The axial length of a compression spring with no load applied.
Friction angle
φ = tan⁻¹μ, the angle whose tangent is the coefficient of friction.
Hoop stress in a rim
Tensile stress around the rim caused by its own centrifugal force at speed.
Lead
The axial distance the nut travels in one full turn of the screw, equal to pitch times the number of starts.
Master leaf
The longest, full-length leaf of a laminated spring, whose ends carry the eyes.
Mean and alternating stress
σ_m = (σ_max + σ_min)/2 and σ_a = (σ_max − σ_min)/2, the steady and cyclic parts of a stress.
Mean thread diameter
The average of the major and minor diameters, d_m = d − p/2 for a square thread; used in all torque formulas.
Nip
The small initial gap given between leaves of a leaf spring so that they share load evenly after assembly.
Pitch
The axial distance between corresponding points on adjacent threads, in mm.
Solid length
Length of a compression spring with all coils touching, L_s = n_t·d, where n_t is the total number of coils.
Spring rate
Stiffness k = W/δ, the load per unit deflection in N/mm.
Tearing
Tensile failure of a member across its net section, as at the eye of a knuckle joint.
