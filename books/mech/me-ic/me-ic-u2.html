2.1 Carburettors: mixture requirements and circuits

A petrol engine needs a different air-fuel mixture at idle, cruise, full load and acceleration. A carburettor uses the suction of the incoming air to draw petrol into it and to change the ratio as conditions change. A mixture set for one condition is wrong for the others: too rich wastes fuel, too lean misfires or knocks. Carburettors ran millions of Indian scooters and cars before fuel injection, and the same mixture-demand idea now drives electronic injection.

You need: the four-stroke SI cycle and the meaning of air-fuel mixture from the engine fundamentals topic.

Mixture demand

Air-fuel ratio (A/F) is the mass of air divided by the mass of fuel entering the engine. Petrol's chemically correct (stoichiometric) ratio is about 14.7:1 by mass. The engine does not always want that ratio. At idle it needs about 12:1, because low air speed, poor vaporisation and leftover exhaust gas dilute the charge. At cruise it runs about 15–16:1 for economy. At full load it needs about 12.5:1 for maximum power. During acceleration it needs a short extra squirt of fuel. Rich means A/F below 14.7; lean means above 14.7.

A/F = ṁair ÷ ṁfuel

ṁair and ṁfuel are the air and fuel mass flows in kg/s; A/F is by mass and has no unit.

Air filter, throttle and the simple carburettor

Air first passes the air filter, which stops dust that would grind the cylinder walls, rings and valve seats. Cars and two-wheelers use a dry pleated paper element; tractors in dusty fields use an oil-bath filter, where the air turns sharply over an oil pool that holds the dust. A choked filter adds pressure drop, so less air flows and the mixture turns rich and smoky.

The throttle is a butterfly valve after the venturi, worked by the accelerator. It sets how much mixture enters, and so the power: an SI engine is governed by quantity, not by mixture strength as a diesel is.

A simple carburettor has a float chamber, a fuel nozzle with the main jet at its foot, a venturi, the throttle and a choke. The float and needle valve hold the petrol at a fixed level, and the nozzle tip sits a few millimetres above it so that petrol cannot spill when the engine stops. The air speeds up in the venturi and its pressure falls; this depression lifts petrol out of the nozzle into the air stream. A simple carburettor's mixture grows richer as air flow rises, because the air's density at the throat falls while petrol's does not, so practical carburettors add a compensating jet or an air bleed.

Venturi flow: air and fuel equations

Treat the air as incompressible across the small depression Δp. Bernoulli's equation from the inlet to the throat gives the air flow. The fuel sees the same Δp, less the head needed to lift it by z to the nozzle tip.

ṁa = Cda At √(2 ρa Δp) ṁf = Cdf Af √(2 ρf (Δp − ρf g z))
At is the throat area and Af the jet area in m²; Cda, Cdf are discharge coefficients; ρa, ρf are the air and petrol densities in kg/m³; Δp is the depression in Pa; z is the tip height above the float level in m.

If z is neglected, A/F = (CdaAt ÷ CdfAf) √(ρa ÷ ρf): two fixed areas give one mixture strength. So the main jet cannot give the rich idle, lean cruise and rich full load. At idle the venturi depression is too small, so an idle circuit is needed; the main jet is sized lean for cruise, so a power valve adds fuel at full load; on a sudden opening the fuel lags the air, so an accelerator pump adds fuel.

Worked example

Question. An engine takes 0.045 kg/s of air. The carburettor is set for 15:1 by mass. Petrol density is 750 kg/m³. Find the fuel mass flow and the fuel volume flow.

  1. ṁfuel = ṁair ÷ (A/F) = 0.045 ÷ 15 = 0.003 kg/s.
  2. Volume flow = ṁfuel ÷ density = 0.003 ÷ 750 = 4×10⁻⁶ m³/s = 4 cm³/s (1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³).

Answer. Fuel mass flow is 0.003 kg/s (3 g/s) and fuel volume flow is 4 cm³/s.

Worked example 2

Question. A simple carburettor must supply 7.2 kg/h of petrol at A/F = 15. Air is at 1.013 bar and 27 °C; the venturi depression is 0.065 bar. The nozzle tip is 5 mm above the float level. Take Cda = 0.85, Cdf = 0.70, petrol density 750 kg/m³ and R = 287 J/kg K, and treat the air as incompressible. Find the throat and fuel jet diameters.

  1. ṁf = 7.2 ÷ 3600 = 0.002 kg/s; ṁa = 15 × 0.002 = 0.030 kg/s.
  2. ρa = p ÷ RT = 101300 ÷ (287 × 300) = 1.177 kg/m³.
  3. Throat speed √(2Δp ÷ ρa) = √(2 × 6500 ÷ 1.177) = 105.1 m/s; At = 0.030 ÷ (0.85 × 1.177 × 105.1) = 2.854×10⁻⁴ m² = 285.4 mm², so dt = √(4 × 285.4 ÷ π) = 19.06 mm.
  4. Lift head ρfgz = 750 × 9.81 × 0.005 = 36.8 Pa, so the fuel sees 6500 − 36.8 = 6463 Pa; fuel speed √(2 × 6463 ÷ 750) = 4.152 m/s.
  5. Af = 0.002 ÷ (0.70 × 750 × 4.152) = 9.176×10⁻⁷ m² = 0.918 mm², so df = √(4 × 0.918 ÷ π) = 1.081 mm.

Exam traps

  • The main jet alone does not set the mixture at all loads; the idle circuit, power valve, accelerator pump and choke add fuel where it falls short.
  • A/F is by mass, not volume. Rich means low A/F (more fuel); lean means high A/F. Students often reverse these.
  • Venturi depression is the pressure drop at the throat, not the manifold vacuum. At idle the closed throttle makes high manifold vacuum, but the venturi signal is weak.
  • In the jet formula the fuel sees Δp minus ρfgz, because it must be lifted to the nozzle tip; and 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa must be converted first.
  • The choke is for cold start only. Left on, it washes oil off the cylinder walls and causes rich misfire.

2.2 Fuel injection systems: requirements and types

A fuel injection system sends the right amount of fuel into each cylinder at the right moment, in a spray fine enough to mix with air. It exists because a carburettor cannot hold a steady air-fuel ratio for every cylinder and load, and because diesel fuel must be injected at high pressure after the air is compressed. It is used on diesel engines of trucks, tractors and generators, and on modern petrol cars with electronic injection. The system must meter, pressurise, time, atomise and distribute the fuel.

You need: Carburettors for the SI mixture requirement, and the four-stroke cycle from the engine fundamentals topic.

What the system must do

A fuel injection system has five jobs: meter, atomise, distribute, time and pressurise the fuel. Metering sets the mass of fuel per cycle to match load and speed. Too little fuel gives a lean mixture, misfire and low power. Too much fuel gives black smoke, high fuel consumption and diluted lubricating oil.

Atomisation breaks the fuel into fine droplets. Small droplets expose more surface area to air, so evaporation and mixing are fast. A solid jet burns slowly and deposits carbon.

Distribution gives every cylinder the same fuel quantity and a spray aimed at the right place. Timing starts delivery at the correct crank angle: in a diesel engine near top dead centre (TDC) at the end of compression; in a petrol engine the start depends on port or direct injection. Pressure must push fuel into the cylinder against the compressed air. Diesel injection pressures are high because cylinder pressure at injection is high.

  • Accurate metering: correct mass per cycle and equal between cylinders.
  • Fine atomisation: small droplets for fast mixing.
  • Even distribution: same quantity and pattern to each cylinder.
  • Correct timing: start of delivery at the right crank angle.
  • Sufficient pressure: fuel enters the cylinder against compressed air.

Types: air, solid, direct and control

Air injection used a blast of compressed air to carry fuel into the cylinder. Early diesel engines used it; the compressor was large and the system slow, and it is obsolete. Solid injection, which some syllabi call airless injection, pressurises the fuel with a pump and forces it through a nozzle. Almost every modern engine uses it.

For diesels, direct injection (DI) sprays fuel straight into a bowl in the piston crown: common rail, unit injector, in-line pump and many distributor-pump engines. Indirect injection (IDI) sprays into a separate prechamber or swirl chamber in the head, joined to the cylinder by a throat; distributor and in-line pumps both feed IDI engines too. For petrol engines the usual words are port (manifold) injection, where fuel meets the air before the inlet valve, and direct injection into the cylinder; some books group port and throttle-body injection under indirect.

By control, a system is mechanical (cam, plunger and spring meter and time the fuel) or electronic (sensors, an electronic control unit, ECU, and solenoid or piezo injectors). By delivery, continuous injection sprays all the time, as in the old mechanical petrol system whose flow was set by an air-flow plate; intermittent injection sprays in timed pulses, as every diesel pump and every modern electronic injector does. The pumps are in Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector and the rail in Common rail direct injection.

Injection timing and injection lag

Injection timing is the crank angle at which the pump starts to deliver fuel. Some texts define it at the nozzle opening. The start of delivery is earlier than the start of spray because of injection lag.

Injection lag is the delay from the start of pump delivery to the nozzle opening. Fuel is slightly compressible, the pressure wave travels along the pipe at about the speed of sound in diesel (near 1400 m/s), and the pressure must build to the nozzle opening pressure. Lag is a time, but the engine turns during it, so it appears as a crank-angle delay of θ = 6 N t degrees, with N in rpm and t in seconds.

Injection duration is the crank angle from nozzle opening to nozzle closing. It grows with fuel quantity. A diesel engine needs injection advance as speed rises so that combustion still starts near TDC. Late injection raises exhaust temperature and smoke; too early injection raises peak pressure and diesel knock. The combustion side is in the topic of CI engine combustion and the delay period.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder, four-stroke diesel engine uses 6 kg/h of fuel at 1800 rpm. Find the fuel delivered per injection per cylinder in kg and mg.

  1. Fuel rate = 6 kg/h ÷ 60 = 0.1 kg/min.
  2. A four-stroke cylinder injects once every two revolutions: 1800 ÷ 2 = 900 injections per cylinder per minute; for 4 cylinders, 3600 per minute.
  3. Fuel per injection = 0.1 ÷ 3600 = 2.778×10⁻⁵ kg = 27.78 mg.

Answer. The fuel delivered per injection per cylinder is 2.78×10⁻⁵ kg, or 27.8 mg.

Worked example 2

Question. A pump starts delivery at 20° before TDC. The high-pressure pipe is 0.7 m long and the pressure wave travels at 1400 m/s; neglect the time to lift the needle. The nozzle then stays open for 1.5 ms. Find the lag in crank degrees, the nozzle opening and closing angles at 2000 rpm, and the lag at 3000 rpm.

  1. Wave time t = 0.7 ÷ 1400 = 0.0005 s = 0.5 ms.
  2. At 2000 rpm the crank turns 6 × 2000 = 12 000 °/s, so lag = 12 000 × 0.0005 = 6.0°.
  3. Nozzle opens at 20° − 6° = 14° before TDC.
  4. Duration = 12 000 × 0.0015 = 18°, so the nozzle closes at 14° − 18° = −4°, that is 4° after TDC.
  5. At 3000 rpm: lag = 6 × 3000 × 0.0005 = 9.0°, so the nozzle opens at 11° before TDC unless the pump timing is advanced.

Answer. Lag 6° at 2000 rpm; the nozzle opens 14° before TDC and closes 4° after TDC. At 3000 rpm the same 0.5 ms costs 9°, which is why injection is advanced with speed.

Exam traps

  • Direct injection is not the same as direct control. Direct means the injector sprays into the cylinder; it does not mean a lever or human directly controls the fuel.
  • For a four-stroke engine, each cylinder injects once every two revolutions. Injections per cylinder per minute are rpm ÷ 2, not rpm.
  • Injection timing is the start of fuel delivery, not the spark timing; spark timing belongs to the ignition system (see Ignition timing and spark advance).
  • Solid injection and airless injection are the same thing. Air injection is the obsolete air-blast method, not a modern air-assisted petrol injector.
  • Injection lag is the delay before the nozzle opens; injection duration is the crank angle from opening to closing. Convert either with θ = 6Nt, not 360Nt.

2.3 Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector

Mechanical diesel injection must create pressure high enough to atomise fuel and time delivery to a few crank degrees. Three mechanical layouts do this without electronics. The in-line jerk pump uses one plunger per cylinder. The distributor pump uses one plunger for all cylinders and sends fuel to each in turn. The unit injector puts the pump and nozzle together at the cylinder. Tractors, gensets, older trucks and large marine engines use them; the plunger-and-spill idea also sits inside modern high-pressure pumps.

You need: Fuel injection systems for the jobs of an injection system and for injection timing.

The in-line jerk pump

The in-line jerk pump, called a plunger pump in some syllabi, has one pumping element per cylinder driven by one camshaft. On a four-stroke engine the pump camshaft turns at half engine speed, so each plunger injects once per two crank revolutions.

Each element is a plunger inside a barrel. Fuel fills the space above the plunger through an inlet port while the plunger is down. A vertical groove and a helical edge, the helix, are cut on the plunger. The helix sets the end of delivery.

  1. The cam lifts the plunger.
  2. The plunger covers the inlet port; trapped fuel cannot return to the gallery.
  3. Pressure rises; first the light-spring delivery valve opens, then, once line pressure reaches the nozzle opening pressure (a few hundred bar), the nozzle needle lifts and injection begins.
  4. The helix uncovers the spill port; fuel spills back, pressure falls, and the delivery valve closes.
  5. The plunger finishes its stroke without delivering, then returns.

Effective stroke seff is the plunger travel from port closing to spill opening. It is shorter than the total cam lift. Volume per injection is the swept volume of that effective stroke:

V = (π d² ÷ 4) × seff

V is the fuel volume per injection (m³); d is the plunger diameter (m); seff is the effective stroke (m).

The control rack rotates the plunger to change fuel quantity. Rotation moves the helix relative to the spill port, so spill happens earlier or later; total stroke stays fixed. A delivery valve holds residual line pressure and stops backflow. Timing marks on the pump flange and flywheel set delivery start for cylinder one.

The distributor pump

The distributor pump, or rotary pump, uses one pumping element for all cylinders and a rotating distributor that sends each charge to the next cylinder in firing order. Two designs are common. In the axial-plunger type, a cam plate with one lobe per cylinder rides over fixed rollers, so the single plunger both rotates and reciprocates; a slot in the plunger lines up with each outlet in turn, and a control sleeve moved by the governor uncovers a spill port to end delivery. In the radial-plunger type, two opposed plungers in a rotor are pushed inward by an internal cam ring; quantity is set by throttling the fuel that fills them.

  • Advantages: compact and light, one pumping element so cylinders get equal fuel, self-lubricated by diesel; common on 3- to 6-cylinder car, jeep and tractor engines.
  • Limits: lower maximum pressure and fuel per stroke than an in-line pump; wear in the rotor, cam plate or ring upsets delivery; it must have clean, water-free fuel because diesel is its only lubricant.

The unit injector

The unit injector puts the pump and nozzle in one body at the cylinder head. The engine cam, through a rocker or a dedicated lobe, pushes the plunger directly. No long high-pressure pipe connects pump and nozzle.

The plunger pressurises fuel just above the nozzle needle. A helix or a spill valve ends injection; the rack or linkage sets fuel quantity. The nozzle opens at its set pressure and sprays into the chamber. Fuel lubricates the plunger and cools the body.

The short, stiff path gives fast pressure rise, precise timing and high pressure. Large marine and locomotive diesels use unit injectors. Limits: the camshaft and rocker carry the full injection load; the cylinder head is crowded; each unit needs setting; service costs more. Timing is set by the cam lobe, rocker clearance or shims, and marks on camshaft and flywheel.

Timing, comparison and limits

All three need timing. For static (spill) timing, the engine is turned by hand to the specified angle before top dead centre on the compression stroke of cylinder one, the delivery valve is removed, and the pump or cam is set where fuel stops spilling from that outlet, because the plunger has just closed the port. Dynamic timing is checked with the engine running, using a timing light or a strobe on the marks. A wrong mark shifts every cylinder together.

Worked example

Question. A jerk pump plunger has diameter 8 mm and effective stroke 3 mm. Find the fuel delivered per injection in m³ and in mm³. Take π = 3.1416.

  2. A = π d² ÷ 4 = 3.1416 × (0.008)² ÷ 4 = 5.027×10⁻⁵ m².
  3. V = A × seff = 5.027×10⁻⁵ × 0.003 = 1.508×10⁻⁷ m³ = 150.8 mm³ (1 m³ = 10⁹ mm³).

Answer. The fuel delivered per injection is 1.508×10⁻⁷ m³, or 150.8 mm³.

Worked example 2

Question. The pump of the first example feeds a six-cylinder four-stroke engine at 1500 rpm with diesel of density 830 kg/m³. Find the fuel per injection in mg and the engine's fuel consumption in kg/h.

  1. Mass per injection = 1.508×10⁻⁷ m³ × 830 kg/m³ = 1.252×10⁻⁴ kg = 125.2 mg.
  2. The pump camshaft turns at 1500 ÷ 2 = 750 rpm, so each element injects 750 times a minute; six elements give 4500 injections a minute.

Exam traps

  • Using total plunger stroke instead of effective stroke. The effective stroke starts only after the inlet and spill ports are covered by the plunger.
  • Thinking that rotating the plunger changes its stroke length. It changes the angular position of the helix, so the spill port opens earlier or later.
  • Confusing the delivery valve with the injector nozzle. The delivery valve is at the pump outlet and holds residual line pressure; the nozzle atomises fuel in the chamber.
  • Assuming a distributor pump feeds all cylinders at the same instant. The rotor lines up with one delivery union at a time in firing order.
  • Forgetting that a unit injector still needs timing. The cam lobe, rocker clearance or shims set its timing even though there is no high-pressure pipe.

2.4 Common rail direct injection (CRDI)

A common rail stores diesel at high pressure in one pipe and lets an electronic control unit fire each injector when it wants. The high-pressure pump now feeds a rail continuously, so pressure generation is separated from the injection event. Rail pressure and injection timing become independent settings. This gives fine sprays, quiet combustion and low emissions. It is used in cars, buses, trucks and tractors, and in petrol direct-injection engines with a similar rail.

You need: Fuel injection systems for what an injection system must do, and Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector for why mechanical pumps tie pressure to engine speed.

The layout: from tank to rail

Start at the tank. A lift pump, usually electric, pushes diesel at a few bar through a filter to the high-pressure pump. The filter matters: the pump and injectors have clearances of a few micrometres, and a particle that size can jam an injector. The high-pressure pump is driven by the engine. It raises fuel to the rail pressure and sends it into the rail. The rail is a thick pipe that acts as an accumulator, so all injectors see the same stored pressure.

A pressure sensor on the rail reports to the electronic control unit (ECU). A pressure control valve, on the pump or on the rail, opens to bleed fuel back to the tank and hold the set pressure. The ECU then commands each injector to open. The injector may use a solenoid or a piezo stack. Some syllabi call this common rail diesel injection.

  1. Tank stores diesel at low pressure.
  2. Lift pump sends fuel through the filter.
  3. High-pressure pump raises fuel to rail pressure.
  4. Rail stores the high-pressure fuel for all cylinders.
  5. Pressure sensor and control valve keep rail pressure at the ECU set point.
  6. ECU opens each injector at the calculated time.

Two settings: pressure and open time

Rail pressure is set by the pump output and the pressure control valve. Injection timing is set by when the ECU opens an injector. Engine speed does not force the injection start, as it does with a jerk pump. Rail pressure is typically 1000 to 2000 bar (100 to 200 MPa), and newer systems go above 2000 bar. The ECU reads speed, load, air mass, coolant temperature and rail pressure, then looks up the pressure and timing for that operating point.

For a fixed nozzle, the volume per injection rises with injector open time and with the square root of the pressure difference across the nozzle, rail pressure minus cylinder pressure:

Q = Cd A t √(2 (prail − pcyl) ÷ ρ)
Q is the fuel volume per injection (m³); CdA is the effective hole area (m²); t is the open time (s); prail and pcyl are in Pa; ρ is the diesel density (kg/m³). So quantity is not set by rail pressure alone: the ECU must know both pressure and open time. This is the central trap in exams and in calibration.

The ECU can split one injection into several pulses. A pilot injection is a small early pulse. It starts a mild burn, shortens the ignition delay of the main charge, and lowers the rate of pressure rise, so diesel knock and noise fall. The main injection delivers most of the fuel, so its combustion gives most of the work. A post injection comes late, after the main pulse, and is used mainly for exhaust after-treatment, such as heating the exhaust for a diesel particulate filter. Multiple injections per cycle are common.

What it buys, what it costs

  • High rail pressure gives small droplets and fast mixing, so combustion is cleaner.
  • Pilot injection lowers combustion noise and the peak pressure rise.
  • Split injection can lower both nitrogen oxides and soot. Retarded timing lowers nitrogen oxides but tends to increase soot, so the ECU trades one against the other.
  • Cold start and altitude operation can be recalibrated without changing hardware.
  • Limits: the high-pressure pump, sensor, control valve and ECU add cost and complexity.
  • The pump takes engine power, filtration must be fine, and injector deposits can disturb the spray.

The system is flexible but not free. A mechanic needs diagnostic tools, and a weak battery or a bad sensor can stop the engine. The gain is a diesel that is quieter, cleaner and easier to calibrate than a mechanical pump line nozzle system.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke diesel engine runs at 3000 rpm. Each injector delivers 30 mm³ per injection. Find the fuel flow in L/h.

  1. Injections per cylinder per minute = 3000 ÷ 2 = 1500; for 4 cylinders, 6000 per minute.
  2. Volume per minute = 30 mm³ × 6000 = 180 000 mm³/min = 0.18 L/min (1 L = 10⁶ mm³).
  3. Per hour: 0.18 × 60 = 10.8 L/h.

Answer. The fuel flow is 10.8 L/h.

Worked example 2

Question. At 1200 bar rail pressure and 60 bar cylinder pressure, an injector open for 1.0 ms delivers 30 mm³. The ECU raises the rail to 1800 bar for a finer spray. What open time gives the same 30 mm³? Treat CdA as constant and neglect the needle's opening and closing.

  1. Q = CdA t √(2Δp ÷ ρ), so for the same Q, t₂ = t₁ √(Δp₁ ÷ Δp₂).
  2. Δp₁ = 1200 − 60 = 1140 bar; Δp₂ = 1800 − 60 = 1740 bar.
  3. t₂ = 1.0 × √(1140 ÷ 1740) = 1.0 × √0.6552 = 1.0 × 0.8094 = 0.809 ms.

Answer. About 0.81 ms. Leaving the pulse at 1.0 ms would give 30 × √(1740 ÷ 1140) = 37.1 mm³, a 24 % overdose, which is why the ECU always reads rail pressure.

Exam traps

  • Saying rail pressure alone sets injection quantity. Quantity depends on rail pressure and injector open time together (Q ∝ t√Δp).
  • Treating common rail as a petrol-only system. This chapter is diesel common rail; petrol direct injection uses a similar rail but different mixture needs.
  • Thinking pilot injection adds power. The pilot pulse mainly shortens the delay of the main charge and cuts noise; the main pulse's fuel gives the work.
  • Calling post injection a late power pulse only. Its main job is exhaust after-treatment, such as diesel particulate filter regeneration.
  • Assuming higher rail pressure is always better. It improves spray but raises pump work, nitrogen oxides and wear, so the ECU balances it.

2.5 Electronic fuel injection: mono-point and multi-point

Electronic fuel injection (EFI) replaces the carburettor with a control loop. Sensors measure air flow, throttle angle, coolant temperature, exhaust oxygen and crank position. An electronic control unit (ECU) turns these signals into an injector pulse width. A fuel pump and pressure regulator keep fuel pressure fixed, so pulse width sets the fuel mass. EFI is used on almost every new petrol car, motorcycle and scooter in India because it holds the air-fuel ratio close to stoichiometric and cuts emissions.

You need: Fuel injection systems for injection requirements and types.

Sensors, ECU and injectors

The loop has five parts. Sensors turn physical quantities into electrical signals: a voltage, a change of resistance or a train of pulses. The ECU is a microprocessor with lookup tables and correction maps. Injectors are solenoid valves (some newer ones are piezo) that open when current flows. The fuel pump supplies more fuel than the engine needs. The pressure regulator returns the excess to the tank and holds delivery pressure constant, commonly about 3 bar for port injection. Because pressure is fixed, the ECU meters fuel only by changing injector open time, called pulse width.

  • Air flow sensor: hot-wire or hot-film type measures intake air mass; a manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor is used instead on speed-density systems.
  • Throttle position sensor (TPS): a potentiometer or Hall sensor reports idle, part load and wide-open throttle.
  • Coolant temperature sensor: a thermistor enriches the mixture when the engine is cold.
  • Crank position sensor: a magnetic or Hall pickup facing a toothed wheel on the crankshaft gives engine speed and piston position; a camshaft sensor tells the ECU which cylinder is on its compression stroke.
  • Oxygen sensor: a zirconia cell in the exhaust gives a voltage that switches either side of stoichiometric, about 14.7:1 by mass for petrol.

Open loop means the ECU uses tables only, with no oxygen feedback. It runs open loop during cold start, hard acceleration and wide-open throttle. Closed loop means the oxygen sensor trims the pulse width to hold the mixture near stoichiometric. The trim has a short-term correction and a long-term learned correction.

Mono-point: one injector at the throttle body

Mono-point EFI uses one injector mounted above the throttle plate in a throttle body. The injector sprays fuel into the incoming air, and the mixture travels through the intake manifold to all cylinders. Some syllabi call this single-point or throttle-body injection (TBI).

Fuel pressure is low, about 1 bar above manifold pressure, and the injector pulses in step with the crank signal, usually once for every cylinder firing, so each pulse serves whichever cylinder is drawing in. The ECU still uses the same sensors and oxygen feedback. Because fuel is delivered at one central point, cylinder-to-cylinder distribution depends on manifold geometry, so the mixture can be uneven. Wall wetting on a cold manifold also delays fuel response. Mono-point was a cheap retrofit for carburettors in the 1980s and 1990s. It is rare on new cars; multi-point and direct injection have replaced it.

Multi-point: one injector per port

Multi-point EFI (MPFI) has one injector per cylinder, mounted in the intake port just upstream of the inlet valve. Each injector sprays onto the back of the valve, so the fuel stays in the port and enters the cylinder as the valve opens. Port injection is another name. Fuel pressure is usually 3 to 4 bar.

Firing can be simultaneous, batch or sequential. In simultaneous firing all injectors open together once per revolution. In batch firing injectors are grouped, for example two groups that alternate. In sequential firing each injector opens just before its own cylinder's intake stroke. Sequential gives the best fuel control and cold-start behaviour, but it needs a camshaft sensor, because the crank sensor alone cannot tell the end of compression from the end of exhaust.

Compared with mono-point, MPFI gives better distribution, faster response and lower wall wetting. The intake manifold carries only air, so it can be tuned for air flow. The ECU can also cut fuel to one injector for a misfire or over-speed limit. MPFI is the common layout on Indian petrol cars and motorcycles. Petrol direct injection, with the injector inside the cylinder, is the next step; see Gasoline direct injection.

Working out the pulse width

The ECU first estimates the air mass that enters one cylinder in one cycle, from the air-flow meter or from manifold pressure, speed and air temperature. It divides by the target A/F to get the fuel mass. Because the pressure across the injector is fixed, the injector passes a fixed static flow (g/s) while open, so the open time is the fuel mass divided by that flow. The needle takes a fraction of a millisecond to open, so the ECU adds a dead time that it raises when the battery voltage is low. Corrections for a cold engine, acceleration and oxygen-sensor trim then multiply the base pulse.

tinj = mair ÷ (A/F × qs) + tdead
mair is the air per cylinder per cycle (g); qs is the static injector flow (g/s); tdead is the opening delay (s).

Worked example

Question. A port injector flows 240 cm³/min when fully open. The ECU holds it open for 5 ms. Find the fuel volume delivered in one injection in cm³ and mm³.

  1. Flow: 240 cm³/min ÷ 60 = 4 cm³/s; open time 5 ms = 0.005 s.
  2. Volume = 4 × 0.005 = 0.02 cm³ = 20 mm³ (1 cm³ = 1000 mm³).

Answer. Fuel delivered per injection is 0.02 cm³, which is 20 mm³.

Worked example 2

Question. A four-cylinder MPFI engine runs at 3000 rpm and draws 0.35 g of air into each cylinder per cycle. The target is A/F = 14.7. Each injector flows 2.5 g/s of petrol when open and has a dead time of 0.8 ms. Find the pulse width and the fraction of the cycle the injector is open (sequential firing).

  1. Fuel per cylinder per cycle = 0.35 ÷ 14.7 = 0.02381 g.
  2. Open time for that fuel = 0.02381 ÷ 2.5 = 0.009524 s = 9.52 ms.
  3. Pulse width = 9.52 + 0.8 = 10.32 ms.
  4. One four-stroke cycle is 2 revolutions: at 3000 rpm that is 2 × 60 ÷ 3000 = 0.040 s = 40 ms.
  5. Duty = 10.32 ÷ 40 = 0.258, about 26 % of the cycle.

Answer. Pulse width about 10.3 ms, open for about 26 % of each cycle. At 6000 rpm with the same air charge the cycle is 20 ms and the duty would be about 52 %.

Exam traps

  • Mono-point does not mean one injector per cylinder. It means one injector in total, at the throttle body; multi-point has one per cylinder at the ports.
  • Pulse width sets fuel mass only because pressure is held constant. If the pressure regulator fails, the same pulse width gives a different fuel mass.
  • Closed loop needs a hot oxygen sensor. A cold engine runs open loop, so the ECU ignores the oxygen signal during warm-up.
  • Sequential injection needs cylinder identification from a camshaft sensor; batch and simultaneous firing can run from the crank sensor alone.
  • Forgetting the dead time. The pulse is the fuel time plus the opening delay; and one four-stroke cycle is two revolutions, not one.

2.6 Gasoline direct injection (GDI)

Gasoline direct injection (GDI) sprays petrol straight into the combustion chamber instead of into the inlet port. The fuel arrives after the air is trapped, so the ECU decides not only how much fuel each cylinder gets but also where in the chamber it goes and at what crank angle. That lets the engine cool its charge, resist knock, raise its compression ratio and, at light load, burn a lean layered charge. Most new turbocharged petrol cars, including small Indian hatchbacks and SUVs, use it.

You need: Electronic fuel injection for sensors and pulse width, and Common rail direct injection for the rail idea.

Layout and pressures

A GDI fuel system has two stages. An electric pump in the tank sends petrol at about 3 to 6 bar to a high-pressure pump on the cylinder head. That pump is a single plunger driven by a lobe on the camshaft. A solenoid quantity control valve lets the unwanted part of each stroke spill back, so the pump delivers only what the rail needs. The rail feeds one injector per cylinder and carries a pressure sensor and a pressure-limiting valve. Rail pressure is roughly 50 to 200 bar in most systems and up to about 350 bar in newer ones, far below a diesel rail but fifty or more times port-injection pressure.

The injector sits in the head with its tip in the chamber, either at the side under the inlet ports or in the centre next to the spark plug. Most are solenoid multi-hole injectors; some use a piezo stack driving an outward-opening needle for a very repeatable spray. The pressure must be high for two reasons: the injector must spray against the rising cylinder pressure late in compression, and the fuel has only a few milliseconds to evaporate.

  • Tank pump: low pressure, about 3 to 6 bar.
  • Cam-driven high-pressure pump with quantity control valve.
  • Rail with pressure sensor and limiting valve.
  • Injector in the chamber, opened by the ECU; spark plug close by.

Homogeneous and stratified operation

In homogeneous mode the fuel is injected early, during the intake stroke. It has the whole intake and compression strokes to mix, so the charge is uniform at about the chemically correct ratio, as with port injection. This is the mode for medium and full load, and the three-way catalyst works normally.

In stratified mode the fuel is injected late in the compression stroke. A small, ignitable rich cloud forms around the spark plug while the rest of the cylinder holds air or very lean mixture. The overall A/F can be far leaner than 14.7, so the throttle can stay wide open and the pumping loss of a throttled engine falls. The cloud must reach the plug at the right moment, and three ways of steering it are named: wall-guided (a shaped piston bowl turns the spray towards the plug), air-guided (tumble or swirl carries it) and spray-guided (the injector sits next to the plug and the spray edge itself is ignited).

ModeInjectionMixtureUsed for
HomogeneousIntake strokeUniform, near 14.7:1Medium and full load
StratifiedLate compression strokeRich at the plug, lean overallLight load, low speed
Split (two pulses)Intake plus a small late pulseMostly uniform, richer at the plugCatalyst heating, knock control

Because lean exhaust cannot be cleaned of NOx by a three-way catalyst, stratified engines need a lean NOx trap, and many production GDI engines run homogeneous at all loads and keep direct injection for its cooling and knock benefits.

Benefits and problems

Petrol absorbs its latent heat as it evaporates. In a port-injected engine much of that heat comes from the hot port and valve; in GDI it comes from the trapped air, so the charge cools. A cooler charge is denser, so more air is trapped, and it is less prone to knock, so the compression ratio can be raised by about one ratio, for example from 10 to 11. With no fuel film on the port walls, the engine also responds faster to the throttle and starts with less excess fuel.

ΔT ≈ hfg ÷ ((A/F) × cp)

ΔT is the fall in charge temperature (K) if all the evaporation heat comes from the air; hfg is the latent heat of petrol (kJ/kg); cp is the specific heat of air, 1.005 kJ/kg K.

  • Soot and particulate number: fuel that hits the piston or liner, or has little time to mix, burns rich; many new GDI cars carry a gasoline particulate filter.
  • Intake valve deposits: no fuel washes the back of the inlet valves, so oil vapour from crankcase ventilation bakes on them.
  • Injector tip deposits change the spray; the high-pressure pump adds cost, noise and engine load.
  • Fuel wetting the liner can dilute the oil, and downsized turbo GDI engines can suffer low-speed pre-ignition.

Worked example

Question. A GDI engine injects during the intake stroke at A/F = 14.7. Take the latent heat of petrol as 305 kJ/kg and cp of air as 1.005 kJ/kg K.   1. Fuel per kg of air = 1 ÷ 14.7 = 0.0680 kg.
  2. Heat taken from 1 kg of air = 0.0680 × 305 = 20.75 kJ.
  3. ΔT = 20.75 ÷ 1.005 = 20.6 K.
  4. Charge temperature = 320 − 20.6 = 299.4 K.
  5. At the same pressure, density ∝ 1/T, so the air mass rises by 320 ÷ 299.4 = 1.069, about 6.9 %.

Answer. The charge cools by about 21 K and traps about 7 % more air. It is an upper limit: some of the heat still comes from the walls and some fuel evaporates after the inlet valve closes.

  • Stratified does not mean the whole charge is rich. The cloud at the plug is rich enough to ignite; the cylinder as a whole is lean.
  • Writing that GDI keeps the inlet valves clean. It is the opposite: port injection washes the valves, GDI does not, so valve deposits are a GDI problem.
  • The charge-cooling estimate uses A/F to get fuel per kg of air; dividing hfg by cp alone gives an impossible 300 K drop.
  • A three-way catalyst cannot remove NOx from lean stratified exhaust; that is why stratified engines need a lean NOx trap.

2.7 Injection nozzles, spray formation and spray penetration

The nozzle is the last part of the fuel system that touches the fuel. It turns high pressure into a spray of tiny droplets, gives the spray a direction and a shape, and sets how far the spray reaches into the cylinder before it mixes with air. Hole size, hole number, opening pressure and injection pressure decide atomisation, penetration and cone angle. Every diesel engine's smoke, starting and fuel economy depends on getting this small part right.

You need: rail and injection pressure from Common rail direct injection.

The injector, nozzle types, opening pressure and sac volume

The fuel injector (nozzle holder) is the body that carries the nozzle into the cylinder head. Fuel from the high-pressure pipe runs down a drilling to the nozzle. A spindle and spring press the needle onto its seat, and an adjusting screw or shims set the spring force. Fuel that leaks past the needle to lubricate it returns through a leak-off pipe.

The nozzle is a hardened needle on a conical seat with one or more holes below it. When fuel pressure on the needle's lifting area beats the spring, the needle lifts and fuel escapes. That pressure is the opening pressure; diesel nozzles commonly open between about 100 and 250 bar, direct injection at the higher end.

  • Pintle nozzle: a pin (pintle) through a single hole leaves an annular gap and gives a hollow-cone spray with short penetration; used in indirect-injection chambers.
  • Single-hole nozzle: one plain hole; simple and cheap, for some indirect-injection chambers.
  • Multi-hole (multi-orifice) nozzle: 3 to 10 holes of about 0.1 to 0.4 mm, the smallest on modern common-rail nozzles, each aimed at a part of the bowl; the standard for direct injection.
  • Throttle (delay) nozzle: a pintle shaped so the flow area stays small at low needle lift; less fuel enters during the delay period, so the pressure rise is softer.

Below the seat is a small cavity, the sac. Fuel trapped there when the needle closes dribbles out late and burns badly, adding unburnt hydrocarbons and smoke. A valve-covered-orifice (VCO) nozzle lets the needle cover the holes and removes the sac.

Nozzle tests: pop test, spray and leak

A nozzle is tested on a pop tester: a hand pump, a pressure gauge and a holder.

  1. Fill the tester with clean diesel and clamp the injector in the holder, spray pointing into a collector.
  2. Pump slowly and watch the gauge. The pressure at which the needle lifts is the opening pressure; compare it with the maker's value and correct it with the screw or shims.
  3. Watch the spray: a fine, even mist from every hole, the same on each; a solid stream, a drip or a blocked hole means a worn or dirty nozzle.
  4. Leak test: hold the pressure about 10 to 20 bar below the opening pressure; the gauge should stay nearly steady and the tip dry for the stated time.

A sharp chatter during the pop shows the needle is free and seating. Keep hands away: fuel at these pressures can pierce skin.

Spray formation, droplet size, cone angle and penetration

A jet leaving a small hole is unstable: air drag and turbulence tear it into ligaments, then drops. Droplet size is given as the Sauter mean diameter (SMD, D₃₂), the diameter of a drop with the same volume-to-surface ratio as the whole spray; diesel sprays are often 10 to 30 μm.

ṁ = Cd A √(2 ρ Δp)

ṁ is the fuel mass flow (kg/s); Cd is the discharge coefficient (about 0.7); A is the total hole area (m²); ρ is the fuel density (about 830 kg/m³); Δp is injection minus cylinder pressure (Pa).

Penetration S is the distance the spray tip has reached from the nozzle at time t after the start of injection. A widely used fit (Hiroyasu and Arai) has the tip moving at constant speed until the jet breaks up at tb, and then:

S = 2.95 (Δp ÷ ρa)0.25 √(d t)
S in m; ρa is the cylinder air density (kg/m³); d is the hole diameter (m); t in s. Penetration grows as the fourth root of pressure but as the square root of hole size, and falls in denser air.

  • Cone angle: the angle of one jet, often 10° to 25°; the angle between opposite jets of a multi-hole nozzle is much larger.
  • Penetration must match the bowl: too short leaves outer air unused, too long wets the wall and makes smoke and oil dilution.
  • Higher injection pressure: finer drops, faster mixing, a little longer penetration.
  • More, smaller holes at the same total area: better atomisation but weaker jets that penetrate less.
  • Denser air (turbocharging): more drag, shorter penetration, wider cone.

Worked example

Question. A nozzle has 5 holes of 0.25 mm diameter and Cd = 0.7. Injection pressure is 700 bar and cylinder pressure 60 bar; diesel density 830 kg/m³, air density in the cylinder 20 kg/m³. Find the total hole area, the fuel flow, the fuel per injection for 1.0 ms and the spray penetration 0.5 ms after the start.

  1. Area of one hole = π d² ÷ 4 = π × (0.25×10⁻³)² ÷ 4 = 4.909×10⁻⁸ m²; total A = 5 × 4.909×10⁻⁸ = 2.454×10⁻⁷ m² = 0.245 mm².
  2. Δp = 700 − 60 = 640 bar = 6.4×10⁷ Pa; √(2ρΔp) = √(2 × 830 × 6.4×10⁷) = 3.259×10⁵.
  3. ṁ = 0.7 × 2.454×10⁻⁷ × 3.259×10⁵ = 0.0560 kg/s; jet speed = ṁ ÷ (ρA) = 0.0560 ÷ (830 × 2.454×10⁻⁷) = 275 m/s.
  4. Fuel in 1.0 ms = 0.0560 × 0.001 = 5.60×10⁻⁵ kg = 56.0 mg (67.5 mm³).

2.8 Governors

A governor is a closed-loop speed controller. It measures engine speed, compares it with a set value, and moves the fuel rack to keep speed nearly constant as load changes. A petrol engine uses a throttle; a diesel has no throttle, so the only easy way to hold speed is to change fuel delivery. Governors appear on generator sets, tractor engines, pump sets and older truck diesels. This chapter covers mechanical centrifugal and pneumatic governors, droop, isochronous running and stability.

You need: the fuel rack from Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector.

Why a diesel engine needs a governor

Petrol engines use a throttle plate. The driver sets air flow, and the mixture drawn in sets power. A diesel has no throttle: at a given speed each cycle takes nearly the same air. Power is set only by the fuel injected per cycle.

Without a governor, a sudden load drop leaves surplus fuel in the cycle. The engine accelerates until the load matches the fuel or something fails. A governor is an automatic speed controller: it senses speed and moves the fuel rack.

The rack is the control rod of the injection pump (see Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector). Rack position sets delivered fuel per cycle at a given speed. The governor does not set a fuel quantity directly. It sets speed by changing fuel; the load then decides the quantity needed to hold that speed.

Signal path: spring tension sets target speed. The governor senses actual speed. The error moves the rack. The engine responds. Speed feeds back.

Vehicle diesels mostly use a two-speed (idle and maximum-speed) governor: it holds a steady idle and stops over-speeding, while the driver's pedal sets the fuel in between. Generator and pump sets use an all-speed or constant-speed governor that holds whatever speed is set.

Mechanical centrifugal governor

Two flyballs (flyweights) are carried on arms on a spindle geared to the engine, usually from the pump camshaft. They turn at a speed proportional to engine speed. Centrifugal force pushes them outward. Their sleeve moves against a spring. The sleeve position is linked to the fuel rack through a lever. Some syllabi call this the mechanical governor.

At steady speed, the outward force of the balls balances the spring force at one radius. That radius fixes the sleeve position and so the rack position. If load falls, speed rises, balls move out, and the sleeve moves the rack to cut fuel. If load rises, speed falls, the spring pushes the balls inward, and the rack increases fuel.

F = m ω² r

F is the centrifugal force on one ball (N); m is the ball mass (kg); ω is the spindle angular speed (rad/s); r is the radius of the ball's circular path about the spindle axis (m).

Because F depends on ω², doubling speed gives four times the force. A small speed error produces a useful rack movement.

Pneumatic, hydraulic and electronic governors

A pneumatic governor senses speed through air flow, not flyballs. A butterfly valve in the intake is set by the accelerator. Air passing the partly closed butterfly creates a pressure drop. The drop grows as engine speed rises.

One side of a diaphragm senses this lower pressure; a spring pushes the other side. The diaphragm is linked to the fuel rack. At the set speed, vacuum force balances spring force. If speed rises, vacuum rises, and the diaphragm moves the rack to reduce fuel. If speed falls, the spring moves the rack to increase fuel.

The butterfly position sets the target speed, and the governor holds it. Pneumatic governors are simple and cheap. They are sensitive to air-filter restriction and are used on small diesel engines.

Hydraulic governors use engine oil pressure and a pilot valve to move the rack with more force. Electronic governors use a speed sensor, an ECU and an actuator; they give easy isochronous control and diagnostics. The sensor and ECU side is the same as in electronic fuel injection.

Droop, isochronous operation and stability

Droop, also called speed regulation, is the fall in speed from no load to full load at a fixed setting:

δ = (Nnl − Nfl) ÷ Nfl
δ is the droop (× 100 for per cent); Nnl is the no-load speed and Nfl the full-load speed, in rpm. Some books divide by the no-load speed; say which you use.

For example, 1500 rpm no load and 1470 rpm full load gives δ = 30 / 1470 × 100 = 2.0%. Generator governors commonly run at 3% to 5% droop.

Droop lets two generators share load. Each set has a falling speed-load line, so when they are tied together at one speed each takes the load its line gives at that speed. If one isochronous set runs with a droop set, the isochronous set holds the frequency, the droop set sees an almost constant speed and carries an almost constant load, and every load change falls on the isochronous set.

Isochronous means zero droop: the governor holds the same speed from no load to full load. It suits a single generator or a single engine driving a pump. It needs a sensitive governor with damping.

Stability matters. Too much governor gain causes overshoot and hunting. Too little gain gives slow recovery. Linkage play, spring friction and a worn rack add dead band. A hydraulic relay or electronic control reduces hunting.

Worked example

Question. A centrifugal governor has a ball of mass 0.1 kg whose path radius is 50 mm. It turns at 1200 rpm. Find the angular speed and the force on one ball, and the force if the speed rises 5 % to 1260 rpm at the same radius.

  1. r = 50 mm = 0.050 m; ω = 2πN ÷ 60 = 2π × 1200 ÷ 60 = 125.7 rad/s.
  2. F = mω²r = 0.1 × (125.7)² × 0.050 = 0.1 × 15 791 × 0.050 = 79.0 N.
  3. At 1260 rpm: ω = 2π × 1260 ÷ 60 = 131.9 rad/s; F = 0.1 × 17 410 × 0.050 = 87.1 N.
  4. Ratio 87.1 ÷ 79.0 = 1.1025 = 1.05²: a 5 % speed rise gives a 10.25 % force rise.

Answer. ω = 125.7 rad/s and F = 79.0 N; at 1260 rpm F = 87.1 N. The extra 8.1 N compresses the spring and pulls the rack towards less fuel.

Worked example 2

Question. A generator set's governor gives 1530 rpm at no load and 1470 rpm at full load. Find the droop, and the speed at half load if the speed-load line is straight.

  1. δ = (1530 − 1470) ÷ 1470 = 60 ÷ 1470 = 0.0408, that is 4.08 %.

Exam traps

  • Saying the governor sets fuel quantity directly. It moves the rack; load and speed decide the matching quantity.
  • Using droop denominator as no-load speed. The usual formula divides by full-load (rated) speed.
  • Taking ω = 2πN with N in rpm. You must divide rpm by 60 first.
  • Calling isochronous a guarantee of fixed speed at every instant. It returns to the same steady speed; transient dips still occur.

2.9 Ignition systems: battery and magneto

A petrol engine needs a spark at the right plug at the right moment. Battery-coil ignition stores energy in a coil's magnetic field and releases it as a high-voltage pulse when a contact breaker opens. Magneto ignition does the same job but generates its own current from a rotating magnet, so it needs no battery. Both systems appear on small engines, older cars and motorcycles, and aircraft piston engines; electronic ignition now replaces the contact breaker in most new cars.

You need: the SI engine cycle from the engine fundamentals topic.

Battery-coil ignition: parts and path

The battery supplies 12 V direct current. The ignition switch connects it to the primary winding, a few hundred turns of thick copper wire. Current rises and builds a magnetic field in the iron core. The contact breaker is a pair of points opened by a cam on the distributor shaft, which turns at half crank speed on a four-stroke engine; the points stay closed while the field builds. A small condenser sits across the breaker points.

When the breaker opens, primary current falls quickly. The magnetic field collapses. The changing field links the secondary winding, about a hundred times as many turns of fine wire on the same core. Mutual induction produces a high voltage, often 15 kV to 30 kV, enough to jump the spark plug gap. The condenser limits arcing at the points and helps the field collapse faster.

The distributor is a rotary switch. It sends the secondary voltage to the plug of the cylinder ready to fire, in the engine's firing order. The spark then ignites the compressed mixture. Some syllabi call battery-coil ignition coil ignition.

E = ½ L I²

E is the energy stored in the primary magnetic field (J); L is the primary inductance (H); I is the primary current (A) when the breaker opens.

Magneto ignition: making its own current

A magneto generates its own primary current. In the common rotating-magnet type, a permanent magnet on a shaft driven by the engine turns past a fixed core that carries the primary and secondary windings; in the older rotating-armature type the windings turn between fixed magnets. Either way the flux through the primary changes and induces a current. The contact breaker interrupts it at the firing point, and the secondary produces the high-voltage spark. A condenser across the breaker works as in battery-coil ignition.

No battery, no ignition switch supply and no separate charging circuit are needed for the spark. That is why magnetos are common on small petrol engines such as chainsaws, lawn mowers and some motorcycles, and on aircraft piston engines. The spark energy comes from mechanical rotation, so a magneto must be turned fast enough to make a good spark. At very low cranking speed the output can be weak; an impulse coupling or a separate starting vibrator may be added on some engines.

The distributor, where fitted, sends the high voltage to each plug in turn. A single-cylinder engine can use the same coil and breaker without a distributor.

Battery-coil versus magneto

Battery-coil and magneto systems both use an induction coil and a breaker, but they suit different duties.

Dwell: why the spark weakens at high speed

The primary current does not jump to its full value when the points close. The coil's inductance makes it rise along a curve, i = (V ÷ R)(1 − e−t/τ), with time constant τ = L ÷ R. The time the points stay closed is set by the dwell angle, the cam angle for which they are shut. Dwell is a fixed angle, so the time falls as speed rises; at high speed the current is cut before it reaches V ÷ R, the stored energy ½LI² falls, and the spark is weaker. This is the battery-coil system's main limit, and electronic systems fix it by controlling dwell time (see Electronic ignition and laser ignition).

2.10 Electronic ignition and laser ignition

Electronic ignition replaces the contact breaker, the part that burns, wears and drifts out of time, with a sensor and a power transistor or thyristor. The spark becomes stronger at high speed, the timing stays set for the life of the engine, and an ECU can choose the spark angle for every speed and load. Laser ignition goes one step further and removes the spark plug's electrodes altogether, igniting the charge with a focused pulse of light. Almost every car and two-wheeler now uses electronic ignition; laser ignition is still at the research stage.

You need: Ignition systems: battery and magneto for the coil, the stored energy ½LI² and dwell.

From points to transistors

In transistor-assisted contact (TAC) ignition the points stay, but they carry only the small base current of a power transistor, and the transistor switches the coil current. The points no longer arc, so they last much longer, but their rubbing block still wears and the timing still drifts.

A breakerless (transistorised) system removes the points. A trigger in the distributor or at the crank tells an ignition module when to fire:

  • Magnetic pulse generator: a toothed reluctor passes a pickup coil and induces an alternating voltage; the module switches at the zero crossing.
  • Hall-effect sensor: a vane passes between a magnet and a Hall chip, giving a clean square wave that works even at zero speed, so the engine can be timed statically.
  • Optical trigger: a slotted disc interrupts a light beam; used on some older systems.
  1. The trigger signal reaches the ignition module.
  2. The module turns the power transistor on, and primary current builds in the coil.
  3. At the firing point the module switches the transistor off; the primary current stops sharply.
  4. The collapsing field induces the high voltage in the secondary, and the plug sparks.

The module also controls dwell. It switches on earlier at high speed so that the current always reaches its limit before the break, and it limits the current at low speed so the coil does not overheat. This constant-energy control removes the high-speed weakness of points ignition.

Distributorless and capacitor discharge ignition

With an ECU the distributor can go too. In wasted-spark ignition one coil serves a pair of cylinders whose pistons rise together, for example 1 and 4 of a four-cylinder engine; it fires both plugs at once, one at the end of compression and one, wasted, at the end of exhaust. In coil-on-plug ignition each plug has its own coil and driver, and the ECU fires each in turn from its spark map.

Capacitor discharge ignition (CDI) stores energy in a capacitor instead of the coil. A small converter charges the capacitor to a few hundred volts; at the firing point a thyristor (SCR) dumps it into the coil, which acts as a step-up transformer. The secondary voltage rises in a few microseconds, so it fires even a fouled plug, but the spark lasts only about a tenth of a millisecond, against a millisecond or more for an inductive spark, which is poorer for lean or slow-burning mixtures. CDI is common on two-wheelers, outboard motors and small engines.

Laser ignition basics

In laser ignition a short pulse from a laser, typically a nanosecond-pulse solid-state laser, passes through a window in the head and is focused by a lens to a tiny spot inside the charge. At the focus the light's intensity is so high that the gas ionises and breaks down into a small, hot plasma, a spark without electrodes. The plasma heats the nearby mixture and starts a flame kernel, which then grows as a normal flame.

  • No electrodes, so no electrode wear and no metal to cool the young flame kernel.
  • The focus can be placed where combustion is best, for example the centre of the chamber, and one beam can be split for several ignition points.
  • Breakdown becomes easier as pressure rises, while a spark plug needs more voltage, so laser ignition suits high boost and lean mixtures.
  • Problems: deposits foul the window, the laser must survive engine heat and vibration, and the hardware is costly and bulky; it is used in research engines and test rigs, not in production cars.

  • Peak laser power is energy ÷ pulse time, not energy × time; with a 6 ns pulse a few millijoules give megawatts.

2.11 Ignition timing and spark advance

Spark timing is the instant the plug fires. Spark advance, also called ignition advance, is how many crank degrees before top dead centre (TDC) the spark occurs. The flame needs time to burn the charge, so the spark must come early enough for peak pressure to arrive just after TDC. As speed rises the same burn time covers more crank degrees, so advance must rise; at high load it must fall to avoid knock. Only a few syllabi list this as a separate topic, but every petrol engine uses it.

You need: Ignition systems: battery and magneto and Electronic ignition.

Spark timing relative to TDC

Spark advance θ is the crank angle before TDC at which the spark fires. Combustion takes time: the flame grows from the plug and pressure rises. Peak pressure should arrive about 10° to 15° after TDC for best torque. If the spark is late, the piston is already moving down and pressure is wasted; if it is too early, pressure peaks before TDC, the piston works against it and the end gas may knock.

θ = 6 N tb − θpk

θ is the advance (° before TDC); N is the speed (rpm); tb is the time from spark to peak pressure (s); θpk is the target peak position (° after TDC). 6N is the crank speed in degrees per second.

The burn time is not fixed: more turbulence at higher speed makes the flame faster, so tb falls, and the advance rises with speed but less than in proportion. The advance that gives the most torque is called MBT timing.

Advance mechanisms and checks

Older distributors used centrifugal and vacuum advance. Centrifugal weights move outward with speed and turn the cam or reluctor ahead. The vacuum unit adds advance at part load, where the charge is diluted by leftover exhaust gas and has low density, so it burns slowly. Many engines take ported vacuum from just above the throttle, which is near zero at idle and at wide-open throttle, so vacuum advance drops away at full load.

Modern engines use an ECU spark map of advance against speed and load (from manifold pressure or air flow). A knock sensor, a piezo accelerometer on the block, detects knock; the ECU retards that cylinder a few degrees, then advances back slowly. To check base timing:

  1. Warm the engine and set the specified idle speed.
  2. Disconnect and plug the vacuum hose if the maker states it.
  3. Find the fixed pointer on the timing cover and the mark on the crank pulley.
  4. Clip the timing light's pickup on the number one plug lead; aim the flashing light at the marks and read the advance.
  5. Compare the reading with the specification and turn the distributor to correct it.

High load needs less advance to avoid knock; light load needs more because the diluted charge burns slowly.

Worked example

Question. At 1500 rpm the time from spark to peak pressure is 4.0 ms; at 3000 rpm more turbulence cuts it to 2.5 ms. Peak pressure should be 14° after TDC. Find the spark advance at both speeds, and the advance that 3000 rpm would need if the burn time had stayed at 4.0 ms.

  1. At 1500 rpm the crank turns 6 × 1500 = 9000 °/s; spark to peak = 9000 × 0.0040 = 36°; θ = 36° − 14° = 22° before TDC.
  2. At 3000 rpm: 6 × 3000 = 18 000 °/s; 18 000 × 0.0025 = 45°; θ = 45° − 14° = 31° before TDC.
  3. With 4.0 ms at 3000 rpm: 18 000 × 0.0040 = 72°; θ = 72° − 14° = 58°, an impossible advance.

Answer. 22° at 1500 rpm and 31° at 3000 rpm. Doubling the speed raised the advance by only 9°, because turbulence shortened the burn time.

Exam traps

  • Advance is not fixed; high load needs retard, even at the same speed, to avoid knock.
  • 10° advance means 10° before TDC, not after.
  • Centrifugal advance follows speed; vacuum advance follows load through manifold vacuum.
  • In θ = 6Ntb − θpk, N is in rpm and t in seconds; using ms or 360N gives answers far too large.
  • Knock-sensor retard is temporary protection; the ECU then advances back.

Takeaways

  1. A carburettor's venturi and main jet give one mixture strength; idle, power, accelerator and choke circuits add fuel where the engine's demand curve departs from it.
  2. A fuel injection system must meter, pressurise, time, atomise and distribute the fuel; injection lag is a fixed time, so its crank angle grows with speed.
  3. In a jerk pump the helix sets the effective stroke and so the quantity; a distributor pump does the same with one element for all cylinders.
  4. Common rail separates pressure from timing: quantity depends on open time and rail pressure together, and split injection lowers noise, NOx and soot.
  5. Electronic fuel injection turns measured air per cycle into a pulse width plus dead time; the oxygen sensor closes the loop near 14.7:1.
  6. Gasoline direct injection cools the charge, allows a higher compression ratio and stratified lean running, at the cost of particulates and valve deposits.
  7. Nozzle hole size, number and pressure trade atomisation against penetration; penetration must reach the bowl's air without wetting the wall.
  8. A governor is a closed-loop speed controller; a mechanical governor needs some droop, while isochronous control holds one speed.
  9. Battery-coil and magneto ignition both break a primary current to induce the spark; a fixed dwell angle weakens the battery-coil spark at high speed.
  10. Electronic ignition controls dwell and timing without points, CDI stores energy in a capacitor, and laser ignition removes the electrodes; spark advance rises with speed and falls with load.

Understand With a neat sketch, explain the working of a simple carburettor. Draw the mixture requirement curve of a petrol engine and explain why a practical carburettor needs idle, main, power, accelerator and choke circuits. 13 marks

Points to cover. Parts: air filter, float chamber, float and needle valve, nozzle and main jet, venturi, throttle, choke; Venturi depression lifts petrol; tip above the float level; Mixture curve: rich idle, lean cruise, rich full load, transient enrichment; Simple carburettor richens as flow rises and cannot follow the curve; Role of each circuit and when it acts.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose: a petrol (SI) engine needs a combustible air-petrol mixture whose strength suits each operating condition. The carburettor meters petrol into the air in proportion to the air flow, atomises it and lets the throttle control how much mixture enters.
  2. Sketch: draw a vertical tube with the air filter at the top, the choke butterfly, a venturi (a smooth narrowing to a throat and widening again), the fuel nozzle ending in the throat, and the throttle butterfly below the venturi. Beside it draw the float chamber with the float, the needle valve at the petrol inlet, the vent, the petrol level, and the main jet at the bottom feeding the nozzle; the nozzle tip is a few millimetres above the petrol level. The figure of the simple carburettor in the carburettor chapter can be reproduced.
  3. Float chamber: the float rises with the petrol and pushes the needle onto its seat, shutting the inlet. The level, and so the head on the jet, stays constant. The chamber is vented to the air-filter side, so it is at near-atmospheric pressure.
  4. Working: on the suction stroke air rushes through the venturi. By continuity its speed is highest at the throat, and by Bernoulli its pressure is lowest there. The difference between the float-chamber pressure and the throat pressure (the venturi depression, typically a few hundredths of a bar) pushes petrol through the main jet and out of the nozzle. The fast air stream breaks the petrol into droplets, which partly evaporate on the way to the cylinder.
  5. Why the tip is above the level: when the engine stops there is no depression, and the 4 to 6 mm lift stops petrol from dribbling out. The fuel therefore sees Δp − ρfgz, and no fuel flows until the depression exceeds this small head.
  6. Air filter and throttle: the filter (dry paper, oil-bath or oiled foam) removes dust that would wear the bore and rings; a choked filter enriches the mixture. The throttle sets the quantity of mixture and so the power, since an SI engine is quantity-governed.
  7. Mixture requirement curve: plot A/F against load from idle to full load. Typical values:
    ConditionA/F (by mass)Reason
    Idle and very low loadabout 12:1 (rich)low air speed, poor vaporisation, high residual-gas dilution
    Cruise (part load)about 15–16:1 (lean)best economy; stoichiometric is 14.7:1
    Full loadabout 12.5:1 (rich)maximum power; extra fuel cools the charge
    Sudden accelerationmomentary extra fuelfuel film in the manifold lags the air
    Cold startvery richonly the light fractions evaporate from cold surfaces
  8. Limit of the simple carburettor: with z neglected, A/F = (CdaAt ÷ CdfAf)√(ρa/ρf), a nearly fixed ratio; in practice the mixture gets richer as air flow rises, because the air density at the throat falls with depression and the fuel discharge coefficient rises. It is too lean (or delivers nothing) at idle and too rich at high flow, so it cannot follow the curve.
  9. Idle circuit: with the throttle nearly shut the venturi depression is tiny, but the manifold vacuum below the throttle is high. A small passage from the float chamber opens just below the throttle edge and draws a rich mixture; an idle mixture screw sets it. A progression (transfer) port just above the throttle edge carries the engine smoothly into the main circuit.
  10. Main circuit with compensation: from part load upward the main jet supplies petrol through the venturi. A compensating jet or an air-bleed emulsion tube admits air into the fuel well as flow rises, so the main circuit holds the lean cruise mixture instead of richening.
  11. Power (economy) circuit: at full load the manifold vacuum falls; a vacuum piston or a metering rod lets a spring open an extra jet, enriching to about 12.5:1. At cruise the high vacuum keeps it shut for economy.
  12. Accelerator pump: a plunger linked to the throttle squirts a short shot of petrol into the venturi when the throttle is opened quickly, filling the lean 'flat spot' while the manifold fuel film catches up.
  13. Choke: a butterfly at the inlet, closed for cold starting. It restricts air, so the depression at the jet rises and a very rich mixture is drawn; it must be opened as the engine warms, or it washes oil from the bore and fouls plugs.
  14. Conclusion: the venturi and main jet meter fuel in proportion to air flow for one mixture strength; the idle, power, accelerator and choke circuits add fuel where the engine's demand curve departs from that strength.

Model answer.

  1. Jerk pump sketch: draw a barrel with an inlet port and a spill port opposite each other, the plunger inside with a vertical groove leading to a helical cut, the delivery valve and spring above, and the cam with a roller tappet below; a toothed control sleeve on the plunger meshes with the rack. The jerk pump figure (start and end of delivery) can be reproduced.
  2. Construction: one pumping element per cylinder sits in a common housing, driven by a camshaft at half engine speed on a four-stroke engine. The plunger is lapped into its barrel to a clearance of a few micrometres, so it seals without rings. A spring returns the plunger and keeps the tappet on the cam.
  3. Filling: with the plunger at the bottom, diesel from the gallery (fed by a feed pump at a few bar) fills the barrel through both ports.
  4. Start of delivery: the cam lifts the plunger. When its top edge covers both ports the fuel is trapped; pressure rises, opens the light delivery valve and travels along the pipe until the nozzle opens at its set pressure.
  5. End of delivery: as the plunger rises further, the helix reaches the spill port. The space above the plunger now connects through the vertical groove to the spill port, pressure collapses and delivery ends; the rest of the stroke only pushes fuel back to the gallery.
  6. Metering: the rack turns the plunger. This moves the helix relative to the spill port, so spill comes earlier or later; the effective stroke changes while the total cam lift stays the same.   7. Delivery valve: its retraction piston draws back a small volume of fuel as it closes, so line pressure drops sharply and the nozzle snaps shut without dribble, while a residual pressure keeps the pipe primed.
  8. Distributor pump (axial-plunger type) sketch: one plunger driven by a cam plate with as many lobes as cylinders, riding on fixed rollers; a distributor slot in the plunger; a control sleeve moved by the governor; outlets to each injector around the head.
  9. Working: the cam plate makes the plunger reciprocate and rotate together. Each lobe gives one pumping stroke, and the rotation lines up the distributor slot with the outlet of the cylinder due to fire, in firing order. Delivery ends when the plunger's spill port leaves the control sleeve; moving the sleeve changes the effective stroke and so the quantity. In the radial-plunger type, opposed plungers in a rotor are driven inward by an internal cam ring and quantity is set by inlet metering.

Model answer.

  1. Battery-coil circuit: 12 V battery → ignition switch → coil primary (a few hundred turns of thick wire) → contact breaker to earth, with a condenser across the points; the secondary (about a hundred times as many fine turns on the same iron core) → distributor rotor → plug leads → plugs to earth. Understand Explain the working of a breakerless (transistorised) electronic ignition system and a capacitor discharge ignition system. Outline the basics, merits and limits of laser ignition. 13 marks

Points to cover. Drawbacks of points; TAC; Trigger types: magnetic pulse, Hall, optical; Module switches the coil; dwell and current control; DIS and coil-on-plug; CDI: capacitor charged to a few hundred volts, thyristor, E = ½CV², fast rise, short spark; Laser: focused pulse, plasma breakdown, no electrodes; window fouling and cost.

Model answer.

  1. Why electronic ignition: contact points burn and pit, the rubbing block wears so the gap and timing drift, the current they can switch is limited, and the spark weakens at high speed. Transistor-assisted contact ignition first let the points carry only a transistor's base current; breakerless systems remove the points entirely.
  2. Breakerless system, block sketch: trigger in the distributor or at the crank → ignition module (power transistor and its control) → coil primary; coil secondary → distributor or directly to the plugs. The breakerless and laser ignition figure can be reproduced.
  3. Triggers: a magnetic pulse generator (toothed reluctor passing a pickup coil, giving an alternating voltage whose zero crossing marks the firing point); a Hall-effect sensor (a vane passing between a magnet and a Hall chip, giving a square wave that also works at zero speed); or an optical slotted disc.
  4. Sequence: the trigger signal reaches the module; the module switches the transistor on and primary current builds; at the firing point it switches the transistor off; the field collapses and the secondary fires the plug.
  5. Dwell and current control: the module switches on earlier as speed rises, so the coil current always reaches its limit before the break, and limits the current at low speed to protect the coil. This keeps the spark energy nearly constant, removing the main weakness of points ignition.
  6. Distributorless forms: wasted-spark ignition uses one double-ended coil per pair of cylinders (1 and 4, 2 and 3), firing both plugs, one at the end of compression and one at the end of exhaust; at 3000 rpm each coil fires 50 times a second. Coil-on-plug ignition gives each plug its own coil and lets the ECU time each cylinder.
  7. Capacitor discharge ignition: a converter charges a capacitor to a few hundred volts; the trigger fires a thyristor, which dumps the charge into the coil primary; the coil acts as a step-up transformer. E = ½CV²: 1 μF at 400 V stores 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ × 160 000 = 80 mJ, almost three times the 29 mJ of a points coil at 6000 rpm. For a four-cylinder four-stroke at 6000 rpm, 200 sparks a second need an average charging power of 200 × 0.080 = 16 W.
  8. CDI merits and limits: very fast voltage rise (microseconds), so it fires fouled plugs and holds its energy at high speed; but the spark lasts only about a tenth of a millisecond, which is poor for lean mixtures. It suits two-wheelers, outboards and small engines.
  9. Laser ignition basics: a nanosecond-pulse laser beam passes through a window in the head and a lens focuses it to a tiny spot. The intensity there ionises the gas into a plasma, which heats the mixture and starts a flame kernel. For 30 mJ in 6 ns, peak power is 0.030/(6 × 10⁻⁹) = 5 MW; over a 50 μm spot (area 1.96 × 10⁻⁵ cm²) the intensity is 2.5 × 10¹¹ W/cm².

Points to cover. Metering method of each; Distribution: manifold film versus port injection versus in-cylinder; Part load: throttling, stratified lean operation, charge cooling; Emissions: closed loop and catalyst; GDI particulates; Cost and complexity; justified choice.

Model answer.

  1. Metering: a carburettor meters by venturi depression with fixed jets, so its A/F drifts with air density, temperature and wear, and it has no feedback. MPFI meters by injector pulse width at fixed pressure, with an oxygen sensor closing the loop, so it holds A/F within a narrow band around 14.7:1. GDI meters the same way but at 50 to 200 bar, and can also choose when in the cycle the fuel arrives.
  2. Distribution: with a carburettor the fuel travels through the manifold partly as liquid film, so cylinders near and far from it get different mixtures, worst when cold. MPFI puts an injector at each port, so each cylinder gets its own measured fuel; some film still forms on the port and valve. GDI injects into each cylinder, so distribution is exact and there is no port film.
  3. Part-load efficiency: carburettor and MPFI engines are throttled at part load and lose pumping work; MPFI can run slightly leaner and cut fuel on overrun. GDI in stratified mode can run unthrottled and lean at light load, and its charge cooling allows about one ratio more compression, both of which raise efficiency.
  4. Emissions: a carburettor cannot hold A/F tightly enough for a three-way catalyst, so it cannot meet current limits. MPFI with closed loop and a three-way catalyst is the established low-emission solution. GDI meets the gaseous limits in homogeneous mode, but produces more particulates (usually needing a gasoline particulate filter) and, if run lean, NOx that needs a lean NOx trap.
  5. Transient response and cold start: carburettor needs an accelerator pump and choke; MPFI handles both by pulse corrections; GDI is fastest, with no wall film, and can use split injection to heat the catalyst.

MPFI meets the limits at moderate cost and is robust and easy to service, which suits a price-sensitive small car with a naturally aspirated engine. GDI gives better fuel economy and suits a downsized turbocharged 1.0 to 1.2-litre engine, but adds cost, needs a particulate filter and is more sensitive to fuel quality and deposits.
  8. Recommendation: for a low-cost naturally aspirated 1.2-litre car, MPFI; if the maker uses a turbocharged downsized engine to gain efficiency, GDI with homogeneous operation and a particulate filter. Either way the decision rests on emissions first, then cost against fuel economy.

Evaluate Explain why a common rail system with split injection can lower both NOx and smoke where an in-line jerk pump cannot, using injection pressure, timing and heat-release reasoning. Use the jerk pump's speed-dependent pressure and the common rail quantity relation Q ∝ t√Δp in your answer, and weigh the cost of the change. 15 marks

Points to cover. Jerk pump: pressure rises with plunger speed and quantity, so low pressure at low speed and load; timing fixed by cam and helix; Common rail: pressure independent of speed; ECU timing; split injection; Pilot shortens delay, smaller premixed spike: less noise and NOx; High pressure at all speeds: fine spray, less soot; after-injection burns soot; Trade-off and cost.

Model answer.

  1. The NOx-soot trade-off: NOx forms where burned gas is hottest (high peak temperature, early combustion); soot forms in fuel-rich zones where fuel and air mix poorly and burns out only if the late cycle is hot and well mixed. Retarding injection lowers NOx but raises soot; raising pressure lowers soot. A system that can control pressure and timing separately, and shape the rate of heat release, can lower both.
  2. Jerk pump limits: injection pressure is made by the plunger moving against the nozzle, so it rises with pump speed and with the fuel quantity. At low speed and light load the pressure is low, the spray is coarse and soot is high. The start of injection is fixed by the cam and helix (with only a mechanical advance), and there is one injection per cycle, so the rate of injection, and the heat release, follow the cam profile.
  3. Ignition delay and the premixed spike: in a single long injection, all fuel injected during the delay period mixes and then burns at once. This premixed burn gives a high rate of pressure rise (diesel knock, noise) and a high peak temperature (NOx).
  4. Common rail pressure: the rail holds 1000 to 2000 bar at any speed, so the spray is fine at idle and low speed as well as at full load: better mixing, less soot. Quantity is set by the open time: Q ∝ t√Δp; for example 30 mm³ takes 1.0 ms at 1200 bar but only 0.81 ms at 1800 bar, so higher pressure also shortens injection and keeps combustion compact.
  5. Pilot injection: a small pilot pulse burns first and heats the charge, so the main fuel ignites with a short delay; little fuel collects before ignition, the premixed spike shrinks, noise and peak temperature fall, and NOx falls.
  6. Main injection timing: the ECU can retard the main pulse slightly to lower NOx further, and recover the soot penalty with the higher pressure and an after-injection.
  7. After and post injection: a small after-injection just after the main keeps the late cycle hot and turbulent and burns soot; a late post injection heats the exhaust to regenerate the particulate filter.
  8. Heat-release picture (describe or sketch): a single-injection curve shows a tall early spike followed by a diffusion tail; a pilot-main-after curve shows a small pilot bump, a lower and broader main peak, and a stronger late burn.
