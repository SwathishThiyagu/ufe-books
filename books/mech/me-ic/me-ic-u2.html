2.1 Carburettors: mixture requirements and circuits

A petrol engine needs a different air-fuel mixture at idle, cruise, full load and acceleration. A carburettor is a mechanical device that uses the engine's own suction to draw petrol into the incoming air and to change the ratio as operating conditions change. It matters because a mixture set for one condition is wrong for the others: too rich wastes fuel, too lean misfires or knocks. Carburettors ran millions of Indian scooters and cars before fuel injection; the same mixture-demand idea now drives electronic injection.

You need: the four-stroke SI cycle and air-fuel mixture basics from Unit 1.

Mixture demand

Air-fuel ratio (A/F) is the mass of air divided by the mass of fuel entering the engine. Petrol's chemically correct (stoichiometric) ratio is about 14.7:1 by mass: 14.7 kg of air just burns 1 kg of petrol. The engine does not always want that ratio. At idle it needs about 12:1 because low air speed, poor vaporisation and leftover exhaust gas dilute the fresh charge. At cruise it needs about 15–16:1 for economy. At full load it needs about 12.5:1 to make maximum power and to cool the charge. During acceleration it needs a short extra squirt of fuel. Rich means A/F below 14.7; lean means A/F above 14.7.

A/F = mair ÷ mfuel

mair is air mass flow in kg/s; mfuel is fuel mass flow in kg/s; A/F is by mass and has no unit.

Venturi and main jet

The carburettor makes a pressure difference, called venturi depression, to lift petrol into the air stream. The incoming air passes a narrowed throat, the venturi. By Bernoulli's principle the air speeds up and its static pressure falls. The float chamber is at near-atmospheric pressure and holds petrol at a constant level using a float and needle valve. The pressure difference between the float chamber and the venturi pushes petrol through the main metering jet, a calibrated hole. A bigger jet gives more fuel for the same depression. A fixed main jet cannot satisfy all loads: at idle the air flow is too small to make useful venturi depression, so a separate idle circuit is needed. At high load the main jet alone tends to be too lean, so a power valve adds fuel. During sudden opening the fuel film lags, so an accelerator pump adds fuel. A single jet is therefore a compromise, not a complete control.

The five circuits

Some syllabi call the circuits idle, main, power, accelerator and choke. The table gives the role of each.

At small throttle openings the idle and main circuits overlap; at larger part throttle the main circuit dominates. The power/economy valve is often a vacuum-operated piston: high manifold vacuum at light load keeps it closed for economy; low vacuum at high load lets a spring open an extra jet. The accelerator pump is a plunger linked to the throttle; quick opening squirts fuel through a nozzle. The choke is a butterfly at the air inlet; closing it for cold start increases depression and enriches the mixture.

Worked example

Question. An engine takes 0.045 kg/s of air. The carburettor is set for 15:1 by mass. Petrol density is 750 kg/m³. Find the fuel mass flow and the fuel volume flow.

  1. A/F = m_air ÷ m_fuel, so m_fuel = m_air ÷ (A/F).
  2. m_fuel = 0.045 ÷ 15 = 0.003 kg/s.
  3. Volume flow = m_fuel ÷ density = 0.003 ÷ 750 = 4×10⁻⁶ m³/s.
  4. Convert: 1 m³ = 10⁶ cm³, so 4×10⁻⁶ m³/s = 4 cm³/s.

Answer. Fuel mass flow is 0.003 kg/s (3 g/s) and fuel volume flow is 4 cm³/s.

Exam traps

  • The main jet alone does not set the mixture at all loads; idle, power valve, accelerator pump and choke add extra fuel.
  • A/F is by mass, not volume. 14.7:1 is a mass ratio; vapour volume ratio is different.
  • Rich means low A/F (more fuel); lean means high A/F. Students often reverse these.
  • Venturi depression is the pressure drop at the throat, not the whole manifold vacuum. At idle the throttle plate creates vacuum but the venturi signal is weak.
  • The choke is for cold start only. Leaving it on washes oil from the cylinder and causes rich misfire.

2.2 Fuel injection systems: requirements and types

A fuel injection system sends the right amount of fuel into each cylinder at the right moment, in a spray fine enough to mix with air. It exists because a carburettor cannot hold a steady air-fuel ratio for every cylinder and load, and because diesel fuel must be injected at high pressure after the air is compressed. It is used on diesel engines of trucks, tractors and generators, and on modern petrol cars with electronic injection. The system must meter, atomise, distribute and time the fuel.

You need chapter 2.1 for the SI mixture requirement, and Unit 1 for the four-stroke cycle.

What the system must do

A fuel injection system has five jobs: meter, atomise, distribute, time and pressurise the fuel. Metering sets the mass of fuel per cycle to match load and speed. Too little fuel gives a lean mixture, misfire and low power. Too much fuel gives black smoke, high fuel consumption and diluted lubricating oil.

Atomisation breaks the fuel into fine droplets. Small droplets expose more surface area to air, so evaporation and mixing are fast. A solid jet burns slowly and deposits carbon.

Distribution gives every cylinder the same fuel quantity and a spray aimed at the right place. Timing starts delivery at the correct crank angle: in a diesel engine near top dead centre (TDC) at the end of compression; in a petrol engine the start depends on port or direct injection. Pressure must push fuel into the cylinder against the compressed air. Diesel injection pressures are high because cylinder pressure at injection is high.

  • Accurate metering: correct mass per cycle and equal between cylinders.
  • Fine atomisation: small droplets for fast mixing.
  • Even distribution: same quantity and pattern to each cylinder.
  • Correct timing: start of delivery at the right crank angle.
  • Sufficient pressure: fuel enters the cylinder against compressed air.

Types: air, solid, direct and control

Air injection uses a blast of compressed air to carry fuel into the cylinder. Early diesel engines used it; the air compressor was large and the system was slow. Air injection is obsolete.

Solid injection (airless injection) pressurises the fuel with a pump and forces it through a nozzle. No compressed air is used. Some syllabi call solid injection airless injection. Both names mean the same family, which is used on almost every modern engine.

Direct injection sprays fuel into the combustion chamber. Examples are common rail, unit injector and distributor pump systems. Indirect injection sprays fuel into a separate prechamber or into the inlet port. Petrol port injection and throttle body injection are indirect because the fuel meets air outside the cylinder.

Control divides systems into mechanical and electronic. A mechanical system uses a cam, plunger and spring to meter and time fuel. An electronic system uses sensors, an electronic control unit (ECU) and solenoid or piezo injectors. Continuous injection sprays all the time; intermittent injection sprays in pulses. See 2.3 for mechanical pumps and 2.4 for common rail.

Injection timing and injection lag

Injection timing is the crank angle at which the pump starts to deliver fuel. Some texts define it at the nozzle opening. The start of delivery is earlier than the start of spray because of injection lag.

Injection lag is the delay from pump delivery to nozzle opening. Fuel is compressible, the pressure wave travels along the pipe, and the nozzle spring must be lifted. Lag is a time in milliseconds, but the engine turns during it, so lag appears as a crank-angle delay.

Injection duration is the crank angle from nozzle opening to nozzle closing. It grows with fuel quantity. A diesel engine needs injection advance as speed rises so that combustion still starts near TDC. Late injection raises exhaust temperature and smoke; too early injection raises peak pressure and diesel knock. See Unit 3 for combustion stages.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder, four-stroke diesel engine uses 6 kg/h of fuel at 1800 rpm. Find the fuel delivered per injection per cylinder in kg and mg.

  1. Fuel rate per minute = 6 kg/h ÷ 60 min/h = 0.1 kg/min.
  2. For a four-stroke engine, each cylinder fires once every two revolutions. Injections per cylinder per minute = 1800 ÷ 2 = 900.
  3. Total injections per minute = 4 cylinders × 900 = 3600.
  4. Fuel per injection = 0.1 kg/min ÷ 3600 = 2.7778×10⁻⁵ kg.
  5. In mg: 2.7778×10⁻⁵ kg × 10⁶ mg/kg = 27.778 mg.

Answer. The fuel delivered per injection per cylinder is 2.78×10⁻⁵ kg, or 27.8 mg.

Exam traps

  • Direct injection is not the same as direct control. Direct means the injector sprays into the cylinder; it does not mean a lever or human directly controls the fuel.
  • For a four-stroke engine, each cylinder injects once every two revolutions. Injections per cylinder per minute are rpm ÷ 2, not rpm.
  • Injection timing is the start of fuel delivery, not the spark timing. Spark timing belongs to the ignition system; see chapter 2.9.
  • Solid injection and airless injection are the same thing. Air injection is the obsolete air-blast method, not a modern air-assisted petrol injector.
  • Injection lag is a time delay before the nozzle opens. Injection duration is the time or crank angle from opening to closing. They are not the same quantity.

2.3 Jerk pump, distributor pump and unit injector

Mechanical diesel injection must create pressure high enough to atomise fuel and time delivery to a few crank degrees. Three mechanical layouts do this without electronics. The in-line jerk pump uses one plunger per cylinder. The distributor pump uses one plunger for all cylinders and sends fuel to each in turn. The unit injector puts the pump and nozzle together at the cylinder. Tractors, gensets, older trucks and large marine engines use them; the plunger-and-spill idea also sits inside modern high-pressure pumps.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for the four jobs of an injection system — meter, atomise, distribute, time — and Unit 1 for four-stroke valve and injection timing.

The in-line jerk pump

The in-line jerk pump, called a plunger pump in some syllabi, has one pumping element per cylinder driven by one camshaft. On a four-stroke engine the pump camshaft turns at half engine speed, so each plunger injects once per two crank revolutions.

Each element is a plunger inside a barrel. Fuel fills the space above the plunger through an inlet port while the plunger is down. A vertical groove and a helical edge, the helix, are cut on the plunger. The helix sets the end of delivery.

  1. The cam lifts the plunger.
  2. The plunger covers the inlet port; trapped fuel cannot return to the gallery.
  3. Pressure opens the delivery valve and the nozzle at its set pressure.
  4. The helix uncovers the spill port; fuel spills back, pressure falls, and the delivery valve closes.
  5. The plunger finishes its stroke without delivering, then returns.

Effective stroke s_eff is the plunger travel from port closing to spill opening. It is shorter than the total cam stroke. Volume per injection is the swept volume of that effective stroke:

V = (π d² / 4) × s_eff

V is fuel volume per injection (m³); d is plunger diameter (m); s_eff is effective stroke (m); π is 3.1416.

The control rack rotates the plunger to change fuel quantity. Rotation moves the helix relative to the spill port, so spill happens earlier or later; total stroke stays fixed. A delivery valve holds residual line pressure and stops backflow. Timing marks on the pump flange and flywheel set delivery start for cylinder one.

The distributor pump

The distributor pump, or rotary pump, uses one plunger for all cylinders. The plunger reciprocates to pressurise fuel and rotates to direct fuel to each cylinder in turn.

A cam ring with one lobe per cylinder pushes rollers inward as the plunger rotates; each push gives one pumping stroke. The rotor outlet port lines up with each delivery union in firing order. A delivery valve at each union keeps the line filled.

A control sleeve or spill ring, moved by the governor, covers a spill port on the plunger. Moving the sleeve changes the spilled volume and so the delivered fuel. Small pumps may meter by inlet throttling instead.

  • Advantages: compact, one pumping element, fewer calibrations; common on 3 to 6 cylinder car and tractor engines.
  • Limits: one plunger supplies every cylinder, so maximum fuel and pressure are lower than a jerk pump.
  • Long high-pressure pipes add pressure lag and can make timing differ slightly between cylinders.
  • Wear in the rotor and cam ring upsets delivery balance.

The unit injector

The unit injector puts the pump and nozzle in one body at the cylinder head. The engine cam, through a rocker or a dedicated lobe, pushes the plunger directly. No long high-pressure pipe connects pump and nozzle.

The plunger pressurises fuel just above the nozzle needle. A helix or a spill valve ends injection; the rack or linkage sets fuel quantity. The nozzle opens at its set pressure and sprays into the chamber. Fuel lubricates the plunger and cools the body.

The short, stiff path gives fast pressure rise, precise timing and high pressure. Large marine and locomotive diesels use unit injectors. Limits: the camshaft and rocker carry the full injection load; the cylinder head is crowded; each unit needs setting; service costs more. Timing is set by the cam lobe, rocker clearance or shims, and marks on camshaft and flywheel.

Timing, comparison and limits

All three pumps need static timing. Turn the engine to the specified angle before top dead centre on the compression stroke of cylinder one, align the pump or cam marks, then check dynamic timing by spill or strobe method. A wrong mark shifts every cylinder together.

Worked example

Question. A jerk pump plunger has diameter 8 mm and effective stroke 3 mm. Find the fuel delivered per injection in m³ and in mm³. Take π = 3.1416.

  1. Convert: d = 8 mm = 0.008 m; s_eff = 3 mm = 0.003 m.
  2. Area A = π d² / 4 = 3.1416 × (0.008)² / 4 = 3.1416 × 6.4×10⁻⁵ / 4 = 5.027×10⁻⁵ m².
  3. Volume V = A × s_eff = 5.027×10⁻⁵ × 0.003 = 1.508×10⁻⁷ m³.
  4. Convert to mm³: 1 m³ = 10⁹ mm³, so V = 1.508×10⁻⁷ × 10⁹ = 150.8 mm³.

Answer. The fuel delivered per injection is 1.508×10⁻⁷ m³, or 150.8 mm³.

Exam traps

  • Using total plunger stroke instead of effective stroke. The effective stroke starts only after the inlet and spill ports are covered by the plunger.
  • Thinking that rotating the plunger changes its stroke length. It changes the angular position of the helix, so the spill port opens earlier or later.
  • Confusing the delivery valve with the injector nozzle. The delivery valve is at the pump outlet and holds residual line pressure; the nozzle atomises fuel in the chamber.
  • Assuming a distributor pump feeds all cylinders at the same instant. The rotor lines up with one delivery union at a time in firing order.
  • Forgetting that a unit injector still needs timing marks. The cam lobe, rocker clearance or shims set injection timing even though there is no long high-pressure pipe.

2.4 Common rail direct injection (CRDI)

A common rail stores diesel at high pressure in one pipe and lets an electronic control unit fire each injector when it wants. The high-pressure pump now feeds a rail continuously, so pressure generation is separated from the injection event. Rail pressure and injection timing become independent settings. This gives fine sprays, quiet combustion and low emissions. It is used in cars, buses, trucks and tractors, and in petrol direct-injection engines with a similar rail.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for what an injection system must do, and Chapter 2.3 for why mechanical pumps tie pressure to engine speed.

The layout: from tank to rail

Start at the tank. A lift pump, usually electric, pushes diesel at a few bar through a filter to the high-pressure pump. The filter matters: the pump and injectors have clearances of a few micrometres, and a particle that size can jam an injector. The high-pressure pump is driven by the engine. It raises fuel to the rail pressure and sends it into the rail. The rail is a thick pipe that acts as an accumulator, so all injectors see the same stored pressure.

A pressure sensor on the rail reports to the electronic control unit (ECU). A pressure control valve, on the pump or on the rail, opens to bleed fuel back to the tank and hold the set pressure. The ECU then commands each injector to open. The injector may use a solenoid or a piezo stack. Some syllabi call this common rail diesel injection.

  1. Tank stores diesel at low pressure.
  2. Lift pump sends fuel through the filter.
  3. High-pressure pump raises fuel to rail pressure.
  4. Rail stores the high-pressure fuel for all cylinders.
  5. Pressure sensor and control valve keep rail pressure at the ECU set point.
  6. ECU opens each injector at the calculated time.

Two settings: pressure and open time

Rail pressure is set by the pump output and the pressure control valve. Injection timing is set by when the ECU opens an injector. Engine speed does not force the injection start, as it does with a jerk pump. Rail pressure is typically 1000 to 2000 bar, which is 100 to 200 MPa. The ECU reads speed, load, air mass, coolant temperature and rail pressure, then looks up the pressure and timing for that operating point.

For a fixed nozzle, volume per injection rises with injector open time and with the square root of the pressure difference across the nozzle. Fuel quantity is therefore not set by rail pressure alone. The ECU must know both rail pressure and open time. This is the central trap in exams and in calibration.

The ECU can split one injection into several pulses. A pilot injection is a small early pulse. It starts a mild burn, shortens the ignition delay of the main charge, and lowers the rate of pressure rise, so diesel knock and noise fall. The main injection delivers most of the fuel, and therefore most of the work. A post injection comes late, after the main pulse, and is used mainly for exhaust after-treatment, such as heating the exhaust for a diesel particulate filter. Multiple injections per cycle are common.

What it buys, what it costs

  • High rail pressure gives small droplets and fast mixing, so combustion is cleaner.
  • Pilot injection lowers combustion noise and the peak pressure rise.
  • Split injection can lower both nitrogen oxides and soot. Retarded timing lowers nitrogen oxides but tends to increase soot, so the ECU trades one against the other.
  • Cold start and altitude operation can be recalibrated without changing hardware.
  • Limits: the high-pressure pump, sensor, control valve and ECU add cost and complexity.
  • The pump takes engine power, filtration must be fine, and injector deposits can disturb the spray.

The system is flexible but not free. A mechanic needs diagnostic tools, and a weak battery or a bad sensor can stop the engine. The gain is a diesel that is quieter, cleaner and easier to calibrate than a mechanical pump line nozzle system.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke diesel engine runs at 3000 rpm. Each injector delivers 30 mm³ per injection. Find the fuel flow in L/h.

  1. A four-stroke cylinder fires once every two revolutions. Injections per cylinder per minute = 3000 ÷ 2 = 1500.
  2. Total injections per minute = 4 cylinders × 1500 = 6000.
  3. Volume per minute = 30 mm³ × 6000 = 180000 mm³/min.
  4. Convert to cm³: 180000 ÷ 1000 = 180 cm³/min.
  5. Convert to litres: 180 ÷ 1000 = 0.18 L/min.
  6. Convert to hours: 0.18 × 60 = 10.8 L/h.

Answer. The fuel flow is 10.8 L/h.

Exam traps

  • Saying rail pressure alone sets injection quantity. Quantity depends on rail pressure and injector open time together.
  • Treating common rail as a petrol-only system. This chapter is diesel common rail; petrol direct injection uses a similar rail but different mixture needs.
  • Thinking pilot injection adds power. The pilot pulse mainly reduces ignition delay and noise; the main pulse does the work.
  • Calling post injection a late power pulse only. Its main job is exhaust after-treatment, such as diesel particulate filter regeneration.
  • Assuming higher rail pressure is always better. It improves spray but raises pump work, nitrogen oxides and wear, so the ECU balances it.

2.5 Electronic fuel injection: mono-point and multi-point

Electronic fuel injection (EFI) replaces the carburettor with a control loop. Sensors measure air flow, throttle angle, coolant temperature, exhaust oxygen and crank position. An electronic control unit (ECU) turns these signals into an injector pulse width. A fuel pump and pressure regulator keep fuel pressure fixed, so pulse width sets the fuel mass. EFI is used on almost every new petrol car, motorcycle and scooter in India because it holds the air-fuel ratio close to stoichiometric and cuts emissions.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for injection requirements and types, and Chapter 2.4 for the high-pressure rail idea behind direct injection.

Sensors, ECU and injectors

The loop has five parts. Sensors turn physical quantities into voltages. The ECU is a microprocessor with lookup tables and correction maps. Injectors are solenoid valves (some newer ones are piezo) that open when current flows. The fuel pump supplies more fuel than the engine needs. The pressure regulator returns the excess to the tank and holds delivery pressure constant, commonly about 3 bar for port injection. Because pressure is fixed, the ECU meters fuel only by changing injector open time, called pulse width.

  • Air flow sensor: hot-wire or hot-film type measures intake air mass; a manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor is used instead on speed-density systems.
  • Throttle position sensor (TPS): a potentiometer or Hall sensor reports idle, part load and wide-open throttle.
  • Coolant temperature sensor: a thermistor enriches the mixture when the engine is cold.
  • Crank position sensor: a magnetic or Hall pickup on the flywheel gives engine speed and piston position, so the ECU knows when to inject.
  • Oxygen sensor: a zirconia cell in the exhaust gives a voltage that switches either side of stoichiometric, about 14.7:1 by mass for petrol.

Open loop means the ECU uses tables only, with no oxygen feedback. It runs open loop during cold start, hard acceleration and wide-open throttle. Closed loop means the oxygen sensor trims the pulse width to hold the mixture near stoichiometric. The trim has a short-term correction and a long-term learned correction.

Mono-point: one injector at the throttle body

Mono-point EFI uses one injector mounted above the throttle plate in a throttle body. The injector sprays fuel into the incoming air, and the mixture travels through the intake manifold to all cylinders. Some syllabi call this single-point or throttle-body injection (TBI).

Fuel pressure is low, about 1 bar above manifold pressure, and the injector fires at a fixed frequency, often once per engine revolution or twice per cycle. The ECU still uses the same sensors and oxygen feedback. Because fuel is delivered at one central point, cylinder-to-cylinder distribution depends on manifold geometry, so the mixture can be uneven. Wall wetting on a cold manifold also delays fuel response. Mono-point was a cheap retrofit for carburettors in the 1980s and 1990s. It is rare on new cars; multi-point and direct injection have replaced it.

Multi-point: one injector per port

Multi-point EFI (MPFI) has one injector per cylinder, mounted in the intake port just upstream of the inlet valve. Each injector sprays onto the back of the valve, so the fuel stays in the port and enters the cylinder as the valve opens. Port injection is another name. Fuel pressure is usually 3 to 4 bar.

Firing can be simultaneous, batch or sequential. In simultaneous firing all injectors open together once per revolution. In batch firing injectors are grouped, for example two groups that alternate. In sequential firing each injector opens just before its own cylinder's intake stroke. Sequential gives the best fuel control and cold-start behaviour but needs a cam or crank sensor that identifies the cylinder.

Compared with mono-point, MPFI gives better distribution, faster response and lower wall wetting. The intake manifold carries only air, so it can be tuned for air flow. The ECU can also cut fuel to one injector for a misfire or over-speed limit. MPFI is the common layout on Indian petrol cars and motorcycles.

Gasoline direct injection

Gasoline direct injection (GDI) puts the injector inside the combustion chamber, so fuel is sprayed directly into the cylinder. The fuel system runs at high pressure, typically 50 to 200 bar, because the injector must overcome cylinder pressure and still atomise. A high-pressure pump is driven by the camshaft; a common rail feeds the injectors, similar in idea to CRDI for diesel.

GDI can run homogeneous or stratified. In homogeneous charge the fuel is injected early in the intake stroke and mixed with all the air, giving a uniform mixture like port injection. In stratified charge the fuel is injected late in the compression stroke, so a rich cloud sits near the spark plug and the rest of the cylinder stays lean. Stratified operation saves fuel at part load but needs precise spray and timing.

Direct injection avoids fuel wetting the port walls and allows a higher compression ratio because the charge is cooled by evaporation. It also has problems: carbon deposits on intake valves, particulate matter, and more complex high-pressure hardware. GDI is not the same as port injection: the injector location and pressure are different.

Worked example

Question. A port injector flows 240 cm³/min when fully open. The ECU holds it open for 5 ms. Find the fuel volume delivered in one injection in cm³ and mm³.

  1. Convert flow: 240 cm³/min ÷ 60 s/min = 4 cm³/s.
  2. Convert open time: 5 ms = 5 × 10⁻³ s = 0.005 s.
  3. Volume = flow × time = 4 cm³/s × 0.005 s = 0.02 cm³.
  4. Convert: 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³, so 0.02 cm³ = 20 mm³.

Answer. Fuel delivered per injection is 0.02 cm³, which is 20 mm³.

Exam traps

  • Mono-point does not mean one injector per cylinder. It means one injector in total, at the throttle body; multi-point has one per cylinder at the ports.
  • GDI is direct injection into the cylinder at high pressure, not port injection. Port pressure is about 3 bar; GDI pressure is tens to hundreds of bar.
  • Pulse width sets fuel mass only because pressure is held constant. If the pressure regulator fails, the same pulse width gives a different fuel mass.
  • Closed loop needs a hot oxygen sensor. A cold engine runs open loop, so the ECU ignores the oxygen signal during warm-up.
  • Sequential injection needs cylinder identification, not just crank speed. Batch and simultaneous firing can run from a crank sensor alone.

2.6 Injection nozzles, spray formation and spray penetration

The nozzle is the last part of the fuel system that touches the fuel. It turns high pressure into a spray of tiny droplets, gives the spray a direction and a shape, and sets how far the spray reaches into the cylinder before it mixes with air. Hole size, hole number, opening pressure and injection pressure decide atomisation, penetration and cone angle. Every diesel engine's smoke, starting and fuel economy depends on getting this small part right.

You need: rail and injection pressure from chapter 2.4, and injector construction from chapter 2.5.

Nozzle types, opening pressure and sac volume

The nozzle is a small spring-loaded valve at the injector tip. A hardened steel needle sits on a conical seat. One or more holes are drilled just below the seat. Fuel from the pump or rail pushes on the needle. When fuel pressure times the needle area exceeds the spring force, the needle lifts and fuel escapes through the holes. The pressure at which this happens is the opening pressure, fixed by the spring. Diesel nozzles commonly open between 100 bar and 250 bar, with direct-injection engines at the higher end.

  • Pintle nozzle: a pin (the pintle) passes through a single hole, leaving an annular gap. It gives a hollow-cone spray with short penetration. Older small direct-injection and indirect-injection engines use it.
  • Single-hole nozzle: one plain hole. Simple and cheap, used in small engines and some indirect-injection chambers.
  • Multi-hole nozzle: 3 to 8 holes, each 0.15 mm to 0.4 mm across, each aimed at a different part of the chamber. This is the standard choice for direct-injection diesel. Some syllabi call it a multi-orifice nozzle.
  • Throttle nozzle: a pintle shaped so the flow area stays small at low needle lift. It slows the first part of injection, so less fuel collects during the ignition delay. This softens the pressure rise and reduces diesel knock.

Below the needle seat is a small cavity called the sac volume. Fuel fills it while the needle is open, and the needle traps it when it closes. This trapped fuel cannot be timed, so it evaporates late in the cycle and burns badly, adding unburnt hydrocarbons and smoke. Nozzles of the VCO type (valve covered orifice) let the needle cover the holes directly and remove the sac.

Nozzle tests: pop tester and leak test

A nozzle is tested with a pop tester: a hand pump with a pressure gauge and a holder that takes the nozzle. No fuel line from the engine is needed.

  1. Clamp the nozzle in the holder and fill the tester with clean diesel.
  2. Pump slowly and watch the gauge. The pressure at which the needle lifts and fuel sprays out is the opening pressure. Compare it with the maker's value.
  3. Watch the spray: it should be a fine, even mist from every hole, with the same shape on each. A solid stream, a drip or a missing hole means a worn or blocked nozzle.
  4. Do the leak test: pump up to about 10 bar to 20 bar below the opening pressure and hold. The gauge should stay nearly steady for the stated time. A fast fall means the needle and seat leak.

A clean buzzing or chattering sound during the pop is good, because it shows the needle is vibrating on its seat and not sticking. Keep fingers away from the spray: fuel at these pressures can pierce skin.

Spray formation, droplet size, cone angle and penetration

Atomisation is the breaking of the liquid jet into droplets. A jet leaving a small hole is unstable: air drag and turbulence tear it into ligaments, and the ligaments into drops. Small drops evaporate fast, so atomisation controls how quickly the fuel turns into vapour that can burn.

Droplet size is reported as the Sauter mean diameter (SMD, symbol D₃₂). It is the diameter of a drop whose volume-to-surface ratio equals that of the whole spray. A small SMD means fine atomisation. Diesel sprays often have SMD in the range of 10 μm to 30 μm.

ṁ = Cd A √(2 ρ Δp)

ṁ is the fuel mass flow through the nozzle in kg/s; Cd is the discharge coefficient (about 0.7, dimensionless); A is the total flow area of all holes in m²; ρ is fuel density in kg/m³ (about 830 for diesel); Δp is the pressure drop across the holes in Pa.

  • Cone angle: the full angle between the spray edges, often 10° to 25° for multi-hole nozzles. It depends on hole shape and length-to-diameter ratio, and narrows slightly as air density rises.
  • Penetration: how far the spray tip travels before it is fully broken up and mixed. It must match the chamber. Too short and the outer air is unused; too long and fuel wets the wall, causing smoke and oil dilution.
  • Higher injection pressure gives smaller droplets, faster mixing and longer penetration, up to a limit.
  • Smaller holes give finer spray but less flow, so injection must last longer or more holes are needed.
  • More holes at the same total area means smaller holes: better atomisation but weaker individual jets and less penetration.
  • Higher air density (turbocharged engines) raises drag on the drops, so penetration shortens and mixing improves.

Worked example

Question. A diesel nozzle has 5 holes, each of diameter 0.25 mm. Find the flow area of one hole and the total flow area of the nozzle, in m² and in mm².

  1. Convert the diameter to metres: d = 0.25 mm = 0.25 × 10⁻³ m = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  2. Radius r = d/2 = 1.25 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  3. Area of one hole A₁ = π r² = π × (1.25 × 10⁻⁴)² = π × 1.5625 × 10⁻⁸ = 4.909 × 10⁻⁸ m².
  4. Total flow area A = 5 × A₁ = 5 × 4.909 × 10⁻⁸ = 2.454 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  5. Convert to mm²: 2.454 × 10⁻⁷ m² × 10⁶ mm²/m² = 0.245 mm².

Answer. One hole has an area of 4.909 × 10⁻⁸ m² and the nozzle total flow area is 2.454 × 10⁻⁷ m², that is 0.245 mm².

Exam traps

  • Putting the diameter straight into A = π d² and forgetting the divide by 4. A 0.25 mm hole gives 4.909 × 10⁻⁸ m², not 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  • Mixing opening pressure with injection pressure. Opening pressure is set by the nozzle spring (the needle lift point, around 100–250 bar). Injection pressure is the pump or rail pressure, which is much higher.
  • Saying more holes always means a better spray. More holes at the same total area make each hole smaller: atomisation improves, but each jet is weaker, penetrates less, and jets may merge in a small chamber.
  • Confusing pintle and throttle nozzles. Both have a pin in the hole; only the throttle nozzle's pin is shaped to throttle the flow at low needle lift.
  • Forgetting sac volume. The fuel trapped below the needle seat after closing burns late and adds hydrocarbons and smoke, so a worn sac is a real fault, not a detail.

Area per hole falls to one quarter (A ∝ d²). Penetration per jet gets weaker because the jet carries less momentum.

2.7 Governors

A governor is a closed-loop speed controller. It measures engine speed, compares it with a set value, and moves the fuel rack to keep speed nearly constant as load changes. A petrol engine uses a throttle; a diesel has no throttle, so the only easy way to hold speed is to change fuel delivery. Governors appear on generator sets, tractor engines, pump sets and older truck diesels. This chapter covers mechanical centrifugal and pneumatic governors, droop, isochronous running and stability.

You need: the fuel rack from Chapter 2.3 and engine speed and load from Unit 1.

Why a diesel engine needs a governor

Petrol engines use a throttle plate. The driver sets air flow, and the mixture drawn in sets power. A diesel has no throttle: at a given speed each cycle takes nearly the same air. Power is set only by the fuel injected per cycle.

Without a governor, a sudden load drop leaves surplus fuel in the cycle. The engine accelerates until the load matches the fuel or something fails. A governor is an automatic speed controller: it senses speed and moves the fuel rack.

The rack is the control rod of the injection pump (Chapter 2.3). Rack position sets delivered fuel per cycle at a given speed. The governor does not set a fuel quantity directly. It sets speed by changing fuel; the load then decides the quantity needed to hold that speed.

Signal path: spring tension sets target speed. The governor senses actual speed. The error moves the rack. The engine responds. Speed feeds back.

Mechanical centrifugal governor

Two flyballs are carried on arms and driven by the engine, often by a belt. The balls rotate at a speed proportional to engine speed. Centrifugal force pushes them outward. Their sleeve moves against a spring. The sleeve position is linked to the fuel rack through a lever. Some syllabi call this the mechanical governor.

At steady speed, the outward force of the balls balances the spring force at one radius. That radius fixes the sleeve position and so the rack position. If load falls, speed rises, balls move out, and the sleeve moves the rack to cut fuel. If load rises, speed falls, the spring pushes the balls inward, and the rack increases fuel.

F = mω²r

F is centrifugal force on one ball, in N; m is ball mass, in kg; ω is angular speed, in rad/s; r is ball radius from the spindle, in m.

Because F depends on ω², doubling speed gives four times the force. A small speed error produces a useful rack movement.

Pneumatic, hydraulic and electronic governors

A pneumatic governor senses speed through air flow, not flyballs. A butterfly valve in the intake is set by the accelerator. Air passing the partly closed butterfly creates a pressure drop. The drop grows as engine speed rises.

One side of a diaphragm senses this lower pressure; a spring pushes the other side. The diaphragm is linked to the fuel rack. At the set speed, vacuum force balances spring force. If speed rises, vacuum rises, and the diaphragm moves the rack to reduce fuel. If speed falls, the spring moves the rack to increase fuel.

The butterfly position sets the target speed, and the governor holds it. Pneumatic governors are simple and cheap. They are sensitive to air-filter restriction and are used on small diesel engines.

Hydraulic governors use engine oil pressure and a pilot valve to move the rack with more force. Electronic governors use a speed sensor, an ECU and an actuator; they give easy isochronous control and diagnostics. Details of electronic management are in Chapter 2.5.

Droop, isochronous operation and stability

Droop, also called speed regulation, is the fall in speed from no load to full load at a fixed setting. It is written:

δ = (N<sub>nl</sub> − N<sub>fl</sub>) / N<sub>fl</sub> × 100%

δ is percentage droop; Nnl is no-load speed, in rpm; Nfl is full-load speed, in rpm.

For example, 1500 rpm no load and 1470 rpm full load gives δ = 30 / 1470 × 100 = 2.0%. Generator governors commonly run at 3% to 5% droop.

Droop lets two generators share load. Each set has a speed-load line. If one set takes more load, its speed falls, and the other set picks up load. Without droop, two isochronous sets fight: each tries to hold the same speed and one may take all the load.

Isochronous means zero droop: the governor holds the same speed from no load to full load. It suits a single generator or a single engine driving a pump. It needs a sensitive governor with damping.

Stability matters. Too much governor gain causes overshoot and hunting. Too little gain gives slow recovery. Linkage play, spring friction and a worn rack add dead band. A hydraulic relay or electronic control reduces hunting.

Worked example

Question. A centrifugal governor has one ball of mass 0.1 kg at radius 50 mm. It rotates at 1200 rpm. Find the angular speed and the centrifugal force on one ball.

  1. Convert radius: r = 50 mm = 0.050 m.
  2. Convert speed: ω = 2πN/60 = 2π(1200)/60 = 125.7 rad/s.
  3. Square: ω² = (125.7)² = 1.58 × 10⁴ s⁻².
  4. Force: F = mω²r = 0.1 × 15791 × 0.050 = 78.96 N.
  5. Round: F ≈ 79.0 N.

Answer. ω = 125.7 rad/s and F = 79.0 N.

Exam traps

  • Saying the governor sets fuel quantity directly. It moves the rack; load and speed decide the matching quantity.
  • Using droop denominator as no-load speed. The usual formula divides by full-load (rated) speed.
  • Taking ω = 2πN with N in rpm. You must divide rpm by 60 first.
  • Calling isochronous a guarantee of fixed speed at every instant. It holds steady speed; transient dips still occur.

2.8 Ignition systems: battery and magneto

A petrol engine needs a spark at the right plug at the right moment. Battery-coil ignition stores energy in a coil's magnetic field and releases it as a high-voltage pulse when a contact breaker opens. Magneto ignition does the same job but generates its own current from a rotating magnet, so it needs no battery. Both systems appear on small engines, older cars and motorcycles, and aircraft piston engines; electronic ignition now replaces the contact breaker in most new cars.

You need: the SI engine cycle from Unit 1 and the sensor-ECU idea from 2.5.

Battery-coil ignition: parts and path

The battery supplies 12 V direct current. The ignition switch connects it to the primary winding, a few hundred turns of thick copper wire. Current rises and builds a magnetic field in the iron core. The contact breaker is a mechanical switch driven by the engine camshaft; it stays closed while the field builds. A small condenser sits across the breaker points.

When the breaker opens, primary current falls quickly. The magnetic field collapses. The changing field links the secondary winding, which has thousands of turns of fine wire on the same core. Mutual induction produces a high voltage, often 15 kV to 30 kV, enough to jump the spark plug gap. The condenser limits arcing at the points and helps the field collapse faster.

The distributor is a rotary switch. It sends the secondary voltage to the plug of the cylinder ready to fire, in the engine's firing order. The spark then ignites the compressed mixture. Some syllabi call battery-coil ignition coil ignition.

E = ½ L I²

E is energy stored in the primary magnetic field in joules (J); L is primary inductance in henry (H); I is primary current in ampere (A) just before the breaker opens.

Magneto ignition: making its own current

A magneto has a permanent magnet and a coil on the same shaft. As the engine turns the magnet or the armature, the magnetic flux through the primary coil changes. This induces a current in the primary winding. The contact breaker interrupts this current at the firing point, and the secondary winding produces the high-voltage spark. A condenser across the breaker works as in battery-coil ignition.

No battery, no ignition switch supply and no separate charging circuit are needed for the spark. That is why magnetos are common on small petrol engines such as chainsaws, lawn mowers and some motorcycles, and on aircraft piston engines. The spark energy comes from mechanical rotation, so a magneto must be turned fast enough to make a good spark. At very low cranking speed the output can be weak; an impulse coupling or a separate starting vibrator may be added on some engines.

The distributor, where fitted, sends the high voltage to each plug in turn. A single-cylinder engine can use the same coil and breaker without a distributor.

Battery-coil versus magneto

Battery-coil and magneto systems both use an induction coil and a breaker, but they suit different duties.

A flat battery stops a battery-coil engine. A magneto engine can be hand-started without one. On cars, battery-coil ignition dominated before electronic systems because the battery is already present for starting, lighting and charging.

Electronic ignition replaces the contact breaker

The contact breaker is the weak part: points burn, gap drifts and rubbing blocks wear. Electronic ignition replaces it with a sensor and a transistor or an ECU. A Hall-effect or inductive sensor reports crank position. The ECU switches the primary current through a power transistor, so no mechanical points open the circuit. The coil, distributor and spark plugs stay, unless the system uses coil-on-plug.

Electronic control gives a clean current cut, higher secondary voltage and no point wear. It also lets the ECU change dwell (the time the primary current is switched on) and timing with speed and load. That timing work belongs to 2.9.

Worked example

Question. A coil has primary inductance L = 6 mH and current I = 4 A at the instant the contact breaker opens. Find the energy stored in the primary magnetic field.

  1. Write the data with consistent units: L = 6 mH = 6 × 10⁻³ H = 0.006 H, I = 4 A.
  2. Use the stored-energy formula for an inductor: E = ½ L I².
  3. Square the current: I² = 4² = 16 A².
  4. Multiply: E = 0.5 × 0.006 H × 16 A² = 0.048 J.
  5. Convert to millijoule: 0.048 J × 1000 = 48 mJ.

Answer. The stored energy is 0.048 J, or 48 mJ.

Exam traps

  • Do not say a magneto needs a battery. A magneto makes its own primary current from a rotating magnet.
  • Primary current, about 4 A, is not the spark current. The secondary sends a short high-voltage pulse at low current.
  • The contact breaker interrupts the primary circuit, not the secondary. The secondary voltage appears because the primary field collapses.
  • A distributor does not make the spark. It routes the coil's high voltage to the correct plug.
  • The condenser is not only a radio filter. It reduces point arcing and helps the primary current fall fast.

2.9 Ignition timing and spark advance

Spark timing is the instant the spark plug fires. Spark advance, also called ignition advance, is how many crank degrees before top dead centre (TDC) the spark occurs. The spark must start combustion early enough that peak pressure arrives just after TDC. As speed rises, the flame covers more crank degrees, so advance must rise. At high load, advance must fall to avoid knock. Only a few universities teach this chapter; every petrol engine uses the idea.

You need Chapter 2.8 and Unit 3 for knock.

Spark timing relative to TDC

Top dead centre (TDC) is the piston position at the end of compression. Spark timing is measured in crank degrees from TDC. Spark advance θ is the angle before TDC at which the spark fires. A 10° advance means the spark fires 10 crank degrees before TDC.

Combustion takes time. The flame grows from the plug and raises pressure. Peak pressure should arrive about 10° to 15° after TDC for best work. So the spark must start before TDC. If it is late, the piston is already moving down and pressure is wasted.

At high speed a fixed flame time covers more crank degrees, so the spark must move earlier. A sample linear schedule is 1000 rpm: 10°, 2000 rpm: 20°, 3000 rpm: 30°, 4000 rpm: 40°. Real maps are curves, but the trend is clear.

Too much advance puts peak pressure before TDC; the piston fights the flame and knock can damage the engine. Too little advance puts pressure late; power falls, fuel use rises and exhaust temperature rises.

Advance mechanisms and checks

Older distributors used centrifugal and vacuum advance. Centrifugal weights move outward with speed and add advance. The vacuum canister senses manifold vacuum, high at part load, and adds advance because the lean mixture burns slowly. At wide-open throttle vacuum falls, so vacuum advance drops and spark retards for high load.

Modern engines use an electronic control unit (ECU) with a spark map of advance against speed and load. Load comes from manifold absolute pressure (MAP) or air mass flow. A knock sensor, a piezo accelerometer on the block, detects knock; the ECU retards a few degrees, then advances back slowly.

  1. Warm the engine and set the specified idle speed.
  2. Disconnect and plug the vacuum hose if the maker states it.
  3. Connect a timing light to number one cylinder.
  4. Aim the light at the crank pulley mark and read advance.
  5. Compare the reading with the specification.

High load needs retard to avoid knock. Low load needs more advance because the mixture is lean and slow. Spark advance is scheduled, never fixed.

Worked example

Question. A linear spark schedule gives 10° advance at 1000 rpm and 30° at 3000 rpm. Find the slope in degrees per rpm and the advance at 2500 rpm.

  1. Given N₀ = 1000 rpm, θ₀ = 10°, N₁ = 3000 rpm, θ₁ = 30°.
  2. Slope = (30° − 10°)/(3000 − 1000) rpm = 20°/2000 rpm = 0.01° per rpm.
  3. θ = 10° + (2500 − 1000) × 0.01 = 10° + 15° = 25°.

Answer. The slope is 0.01° per rpm and the advance at 2500 rpm is 25°.

Exam traps

  • Advance is not fixed; high load needs retard, even at the same speed, to avoid knock.
  • 10° advance means 10° before TDC, not after.
  • Centrifugal advance follows speed; vacuum advance follows load through manifold vacuum.
  • For a timing-light check, follow the maker's idle-speed and vacuum-hose instructions.
  • Knock-sensor retard is temporary protection; the ECU then advances back.

Takeaways

  1. A carburettor cannot meet all demands with one fuel passage, so separate idle, main, power and acceleration circuits switch in as airflow rises.
  2. A fuel injection system must meter, atomise, distribute and time the fuel; the pump only pressurises and times, the nozzle does the atomising.
  3. In a jerk pump the helical control edge on the plunger sets the delivered quantity by deciding when spill begins.
  4. Common rail separates pressure generation from injection, so injection pressure can be chosen for each operating point, and split injections become possible.
  5. Electronic fuel injection replaced mechanical metering because the ECU can hold the air-fuel ratio close to target from cold start to full load.
  6. Nozzle hole size and number trade atomisation against penetration; small holes atomise well but penetrate poorly, which matters in a deep bowl.
  7. A governor is a closed-loop speed controller: it compares actual speed with the set speed and corrects the fuel rack, always with some droop.
  8. Battery-coil ignition gets its energy from the battery, magneto ignition makes its own; both rely on the collapsing field of an induction coil.
  9. Spark advance is scheduled so peak cylinder pressure lands near 10–15° after TDC; too much advance and too slow a flame both lead to knock.

Glossary

Ballast resistor
Resistor in series with the primary winding that limits running current and is bypassed during cranking for a stronger spark.
Choke valve
Butterfly above the venturi that is closed during cold starting to raise manifold vacuum and draw extra fuel.
Delivery valve
Non-return valve on a pump outlet that keeps the high-pressure pipe full and makes the pressure fall sharply at the end of injection.
Distributor pump
Rotary diesel pump with one pumping plunger that sends fuel to each cylinder in turn through a rotating distributor head.
Dwell angle
Crank angle for which the primary circuit stays closed and current builds in the coil before the spark.
ECU
Electronic control unit; the computer that reads sensors, calculates the fuel and spark demands, and drives the injectors and coil.
Float chamber
Small fuel bowl in a carburettor kept at constant level by a float and needle valve, so jet flow depends only on venturi suction.
Governor droop
Small fall in engine speed from no load to full load in a governed engine, caused by the need for a finite sleeve movement.
Hole-type nozzle
Nozzle whose spray comes from one or more drilled holes in the tip, giving fine atomisation and long penetration.
Ignition coil
Two-winding induction coil whose primary current collapse induces a high secondary voltage across the plug gap.
Injection lag
Short delay between the start of pump delivery and the moment the nozzle actually opens, caused by pressure-wave travel and nozzle opening pressure.
Injector
Electrically or mechanically opened valve that delivers a metered, atomised charge of fuel.
Knock sensor
Piezoelectric accelerometer bolted to the block that detects the characteristic vibration of knock so the ECU can retard spark.
Main jet
Calibrated hole that limits fuel flow from the float chamber into the main well and venturi.
Nozzle chatter
Rapid opening and closing of the needle seen on a nozzle tester, taken as a sign of correct seating and free movement.
Nozzle opening pressure
Pressure at which the spring-loaded needle lifts off its seat and fuel flow begins.
Piezo injector
Injector opened by a stack of piezo crystals that expand when charged; it switches faster than a solenoid and allows closely spaced injections.
Pneumatic governor
Governor in which the venturi vacuum of the intake air acts on a diaphragm to move the fuel rack, used on small petrol engines.
Rail pressure
The pressure held in the common rail, measured by a sensor and held by a control valve; typically several hundred to over two thousand bar.
Spark plug gap
Air gap between the centre and earth electrodes, typically 0.8 to 1.1 mm, which sets the voltage needed to spark.
Split injection
Dividing one injection event into pilot, main and post pulses to control ignition delay, noise, NOx and soot.
Stoichiometric air-fuel ratio
The chemically exact ratio at which fuel burns completely in air, about 14.7:1 by mass for petrol and 14.5:1 for diesel.
Throttle body injection
Mono-point electronic injection with one or two injectors mounted above the throttle plate, replacing the carburettor.
Throttle nozzle
Nozzle with a flattened pin that changes spray direction and speed with needle lift, used to soften early injection.
