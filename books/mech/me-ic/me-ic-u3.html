3.1 Stages of combustion and flame propagation in SI engines

A spark-ignition (SI) engine burns a fuel–air mixture that was mixed before the spark. The spark starts a small flame at the plug, the flame grows into a thin front that sweeps across the chamber, and the last pockets of charge burn slowly during expansion. Textbooks split this into three stages: ignition lag, flame propagation and afterburning. You see all three on the cylinder pressure–crank angle (p–θ) diagram, and that diagram is how engineers set spark timing and judge whether combustion is healthy.

You need: the four-stroke cycle, top dead centre (TDC) and crank angle, and how the spark is timed (ignition systems).

The three stages on the p–θ diagram

Plot cylinder pressure p against crank angle θ, measured from TDC, with angles before TDC (bTDC) negative. Draw two curves: the firing curve with combustion, and the motoring curve, the pressure when the engine is turned over with no combustion. The gap between them is the pressure that combustion adds. Three points mark the stages.

  • A, the spark. The spark jumps at a set angle before TDC, for example 28° bTDC.
  • B, first pressure rise. The firing curve leaves the motoring curve. Between A and B lies stage I, the ignition lag (some books call it the preparation phase). A small flame kernel forms and grows around the plug gap, but it is too small to move the pressure.
  • C, peak pressure. Between B and C lies stage II, flame propagation. The flame front sweeps the chamber and burns most of the charge, so pressure climbs steeply even though the piston has started down.
  • After C comes stage III, afterburning. The charge near the walls, in crevices and behind the front finishes burning while the gas expands, so the curve falls.

Ignition lag is a chemical and physical delay: radicals must build up and the kernel must grow before burning becomes self-sustaining. It lasts about 1 to 2 ms in a normal engine. It is shortest near the chemically correct, slightly rich mixture and becomes longer with lean mixtures, residual exhaust gas, low temperature and a weak spark.

Flame propagation and the mass burned

Once the kernel is about a few millimetres across, a flame front grows out of it. The front is a thin reaction zone, from a fraction of a millimetre to about a millimetre thick, with burnt gas behind it and fresh charge ahead. Turbulence wrinkles the front, which raises its area and the rate at which it eats into the fresh charge.

The burnt gas is three to four times less dense than the unburnt charge at the same pressure. So the burning gas expands, pushes the unburnt charge ahead of it and squeezes it. This squeezing is why the last charge to burn, the end gas, gets hot before the flame reaches it; that matters for knock (see abnormal combustion).

Laboratory engines describe the burn by the mass fraction burned xb, from 0 at the spark to 1 at the end. Two angles summarise it: the flame development angle, spark to 10 % burned, which matches stage I and the start of stage II; and the rapid burning angle, 10 % to 90 % burned, which is the bulk of stage II. Peak pressure normally falls at about 12° to 18° after TDC (aTDC); a peak much earlier means too much spark advance, much later means too little.

Why spark advance must change with speed

Ignition lag is roughly a fixed time, in milliseconds, set by chemistry. Flame propagation, in contrast, speeds up almost in proportion to engine speed, because the turbulence that drives it scales with piston speed. So stage II takes about the same number of crank degrees at any speed, but stage I takes more degrees as the engine runs faster.

To keep peak pressure near 15° aTDC, the spark must therefore be advanced as speed rises. That is the job of the spark advance map in the engine control unit, and of the centrifugal advance in older distributors (see ignition timing and spark advance). Load matters too: at part throttle the charge is thinner and diluted by more residual gas, the lag grows, and the spark is advanced further.

Read the three stages as a checklist when an engine runs badly. A long lag points to a weak spark, a lean mixture or heavy dilution. Slow propagation puts the peak late and loses work. Heavy afterburning raises the exhaust temperature and the unburnt hydrocarbons. Flame speed and the slope of the pressure rise are taken up in flame velocity and rate of pressure rise.

Worked example

Question. An SI engine (bore 86 mm, stroke 86 mm, compression ratio 9) runs at 3000 rev/min with the spark at 28° bTDC. The ignition lag is 1.0 ms. The measured trace peaks at 50.3 bar at 15.6° aTDC. (a) Find the lag in crank degrees and the angle at which pressure first rises. (b) Find the duration of flame propagation in degrees and in milliseconds. (c) At 4500 rev/min the lag is still 1.0 ms and stage II still ends at 15.6° aTDC. What spark timing keeps the first pressure rise at the same angle?

  1. Degrees per millisecond at 3000 rev/min: 0.006 × 3000 = 18 °/ms.
  2. Lag in degrees: θ = 18 × 1.0 = 18°.
  3. Start the count at the spark: −28° + 18° = −10°. Pressure first rises at 10° bTDC (point B).
  4. Stage II runs from −10° to +15.6°: 15.6 − (−10) = 25.6°.
  5. In time: 25.6 ÷ 18 = 1.42 ms.
  6. At 4500 rev/min: 0.006 × 4500 = 27 °/ms, so the same 1.0 ms lag is 27°.
  7. For B to stay at −10°, the spark must be at −10° − 27° = −37°, that is 37° bTDC: 9° more advance than at 3000 rev/min.
  8. Check: −37° + 27° = −10°, the same point B as before.

Answer. (a) 18° of lag; pressure first rises at 10° bTDC. (b) Flame propagation lasts 25.6°, or 1.42 ms. (c) Advance the spark to 37° bTDC.

Exam traps

  • The motoring curve is the pressure with no combustion (the engine turned over with the ignition off). Do not call it "the curve with fuel but no spark"; fuel has nothing to do with its definition.
  • Stage I ends at point B, where the firing curve leaves the motoring curve, not when the flame reaches the cylinder wall.
  • In the three-stage picture, flame propagation ends at peak pressure (point C). Do not put peak pressure at the end of afterburning.
  • Crank degrees and milliseconds are different units. A 1.0 ms lag is 18° at 3000 rev/min and 27° at 4500 rev/min.
  • Advancing the spark does not shorten the lag in milliseconds; it starts the same lag earlier so that point B stays where it should.

3.2 Flame velocity, flame front and rate of pressure rise

Flame velocity is how fast the flame front moves through the chamber. Together with the area of the front and the density of the unburnt charge, it fixes the mass burning rate, the kilograms of charge burnt each second. The mass burning rate, in turn, sets the rate of pressure rise, dp/dθ, in bar per degree of crank angle. That slope decides where peak pressure falls, how much work comes out, how rough the engine sounds and how close it runs to knock.

You need: the three stages of SI combustion and the p–θ diagram (stages of combustion).

Flame speed, burning velocity and flame velocity

  • Laminar burning velocity SL: the speed of a smooth flame front relative to the unburnt gas just ahead of it, in still gas. For petrol–air at room conditions it is about 0.3 to 0.5 m/s. It is highest for a slightly rich mixture (equivalence ratio φ ≈ 1.1, where φ is the actual fuel–air ratio divided by the chemically correct one) and rises with the unburnt gas temperature.
  • Turbulent burning velocity ST: the same idea in turbulent gas. Eddies wrinkle the front, so more fresh charge meets the reaction zone each second. A simple estimate is ST ≈ SL + u′, where u′ is the turbulence intensity (the r.m.s. fluctuation of gas velocity). In an engine u′ is about half the mean piston speed, a few m/s, so ST is a few m/s.
  • Flame travel velocity (often just "flame velocity" or "flame speed" in textbooks): the distance the front moves across the chamber divided by the time, measured relative to the cylinder head. It is larger again, of the order of 15 to 40 m/s, because the expanding burnt gas pushes the front outward on top of its burning velocity.

So a 40 mm flame path burnt in 1.4 ms gives a flame travel velocity of 0.040 ÷ 0.0014 ≈ 29 m/s, even though the front burns into the charge at only a few m/s.

Flame front area and the mass burning rate

ṁb = ρu Af Sb

ṁb is the mass burning rate (kg/s); ρu is the density of the unburnt charge (kg/m³); Af is the flame front area (m²); Sb is the burning velocity of the front relative to the unburnt gas (m/s). In a naturally aspirated engine ρu is about 6 kg/m³ at the spark and rises to about 12 to 13 kg/m³ during the main burn, because the charge is compressed.

The flame front area starts near zero at the plug, grows as the kernel becomes a large front, and shrinks again when the front reaches the piston, the head and the far wall. So the burning rate is small at first, peaks in the middle of the burn and tails off at the end: the S-shaped mass fraction burned curve.

This is why chamber shape and plug position matter. A compact chamber with a central plug gives a short flame path and a large front for most of the burn (see combustion chambers).

Rate of pressure rise

The rate of pressure rise is the slope of the p–θ curve, dp/dθ, in bar per degree of crank angle (bar/°CA). Between two measured points the average is (p2 − p1) ÷ (θ2 − θ1). The first law of thermodynamics for the gas in the cylinder links it to the heat released:

Two things follow. The same heat released into a larger volume raises the pressure less, so a burn that happens late, when the piston is far down, gives a gentle slope. And a fast burn near TDC gives a steep slope and a high, early peak. A smooth petrol engine peaks at about 1 to 3 bar/°CA; much steeper slopes make the engine rough and noisy and load the bearings.

The figure compares three burn lengths in one engine. The fast burn gives the highest, earliest peak and the most work, but the roughest running; the slow burn peaks low and late and wastes heat to the exhaust. Good design aims at a burn that is fast but not violent.

What changes flame speed

  • Turbulence: the strongest effect. More turbulence wrinkles the front and raises ST; squish and tumble supply it (see air motion).
  • Fuel–air ratio: fastest slightly rich of stoichiometric; very lean or very rich mixtures burn slowly.
  • Temperature and pressure: a hotter charge burns faster; a higher compression ratio raises both and so raises flame speed.
  • Residual gas and EGR: burnt gas left in or fed back dilutes the charge and slows the flame.
  • Engine speed: flame speed rises almost in proportion to speed through turbulence, so the burn takes about the same crank degrees.
  • Engine size: a larger bore means a longer flame path, so the burn takes more time and more degrees at the same speed.

Worked example

Question. The normal trace in the figure reads 24.9 bar at TDC and 45.4 bar at 10° aTDC. The engine runs at 3000 rev/min. (a) Find the average rate of pressure rise between the two points in bar/°CA. (b) Convert it to bar/ms. (c) The steepest slope of the same trace is 2.3 bar/°CA. Why is the average lower?

  1. Δp = 45.4 − 24.9 = 20.5 bar.
  2. Δθ = 10 − 0 = 10°CA.
  3. Average dp/dθ = 20.5 ÷ 10 = 2.05 bar/°CA.
  4. At 3000 rev/min the crank turns 0.006 × 3000 = 18° each millisecond.
  5. Rate per millisecond = 2.05 × 18 = 36.9 bar/ms.
  6. The average over 10° includes the gentler parts of the curve on either side of the steepest point, which lies near 5° aTDC, so it is below the 2.3 bar/°CA peak.

Answer. (a) 2.05 bar/°CA. (b) 36.9 bar/ms. (c) An average over an interval is always at or below the steepest slope inside it.

Worked example 2

Question. In the same engine (clearance volume 62.4 cm³, a flat disc 10.75 mm deep at TDC) the flame near TDC is a cylinder of radius 35 mm spanning the full depth. The unburnt charge has density 12 kg/m³ and the front burns into it at 6 m/s. The mixture is stoichiometric (air–fuel ratio 14.7, so fuel is 1/15.7 of the charge) and the fuel releases 44 MJ/kg. Estimate the mass burning rate, the heat release per degree at 3000 rev/min, and dp/dθ (take γ = 1.35 and V ≈ clearance volume).

  1. Front area: Af = 2π r h = 2π × 0.035 × 0.01075 = 2.364 × 10⁻³ m².
  2. Mass burning rate: ṁb = 12 × 2.364 × 10⁻³ × 6 = 0.170 kg/s.
  3. Per degree: the crank turns 18 000 °/s, so 0.170 ÷ 18 000 = 9.46 × 10⁻⁶ kg = 9.46 mg of charge per degree.
  4. Fuel per degree: 9.46 ÷ 15.7 = 0.602 mg/°CA.
  5. Heat release: 0.602 × 10⁻⁶ × 44 × 10⁶ = 26.5 J/°CA.
  6. Pressure rise: dp/dθ = 0.35 × 26.5 ÷ (62.4 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.49 × 10⁵ Pa/°CA = 1.49 bar/°CA.
  7. Check: this lies within the 1 to 3 bar/°CA of a smooth engine.

Answer. About 0.170 kg/s of charge burns, releasing 26.5 J/°CA and raising the pressure by about 1.5 bar/°CA.

Exam traps

  • Laminar burning velocity is below 1 m/s; quoting 20 m/s for SL is wrong. Tens of m/s is the flame travel velocity seen from the cylinder head.
  • High flame speed alone does not fix dp/dθ: the front area, the unburnt density and the cylinder volume at that moment all enter.
  • bar/°CA, bar/ms and bar/s are different units: 2.05 bar/°CA is 36.9 bar/ms at 3000 rev/min but 24.6 bar/ms at 2000 rev/min.
  • The slope between two points is an average; the steepest slope lies inside the interval and is higher.
  • A faster burn gives more work only up to a point: past it the engine becomes rough and the end gas, though it has less time, is squeezed harder.

3.3 Gasoline fuel properties and cycle-to-cycle variations

How a petrol engine burns depends first on its fuel. Volatility decides whether the fuel evaporates in time, octane number decides how well it resists knock, and calorific value and the chemically correct air–fuel ratio decide how much energy each cylinder full of charge carries. Even with the same fuel and settings, no two cycles burn alike: peak pressure scatters from cycle to cycle. This chapter covers both ideas, which some syllabi list as "basic fuel properties of gasoline" and "cycle-to-cycle variations".

You need: the stages of SI combustion and the mass fraction burned (stages of combustion), and flame speed (flame velocity).

Gasoline and its properties

Gasoline (petrol) is a blend of a few hundred hydrocarbons, mostly with 4 to 12 carbon atoms: paraffins, iso-paraffins, olefins, naphthenes and aromatics. Indian pump petrol now also carries ethanol, up to 20 % by volume (E20). The properties an engine cares about are these.

Volatility is the readiness to evaporate. It is described by the distillation curve (the temperature at which 10 %, 50 % and 90 % of a sample has boiled off) and by the Reid vapour pressure, measured at 37.8 °C. The front end (10 % point) decides cold starting, and if it is too volatile it causes vapour lock in hot fuel lines. The middle (50 % point) decides warm-up and smooth acceleration. The tail (90 % point) decides whether heavy ends wash past the rings into the oil, leave deposits and waste fuel.

Octane number is the percentage by volume of iso-octane (rated 100) in a blend with n-heptane (rated 0) that knocks like the fuel in a standard test engine. The research method (RON) runs at 600 rev/min with a cool intake; the motor method (MON) runs at 900 rev/min with a hot intake and gives a lower number. The difference RON − MON is the fuel's sensitivity.

Air–fuel ratio, λ and blends

An SI engine runs near λ = 1 for most of its life so that the three-way catalyst works. Maximum power comes slightly rich (φ about 1.1, the same mixture that burns fastest). Best economy comes slightly lean. Too lean, and the flame becomes slow and unreliable, which is where cycle-to-cycle variation takes over.

Ethanol carries oxygen in its molecule, so it needs less air (stoichiometric air–fuel ratio 9.0) and has less energy per kg (about 26.8 MJ/kg). Its octane number is high, so blends resist knock better. The worked example shows why the energy per kg of mixture barely changes.

Cycle-to-cycle variations

At a steady speed, throttle and spark, consecutive traces still do not lie on top of each other. Peak pressure and its crank angle scatter; some cycles burn fast and some slowly. This is cycle-to-cycle variation (cyclic dispersion). The figure shows ten such cycles.

Causes. The early flame kernel is tiny, so whatever happens near the plug gap at the moment of the spark decides how fast the whole cycle burns: the local gas velocity and turbulence, which differ every cycle; the local air–fuel ratio and the amount of residual gas, which are never perfectly uniform; and small differences in spark energy.

Effects. The spark can be set for only the average cycle. Fast cycles then run over-advanced and are the first to knock, so the knock limit is set by the fastest cycles. Slow cycles run retarded and lose work. Variation also limits how lean, how dilute (with EGR) and how high a compression ratio the engine can use,.

Reducing it. Make the early flame fast and robust: more turbulence near the plug at the spark (squish, tumble), a central plug and a compact chamber, a higher spark energy or a wider gap, well-mixed charge with less residual gas, and avoiding very lean or very dilute mixtures.

Worked example

Question. (a) Find the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio of iso-octane, C8H18, taking air as 21 % O2 and 79 % N2 by volume (molar mass of air 28.97). (b) E20 petrol is 20 % ethanol by volume. Take the densities of ethanol and petrol as 789 and 745 kg/m³, stoichiometric air–fuel ratios 9.0 and 14.7, and calorific values 26.8 and 44 MJ/kg. Find the blend's stoichiometric air–fuel ratio and calorific value, and compare the energy per kg of stoichiometric mixture.

  1. Balance: C8H18 + a(O2 + 3.76 N2) → 8 CO2 + 9 H2O + 3.76a N2. Oxygen: 2a = 16 + 9, so a = 12.5.
  2. Air per kmol of fuel: 12.5 × 4.76 = 59.5 kmol, that is 59.5 × 28.97 = 1723.7 kg.
  3. Fuel: 8 × 12.011 + 18 × 1.008 = 114.23 kg. Air–fuel ratio = 1723.7 ÷ 114.23 = 15.09.
  4. Mass fraction of ethanol in E20: (0.2 × 789) ÷ (0.2 × 789 + 0.8 × 745) = 157.8 ÷ 753.8 = 0.209.
  5. Blend air–fuel ratio (by mass): 0.209 × 9.0 + 0.791 × 14.7 = 1.88 + 11.63 = 13.5.
  6. Blend calorific value: 0.209 × 26.8 + 0.791 × 44 = 5.61 + 34.79 = 40.4 MJ/kg, about 8 % less than petrol.
  7. Energy per kg of stoichiometric mixture: petrol 44 ÷ (14.7 + 1) = 2.80 MJ/kg; E20 40.4 ÷ (13.5 + 1) = 2.79 MJ/kg.

Answer. (a) 15.1 : 1. (b) E20 needs 13.5 : 1 and gives 40.4 MJ/kg, yet a cylinder full of stoichiometric E20 mixture carries almost the same energy as petrol (2.79 against 2.80 MJ/kg); the engine simply injects about 9 % more fuel by mass (14.7 ÷ 13.5 = 1.09).

Worked example 2

Question. Ten consecutive cycles of the engine in the figure give IMEP (bar): 9.40, 9.51, 9.23, 9.44, 9.31, 9.44, 9.14, 9.52, 9.38, 9.34. (a) Find the mean, the standard deviation and COVimep. (b) The peak pressures of the same cycles range from 42.0 to 56.3 bar with a COV of 9.1 %. Comment.

  1. Mean = 93.71 ÷ 10 = 9.371 bar.
  2. Deviations from the mean: 0.029, 0.139, −0.141, 0.069, −0.061, 0.069, −0.231, 0.149, 0.009, −0.031.
  3. Sum of squares = 0.1299 bar².
  4. Standard deviation (n − 1 = 9): s = √(0.1299 ÷ 9) = √0.01443 = 0.120 bar.
  5. COVimep = 0.120 ÷ 9.371 × 100 = 1.28 %.
  6. Peak pressure scatters seven times more (9.1 %) because it depends strongly on where the burn falls; IMEP averages the whole cycle, and an early cycle's extra peak is partly paid back as less work later.

Answer. (a) Mean 9.37 bar, s = 0.120 bar, COVimep = 1.3 %, well below the 10 % limit, so the engine runs smoothly. (b) Peak pressure is a much more sensitive, but less useful, measure of variation.

Exam traps

  • Octane number is a volume percentage of iso-octane in a reference blend, not a percentage of iso-octane in the petrol itself.
  • MON is lower than RON for the same fuel because the motor test is harsher; sensitivity is RON − MON, not MON − RON.
  • The 10 % point governs cold starting and vapour lock, the 90 % point governs oil dilution and deposits. Swapping them is a common slip.
  • A lower calorific value per kg does not mean less energy per cylinder: what counts is the energy per kg of mixture.
  • Use the standard deviation, not the range, in COVimep, and divide by the mean IMEP, not by the peak pressure.

3.4 Abnormal combustion: knock, detonation and pre-ignition

Normal SI combustion is a single flame front that starts at the spark and sweeps the whole charge. Abnormal combustion is any burning that breaks this pattern: part of the charge ignites by itself, ahead of the front or before the spark. The two main forms are knock, where the end gas autoignites after the spark, and surface ignition, where a hot spot lights the charge, often before the spark (pre-ignition). Both raise pressure suddenly, cost power and, if they persist, damage pistons, rings and gaskets.

You need: the end gas and flame propagation (stages of combustion), and the rate of pressure rise (flame velocity).

The race between the flame and the end gas

In knock the spark-plug flame still burns most of the charge normally. The trouble is the end gas, the last 5 to 25 % of the charge, which lies farthest from the plug. As the front advances, the expanding burnt gas and the rising piston compress the end gas, and its temperature climbs. Like any fuel–air mixture held hot, it starts pre-flame reactions and will ignite by itself after a delay, the end-gas ignition delay τ.

So there is a race. If the flame reaches the end gas before τ runs out, the end gas burns normally. If τ runs out first, the end gas autoignites at many points almost at once. That near-instant release of energy in a small volume sends pressure waves across the chamber; they reflect from the walls and ring at a few kHz, heard as a metallic pinking or knock. This is the autoignition theory of knock, the explanation accepted today.

  • Knock is helped by anything that shortens τ (a hotter, denser end gas, a low-octane fuel) or lengthens the flame's travel time (a long flame path, little turbulence). The full list is in factors affecting knock.
  • The ringing frequency is set by the bore and the speed of sound in the hot gas: f ≈ 1.84 c ÷ (π B) for the first mode across the bore, about 6 to 7 kHz in a car engine. Knock sensors listen for it.

Knock, detonation and surface ignition

Knock (spark knock) begins after the spark, in the end gas. On a p–θ trace it shows as high-frequency oscillations near and after peak pressure.

Detonation. Many textbooks use "detonation" as another word for knock. Strictly, a detonation is a combustion wave driven by a shock, moving faster than sound in the unburnt gas. In heavy knock the end-gas reaction can approach this, with very high local pressures that erode the piston crown. If a question separates the two, call detonation the severe, shock-driven form of knock.

Surface ignition is ignition by a hot surface: an overheated plug electrode or insulator, a glowing carbon deposit, a hot exhaust valve or a sharp edge. If it happens before the spark it is pre-ignition; if after the spark, post-ignition, which simply adds a second flame front. Pre-ignition is the dangerous one. It starts the burn too early, so the charge is compressed while burning, peak pressure and temperature rise steeply near TDC, more heat flows into the metal, the hot spot gets hotter and the next cycle pre-ignites earlier still. This runaway can hole a piston in minutes.

Two related effects: run-on (dieseling), where a hot spot keeps the engine firing after the ignition is switched off; and rumble, a low-frequency roughness from several deposit-ignited fronts in high-compression engines. Pre-ignition and knock feed each other: pre-ignition heats the end gas and causes knock, and repeated knock overheats surfaces and causes pre-ignition.

Effects and how to tell them apart

  • Noise and vibration: the ping of knock; a dull thud from pre-ignition.
  • Mechanical damage: pitted or holed piston crowns, broken ring lands, blown head gaskets, bearing wear.
  • Higher heat transfer: the pressure waves scrub away the cooler boundary layer, so the walls run hotter and power and efficiency fall.
  • Carbon deposits and pre-ignition follow from the overheating.

To classify an event, mark the spark angle on the trace and compare times, not signs. An event at 6° bTDC is after a spark at 18° bTDC, because 6° bTDC comes later in the cycle. Ignition at 22° bTDC with the same spark is before it, so it is pre-ignition. Retarding the spark cures knock; it does not cure pre-ignition, because the hot spot does not wait for the spark.

Worked example

Question. An SI engine (bore 86 mm) runs at 3000 rev/min. At TDC the flame front still has 20 mm to travel to the end gas, and it travels at 25 m/s. 3.5 Factors affecting knock

Whether an SI engine knocks depends on the race between the flame and the end gas. Anything that makes the end gas hotter and denser shortens its ignition delay; anything that makes the flame slower or its path longer gives the end gas more time; and the fuel and mixture decide how quickly the end gas reacts at all. Textbooks group the factors in exactly these three ways: density (temperature and pressure) factors, time factors and composition factors. The same list explains the design and operating choices made to keep an engine knock-free.

You need: the race between the flame and the end gas (abnormal combustion).

Density factors: temperature and pressure of the end gas

A hotter, denser end gas reacts faster, so its ignition delay τ shrinks and knock becomes more likely.

  • Compression ratio: a higher ratio raises the temperature and pressure of the charge at the end of compression, so knock increases. This is the main limit on the compression ratio of a petrol engine.
  • Mass of charge inducted (load): a wider throttle, or supercharging, packs in more charge at higher pressure, so knock increases. Part throttle knocks less.
  • Inlet temperature: a hotter intake charge gives a hotter end gas, so knock increases. An intercooler on a turbocharged engine exists largely for this reason.
  • Temperature of the chamber walls: hot cylinder walls, a hot exhaust valve or a hot plug heat the end gas; poor cooling or a raised coolant temperature increases knock.
  • Spark timing: advancing the spark puts peak pressure nearer TDC, so the end gas reaches a higher pressure and temperature; knock increases. Retarding the spark is the quickest cure.
  • Power output: more power means more heat released per cycle and hotter metal, so knock increases.

Time factors: how long the end gas must wait

The end gas knocks if τ runs out before the flame arrives, so anything that shortens the flame's travel time reduces knock.

  • Turbulence: more turbulence (from squish, tumble and swirl) raises flame speed and reduces knock.
  • Engine speed: turbulence and flame speed rise with speed, so the burn takes less time; knock generally decreases as speed rises. Low speed and full throttle, such as climbing a ghat road in top gear, is the worst case.
  • Flame travel distance and plug position: a long path, as from a side-mounted plug, increases knock; a central plug or two plugs shorten it.
  • Engine size: a larger bore means a longer flame path and more time, so a large engine knocks more at the same speed; large petrol engines are therefore built with small cylinders, or many of them.
  • Combustion chamber shape: a compact chamber with squish keeps the end gas small, cool and close to the walls (see combustion chambers).

Composition factors: fuel and mixture

  • Octane number: a higher-octane fuel has a longer τ at the same temperature and pressure, so knock decreases.
  • Fuel–air ratio: knock is worst slightly rich of stoichiometric, at about φ = 1.1 (an air–fuel ratio near 13 : 1), where flame temperature and reaction rate are highest. Leaner mixtures (φ well below 1) release less heat and knock less; very rich mixtures also knock less, because the excess fuel absorbs heat as it evaporates.
  • Humidity: water vapour in the air acts as a diluent and lowers the flame temperature, so humid air reduces knock slightly.
  • Residual gas and EGR: exhaust gas left in or fed back dilutes the charge and slows the reactions, so it reduces knock, at the cost of a slower flame.

These factors act together. The worst case is a high-compression engine at low speed and full throttle, on hot intake air and hot coolant, with too much spark advance and a low-octane fuel. Modern engines stay near the knock limit by listening with a knock sensor and retarding the spark only when needed, so the driver gets most of the efficiency of a high compression ratio.

Worked example

Question.   4. (d) Composition factor. φ = 0.85 is lean (85 % of the stoichiometric fuel–air ratio), well away from the worst mixture at φ ≈ 1.1: knock decreases.
  5. (e) Composition factor. Higher octane lengthens τ: knock decreases.

3.6 CI engine combustion: stages and delay period

A compression-ignition (CI) engine compresses only air, to about 17 : 1, so the air near TDC (about 800 to 900 K) is far above the temperature at which diesel fuel ignites by itself. Fuel is sprayed in near the end of compression and has to break up, evaporate and mix before it can burn. So the charge is never uniform, mixing and burning overlap, and combustion runs in four stages: ignition delay, premixed burning, mixing-controlled burning and late burning. The ignition delay decides how much fuel collects before burning starts, and that decides how hard the engine runs.

You need: fuel injection, nozzles and spray formation (fuel injection systems), and crank-angle timing (stages of combustion).

Nature of the charge and mixture formation

The CI charge is heterogeneous: at any instant the cylinder holds liquid droplets, fuel vapour, rich zones, lean zones and plain air. The overall air–fuel ratio is always lean (from about 18 : 1 at full load to over 80 : 1 at idle), because load is controlled by the amount of fuel, not by throttling the air. Yet burning happens only where the local mixture is near stoichiometric.

Mixture formation happens in three steps. The jet leaving the nozzle at high pressure breaks up into droplets (atomisation); the droplets take heat from the hot air and evaporate (vaporisation); and air motion and the spray's own momentum carry air into the vapour (mixing). Swirl and squish help the last step (see air motion).

The four stages

Plot the heat release rate, dQ/dθ in joules per degree, against crank angle. It shows the stages more clearly than the pressure trace.

  1. Ignition delay (A to B): from the start of injection to the start of combustion. Nothing burns yet, but fuel keeps entering, breaking up and mixing. The delay has a physical part (atomisation, vaporisation, mixing) and a chemical part (pre-flame reactions that build up radicals). It ends where the pressure trace first rises above the motoring curve, or where the heat release rate first turns positive.
  2. Premixed (rapid) combustion (B to C): the fuel that mixed during the delay ignites at many points almost at once and burns very fast. The heat release rate shows a sharp spike and the pressure rises steeply. A long delay means more fuel in this stage and a harder, noisier spike.
  3. Mixing-controlled combustion (C to D): the rest of the fuel burns as fast as it can mix with air, in a diffusion flame round each spray plume. The rate is set by mixing, not chemistry, so it can be controlled by the injection rate. Most of the heat is released here, at a gentler slope.
  4. Late combustion (after D): after injection ends, the last fuel, soot and CO burn slowly in the expansion stroke as mixing finds them. Too long a late stage wastes heat and raises smoke and exhaust temperature.

Some books call the first three stages the delay period, the period of rapid combustion and the period of controlled combustion; the ideas are the same. A clean CI pressure trace peaks a few degrees after TDC.

3.7 Diesel knock and its control

Diesel knock is the hard, metallic combustion noise of a CI engine. Its cause is a long ignition delay: fuel keeps arriving while nothing burns, and when ignition finally comes, all of it burns at once in the premixed stage. The pressure jumps steeply, the structure rings and the bearings take a hammer blow. Some syllabi treat diesel knock as a topic of its own, while others fold it into CI combustion; either way, examiners like to ask how it differs from SI knock and how it is controlled.

You need: the four CI stages and the factors affecting delay (CI combustion), and SI knock (abnormal combustion).

The mechanism

Every CI cycle has a premixed spike; diesel knock is simply that spike grown too large. A long delay lets more fuel into the cylinder before ignition, more of it mixes, and the premixed stage releases a large share of the heat in a few degrees. The rate of pressure rise, not the peak pressure, is what the ear and the bearings feel. A smooth diesel stays near or below about 5 bar/°CA; knocking cycles go well beyond that.

Note the contrast with SI knock. In an SI engine knock comes at the end of combustion, from end gas that ignites too early. In a CI engine knock comes at the start of combustion, from fuel that ignites too late. So what cures one causes the other.

Methods of controlling diesel knock

  • Fuel with a higher cetane number, or a cetane improver additive such as an alkyl nitrate: shorter chemical delay.
  • Hotter, denser air: a higher compression ratio, supercharging, or a hotter intake. At cold start an intake air heater warms the incoming air, while a glow plug heats the air inside the chamber near the spray; both shorten the delay.
  • Injection timing close to the optimum (about 10° to 15° bTDC), where the air is hottest.
  • Pilot injection or a low initial injection rate: a small first shot ignites and heats the chamber, so the main shot meets a flame instead of waiting; less fuel collects.
  • Finer atomisation: high injection pressure and a clean nozzle shorten the physical delay.
  • Chamber design and air motion: organised swirl and hot surfaces help early mixing and ignition; indirect injection chambers run quieter (see combustion chambers).

3.8 Air motion: swirl, squish and tumble

The air in a cylinder is never still. The inlet port, the valve and the piston set up organised, large-scale motions: swirl, a rotation about the cylinder axis; tumble, a rotation about an axis across the cylinder; and squish, a radial inward flow as the piston nears the head at TDC. Near TDC these motions break down into turbulence. In a CI engine they mix the injected fuel with air; in an SI engine they wrinkle the flame front and speed up the burn. The swirl ratio puts a number on swirl.

You need: flame speed and turbulence (flame velocity), and CI mixture formation (CI combustion).

Why air motion matters

A CI engine has only about 1 to 2 ms to mix its fuel with air, and the fuel arrives as a few narrow sprays into a chamber full of air. Unless the air moves past the sprays, each plume uses up the oxygen around it and the rest of the air is wasted: power falls and smoke rises. An SI engine has a ready-mixed charge, but a flame in still gas creeps at well under 1 m/s; turbulence raises the burning speed several times, so the charge burns in time, cycles vary less and the engine tolerates leaner mixtures and more EGR.

Organised motion and turbulence are different things. Swirl, tumble and squish are bulk flows with a clear direction. Turbulence is the small, random eddies that actually wrinkle a flame and mix fuel at the smallest scales. The organised flows store kinetic energy during the intake stroke and release it as turbulence near TDC, just when combustion needs it. Some syllabi list "swirl, squish and turbulence" together for this reason.

Swirl and swirl ratio

Swirl is rotation of the charge about the cylinder axis. It is produced during intake by the port and valve: a directed port that aims the flow at the cylinder wall, a helical port that spins the flow before it reaches the valve, or a shrouded (masked) valve that lets air enter on one side only. A shroud is simple but cuts the flow area, so volumetric efficiency suffers; helical ports are the usual choice.

Swirl persists through compression, losing some energy to wall friction. In a diesel with a bowl in the piston, the air is pushed into the bowl near TDC. Its angular momentum is roughly conserved while its radius shrinks, so it spins faster, like a skater pulling in their arms. For solid-body rotation the ideal gain is (bore ÷ bowl diameter)². Real gains are smaller because of friction and because not all the air enters the bowl. Too much swirl blows one spray plume onto the next, raises heat loss to the walls and costs volumetric efficiency.

Squish and tumble

Squish is the radial inward flow created when the piston crown comes very close to a flat part of the head, the squish band or squish area, near TDC. The gas in that thin gap is squeezed out towards the bowl or the centre of the chamber. It is strongest in the last 10° or so before TDC, when the gap is smallest (about 1 mm in a diesel). Squish is not a rotation; it is a squeezing flow that adds turbulence exactly at TDC. After TDC it reverses as reverse squish.

Tumble is rotation about an axis at right angles to the cylinder axis, so the charge rolls over like a barrel. A steep, straight inlet port in a four-valve pent-roof head produces it. As the piston rises, the tumble vortex is squashed into an ever flatter shape until it breaks up near TDC into intense turbulence. That is why modern petrol engines favour tumble: its energy arrives at the spark. The tumble ratio is defined like the swirl ratio, as the rotational speed of the tumble vortex ÷ engine speed.

In short: diesels rely mainly on swirl and squish; petrol engines mainly on tumble and squish. All three end as turbulence, and the right amount is a balance between faster mixing and burning on one side, and heat loss and lost breathing on the other (see combustion chambers).

Worked example

Question. A DI diesel (bore 100 mm, stroke 110 mm) runs at 2000 rev/min with an in-cylinder swirl ratio of 2.2. Its piston bowl is 60 mm in diameter. Find (a) the swirl speed, (b) the tangential air velocity at the cylinder wall, (c) the mean piston speed and compare, and (d) the ideal swirl ratio in the bowl at TDC.

  1. (a) Ns = Rs × N = 2.2 × 2000 = 4400 rev/min.
  2. (b) Angular speed ω = 2π × 4400 ÷ 60 = 460.8 rad/s. At the wall, r = 0.050 m: v = ω r = 460.8 × 0.050 = 23.0 m/s.
  3. (c) Mean piston speed = 2 L N ÷ 60 = 2 × 0.110 × 2000 ÷ 60 = 7.33 m/s. The swirl at the wall moves about 3.1 times faster than the average piston.
  4. (d) Ideal gain from angular momentum: (bore ÷ bowl)² = (100 ÷ 60)² = 2.78. Ideal bowl swirl ratio = 2.2 × 2.78 = 6.1.
  5. Check: the gain is an upper bound; friction and the air left in the squish gap make the real bowl swirl ratio lower.

Answer. (a) 4400 rev/min. (b) 23.0 m/s at the wall. (c) 7.33 m/s, so the air sweeps the sprays about three times faster than the piston moves. (d) Ideally about 6.1 in the bowl, less in practice.

Exam traps

  • Squish is a radial inward flow near TDC, not a rotation; swirl and tumble are rotations.
  • Swirl rotates about the cylinder axis; tumble rotates about an axis across the cylinder. Do not swap the axes.
  • The swirl ratio has no unit: both speeds are in rev/min (or both in rad/s).
  • Organised motion is not turbulence; it decays into turbulence near TDC.
  • More swirl is not always better: it raises heat loss, can overlap the spray plumes and cuts volumetric efficiency.

3.9 Combustion chambers for SI and CI engines

The combustion chamber is the space above the piston at TDC, where the charge burns. Its shape decides how fast the charge burns, how much heat leaks into the walls, whether the end gas knocks, how well the engine breathes and, in a diesel, how well the fuel meets the air. SI engines use compact shapes with a central plug, such as the hemispherical, pent-roof, wedge and bathtub heads. CI engines use either an open chamber with a bowl in the piston (direct injection) or a divided chamber with a small pre-chamber (indirect injection).

You need: knock and its factors (factors affecting knock), the CI delay (CI combustion) and air motion (swirl, squish and tumble).

What a chamber must do

The chamber volume at TDC is the clearance volume Vc, and any bowl or pre-chamber is part of it. Compression ratio r = (Vs + Vc) ÷ Vc, where Vs is the swept volume. Every design balances these objectives:

  • High volumetric efficiency: room for large valves and smooth ports.
  • Short flame path and fast burn: a compact chamber with the plug near the centre (SI); good spray–air matching (CI).
  • High anti-knock quality (SI): the end gas kept small, cool and close to a cooled wall, often in a squish band.
  • Low surface-to-volume ratio: less wall area means less heat loss and fewer flame-quench layers that leave unburnt hydrocarbons.
  • No hot spots: the exhaust valve and plug well cooled, no sharp edges.
  • Controlled turbulence: enough squish, swirl or tumble for a fast burn, but not so much that heat loss soars.

SI chambers

  • Hemispherical: a dome-shaped head with inclined valves on both sides and a central plug. Short flame path, large valves and good breathing, and the lowest surface-to-volume ratio of the common shapes, so heat loss is low and high compression ratios suit it. It gives little squish, and the valve gear is complex and costly.
  • Pent-roof: the modern four-valve version of the hemispherical idea, with two flat roof faces, the plug in the middle and strong tumble from the steep inlet ports.
  • Wedge: valves in a line, inclined; the thin end of the wedge forms a squish area that keeps the end gas cool and adds turbulence. Cheap and knock-resistant, but the flame path from a plug at the thick end is longer.
  • Bathtub: an oval recess in a flat head with vertical in-line valves and squish areas on both sides. Simple and cheap, with good squish, but the valves are small, so breathing is weaker.

Older layouts (the side-valve L-head and T-head) had long flame paths and large surfaces; they survive only in textbooks and small stationary engines.

CI chambers: direct and indirect injection

Direct injection (DI), open chambers. Fuel is sprayed straight into a bowl in the piston crown through a central multi-hole nozzle at high pressure. Variants: a shallow open (quiescent) chamber for large slow engines, where the sprays do the mixing; and deep bowls, such as hemispherical, cylindrical and toroidal or re-entrant bowls, which use swirl and squish for mixing in smaller, faster engines. DI gives the best fuel economy and easy cold starting, because the chamber surface is small, but it needs high injection pressure, a high-cetane fuel and careful spray–bowl matching, and it is louder.

Indirect injection (IDI), divided chambers. Fuel is sprayed into a small chamber in the head joined to the main chamber by a throat. In a swirl chamber (such as the Ricardo Comet) the air is forced in through a tangential throat during compression and swirls hard; in a pre-combustion chamber the burning starts in the small chamber and a jet of burning gas blasts into the main chamber; air-cell and energy-cell designs work on the same idea. IDI needs only a single-hole pintle nozzle at low pressure, runs smoothly and quietly, tolerates poorer fuel and reaches high speeds. But the extra surface and throat losses waste heat, so fuel economy is 10 to 15 % worse than DI and a glow plug is needed for cold starting.

Factors that shape the design: bore (flame path and spray length), compression ratio (knock in SI, cold start in CI), valve size and number (two valves push the plug or injector off-centre), injector position and spray angle, cooling of the exhaust side, and cost.

  • IDI runs quieter and tolerates poor fuel, but DI gives the better fuel economy and cold starting.
  • Convert bowl depth from mm to cm before working in cm³.

Takeaways

  1. SI combustion has three stages on the p–θ diagram: ignition lag (A to B), flame propagation (B to peak C) and afterburning.
  2. Ignition lag is roughly fixed in milliseconds, so the spark is advanced as speed rises; flame propagation keeps about the same crank degrees.
  3. The mass burning rate ρu Af Sb sets the heat release, and dp/dθ ≈ (γ − 1)(dQ/dθ)/V near TDC sets the slope of the trace.
  4. Gasoline is judged by volatility, octane number, calorific value and air–fuel ratio; E20 carries almost the same energy per kg of mixture as petrol.
  5. No two cycles burn alike; COVimep measures the scatter, and the fastest cycles set the knock limit.
  6. Knock is end-gas autoignition after the spark; pre-ignition is hot-spot ignition before it; retarding the spark cures only knock.
  7. Knock factors come in three groups: density, time and composition.
  8. CI combustion runs through delay, premixed, mixing-controlled and late burning; the delay decides how much fuel burns at once.
  9. Diesel knock comes at the start of combustion from a long delay, so its cures are the opposite of SI knock's.
  10. Swirl, squish and tumble store energy as organised flow and release it as turbulence near TDC; chambers are shaped to use them.

Analyze Analyse how turbulence from air motion affects flame speed, the rate of pressure rise, cycle-to-cycle variation and knock in an SI engine. What limits the amount of turbulence a designer should aim for? 13 marks

Model answer.

  1. Flame speed. In still gas petrol–air burns at SL ≈ 0.3 to 0.5 m/s. Turbulence wrinkles the front, so ST ≈ SL + u′, with u′ about half the mean piston speed. Since u′ rises with engine speed, the burn keeps about the same crank degrees at all speeds, which is what lets petrol engines run fast. For the worked examples' petrol engine at 3000 rev/min, the mean piston speed is 2 × 0.086 × 3000/60 = 8.6 m/s, so u′ ≈ 4.3 m/s and ST ≈ 0.4 + 4.3 ≈ 4.7 m/s, about eleven times the laminar value.
  2. Rate of pressure rise. A shorter burn raises dp/dθ and moves the peak towards TDC. In the worked examples' petrol engine (burn starting 10° bTDC):

    35°50.3 bar15.6° aTDC2.3 bar/°9.40 bar
    50°37.8 bar21.9° aTDC1.0 bar/°8.97 bar
  3. More turbulence moves the engine from the slow row towards the fast row: more work, cooler exhaust, but rougher running past about 3 bar/°CA.
  4. Cycle-to-cycle variation. The early kernel is small, so the turbulence and mixture at the plug when the spark fires decide each cycle's burn. Well-organised motion (tumble breaking down at TDC) gives strong, repeatable turbulence and a fast early flame, so COVimep falls; in the ten-cycle example it is 1.3 %, against 13.7 % for a lean, slow-burning set.
  5. Knock. Turbulence is a time factor: a faster flame reaches the end gas before its ignition delay runs out. Squish also keeps the end gas thin and close to the cool head. So more turbulence allows a higher compression ratio or more spark advance.
  6. Limits. (1) Heat transfer: faster gas scrubs the walls, so heat loss rises with turbulence and can cancel the gain. (2) Volumetric efficiency: ports and masks that make swirl or tumble restrict the flow at full load. (3) Flame quench: very intense turbulence at the kernel can stretch and blow out a weak flame, worst with lean mixtures. (4) Rough running and NOx from very fast burns.
  7. Where the turbulence comes from. Tumble from steep inlet ports in pent-roof heads is preferred in petrol engines, because the vortex breaks up just before the spark; squish adds turbulence at TDC and cools the end gas; swirl helps less in SI engines and costs flow area.
  8. Conclusion. The aim is enough turbulence for a burn of about 25° to 35° (10 to 90 % burned about 20°), stable kernels and a good knock margin, delivered near TDC by tumble and squish rather than by losses in the intake.

Glossary

Afterburning
The last SI stage, after peak pressure, when the remaining charge burns during expansion.
Autoignition
Ignition of a fuel–air mixture by its own temperature, with no spark or flame.
Cetane number
A measure of how easily a diesel fuel ignites; higher means a shorter delay.
Clearance volume
The cylinder volume at TDC, including any bowl or pre-chamber.
Coefficient of variation (COV)
Standard deviation divided by the mean, as a percentage.
Delay period
In a CI engine, the time or crank angle from the start of injection to the start of combustion.
Detonation
Severe, shock-driven knock; in many books simply another word for knock.
Distillation curve
The temperatures at which given fractions of a fuel sample boil off.
End gas
The last part of the charge to burn, compressed and heated ahead of the flame.
Equivalence ratio
Actual fuel–air ratio divided by the stoichiometric one; above 1 is rich.
Flame front
The thin reaction zone between burnt gas and fresh charge.
Flame travel velocity
Speed at which the flame front crosses the chamber, relative to the cylinder head.
Heat release rate
Energy released by combustion per degree of crank angle, dQ/dθ.
Ignition lag
The first SI stage, from the spark to the first pressure rise above motoring.
IMEP
Indicated mean effective pressure: indicated work per cycle divided by the swept volume.
Knock
Autoignition of the end gas ahead of the flame, giving pressure oscillations and noise.
Motoring curve
Cylinder pressure against crank angle with no combustion.
Octane number
Percentage of iso-octane in a blend with n-heptane that knocks like the fuel.
Pre-chamber
A small chamber in the head of an IDI diesel, joined to the main chamber by a throat.
Pre-ignition
Ignition of the charge by a hot surface before the spark.
Reid vapour pressure
Vapour pressure of a fuel measured at 37.8 °C; a measure of front-end volatility.
Squish band
The flat area where the piston crown comes close to the head at TDC.
Swirl ratio
Swirl speed of the charge divided by engine speed.
Tumble
Rotation of the charge about an axis at right angles to the cylinder axis.
Turbulence intensity
The r.m.s. fluctuation of gas velocity, u′.
