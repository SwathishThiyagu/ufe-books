3.1 Stages of combustion and flame propagation in SI engines

An SI engine burns a premixed fuel–air charge after a spark. Combustion runs in three stages: ignition lag, flame propagation and afterburning. Ignition lag is the short delay before a flame kernel grows enough to raise pressure. Flame propagation is the thin flame front sweeping the unburnt mixture. Afterburning finishes the last gas near the walls. Mechanics read these stages on the pressure–crank angle trace to judge spark timing and cyclic variation.

You need: the four-stroke SI working principle from Unit 1 and spark timing from Unit 2.

From spark to burnt gas

Combustion starts at the spark plug. The spark leaves a small flame kernel, but the kernel needs time to become self-sustaining. That delay is ignition lag. In a normal engine it is about 0.001 s to 0.002 s. Some syllabi call it the preparation phase: the kernel grows, radical species build up and local temperature rises. Pressure barely changes during most of this delay. The stage ends when pressure first rises above the motoring curve.

Flame propagation follows. A thin flame front, a reaction zone perhaps a millimetre thick, moves into the unburnt mixture. Behind it is burnt gas; ahead is fresh charge. The front area and flame speed decide how fast fresh charge is consumed. Cylinder pressure rises steeply. The stage ends when the flame has swept the main chamber, usually a few tens of crank degrees after top dead centre (TDC).

Afterburning is the last stage. Unburnt gas trapped in crevices, near the cold wall or in a lean pocket burns slowly. Pressure falls as the piston moves down. Oxidation of CO and hydrocarbons continues into the expansion stroke. It adds little useful work but affects emissions.

Each stage has a crank-angle width, not a fixed time. At higher speed the same milliseconds cover more degrees. The spark must be advanced so that the steep pressure rise happens near TDC.

Reading the pressure–crank angle trace

The pressure–crank angle trace plots cylinder pressure p against crank angle θ. θ is measured from TDC; negative θ is before TDC. The motoring curve is the pressure with fuel but no ignition. The spark marker sits at spark timing. The trace leaves the motoring curve at the end of ignition lag; that knee is the practical end of lag.

During flame propagation the trace climbs steeply to peak pressure. Peak pressure normally occurs near 10° to 15° after TDC. Too early a peak means over-advanced spark; too late means retarded spark. The main flame sweep ends near 40° to 45° after TDC in many engines. The remaining slow fall is afterburning.

θ = 6 N t = 0.006 N tms

θ is crank angle in degrees; N is engine speed in rev/min; t is time in seconds; tms is the same time in milliseconds.

At 3000 rpm, 1 ms is 18° CA. The same 1.5 ms lag is 27° at 3000 rpm but 9° at 1000 rpm.

Cyclic variation is normal: the same settings give slightly different traces cycle to cycle. Peak pressure and peak angle scatter. Heavy scatter points to a weak flame kernel, poor mixture preparation or too lean a charge. It is tracked by the coefficient of variation of indicated mean effective pressure, COV of IMEP.

What the stages tell you

The three-stage picture sets the language for the rest of the unit. Flame speed and mass burning rate control the slope of the pressure rise; see 3.2. If the end gas ahead of the flame autoignites, the trace shows a pressure spike and knock; see 3.3. Ignition lag is not flame propagation: lag is delay before burning, propagation is the burning wave.

If ignition lag is too long, the spark needs more advance. If flame propagation is slow, peak pressure arrives late and work falls. If afterburning is heavy, exhaust temperature and unburnt hydrocarbons rise. These three checks are the first things to look at when an engine loses power or knocks. Chamber shape and air motion change flame speed; see 3.7 and 3.8.

Worked example

Question. A spark fires at 15° bTDC. Ignition lag is 1.5 ms. The engine runs at 3000 rpm. Find the ignition lag in crank degrees and the crank angle where the pressure starts to rise (end of ignition lag). Then mark flame propagation of 30° CA and afterburning of 20° CA on a crank-angle timeline.

  1. Engine speed N = 3000 rpm. Time for one revolution = 60 / 3000 = 0.020 s = 20 ms.
  2. Crank degrees per millisecond = 360° / 20 ms = 18 °CA/ms.
  3. Ignition lag in crank degrees = 1.5 ms × 18 °CA/ms = 27° CA.
  4. Spark at 15° bTDC is −15° from TDC. End of lag = −15° + 27° = +12° aTDC.
  5. Flame propagation of 30°: end = 12° + 30° = 42° aTDC.
  6. Afterburning of 20°: end = 42° + 20° = 62° aTDC.

Answer. Ignition lag is 27° CA; it ends at 12° aTDC; flame propagation ends at 42° aTDC; afterburning ends at 62° aTDC.

Exam traps

  • Ignition lag ends at the first pressure rise above the motoring curve, not when the flame reaches the cylinder wall.
  • Ignition lag is delay before burning; flame propagation is the burning wave. Do not use the two terms for the same stage.
  • Crank degrees and milliseconds are different units. A 1.5 ms lag is 27° CA at 3000 rpm but only 9° CA at 1000 rpm.
  • Spark advance is a setting before TDC; ignition lag is the delay after the spark. Advancing the spark does not shorten the chemical lag in milliseconds.
  • Afterburning is the slow oxidation tail, not the main flame sweep. Peak pressure belongs to the end of flame propagation, not to afterburning.

3.2 Flame velocity, flame front and rate of pressure rise

A spark-ignition engine burns its charge through a thin flame front that sweeps out from the spark plug. Flame velocity is how fast that front travels. The mass burning rate — flame front area × unburnt charge density × flame speed — decides how quickly the cylinder pressure climbs. That climb, the rate of pressure rise in bar per degree of crank angle, fixes where the peak pressure sits, how much work comes out and how close the engine is to knock. You read it directly from a pressure–crank angle trace.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for the three stages of SI combustion and the ignition lag that starts the flame.

Flame velocity and flame speed

Combustion in a petrol engine takes place in a thin zone called the flame front, a few tenths of a millimetre thick. Behind it is burnt gas; ahead of it is unburnt charge. Reactions inside the front release heat and raise the temperature of the gas.

Flame velocity is the distance the front travels divided by the time taken. Its unit is m/s. If the front crosses 40 mm of chamber in 2 ms, the velocity is 0.040 m ÷ 0.002 s = 20 m/s.

Flame speed is the speed of the front measured normal to itself, relative to the unburnt gas just ahead of it. It is a property of the mixture, not of the engine. Flame velocity measured in a running engine is relative to the cylinder head, so it also contains the gas motion.

ST ≈ SL + u′

ST is the turbulent flame speed (m/s); SL is the laminar flame speed (m/s); u′ is the turbulence intensity, the rms fluctuation of gas velocity (m/s).

SL peaks slightly rich of stoichiometric, near an equivalence ratio φ of 1.1, where φ is the actual fuel-air ratio divided by the stoichiometric one. It rises with temperature and falls with pressure. ST is much larger because turbulence wrinkles and folds the front, so more fresh mixture meets the reaction zone every second.

Flame front area and mass burning rate

Pressure rise comes from burning mass, not from flame position. The quantity that matters is the mass burning rate, the kilograms of charge burnt per second.

ṁ<sub>b</sub> = ρ<sub>u</sub> · A<sub>f</sub> · S<sub>T</sub>

ṁb is the mass burning rate (kg/s); ρu is the density of the unburnt charge (kg/m³), roughly 10 to 15 kg/m³ just before the spark in a naturally aspirated engine; Af is the flame front area (m²); ST is the turbulent flame speed (m/s).

Af is small at the spark, grows as the kernel becomes a sphere, and shrinks again when the front reaches the walls and the piston crown. Because the flame first grows and then dies, the burning rate peaks around the middle of combustion and tails off near the end.

This is why chamber shape matters. A compact chamber with the spark near the centre lets the front sweep a short path with a large area for most of the burn. See Chapter 3.8 for chamber layouts.

Rate of pressure rise

The rate of pressure rise is how fast cylinder pressure changes with crank angle, written dp/dθ in bar per degree of crank angle (°CA). Over an interval it is the average slope of the pressure trace.

(dp/dθ)<sub>avg</sub> = (p<sub>2</sub> − p<sub>1</sub>) / (θ<sub>2</sub> − θ<sub>1</sub>)

p1 and p2 are cylinder pressures (bar) at crank angles θ1 and θ2 (°CA, measured from TDC, top dead centre).

The first law of thermodynamics for the cylinder gives the instantaneous value.

dp/dt = ((γ − 1)/V) · dQ/dt − (γ p/V) · dV/dt

γ is the ratio of specific heats of the gas, about 1.35; V is the instantaneous cylinder volume (m³); dQ/dt is the heat release rate (W), roughly ṁb × the lower calorific value of the fuel; p is the cylinder pressure (Pa).

The first term is combustion and the second is piston motion. Volume sits in the denominator of both. The same heat released into a larger volume raises the pressure less. That is the trap in this chapter: high flame speed alone does not give a high rate of pressure rise.

A normal SI engine peaks at about 1 to 2 bar/°CA. Knocking cycles reach several times this, which is what produces the sharp pressure spike and the metallic noise; Chapter 3.3 takes that up.

Fast burn pushes the peak pressure nearer TDC. More of the heat is released at small volume, so more of it becomes work and efficiency goes up, and the end gas has less time to self-ignite. Too fast a burn, however, loads the piston and bearings and makes the engine noisy. Sluggish burn puts the peak late, wastes heat to the exhaust and runs hot.

Worked example

Question. In an SI engine test, the cylinder pressure is 30 bar at 10° aTDC and 60 bar at 25° aTDC. (a) Find the average rate of pressure rise in bar/°CA. (b) The engine runs at 3000 rpm. Convert the answer to bar/ms.

  1. Read the two points: p₁ = 30 bar at θ₁ = 10° aTDC, p₂ = 60 bar at θ₂ = 25° aTDC.
  2. Pressure interval: Δp = p₂ − p₁ = 60 − 30 = 30 bar.
  3. Crank angle interval: Δθ = θ₂ − θ₁ = 25 − 10 = 15 °CA.
  4. Average rate of pressure rise = Δp / Δθ = 30 bar / 15 °CA = 2 bar/°CA.
  5. Time for one revolution at 3000 rpm = 60 s / 3000 = 0.02 s. One revolution is 360°, so one degree takes 0.02 / 360 = 5.556 × 10⁻⁵ s = 0.05556 ms.
  6. Rate in bar/ms = 2 bar/°CA ÷ 0.05556 ms/°CA = 36 bar/ms.
  7. Check by the other route: 3000 rpm = 50 rev/s = 18 000 °/s. Then 2 bar/° × 18 000 °/s = 36 000 bar/s = 36 bar/ms.

Answer. The average rate of pressure rise is 2 bar per degree of crank angle, which equals 36 bar per millisecond at 3000 rpm.

Exam traps

  • A high flame speed alone does not fix the rate of pressure rise. Mass burning rate (density × flame area × flame speed) and chamber volume also control it.
  • bar/°CA, bar/ms and bar/s are different units. At N rpm one degree takes 1000/(6N) ms, so 2 bar/°CA is 36 bar/ms at 3000 rpm but 24 bar/ms at 2000 rpm.
  • Flame speed is measured relative to the unburnt gas; flame velocity in the engine is measured relative to the cylinder head. Do not use the two words interchangeably in a definition question.
  • The slope read between two points is the average rate; the peak instantaneous rate is higher and occurs near the middle of the burn.
  • Quoting SL = 20 m/s is wrong. That order of magnitude belongs to the turbulent flame speed; laminar values are below 1 m/s.

3.3 Abnormal combustion: knock, detonation and pre-ignition

Abnormal combustion is any burning that does not follow the spark-plug flame front. The idea exists because normal SI combustion is a controlled flame sweep; when the end gas self-ignites or a hot spot fires early, the pressure rise becomes uncontrolled. You meet it in petrol engines during hard acceleration, low-octane fuel use, overheating or a Chennai summer traffic crawl. It causes the metallic ping called knock, power loss and, if repeated, piston damage.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for the three stages of SI combustion and Chapter 3.2 for flame speed and rate of pressure rise.

Normal flame travel and the end gas

In normal SI combustion the spark at the plug starts a small flame kernel. The flame front moves outward through the unburnt mixture. The mixture ahead of the flame is the end gas. It has not burnt yet.

As the flame grows, pressure and temperature rise in the cylinder. The end gas is compressed by the moving flame and by the rising piston. It gets hotter even before the flame reaches it. Designers keep this end gas below its autoignition temperature until the flame arrives.

Normal combustion is therefore a race. The flame must sweep the last pocket of end gas before that pocket ignites on its own. Flame speed and burn rate decide the race; see Chapter 3.2. If the flame wins, you get a smooth pressure curve with one peak near TDC.

Abnormal combustion is the case where something else wins. Two different events can make this happen. The end gas can self-ignite after the spark. Or a hot surface can ignite the mixture before the spark. The first is knock. The second is pre-ignition. Keep the order clear: the spark timing is the reference line.

Knock, detonation and pre-ignition

Knock begins after the spark. The flame has already started from the plug. The end gas, the last unburnt part, reaches its autoignition temperature and burns very quickly. This self-ignition releases a local pressure pulse. The pulse reflects around the chamber and sets up pressure oscillations. You hear a metallic ping or pinking. On a pressure-crank angle trace, the normal smooth rise is followed by high-frequency wiggles. The crank angle at which those wiggles start is after the spark event.

Pre-ignition begins before the spark. A hot spot ignites the mixture early. The hot spot can be a glowing spark plug electrode, a carbon deposit, an exhaust valve edge or a sharp metal corner. Pre-ignition is not caused by the spark. It can start many degrees before the spark. It raises pressure and temperature early, which can then cause knock. The engine may run on after the ignition is switched off.

Detonation is severe knock. The end-gas autoignition is so fast that it sends pressure waves through the charge. These waves move at supersonic speed relative to the unburnt gas. They can reflect from the cylinder walls and piston crown. Detonation gives very high local pressures and temperatures. It can damage the piston crown, ring lands, head gasket and bearings. Some syllabi call ordinary knock 'detonation' loosely. In this book, use detonation only for the severe pressure-wave form of knock.

Visible symptoms are a metallic noise, vibration, loss of power and rising coolant temperature. Bad cases can end with a hole in the piston.

Reading the pressure trace and symptoms

To classify an event, mark the spark timing on the crank-angle axis. If pressure oscillations start after that mark, it is knock. If ignition starts before that mark, it is pre-ignition. Crank angles before top dead centre (bTDC) are negative in many data systems; compare numbers by time order, not by sign alone. Example: spark at 18° bTDC and event at 6° bTDC. 6° bTDC is later than 18° bTDC, so the event is after spark. Spark at 18° bTDC and event at 22° bTDC: 22° bTDC is earlier, so the event is before spark.

Mild knock is common and tolerable for short periods. Heavy knock or repeated pre-ignition is not. Damage starts at the hottest, most highly stressed metal: exhaust valve, piston crown, top ring land, head gasket. Pre-ignition can melt the spark plug electrode and then the engine may misfire or stop. For factors that make knock worse, see Chapter 3.4. For chamber shapes that avoid hot spots, see Chapter 3.8. We leave knock sensors and ignition control to Unit 2, and emission effects to Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A petrol engine has spark at 18° bTDC. In case A, pressure oscillations begin at 6° bTDC. In case B, a hot spot ignites the mixture at 22° bTDC. In case C, the pressure wave after knock travels at supersonic speed. Identify each case. Also give the crank-angle interval between the spark and each event.

  1. Fix the spark timing: θ_spark = 18° bTDC. A larger bTDC number is earlier in the cycle.
  2. Case A: θ_A = 6° bTDC. 6° bTDC is later than 18° bTDC by 18 − 6 = 12°. The event is after spark. Pressure oscillation after spark is knock.
  3. Case B: θ_B = 22° bTDC. 22° bTDC is earlier than 18° bTDC by 22 − 18 = 4°. The event is before spark. Ignition before spark is pre-ignition.
  4. Case C: supersonic pressure waves after knock are the severe form. Name it detonation.

Answer. Case A is knock with the oscillation 12° after spark; case B is pre-ignition with hot-spot ignition 4° before spark; case C is detonation.

Exam traps

  • Do not classify by TDC. A pressure oscillation at 6° bTDC can be after a spark at 18° bTDC, because 6° bTDC is later in the cycle. Compare event time with spark time.
  • Knock starts after spark. Pre-ignition starts before spark. A hot spot at 22° bTDC with spark at 18° bTDC is pre-ignition, not knock.
  • Detonation is severe knock, not a third independent everyday event. If a question asks for the severe pressure-wave form of knock, answer detonation.
  • Ordinary knock is not early ignition. The spark has already fired. The end gas self-ignites later. Pre-ignition can lead to knock, but the names stay separate.

3.4 Factors affecting knock

Knock in an SI engine happens when the end gas autoignites before the flame front reaches it. Whether knock occurs depends on how fast the end gas heats up and how long it waits. Three groups of factors control this: the fuel's resistance to autoignition, the engine's geometry and timing, and how the engine is operated. Changing any one factor can make knock stronger or weaker. Mechanics and drivers use this list to choose fuel, set spark timing and diagnose knock in a Chennai traffic jam or on a highway.

You need Chapter 3.3: knock and pre-ignition for the end-gas picture, and Chapter 3.2 for flame speed.

Fuel factors

Octane number (ON) measures a fuel's resistance to autoignition. A higher ON means the end gas waits longer before it self-ignites, so knock is less likely. Some syllabi call ON the anti-knock rating. Fuel composition matters too: aromatics and iso-paraffins generally have high ON; normal paraffins have low ON. Petrol in India is sold as 91, 95 or 97 RON, where RON is the research octane number.

Mixture strength tells how far the actual fuel-air ratio is from the chemically correct one. It is written as the equivalence ratio φ = (F/A) ÷ (F/A)stoich, where F/A is the fuel-air ratio by mass and the subscript stoich means the chemically correct ratio. Knock tendency is highest for slightly rich mixtures, about φ = 1.05 to 1.15, which is an air-fuel ratio of roughly 13:1 — close to but richer than stoichiometric. Leaner mixtures (φ below about 0.9) knock less because less energy is released. Very rich mixtures (φ above about 1.2) also knock less because the excess fuel cools the charge and lowers the flame temperature.

φ = (F/A) ÷ (F/A)stoich

φ is the equivalence ratio (no unit); F/A is the actual fuel-air mass ratio; (F/A)stoich is the stoichiometric fuel-air mass ratio, about 0.068 for petrol.

Engine design factors

Compression ratio r = (Vs + Vc) ÷ Vc, where Vs is the swept volume and Vc is the clearance volume. A higher r squeezes the charge more, raising its pressure and temperature before the flame arrives. This makes the end gas hotter and closer to autoignition, so knock increases. Raising r from 8 to 10 in a petrol engine almost always increases knock unless the fuel's ON is raised.

Spark timing is measured in degrees before top dead centre (bTDC). Advancing spark, for example from 15° to 25° bTDC, starts combustion earlier. The peak pressure and temperature rise, and the end gas is compressed for a longer time before the flame reaches it. So more advance increases knock. Retarding spark reduces knock.

Combustion chamber shape controls flame travel distance and turbulence. A compact chamber with a central spark plug and good squish gives a short flame path and fast burning, which reduces knock. A long, narrow chamber or a side-mounted spark plug increases flame travel and knock. A larger bore also increases flame travel distance, so knock tends to rise with bore. Better cooling of the head and valves lowers metal temperatures and reduces hot spots that can trigger knock.

Operating factors

Speed affects knock through turbulence. As engine speed rises, air motion and flame speed increase. The flame crosses the chamber faster, leaving less time for end-gas reactions. So knock tendency generally decreases as speed increases from low to moderate values. Very high speeds can raise temperatures, but the dominant effect in most SI engines is that low-speed, high-load operation is the knock-prone zone.

Load is the throttle opening, which sets how much air-fuel mixture enters the cylinder. At higher load, the pressure and temperature inside the cylinder rise, so the end gas is hotter and knock increases. Coolant temperature also matters: raising coolant temperature from 80°C to 95°C makes the cylinder walls and the end gas hotter, so knock increases. Higher inlet air temperature has the same effect. Cooler intake air and cooler coolant both reduce knock.

These factors act together. A high-compression engine running at low speed, high load and hot coolant with advanced spark is the worst case. The same engine with high-octane fuel, retarded spark and cool coolant runs knock-free. The chamber shape chapter shows how geometry sets the flame travel that these factors act on.

Worked example

Question. Classify each change as increasing or decreasing knock tendency: (a) compression ratio 8 to 10, (b) spark advanced from 15° to 25° bTDC, (c) coolant temperature 80°C to 95°C, (d) octane number 91 to 95.

  1. (a) Compression ratio rises from 8 to 10. Higher compression raises the pressure and temperature of the end gas before the flame arrives. The end gas reaches its autoignition temperature sooner. Knock tendency increases.
  2. (b) Spark is advanced from 15° to 25° bTDC. Combustion starts earlier, peak pressure and temperature rise, and the end gas is compressed for a longer time before the flame front reaches it. Knock tendency increases.
  3. (c) Coolant temperature rises from 80°C to 95°C. The cylinder walls and the end gas become hotter. The end gas needs less additional compression to autoignite. Knock tendency increases.
  4. (d) Octane number rises from 91 to 95. Higher octane means the fuel resists autoignition for a longer time. The end gas waits longer before self-igniting. Knock tendency decreases.

Answer. (a), (b) and (c) increase knock tendency; (d) decreases knock tendency.

Exam traps

  • A higher octane number reduces knock; a higher compression ratio or more spark advance increases knock. More is not always worse.
  • Advancing spark means moving the spark earlier (more bTDC). That increases knock. Retarding spark reduces knock.
  • Knock is worst at a slightly rich mixture near stoichiometric, not at the richest mixture. Very rich mixtures reduce knock.
  • Better cooling reduces knock. Raising coolant temperature from 80°C to 95°C increases knock, not decreases it.
  • A larger bore increases flame travel distance and knock. A compact chamber with a central spark plug reduces knock.

3.5 CI engine combustion: stages and delay period

In a CI engine, fuel is injected into hot compressed air, not mixed with air before compression. The mixture forms while ignition is already being prepared. The delay between the start of injection and the first pressure rise decides how much fuel collects before burning starts. That collected fuel burns fast in the premixed phase, and the rest burns as it mixes with air. These stages explain diesel noise, efficiency and load behaviour.

You need: Unit 1 CI working principle and Unit 2 injection timing and nozzles, plus the SI stage picture in 3.1 for contrast.

Heterogeneous charge and ignition delay

A CI engine has a heterogeneous charge. Liquid fuel, fuel vapour and air exist together in the cylinder. Fuel is injected near the end of compression into air hot enough to ignite it. Mixture formation and ignition therefore overlap: some fuel is still breaking into droplets and vapour while the first chemical reactions begin.

θd = 6 N td

θd is ignition delay in crank degrees (°CA); N is engine speed in rev/min; td is delay in seconds. With td in milliseconds: θd = 0.006 N tms. At 2000 rpm, 1 ms equals 12°CA.

Ignition delay (delay period) is measured from the start of injection to the first measurable pressure rise caused by combustion. It has two parts. Physical delay covers atomisation, vaporisation and mixing. Chemical delay covers pre-flame reactions that break fuel molecules into radicals. The pressure rise is small at first; the delay ends when heat release becomes visible on the pressure trace. Some syllabi call it the delay period.

The four stages

The stages are a timeline, not separate boxes; they overlap. The figure uses injection at 12° before top dead centre (bTDC).

  • Ignition delay: start of injection to first pressure rise. Fuel accumulates in the chamber; heat release is small.
  • Premixed burn: fuel mixed during delay ignites quickly. The burning rate is high, so the pressure rises fast. This phase sets diesel knock; see 3.6.
  • Mixing-controlled burn: fuel burns as it mixes with hot air. The rate follows mixing, not chemical kinetics. Most heat is released here, but the pressure rise is gentler.
  • Late burning: remaining fuel burns late in expansion. The rate is low. It can raise exhaust temperature and smoke.

The first two stages are sometimes called the ignition and rapid-burning phases. The third is the main phase and controls torque. The fourth is afterburning. In a well-timed engine the main phase ends well before exhaust valve opening; a very long late phase wastes heat.

Factors affecting delay

Delay changes with fuel, air and injection. A short delay gives a small premixed burn and soft combustion; a long delay gives a large premixed burn and diesel knock.

  • Cetane number: measure of ignition ease. Higher cetane number shortens delay; lower cetane lengthens it.
  • Air temperature and pressure: hotter, denser air shortens both physical and chemical delay. Higher compression ratio or boost raises air temperature and pressure.
  • Injection timing: advanced injection starts in cooler air, so delay in time can grow. Retarded injection starts nearer TDC in hotter air, so delay shortens, but too much retard raises exhaust temperature and smoke.
  • Injection pressure and atomisation: higher injection pressure gives finer droplets, more surface area, faster vaporisation, shorter physical delay.
  • Engine speed: at higher speed the same delay in milliseconds covers more crank degrees. Use the measured delay at that speed; do not scale a low-speed value.
  • Load: higher load usually gives a hotter charge and shorter delay. Very light load can give a longer delay because the charge is cooler.

A cold start is noisy for this reason: low air temperature and low speed lengthen delay, fuel accumulates, and premixed burn is strong.

Worked example

Question. Injection starts at 12° bTDC. The ignition delay is 1.2 ms at 2000 rpm. Find the delay in crank degrees and the crank angle where ignition starts. Premixed burn lasts 8°CA and mixing-controlled burn lasts 30°CA. Find the end of mixing-controlled burn. Take late burning as 15°CA and find its end.

  1. Crank angle per millisecond at 2000 rpm is 6N/1000 = 6×2000/1000 = 12°CA/ms.
  2. Delay θd = 12°CA/ms × 1.2 ms = 14.4°CA.
  3. Start of injection is 12° bTDC, which is −12° aTDC. Ignition starts at −12 + 14.4 = 2.4° after top dead centre (aTDC).
  4. Premixed burn ends at 2.4 + 8 = 10.4° aTDC.
  5. Mixing-controlled burn ends at 10.4 + 30 = 40.4° aTDC.
  6. Late burning ends at 40.4 + 15 = 55.4° aTDC.

Answer. The ignition delay is 14.4°CA; ignition starts at 2.4° aTDC; premixed burn ends at 10.4° aTDC; mixing-controlled burn ends at 40.4° aTDC; late burning ends at 55.4° aTDC.

Exam traps

  • Ignition delay is measured from start of injection to the first pressure rise, not from TDC and not from a spark; a CI engine has no spark.
  • Do not mix ms and °CA. Convert with θd = 0.006 N tms; at 2000 rpm, 1 ms is 12°CA.
  • Signed crank angles: mark bTDC as negative aTDC before adding the delay and stage lengths, or you will place ignition on the wrong side of TDC.
  • A long delay increases the premixed burn and diesel knock; it does not increase the mixing-controlled phase.
  • Cetane number is not octane number: high cetane shortens CI delay, high octane resists SI knock.

3.6 Diesel knock and its control

Diesel knock is the hard, metallic combustion noise of a CI engine when ignition delay is too long. Fuel injected during the delay has time to mix with air and collect. When ignition finally starts, this collected fuel burns fast in the premixed phase. Pressure rises sharply, the block rings, and we hear knock. The idea matters for engine noise, durability and efficiency. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail. You meet it in diesel generators, trucks and some cars, especially during cold starts or with poor fuel.

You need: the four CI combustion stages and delay period from Chapter 3.5, and the difference between knock and pre-ignition from Chapter 3.3.

The mechanism

Long ignition delay starts the chain. Fuel injected during delay mixes with hot air but does not burn. More fuel collects as a premixed cloud. Autoignition then starts at many points and the cloud burns fast. Pressure rises sharply in a short crank angle. The pressure wave hits the chamber walls and we hear the hard diesel knock.

Normal CI combustion has a small premixed spike. A long delay makes the spike large. Peak pressure and rate of pressure rise increase. This adds noise and mechanical stress. The spike is worst at cold start, when the air charge is cool.

Diesel knock is not spark knock. Spark knock starts after the spark and comes from end-gas autoignition (Chapter 3.3). Diesel knock starts at the end of a long delay and comes from too much premixed fuel. Pre-ignition also starts before the spark.

Factors and controls

Delay depends on fuel ignition quality, air temperature, injection timing, spray quality and chamber design.

  • Higher cetane number: cetane measures ignition quality. A higher value shortens delay. Add a cetane booster to diesel.
  • Retarded injection: start injection later, closer to top dead centre where air is hotter. This cuts delay and peak pressure. Too much retard raises exhaust temperature and lowers efficiency.
  • Warmer intake air: a glow plug or intake heater shortens chemical delay at cold start. Cold diesel generators in hill areas often need this.
  • Pilot injection: a small early spray starts a flame. The main spray then burns after a short delay.
  • Better atomization: high injection pressure and a clean nozzle make fine drops that vaporise fast.
  • Chamber design: hot walls and organised air motion help mixing. Avoid fuel hitting cold walls. See Chapters 3.7 and 3.8.

θ = 0.006 N t

θ is delay in crank degrees, N is engine speed in rpm, t is delay in ms.

Worked example

Question. A diesel engine runs at 1500 rpm. Ignition delay is 1.4 ms. Find the delay in crank degrees. Raising cetane number from 45 to 50 cuts delay to 1.0 ms. Find the reduction in crank degrees.

  1. Use θ = 0.006 N t, with θ in crank degrees, N in rpm and t in ms.
  2. At N = 1500 rpm, 0.006 × 1500 = 9.0 degrees per ms.
  3. For t = 1.4 ms: θ₁ = 9.0 × 1.4 = 12.6 crank degrees.
  4. For t = 1.0 ms: θ₂ = 9.0 × 1.0 = 9.0 crank degrees.
  5. Reduction = θ₁ − θ₂ = 12.6 − 9.0 = 3.6 crank degrees.

Answer. The delay is 12.6 crank degrees at 1.4 ms, 9.0 crank degrees at 1.0 ms, and the reduction is 3.6 crank degrees.

Exam traps

  • Diesel knock is not spark knock. It comes from long ignition delay and too much premixed fuel, not from a spark.
  • Cetane number is for diesel ignition quality. Octane number is for petrol knock resistance. Do not swap them.
  • Retarding injection reduces knock but too much retard lowers efficiency and raises exhaust temperature.
  • A long delay is the cause, not the cure. A short delay gives a small premixed spike and quieter combustion.
  • Knock is judged by rate of pressure rise; peak pressure alone can mislead.

3.7 Air motion: swirl, squish and tumble

Air inside a cylinder is never still. Inlet ports, the piston and the chamber shape set up large, organised rotations: swirl about the cylinder axis, squish as the piston pushes gas inward near top dead centre, and tumble about a diameter. These motions break up fuel, carry it through the charge and raise flame speed. They matter in diesel (CI) engines for fuel-air mixing and in petrol (SI) engines for fast, stable combustion. The swirl ratio compares swirl speed with engine speed.

You need: engine components from Unit 1, and CI mixture formation from Chapter 3.5.

The three motions

Air motion is the organised movement of the charge inside the cylinder. Inlet ports, valve masks and chamber shape start it during intake; the rising piston changes it near TDC. The three named patterns are:

  • Swirl: bulk rotation about the cylinder axis.
  • Squish: radial inward flow from the clearance space as the piston nears top dead centre (TDC). Some syllabi call it squish flow.
  • Tumble: bulk rotation about a diameter of the cylinder, so the charge rolls like a wheel.

These motions are not random turbulence. They are large-scale rotations with a clear direction. As they decay, they break into turbulence: small, random eddies that mix fuel and air at the smallest scale. Organised motion and turbulence are linked, but not the same. The purpose is practical. In a diesel (CI) engine, air motion must reach the injected fuel and mix it with air. In a petrol (SI) engine, air motion must wrinkle the flame front and carry the flame through the charge quickly. Without organised motion, mixing is slow and combustion is uneven.

Swirl and swirl ratio

Swirl is rotation of the whole charge about the cylinder axis. A directed inlet port, a helical port or a masked valve can create it. The swirl ratio compares swirl speed with engine speed:

R<sub>s</sub> = N<sub>swirl</sub> / N<sub>engine</sub>

Rs is swirl ratio (no units); Nswirl is swirl speed in rpm; Nengine is engine speed in rpm. The rpm units cancel, so the ratio has no units.

A ratio of 2.2 means the charge turns 2.2 times for each engine revolution. In a CI engine, swirl carries the fuel spray around the chamber. Fuel that leaves the nozzle into moving air meets fresh oxygen sooner. This shortens the mixing-controlled burn and reduces smoke. In an SI engine, swirl can raise flame speed by increasing turbulence near the spark plug. Too much swirl can increase heat loss to the walls and pump loss. The right amount depends on chamber shape and speed. See Chapter 3.8 for chamber designs that use swirl.

Squish, tumble and the link to combustion

Squish is the radial inward flow that appears as the piston nears TDC. The squish area is the flat part of the piston crown or head outside the bowl. The squish clearance is the gap between piston and head at TDC. As the piston rises, gas in that gap is pushed inward into the bowl or central chamber. The flow is strongest near TDC, when the clearance is smallest.

Squish helps in two ways. In a CI engine it disturbs the fuel spray and pushes air into the bowl, so fuel and air mix faster. In an SI engine it adds turbulence near TDC, which raises flame speed and shortens combustion. Some syllabi call squish flow by that name.

Tumble is rotation about a diameter. The charge rolls from the intake side to the exhaust side. Tumble is common in SI engines with upright intake ports. It stores motion during intake and releases it as turbulence near TDC. That turbulence raises flame speed, so the engine can run lean or with more exhaust gas recirculation.

All three motions feed turbulence. Turbulence increases the area of the flame front and the contact between fuel and air. The result is faster combustion and less cycle-to-cycle variation. See Chapter 3.2 for flame speed and pressure rise.

Worked example

Question. A four-stroke engine runs at 2000 rpm. Its swirl ratio is 2.2. The squish clearance is 2 mm and the average squish velocity is 10 m/s. Find the swirl speed in rpm and the time for the squish flow to move 2 mm. Give the time in milliseconds.

  1. Swirl ratio definition: Rs = Nswirl / Nengine, so Nswirl = Rs × Nengine.
  2. Substitute: Nswirl = 2.2 × 2000 rpm = 4400 rpm.
  3. Squish distance: s = 2 mm = 0.002 m.
  4. Average velocity: v = 10 m/s. Time t = s / v = 0.002 m / 10 m/s = 0.0002 s.
  5. Convert to milliseconds: t = 0.0002 s × 1000 ms/s = 0.2 ms.

Answer. The swirl speed is 4400 rpm and the squish flow takes 0.2 ms to move 2 mm.

Exam traps

  • Swirl ratio is not an angle. It is Nswirl / Nengine and has no units.
  • Squish is radial inward flow as the piston nears TDC. It is not rotation about the cylinder axis.
  • Swirl and tumble have different axes: swirl about the cylinder axis, tumble about a diameter.
  • In time calculations, convert mm to m. Mixing mm with m/s is a common unit slip.
  • Swirl is organised motion. Turbulence is small random eddies. Swirl can create turbulence, but the two terms are not interchangeable.

3.8 Combustion chambers for SI and CI engines

A combustion chamber is the space the burning charge fills. Its shape sets mixing, flame speed, and whether hot metal or carbon ignites the charge on its own. In an SI engine the mixture is prepared outside the cylinder, so the chamber burns it quickly without knock. In a CI engine fuel is injected into hot air, so the chamber moves air and spreads the spray. Wedge, hemispherical and bathtub chambers are SI types; bowl-in-piston and prechamber chambers are CI types.

You need Chapter 3.1 on SI stages, 3.4 on knock, 3.5 on CI stages and 3.7 on swirl, squish and tumble.

What the chamber must do

The combustion chamber is the space above the piston at top dead centre. Its volume is the clearance volume Vc. Its shape decides mixing, burn rate and knock. Every design is a compromise between these demands.

  • Good mixing. Turbulence and squish bring fuel and air together. In CI engines mixing time is only a few milliseconds.
  • Short flame travel. The flame should start near the centre and reach the far wall quickly; late-burning end gas knocks.
  • No hot spots. Sharp edges, a hot exhaust valve, a protruding plug tip or glowing carbon can ignite the charge before the spark.
  • Good breathing. Large valves and open ports raise volumetric efficiency and power.
  • Low heat loss. A compact chamber has less wall area, so less heat escapes and cold starting is easier.
CR = (V<sub>s</sub> + V<sub>c</sub>) / V<sub>c</sub>

CR is compression ratio (no unit); Vs is swept volume in cm³; Vc is clearance volume in cm³. Any piston bowl is part of Vc.

SI chamber shapes: wedge, hemispherical, bathtub

An SI engine prepares its mixture outside the cylinder, so the chamber only has to burn it fast and without knock. Three shapes cover most engines.

  • Wedge chamber. The head is a shallow wedge with tilted valves in a row. The rising piston squeezes air from the thin end; that squish adds turbulence near the plug. It is cheap, gives good squish, and suits two-valve pushrod engines. Flame travel across the wedge is long, so large-bore versions knock more easily.
  • Hemispherical chamber. The head is a dome, valves are inclined on both sides and the plug is central. Flame travel is shortest, breathing is best because large valves fit, and high compression ratios suit it. The dome encloses its volume with the smallest wall area of the three shapes, so heat loss is low. Its drawback is negligible squish, that is, low turbulence, which slows the burn at part load; higher unburnt hydrocarbon comes from wall quenching and poor turbulence, not from a large surface area.
  • Bathtub chamber. A flat head with a shallow tub-shaped recess over the piston and valves in line. It is simple and cheap, gives useful squish and swirl, and is common on small two-valve engines. Breathing is weaker than a hemispherical head because the valves cannot be made large.

Squish and swirl (Chapter 3.7) separate these shapes in practice. The wedge and bathtub generate squish; the hemispherical does not.

CI chambers: direct and indirect injection

A diesel engine injects fuel into hot air, so its chamber must move air and spread the spray. Two families exist.

  • Direct injection (DI). The chamber is open and fuel is sprayed into a bowl in the piston crown. A helical inlet port makes swirl, and the rising piston pushes squish flow into the bowl. The bowl may be shallow and wide (toroidal) or deep with a re-entrant lip that turns the spray upward. Injection pressure is high, the spray must match the bowl and swirl, and fuel economy and cold starting are best. It is noisier because the premixed burn is sharp.
  • Indirect injection (IDI). The chamber is divided. Fuel is sprayed into a small prechamber or swirl chamber joined to the main chamber by a narrow throat. During compression air rushes in through the throat; combustion starts there and the burning jet shoots back, mixing hard. Injection pressure can be low, the burn is smoother and quieter, and the engine runs over a wider speed range. The throat surface loses heat, so fuel economy is poorer and a glow plug is often needed for cold starting.

The delay period and diesel knock (3.5, 3.6) depend on which layout you have.

Factors that shape the design

  • Bore. Flame travel grows roughly with half the bore. A large bore in an SI engine lengthens the flame path and increases knock, so big petrol engines use a central plug or two plugs.
  • Compression ratio. A high CR raises thermal efficiency but also peak pressure and temperature. In SI engines it is limited by knock; in CI engines a high CR shortens ignition delay and helps cold starting.
  • Valve size and number. Four valves allow a central plug or injector, better breathing and a compact chamber. Two valves restrict valve area and push the plug to one side.
  • Injection location and spray. In DI the injector is central and the spray angle must fit the bowl; a wrong angle wets the wall and makes smoke. In IDI the injector sits in the prechamber, aimed at the throat.
  • Cooling. The exhaust valve side runs hottest and glows first, so the chamber keeps the plug and end gas away from it. Cooling system design is in Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. A CI engine has bore 100 mm and stroke 110 mm. Its DI bowl is 55 mm diameter and 18 mm deep. Clearance volume is 70 cm³. Estimate the bowl volume as a cylinder and its percentage of clearance volume.

  2. Vbowl = (π/4) × d² × h.
  3. d² = 30.25 cm².
  4. (π/4) × 30.25 = 23.758 cm².
  5. Vbowl = 23.758 × 1.8 = 42.76 cm³.
  6. Percentage = (42.76 / 70) × 100 = 61.09 %.
  7. The bowl holds most of the clearance volume in a DI engine; here it is about 61 %, the rest being the squish gap above the crown.

Answer. The bowl volume is about 42.76 cm³, which is about 61.1 % of the 70 cm³ clearance volume.

Apply A DI diesel has a bowl 60 mm diameter and 15 mm deep, with clearance volume 65 cm³. Estimate the bowl volume as a cylinder and its percentage of clearance volume. 4 marks

d = 6.0 cm, h = 1.5 cm. V = (π/4) × 36 × 1.5 = 0.7854 × 54 = 42.41 cm³. Percentage = 42.41 / 65 × 100 = 65.2 %.

Analyze Why does a large bore make an SI engine more knock-prone, and what do designers do about it? 5 marks

Flame travel grows with bore, so end gas is heated longer before the flame arrives and may autoignite. Designers centralise the plug, use a compact chamber, add squish, and use two plugs in very large bores.

Analyze Why must the injector position and spray angle match the bowl shape in a DI diesel? 5 marks

The spray must meet swirling air inside the bowl, not the wall. A wall film burns late and makes smoke; a spray that misses the bowl mixes too slowly and lengthens ignition delay.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern car diesels use a re-entrant bowl with a central multi-hole injector and high injection pressure; the bowl is tuned in CFD before machining. Petrol engines use a pent-roof head with a central plug under four valves, a refined hemispherical idea. Optical engines and laser diagnostics study mixing (Unit 6). Low-temperature modes such as HCCI and RCCI need mixing before ignition rather than after injection. Search terms: pent-roof chamber, re-entrant bowl, squish area ratio, spray-bowl matching.

Takeaways

  1. SI combustion runs through ignition lag, flame propagation and afterburning, and only the middle stage is fast enough to matter for pressure rise.
  2. Flame speed controls how fast the charge burns; mass burning rate controls how fast pressure rises, and the two are linked but not identical.
  3. Knock starts after the spark in the end gas, pre-ignition starts before the spark at a hot spot, and detonation is knock severe enough to send pressure waves through the chamber.
  4. Knock depends on fuel, engine design and operating conditions together, so any single change can help or hurt depending on the others.
  5. CI combustion has four stages, and the ignition delay at the start decides how much fuel burns in the violent premixed phase.
  6. Diesel knock is cured by shortening the delay or cutting the fuel that accumulates during it, using cetane number, intake temperature, injection timing and chamber design.
  7. Swirl, squish and tumble are ordered air motions that break into turbulence at TDC and decide how well fuel and air mix in both engine types.
  8. A good combustion chamber mixes the charge, burns it quickly, breathes well and keeps hot spots away; SI and CI engines meet these goals with quite different shapes.

Apply

Apply An SI engine knocks after a tune-up that raised compression ratio and advanced the spark. Using the factors affecting knock, explain what happened and suggest four corrective steps. 12 marks

Higher compression ratio raises end gas temperature and pressure; Advanced spark gives the end gas more time at high pressure; Correctives: retard spark, lower compression ratio, use higher octane fuel, cool the intake charge, reduce deposit-forming richness, redesign chamber to shorten flame travel; Explain each corrective in terms of end gas temperature or reaction time.

Apply A diesel engine shows a sharp premixed heat release spike and audible knock. Describe five methods of controlling diesel knock and explain how each works. 10 marks

Use higher cetane fuel to shorten the delay; Advance injection timing or use pilot injection to cut fuel accumulation; Raise intake air temperature and compression ratio to shorten delay; Increase air motion so mixing during the delay improves; Re-entrant bowl or IDI chamber to limit premixed burn; Smaller ignition delay means less fuel ready to burn at once.

Apply Compare a DI and an IDI diesel combustion chamber for a small tractor engine and a large truck engine, and justify your choice on heat loss, mixing, cost and fuel quality tolerance. 12 marks

DI: high efficiency, low heat loss, needs high injection pressure and good fuel; IDI: strong swirl in pre-chamber, faster mixing, tolerates poorer fuel, lower efficiency due to throat and heat loss; Truck: DI for fuel economy and power; Tractor: IDI simple, quieter, cheaper, tolerates fuel quality; Mention injection location and spray in each.

Apply For an SI engine, explain how increasing flame speed changes the rate of pressure rise and the tendency to knock. 10 marks

Faster flame speed burns the charge earlier and shortens the time the end gas spends at high pressure; Steeper dp/dθ and peak pressure closer to TDC; Less time for pre-flame reactions in the end gas, so knock tendency falls; Ways to raise flame speed: turbulence, short flame travel, central plug, richer mixture; Trade-off: very fast burn raises mechanical loading and NOx.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare ignition lag in an SI engine with ignition delay in a CI engine, and explain how each is linked to knocking in its own engine. 14 marks

SI lag: chemical and physical, ends when flame nucleus forms; long lag gives more end gas reaction time; CI delay: physical atomisation and vaporisation plus chemical pre-flame reactions; SI long lag is only mildly important because the spark is fixed, but retarded spark increases knock; CI long delay causes fuel accumulation and violent premixed burn; Both are shortened by higher charge temperature and pressure; Conclusion: in SI the end gas time scale matters, in CI the accumulated fuel mass matters.

Analyze Examine the statement: 'A long ignition delay is the only cause of diesel knock.' Support or refute it with reference to chamber design and operating conditions. 13 marks

Delay is the main cause, but rate of pressure rise depends also on fuel quantity in the delay and on mixing; Poor air motion increases the accumulated fuel; Low compression ratio, cold starting, light load all lengthen delay; IDI chamber geometry and injection characteristics change the premixed spike; Verdict: delay is dominant but not the only factor.

Evaluate Evaluate the conflicting demands in combustion chamber design for SI engines and judge which objectives you would relax first in a low-cost small car engine. 12 marks

Good breathing versus high compression ratio versus short flame travel; No hot spots versus low cost of manufacture; Low heat loss versus fast burn; Relax low heat loss or use a simple bathtub shape, keeping detonation control through spark timing and fuel quality; Justify with efficiency, knock limit and emission trade-offs.

Evaluate Assess how swirl, squish and tumble each affect delay period, rate of pressure rise and emissions in SI and CI engines, and state the limits of increasing them. 13 marks

Swirl and squish raise turbulence, shorten delay, speed up burning; Higher turbulence increases heat transfer and reduces volumetric efficiency; Excessive swirl causes spray impingement and higher NOx; Squish limited by clearance and by tendency to increase heat loss; Trade-off between fast mixing and pumping or heat-loss penalties.

Glossary

Air–fuel ratio
Mass of air supplied divided by mass of fuel supplied in the charge; petrol engines run near 14.7:1 at stoichiometric.
Autoignition
Spontaneous ignition of a fuel–air mixture when its temperature reaches the point where the pre-flame reactions run away on their own.
Bore
The internal diameter of the engine cylinder, in mm.
Bowl-in-piston
The recess in the piston crown of a DI diesel engine in which fuel is injected and mixing takes place.
Cetane number
Measure of a diesel fuel's ease of self-ignition; a higher cetane number means a shorter ignition delay.
Cooling
Removal of heat from the cylinder walls, head and piston by water or air so that hot spots and pre-ignition are avoided.
Delay period
The CI ignition delay measured in crank angle degrees or milliseconds, ending when the first detectable heat release appears.
Flame front
The thin moving zone where the reaction is taking place and that separates burnt products from unburnt charge.
Heat release rate
Rate at which chemical energy of the fuel is set free in the cylinder, usually plotted against crank angle.
Homogeneous charge compression ignition
A low-temperature combustion concept in which a pre-mixed lean charge ignites by compression at many points at once.
Ignition advance
Degrees by which the spark or injection is set before top dead centre.
Knock-limited
Describing the highest compression ratio or spark advance an engine can use before knock sets in.
Lean burn
Running an SI engine with excess air to raise efficiency and reduce knock, at the cost of ignition stability.
Octane number
Measure of a petrol's resistance to knock, compared with iso-octane as 100 and n-heptane as 0.
Peak pressure
The maximum cylinder pressure in the cycle, usually in bar, and the crank angle at which it occurs.
Pre-chamber
A small auxiliary chamber used in IDI engines, connected to the main chamber by a narrow throat.
Spark timing
Crank angle at which the spark occurs, usually expressed as degrees before top dead centre.
Squish band
The narrow gap between the piston crown and the cylinder head at TDC, from which charge is squeezed inward.
Surge tank
The volume on an engine test bed that smooths intake pulsations so the airflow measurement is steady.
Turbocharging
Using an exhaust-driven turbine to compress the intake air and pack more mass into the cylinder.
Turbulence
Random, small-scale, swirling motion of the gas that wrinkles the flame front and speeds up burning.
Volumetric efficiency
Mass of air actually drawn in per cycle divided by the mass that would fill the swept volume at ambient conditions.
