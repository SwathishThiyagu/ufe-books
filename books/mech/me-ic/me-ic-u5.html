5.1 Engine performance parameters and efficiencies

An engine test gives three raw measurements: torque at the shaft, speed of the shaft, and the rate at which fuel disappears. Every performance number in this chapter is built from those three. Torque and speed give brake power, fuel flow and calorific value give fuel power, and efficiency is one divided by the other. Mean effective pressure rescales torque by engine size, so a 100 cc scooter engine and a 10-litre truck engine can be compared honestly. Datasheets, test beds and the fuel-consumption maps manufacturers publish all use these same definitions.

You need: bore, stroke, swept volume and the difference between a four-stroke and a two-stroke cycle (see the topics on engine terminology and on four-stroke and two-stroke engines).

Power is torque times speed

On the test bed only the shaft carries work out of the engine, so two quantities are measured there: torque T, the turning moment in newton metres (N·m), and speed N, the rotation rate in revolutions per second (rev/s). One revolution turns the shaft through 2π radians, so each revolution delivers work of 2πT joules.

BP = 2πNT

BP is brake power in watts (W); N is shaft speed in rev/s; T is torque in N·m. If the tachometer reads rpm, use BP = 2πNT/60 with N in rpm, or divide rpm by 60 first.

Brake refers to the brake that loads the engine, so BP is the power handed to the outside world. The gas inside the cylinder pushes harder than the shaft delivers. The rate at which the gas does work on the piston is the indicated power IP, taken from an indicator diagram (chapter 5.2). The shortfall is friction power FP = IP − BP: rings on the bore, bearings, and the oil and water pumps. Mechanical efficiency is the fraction that survives, ηmech = BP/IP = 1 − FP/IP.

Mean effective pressure: the size-independent number

Torque alone cannot compare two engines of different size. Mean effective pressure puts them on a common footing. It is a fictitious constant pressure that, acting on the piston through the whole stroke, would produce the same work per cycle as the real, constantly changing pressure.

BP = bmep × Vd × N / nR

bmep is brake mean effective pressure in pascals (N/m²); Vd is the swept volume in m³; N is speed in rev/s; nR = 2 for a four-stroke (one cycle in two revolutions) and nR = 1 for a two-stroke. Equating this to BP = 2πNT gives bmep = 2π nR T / Vd.

Put imep in place of bmep, and indicated torque in place of T, to get indicated mean effective pressure. Then fmep = imep − bmep. All three are quoted in bar, where 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa. A naturally aspirated petrol engine reaches roughly 9–12 bar at peak torque; a turbocharged diesel reaches 18–25 bar. The factor nR stays visible in every formula, because forgetting it is the commonest error in this chapter.

Thermal efficiency and air breathing

Fuel power is the rate at which chemical energy enters the engine: fuel power = mf × CV, where mf is fuel mass flow in kg/s and CV is the calorific value in J/kg (lower calorific value about 44 MJ/kg for petrol and 42.5 MJ/kg for diesel).

ηbth = BP / (mf × CV) and ηith = IP / (mf × CV)

ηbth is brake thermal efficiency, ηith is indicated thermal efficiency, both pure fractions (multiply by 100 for per cent). They are linked by ηbth = ηith × ηmech.

Volumetric efficiency measures breathing: ηvol = mass of air actually trapped per cycle ÷ (ρa × Vd), where ρa is the density of air outside the engine in kg/m³. A naturally aspirated engine at full throttle manages 0.80–0.90; throttling at part load drops it far below. Relative efficiency compares the real engine with the ideal one, ηrel = ηith / ηair-std, the air-standard efficiency at the same compression ratio (see Ideal air-standard cycles and their comparison). It isolates losses the ideal cycle ignores: heat transfer, slow combustion, imperfect valve timing. Equivalence ratio is φ = (F/A) / (F/A)stoich, actual fuel-air ratio over stoichiometric fuel-air ratio; petrol needs about 14.7 kg air per kg fuel, diesel about 14.5, so φ < 1 is lean and φ > 1 is rich.

Specific fuel consumption, specific power and the curves

Brake specific fuel consumption is the fuel bill per unit of work: BSFC = mf / BP, with mf in kg/h and BP in kW, giving kg/kWh; multiply by 1000 for g/kWh. Indicated specific fuel consumption is ISFC = mf / IP, and BSFC = ISFC / ηmech. A good petrol engine reaches about 250 g/kWh near its best point, a diesel about 200 g/kWh because of its higher compression ratio. Specific power compares engines by size: BP / Vd in kW per litre, and BP / mass in kW per kg. Turbocharging lifts both (see Supercharging: objectives, types and effects).

Three curves carry most of the marks. At fixed throttle, BP climbs with speed because more cycles run each second, then falls when friction and breathing losses win; bmep peaks at a lower speed than BP. BSFC is a U against speed: high at low speed, lowest where ηbth is highest, rising again at high speed. Against load at fixed speed, BSFC is poor at light load, because friction is a nearly fixed tax on a small output, and best around three-quarter load.

Worked example

Question. A single-cylinder four-stroke engine has bore 100 mm and stroke 120 mm. At 1500 rpm it holds a bmep of 6 bar and burns 2.4 kg/h of fuel of calorific value 43 MJ/kg. Mechanical efficiency is 85 %. Find the swept volume, brake power, torque, brake thermal efficiency, BSFC, indicated power and friction power.

  1. Swept volume: Vd = (π/4) × D² × L = (π/4) × (0.100 m)² × (0.120 m) = 9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³, that is 0.9425 litre.
  2. Speed in rev/s: N = 1500/60 = 25 rev/s. The engine is a four-stroke, so nR = 2.
  3. Brake power: BP = bmep × Vd × N/nR = (6 × 10⁵ Pa) × (9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³) × (25/2) = 7069 W = 7.07 kW.
  4. Torque: T = BP/(2πN) = 7069 W / (2π × 25) = 45.0 N·m.
  5. Fuel flow: mf = 2.4/3600 = 6.667 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s. Fuel power = mf × CV = 6.667 × 10⁻⁴ × 43 × 10⁶ = 28 667 W = 28.67 kW.
  6. Brake thermal efficiency: ηbth = BP/fuel power = 7.069/28.67 = 0.2466 = 24.7 %.
  7. Brake specific fuel consumption: BSFC = mf/BP = 2.4/7.069 = 0.3395 kg/kWh = 339.5 g/kWh.
  8. Indicated power: IP = BP/ηmech = 7.069/0.85 = 8.32 kW.
  9. Friction power: FP = IP − BP = 8.32 − 7.07 = 1.25 kW.

Answer. Vd = 9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³, BP = 7.07 kW, T = 45.0 N·m, brake thermal efficiency = 24.7 %, BSFC = 339.5 g/kWh, IP = 8.32 kW and FP = 1.25 kW.

Exam traps

  • A four-stroke completes one cycle in two revolutions. Using BP = bmep × Vd × N instead of × N/2 doubles the answer.
  • The formula 2πNT wants rev/s. Feeding 1500 (rpm) straight in without dividing by 60 inflates the power 60 times.
  • Bar and pascal are 10⁵ apart. Convert bmep to Pa before combining it with Vd in m³, or the power comes out 10⁵ times too small.
  • BSFC is usually quoted in g/kWh but the mass flow in mf/BP must be in kg/h. A factor of 1000 slips in silently.
  • bmep is a fictitious pressure that gives the work per cycle; it is not the peak cylinder pressure, and volumetric efficiency is about air trapped, not about power surviving friction.

Analyze At constant speed, why does BSFC rise steeply as the load is reduced? 5 marks

Friction power is nearly constant while brake power falls with load, so FP/BP grows and ηmech drops. Since ηbth = ηith × ηmech, brake thermal efficiency falls, and BSFC = 1/(ηbth × CV) rises sharply. In a diesel, light load also means a weaker overall mixture and poorer combustion; a throttled petrol engine instead loses more to pumping.

Analyze A two-stroke and a four-stroke have the same swept volume, the same bmep and the same speed. Compare their torques and powers. 5 marks

T = bmep × Vd / (2π nR), so at the same bmep, swept volume and speed the two-stroke (nR = 1) makes twice the torque of the four-stroke (nR = 2), because it completes one cycle every revolution instead of every second revolution. BP = bmep × Vd × N / nR, so the two-stroke also makes twice the power at the same speed.

5.2 Measuring indicated, brake and friction power

An engine makes two powers at once: indicated power at the piston face and brake power at the shaft. Only brake power is measured directly, with a brake. Indicated power comes from the p-V loop or a Morse test; friction power from the motoring test, Willans line or subtraction.

You need: the power and efficiency definitions in the chapter on performance parameters, and swept volume and the four-stroke cycle (see Four-stroke and two-stroke engines).

Three powers and one subtraction

Power leaves an engine down the shaft. The gas first spends part of its work on the engine's own friction — bearings, rings, valve train, oil and water pumps — and only the remainder reaches the shaft. Three numbers therefore exist.

Indicated power IP is the rate of work done by the gas on the piston. Brake power BP is the rate of work at the shaft. Friction power FP is the difference, and mechanical efficiency is the ratio of the two.

FP = IP − BP ηmech = BP / IP

IP, BP and FP are in the same unit, usually kW; ηmech is a pure number, often given as a percentage.

Brake power is computed from two measured quantities, torque and speed.

BP = 2πNT / 60

BP is brake power in W; T is the torque absorbed by the dynamometer in N·m; N is engine speed in rev/min; the factor 2π/60 converts rev/min to rad/s. Divide by 1000 for kW.

Torque and speed are read directly, so BP is never in doubt. IP needs a pressure record or a Morse test; FP needs a motoring test, a Willans line or a subtraction. The dynamometer itself is in the chapter on dynamometers.

Indicated power from the p-V loop

  1. Record cylinder pressure against volume for one full cycle of one cylinder.
  2. The area inside the closed loop is the net work per cycle, to the pressure and volume scales.
  3. Measure the area in mm² with a planimeter, or count squares.
  4. Divide the area by the diagram length in mm to get the mean height in mm.
  5. Multiply the mean height by the indicator spring constant in bar/mm or kPa/mm. That is the imep.

IP = imep × Vd × N / (60 × nR)

imep is indicated mean effective pressure in kPa; Vd is swept volume of one cylinder in m³; N is speed in rev/min; nR is 2 for a four-stroke and 1 for a two-stroke; IP is in kW for one cylinder. Multiply by the number of cylinders.

nR is the number of revolutions per power stroke. A four-stroke fires once in two revolutions, so at the same speed and imep it gives half the power of a two-stroke.

The old mechanical indicator scratched the loop on a card; a modern engine uses a piezoelectric transducer and a crank-angle encoder to draw the same loop in software (see the chapter on testing standards and pressure measurement. On a four-stroke the record has two loops: the large power loop of compression and expansion, and the small pumping loop of the exhaust and intake strokes, traced the other way round. Net imep comes from the power-loop area minus the pumping-loop area.

Morse test: cut one cylinder at a time

The Morse test works on engines with three or more cylinders. Hold speed and load fixed and note BP with all cylinders firing. Cut the ignition (SI) or fuel (CI) of cylinder 1 only; the engine slows, so reduce the dynamometer load until the speed is back at the same value. Note the new BP. Repeat for every cylinder.

IP = Σ (BP − BPi), i = 1 … k

BP is brake power with all k cylinders firing in kW; BPi is brake power with cylinder i cut in kW; the drop BP − BPi is the indicated power of that one cylinder.

  • Assumption 1: friction power is the same whether a cylinder fires or not.
  • Assumption 2: the firing cylinders deliver the same power as before the cut.
  • Assumption 3: the speed is brought back to the same value for every reading.
  • Then FP = IP − BP and ηmech = BP/IP. FP must come out small and positive.
  • Weakness: it needs a multi-cylinder engine, and in a petrol engine cutting one cylinder disturbs the mixture and exhaust flow of the others, so readings must be taken quickly.

Motoring test and Willans line

The motoring test measures friction directly. Couple the engine to a dynamometer, switch off fuel and ignition, and drive it at the test speed. The dynamometer then reads the power needed to turn it over, which is the friction power. It is quick and works on a single-cylinder engine. Weakness: with no combustion the walls, piston and oil are cooler and no gas pressure pushes the rings out, so the friction measured is not the friction at running temperature.

Willans line uses fuel flow. Hold speed constant and vary the load. Plot fuel consumption rate in kg/h against BP in kW. At moderate and heavy load the points lie on a straight line. Extend it to zero fuel flow: it meets the BP axis at BP = −FP, so the size of that negative intercept is FP. The assumptions are a straight line and friction power independent of load. Near zero load the line curves because combustion becomes poor, so only the straight part may be extrapolated. See the chapter on fuel and air measurement for the fuel-flow measurement.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine runs at constant speed. BP with all four cylinders firing is 20.0 kW. Cutting each cylinder in turn and restoring the same speed gives brake powers 14.2, 14.0, 14.3 and 14.1 kW. Find IP, FP and mechanical efficiency.

  1. Cylinder 1 cut: drop = BP − BP₁ = 20.0 − 14.2 = 5.8 kW, so cylinder 1 indicates 5.8 kW.
  2. Cylinder 2 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.0 = 6.0 kW.
  3. Cylinder 3 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.3 = 5.7 kW.
  4. Cylinder 4 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.1 = 5.9 kW.
  5. Total indicated power IP = 5.8 + 6.0 + 5.7 + 5.9 = 23.4 kW.
  6. Friction power FP = IP − BP = 23.4 − 20.0 = 3.4 kW.
  7. Mechanical efficiency ηmech = BP / IP = 20.0 / 23.4 = 0.855 = 85.5 %.

Answer. IP = 23.4 kW, FP = 3.4 kW and ηmech = 85.5 %.

Worked example 2

Question. A diesel engine runs at a constant 1500 rpm. At brake powers of 5, 10, 15 and 20 kW its fuel flow is 2.10, 3.30, 4.50 and 5.70 kg/h. Find the friction power by Willans line, and the indicated power and mechanical efficiency at 20 kW.

  1. Slope of the line: (5.70 − 2.10) ÷ (20 − 5) = 3.60 ÷ 15 = 0.24 kg/h per kW. Each step of 5 kW adds 1.20 kg/h, so the four points lie on one straight line.
  2. Equation: ṁf = 2.10 + 0.24 × (BP − 5) = 0.90 + 0.24 BP, with ṁf in kg/h and BP in kW.
  3. Extend to zero fuel flow: 0 = 0.90 + 0.24 BP, so BP = −0.90 ÷ 0.24 = −3.75 kW.
  4. Friction power is the size of that intercept: FP = 3.75 kW, taken as the same at every load at this speed.
  5. At 20 kW: IP = BP + FP = 20 + 3.75 = 23.75 kW, and ηmech = 20 ÷ 23.75 = 0.842 = 84.2 %.

Answer. FP = 3.75 kW; at 20 kW, IP = 23.75 kW and mechanical efficiency 84.2 %.

Exam traps

  • Adding the four cut readings (56.6 kW) instead of the four drops. A cut reading still contains the brake power of the other cylinders.
  • Reporting a negative friction power. FP is small and positive; a negative value means a cut reading was copied wrongly or BP was noted with the wrong number of cylinders firing.
  • Using N instead of N/2 in the indicated power formula for a four-stroke. That doubles IP, so the efficiency you calculate is half the true value: the 85.5 % example would come out near 43 %.
  • Mixing units in IP = imep × Vd × N/(60 nR). Use kPa with m³, or convert bar to kPa (×100) and litres to m³ (×10⁻³).

Analyze The motoring test on a cold engine gives a higher friction power than the Morse test on the same warm engine. Give two reasons. 4 marks

Cold oil is more viscous, so bearing, piston and ring friction is larger, and cooler walls keep the oil film colder and thicker still. (The missing gas pressure behind the rings works the other way, so a warm motoring test reads slightly low.)

5.3 Measuring fuel and air consumption

A power reading alone does not show how good an engine is. Judge it with the fuel and air that produced the power, measured while load is steady. Fuel is timed or weighed; air is metered by orifice, venturi or hot wire. Their mass ratio is air-fuel ratio; air flow against swept volume gives volumetric efficiency. Every test bed, type-approval run and workshop diagnosis starts here.

You need: power and efficiency from the chapter on performance parameters.

Fuel flow: burette, balance and meters

Fuel flow is a mass flow in kg/h, because combustion needs a fixed air mass per fuel mass. Three methods are common.

Volumetric method: the engine draws fuel from a glass burette with two marks setting volume V. A stopwatch times the fall between marks.

ṁf = (V × ρ × 3600) ÷ t

ṁf is fuel mass flow in kg/h; V is burette volume in L; ρ is fuel density in kg/L at fuel temperature (about 0.83 diesel, 0.74 petrol); t is time in s; 3600 converts s to h.

  1. Fill the burette above the top mark while the engine runs from the main tank.
  2. Turn the three-way cock so the engine draws only from the burette.
  3. Start the stopwatch at the top mark; stop at the bottom mark; note t.
  4. Repeat three times and average; correct ρ to fuel temperature.

The three-way cock lets the engine run from the tank while the burette refills, so filling time is never timed.

Small volume and short time increase error. A 50 mL burette read to ±1 mL is ±2 %; 25 s timed to ±0.2 s is ±0.8 %. A 5 s test makes timing error ±4 %. Use the largest volume and longest steady time.

Gravimetric method weighs fuel from a can on a balance and clocks the time to lose a set mass, say 100 g. No density is needed; accuracy is better, so it calibrates others.

  • Rotameter: float rises with flow; cheap, steady flow only, needs density correction.
  • Turbine meter: rotor speed gives volume as pulses.
  • Coriolis meter: measures mass directly; best accuracy, highest cost.
  • Gas fuels: a gas meter gives volume at meter pressure and temperature; convert it to mass with ρ = p/(R T), using the gas constant of that gas, R = 8314/M J/(kg·K) with M its molar mass, not 287.

Air flow: air box, orifice and hot wire

Air is harder to meter because intake flow pulses every stroke, so a settling volume smooths it.

Air box: a large tank the engine breathes from, with a sharp-edged orifice open to the room. The box damps pulses; the orifice sees steady flow. A U-tube manometer reads pressure drop Δp across the orifice, usually as water column h in mm.

ṁa = Cd A √(2 ρ Δp)

ṁa is air mass flow in kg/s; Cd is discharge coefficient (about 0.6 sharp-edged orifice, 0.98 venturi); A is orifice area in m²; ρ is air density in kg/m³; Δp is pressure drop in Pa, equal to 9.81 × h for h in mm of water.

Air density comes from the test room: ρ = p ÷ (R T), where p is absolute room pressure in Pa, T is room temperature in K, and R = 287 J/(kg·K) for air. A hot Chennai afternoon changes ρ by several per cent, moving air flow and volumetric efficiency.

A venturi meter uses the same relation with higher Cd and smaller permanent loss; flow benches use it. A hot-wire anemometer heats a thin wire and measures current to keep it hot; flow cools the wire, so current reads velocity. It responds fast enough for pulsating intake and in-cylinder work.

Viscous-flow air meter (the Alcock meter): the air passes through many fine passages in which the flow is laminar, so the pressure drop is directly proportional to the flow rate. Pulsations then average out correctly, whereas an orifice, whose drop goes with the square of the flow, reads high on a pulsating flow unless a large air box smooths it.

Air-fuel ratio and volumetric efficiency

Both flows must be in the same units before dividing. With ṁa and ṁf in kg/s, air-fuel ratio is AFR = ṁa ÷ ṁf, always by mass, never by volume.

Stoichiometric values are about 14.7 for petrol and 14.5 for diesel. A light-load diesel may run at 30 or more; a petrol engine at full power runs near 12.5 to 13.5.

Volumetric efficiency compares air actually trapped per cycle with the mass that would fill swept volume at intake density.

ηvol = ṁa ÷ (ρa × Vs × (N ÷ 60) ÷ 2)

ηvol is volumetric efficiency; ṁa is measured air flow in kg/s; ρa is intake air density in kg/m³; Vs is total swept volume in m³; N is engine speed in rev/min. For a four-stroke, ÷ 2 gives one intake stroke in two revolutions; a two-stroke uses (N ÷ 60) with no halving.

About 80 to 90 % is normal for a naturally aspirated four-stroke at full throttle; a well-tuned intake can come close to 95 %. A much lower value points to a blocked filter, poor valve timing or hot intake air (see the chapter on variables affecting performance).

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke engine of 2.0 L swept volume runs at 3000 rpm. Air box orifice gives 0.052 kg/s; intake air density is 1.18 kg/m³. A burette test uses 50 mL diesel of density 830 kg/m³ in 25 s. Find fuel flow in kg/h and kg/s, air-fuel ratio by mass, and volumetric efficiency.

  1. Fuel mass: m = V × ρ = 0.050 L × 0.83 kg/L = 0.0415 kg. (830 kg/m³ = 0.83 kg/L.)
  2. Fuel flow: ṁf = m × 3600 ÷ t = 0.0415 × 3600 ÷ 25 = 5.976 kg/h, i.e. 5.98 kg/h.
  3. In kg/s: 5.976 ÷ 3600 = 1.66 × 10⁻³ kg/s.
  4. AFR = ṁa ÷ ṁf = 0.052 ÷ 1.66 × 10⁻³ = 31.3 (light-load diesel).
  5. Ideal air flow for four-stroke: ṁa,ideal = ρa × Vs × (N ÷ 60) ÷ 2 = 1.18 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ × (3000 ÷ 60) ÷ 2.
  6. Numbers: 1.18 × 0.002 = 0.00236; × 50 = 0.118; ÷ 2 = 0.0590 kg/s.
  7. ηvol = 0.052 ÷ 0.0590 = 0.881 = 88.1 %.

Answer. Fuel flow 5.98 kg/h (1.66 × 10⁻³ kg/s), air-fuel ratio 31.3 by mass, volumetric efficiency 88.1 %.

Exam traps

  • Quoting AFR by volume. Air and fuel have very different densities, so the air-fuel ratio is always a mass ratio.
  • Skipping air density correction. ρ = p ÷ (R T) changes AFR and volumetric efficiency by several per cent.
  • Timing the refill. Start the stopwatch only after the three-way cock has switched the engine onto the burette, and time the fall between the two marks.
  • Forgetting ÷ 2 for a four-stroke in the ideal air flow. That doubles the ideal flow and halves the volumetric efficiency: 88.1 % would come out as 44.1 %.
  • Mixing units: mL with L, mm water with Pa, kg/h with kg/s. Convert and check answer size.

5.4 Dynamometers and engine test cells

A dynamometer is a brake that measures the torque it absorbs. The engine turns, the dynamometer resists, and a torque arm and load cell turn that resistance into a number. A test cell is the room built around that brake: rigid bed, guarded coupling, fuel and coolant lines, exhaust extraction, fire safety and a panel that logs speed, torque, temperatures and flows. Together they make an engine measurement repeatable and safe. Every brake power figure in the other chapters comes from a dynamometer.

You need: brake power from torque and speed in the chapter on measuring indicated, brake and friction power, and bmep in the chapter on performance parameters.

Absorption, transmission and driving dynamometers

A dynamometer must do two things: take the engine's power, and measure the torque. Dynamometers fall into three groups.

  • Absorption dynamometers turn the power into heat and measure the reaction torque: the Prony brake, the rope brake, the hydraulic (water) brake and the eddy-current brake.
  • Transmission dynamometers sit in the drive line and measure the torque while the power passes on to the load, from the twist of a shaft or from strain gauges on a torque flange; they absorb almost nothing.
  • Driving (motoring) dynamometers are electric machines that can absorb power as a generator and also drive the engine as a motor: the swinging-field DC machine and the AC machine.

In the Prony brake a lined block is clamped on the flywheel and a lever carries the reaction to a spring balance. Torque is force times perpendicular distance: T = W × L, where W is the net force on the arm in N (reading minus the zero reading) and L is the arm length from the shaft centre in m. In the rope brake a rope is wrapped round the flywheel rim with a dead load W hanging on one end and a spring balance reading S on the other. Then T = (W − S) × Re, where Re = (D + d)/2 is the effective radius, D the drum diameter and d the rope diameter.

BP = 2πNT/60

BP is brake power in W; N is engine speed in rev/min; T is torque in N·m. Divide by 1000 for kW.

  • Prony and rope brakes: cheap and simple, but the grip changes as they heat, they need water cooling and they suit only small engines at low speed.
  • Hydraulic brake: a rotor churns water inside a casing mounted on trunnions; the water level sets the load, and the casing reaction on a torque arm gives T. It suits large powers.
  • Eddy-current brake: a toothed rotor turns in the field of coils, and eddy currents drag on it; the coil current sets the load, so it changes quickly and smoothly. Cooling water carries the heat away. It can only absorb.
  • Swinging-field DC machine: the field casing sits on trunnions and its reaction torque is read on an arm; as a generator it absorbs power into a resistor bank, as a motor it drives the engine.

Motoring and transient dynamometers

An absorption brake can only load the engine. A motoring dynamometer is an electric machine that can also drive the engine. A DC machine motoring at controlled speed gives the motoring test: the engine is turned without fuel, and the shaft power needed equals friction power (see the chapter on measuring indicated, brake and friction power). The same machine can generate, so one unit covers both directions.

A transient dynamometer goes further. It changes speed and torque fast enough to follow a driving cycle second by second. An AC dynamometer uses a vector-controlled induction or synchronous machine with low inertia. It works in four quadrants: forward motoring, forward generating, reverse motoring, reverse generating. That lets it reproduce a stop-start city pattern, a cold start, or a turbo lag event.

An absorption dynamometer cannot do this. It cannot motor the engine, so it cannot follow the overrun (engine-braking) parts of a cycle, and friction and water brakes respond too slowly to follow a prescribed speed-torque trace. An eddy-current brake responds fast but still only absorbs.

The test cell makes the measurement repeatable

A test cell is a room designed around the dynamometer. The engine sits on a rigid bed, often on anti-vibration mounts, aligned to the dynamometer shaft. The coupling between engine and dynamometer carries a fixed guard: a spinning shaft at 5000 rev/min must never be exposed.

Services are fixed: fuel supply with a measured tank or flow meter (see the chapter on fuel and air measurement), coolant circuit with a heat exchanger and temperature control, ventilation to supply combustion air and remove fumes, exhaust extraction to carry gases outside, and fire detection with a clean-agent extinguisher.

The instrument panel logs speed, torque, temperatures and flows at the same time. Repeatability comes from keeping all of these the same from run to run.

  1. Warm the engine to a steady coolant temperature.
  2. Set the speed and load on the dynamometer controller.
  3. Log torque, speed, fuel flow and temperatures together.
  4. Repeat the point once to check drift.

Worked example

Question. A hydraulic dynamometer has a torque arm of 0.5 m. At 1500 rev/min the spring balance reads 120 N. The engine is a 1.0 L four-stroke. Find the torque, the brake power and the bmep.

  1. Torque: T = W × L = 120 N × 0.5 m = 60 N·m.
  2. Angular speed: ω = 2πN/60 = 2π × 1500 ÷ 60 = 157.08 rad/s. Brake power: BP = T × ω = 60 × 157.08 = 9425 W = 9.42 kW.
  3. Swept volume: Vd = 1.0 L = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m³; a four-stroke has nR = 2.
  4. bmep = 2π nR T / Vd = 2π × 2 × 60 ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻³) = 7.54 × 10⁵ Pa = 7.54 bar.

Answer. T = 60 N·m, BP = 9.42 kW, bmep = 7.54 bar.

Worked example 2

Question. A rope brake is fitted to a flywheel of diameter 1.0 m with a rope of diameter 20 mm. At 600 rev/min the dead load is 300 N and the spring balance reads 50 N. Find the torque and the brake power.

  1. Effective radius: Re = (D + d)/2 = (1.0 + 0.020) ÷ 2 = 0.51 m.
  2. Net force: W − S = 300 − 50 = 250 N.
  3. Torque: T = 250 × 0.51 = 127.5 N·m.
  4. Brake power: BP = 2π × 600 × 127.5 ÷ 60 = 8011 W = 8.01 kW.

Answer. T = 127.5 N·m and BP = 8.01 kW.

Exam traps

  • Using the drum diameter as the arm of a rope brake. The arm is the effective radius (D + d)/2, 0.51 m in worked example 2, not 1.0 m.
  • Forgetting the zero reading of a torque-arm balance, or adding the spring reading in a rope brake. The net force is the reading minus the zero, or W − S.
  • Dropping the 60. With N in rev/min, BP = 2πNT/60; leaving out the 60 makes the power 60 times too large. With ω in rad/s, use BP = Tω.
  • Mixing mm and m. A 500 mm arm is 0.5 m, not 500.
  • Confusing bmep with brake power. bmep is pressure, in bar or Pa; BP is power, in kW.

5.5 Heat balance

The engine takes chemical energy in with the fuel and turns part of it into work at the shaft. The rest leaves as heat, and it must go somewhere: into the cooling water, up the exhaust pipe, or out through the block by radiation and convection. A heat balance is a sheet that lists these streams in kW and as a percentage of the fuel power, and shows that they add up. It is how a test cell answers the question 'where did the fuel money go?', and it is how radiators and exhaust heat recovery equipment are sized.

You need: fuel power and brake thermal efficiency from the chapter on performance parameters, and brake power and fuel flow measurement from the chapter on fuel and air measurement.

The sheet, and why it must close

Chemical energy enters the engine at one rate and leaves at several. The rate at which the fuel brings energy in is the fuel power:

Pf = mf × CV / 3.6

Pf is the fuel power in kW, mf is the fuel mass flow in kg/h, CV is the calorific value of the fuel in MJ/kg, and 3.6 converts kg/h × MJ/kg into kW.

On the output side there are four streams: brake power Pb at the shaft, heat to the cooling water Pc, heat carried away by the exhaust gas Pe, and the rest Pu, which covers radiation, convection, heat to the lubricating oil (when an oil cooler is not measured separately) and any fuel that did not burn completely. Energy is conserved, so:

Pf = Pb + Pc + Pe + Pu

Pf is fuel power, Pb is brake power, Pc is heat to cooling water, Pe is heat in the exhaust gas, and Pu is the unaccounted remainder, all in kW.

Test cells draw a heat balance for every new engine. Each term is then also written as a percentage of Pf.

Measuring each stream

Brake power comes from the dynamometer (see the chapter on measuring indicated, brake and friction power. The cooling water term comes from the water itself:

Pc = mw × cpw × ΔTw

mw is the cooling water mass flow in kg/s, cpw is the specific heat of water, 4.18 kJ/kgK, and ΔTw is the rise in water temperature across the jacket in K.

The exhaust term is the sensible heat in the gas above ambient:

Pe = mg × cpg × ΔTg

mg is the exhaust gas mass flow in kg/s, cpg is about 1.05 kJ/kgK for exhaust gas, and ΔTg is the exhaust temperature minus the ambient air temperature in K.

  • Water flow by rotameter, or by filling a bucket for a measured time; water temperatures by thermometers at the jacket inlet and outlet.
  • Exhaust gas flow is air flow plus fuel flow, mg = ma + mf, both measured as in the chapter on fuel and air measurement. Using air alone understates Pe.
  • An exhaust gas calorimeter checks the exhaust figure: the gas passes through a water-cooled heat exchanger, and the exhaust heat is the heat taken by the calorimeter water plus the heat still in the gas leaving it, Pe = mwc × cpw × ΔTwc + mg × cpg × (Tout − Troom).
  • Pu is not measured. It is Pf minus the other three terms.

Reading and drawing the sheet

Two rules keep the sheet honest. First, every percentage is taken on Pf, never on Pb. Second, all four terms must be in the same unit before they are added; kW throughout is easiest, so a figure in kJ/h must be divided by 3600 first.

Draw the sheet as a bar or a divided block: one long bar of 100 % cut into four pieces, largest first. A pie chart with four thin slices is hard to read at a glance; a bar shows at once that brake power, cooling water and exhaust each take close to a third of the fuel energy in the worked example below.

The unaccounted term shrinks as the engine gets hotter. A cold engine stores heat in its metal and oil, and that stored heat lands in Pu. Once the engine holds a steady temperature, the storage stops and Pu falls to a small radiation and convection figure.

Worked example

Question. An engine burns 3.0 kg/h of fuel of calorific value 42 MJ/kg and delivers 10.5 kW at the brake. It draws 90 kg/h of air. Cooling water flows at 8 kg/min and rises 18 °C across the jacket. The exhaust leaves 400 K above room temperature, with cpg = 1.05 kJ/(kg·K). Draw up the heat balance in kW and in percent.

  1. Fuel power: Pf = mf × CV / 3.6 = 3.0 × 42 ÷ 3.6 = 35.0 kW.
  2. Cooling water: mw = 8 ÷ 60 = 0.1333 kg/s, so Pc = 0.1333 × 4.18 × 18 = 10.03 kW.
  3. Exhaust gas flow: mg = ma + mf = 90 + 3.0 = 93 kg/h = 93 ÷ 3600 = 0.02583 kg/s. (The air-fuel ratio is 90 ÷ 3.0 = 30, a normal diesel value.)
  4. Exhaust heat: Pe = 0.02583 × 1.05 × 400 = 10.85 kW.
  5. Unaccounted: Pu = 35.0 − 10.5 − 10.03 − 10.85 = 3.62 kW.
  6. Percentages of fuel power: brake 10.5 ÷ 35.0 = 30.0 %; cooling 10.03 ÷ 35.0 = 28.7 %; exhaust 10.85 ÷ 35.0 = 31.0 %; unaccounted 3.62 ÷ 35.0 = 10.3 %.
  7. Check: 30.0 + 28.7 + 31.0 + 10.3 = 100.0 %, so the sheet closes on fuel power.

Answer. Fuel power 35.0 kW splits into 10.5 kW brake (30.0 %), 10.03 kW to cooling water (28.7 %), 10.85 kW in the exhaust (31.0 %) and 3.62 kW unaccounted (10.3 %).

Exam traps

  • The sheet closes on fuel power, not on brake power. Percentages taken on the 10.5 kW of brake power would give 100 % for the brake and nonsense for the rest.
  • Exhaust mass flow is air plus fuel, ma + mf, not air alone; using air alone loses the fuel mass and understates the exhaust heat.
  • Never mix units on one sheet: kW, kJ/h and kcal/min must all be converted to a single unit before adding.
  • Use the temperature rise, not the absolute temperature: ΔTg is exhaust minus ambient, and ΔTw is outlet minus inlet. A rise of 18 K is the same as a rise of 18 °C.
  • Mass flows must be in kg/s before multiplying by cp × ΔT: divide kg/h by 3600 and kg/min by 60.

5.6 Variables affecting engine performance

Any variable that changes the trapped air mass, the mixture strength or the timing moves brake power, brake thermal efficiency and emissions together. Weather matters too, so ratings are quoted at agreed reference air conditions, and a result measured on a hot day is corrected to those conditions before it is compared with the maker's figure. Tuning a vehicle for altitude, humidity or fuel quality uses the same ideas. The aim of this chapter is the shape of each curve and the reason for it, not just a list.

You need: the measured quantities from the chapters on performance parameters and fuel and air measurement, plus spark advance and knock from the ignition and combustion topics.

Speed, load and the shape of BSFC

Speed changes three things at once: the trapped air mass per cycle, friction and pumping loss. Indicated power rises with speed until breathing becomes the limit, then falls. Friction power rises faster than indicated power at high speed, so the gap between indicated and brake power grows.

Brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) is fuel mass flow divided by brake power. Against speed it has a minimum at mid-speed: at low speed heat loss and friction are a large share of each cycle, and at high speed friction and pumping are large. Brake thermal efficiency is inversely proportional to BSFC, ηbth = 3600 / (BSFC × LCV) with BSFC in kg/kWh and LCV in kJ/kg, so the speed of minimum BSFC is also the speed of maximum brake thermal efficiency.

Load has a similar curve. At low load, friction and auxiliary losses dominate, so BSFC is high. It falls as load rises and is lowest around three-quarter load. At full load, SI engines enrich the mixture and CI engines reach the smoke limit, so BSFC rises again.

BSFC = ṁf / BP

ṁf is fuel mass flow in kg/h and BP is brake power in kW, so BSFC is in kg/kWh. LCV is the lower calorific value of the fuel in kJ/kg.

Mixture strength, equivalence ratio and timing

Mixture strength is best described by the equivalence ratio φ, the actual fuel-air ratio over the stoichiometric fuel-air ratio: φ = (F/A)/(F/A)stoich = (A/F)stoich/(A/F). φ = 1 is stoichiometric, φ > 1 is rich and φ < 1 is lean.

For SI engines, maximum brake power comes from a slightly rich mixture, φ about 1.1: all the oxygen of the trapped air is used and the flame is fast. Best brake thermal efficiency comes from a slightly lean mixture, φ about 0.85 to 0.95: all the fuel burns, the burnt gas has a higher ratio of specific heats and less dissociation, less heat is lost to the walls, and at part load the throttle opens wider, cutting pumping loss. Very rich mixtures raise CO and HC; very lean mixtures burn slowly and can misfire. In CI engines the overall mixture is always lean, and power is limited by smoke.

Spark timing or injection timing sets where combustion occurs relative to top dead centre. Maximum brake torque (MBT) timing gives the highest brake torque at a given speed and mixture; it places the peak pressure roughly 15° after TDC. Too much advance raises peak pressure, causes knock and raises NOx. Too much retard burns the charge late in the expansion stroke, so less work is obtained, the exhaust temperature rises and HC can rise. Timing and mixture together set CO, HC and NOx (see Formation of CO, unburnt hydrocarbons and NOx).

Air, temperature, deposits and fuel

Intake air density sets the trapped air mass. Density is ρ = p/(R T), where p is absolute pressure in Pa, T is absolute temperature in K and R is the gas constant for air, 287 J/(kg·K). A hot day or a high-altitude site lowers density, so brake power falls unless the engine is boosted. Humidity adds water vapour, which displaces some air and gives a small loss. Test standards correct the measured power to reference air temperature, pressure and humidity, so observed power and corrected power are different numbers; always state which one you mean (see the chapter on testing standards and pressure measurement).

Coolant and oil temperature matter. Cold coolant and cold oil raise friction and leave the fuel poorly vaporised, so BSFC rises. Very hot coolant can cause knock and thin the oil. Carbon deposits raise the compression ratio, create hot spots and restrict breathing, so they can cause knock and raise NOx. Valve clearance that is too tight holds the valve open, leaks compression and can burn the valve; too loose reduces lift and makes noise. Exhaust back pressure raises pumping work and reduces scavenging.

Fuel quality matters. Octane number controls SI knock; cetane number controls CI ignition delay; calorific value sets energy per kg; density sets the injected mass for a given volume. Compression ratio raises ideal efficiency but is limited by knock in SI engines and by peak pressure and NOx in diesels.

Worked example

Question. A naturally aspirated diesel engine gives 40 kW brake power at 25 °C and 1.00 bar on a standard day. It is retested on a hot afternoon at 40 °C and 0.90 bar. Assume brake power follows intake air density. Find the density ratio, the observed brake power and the percentage drop.

  1. Convert the temperatures to kelvin: Tref = 25 + 273 = 298 K and Ttest = 40 + 273 = 313 K.
  2. Use the density ratio: (ptest / pref) × (Tref / Ttest) = (0.90 / 1.00) × (298 / 313) = 0.8569.
  3. Multiply by the reference brake power: Pobs = 40 × 0.8569 = 34.27 kW.
  4. Find the drop: (1 − 0.8569) × 100 = 14.31 %.

Answer. Density ratio = 0.857, observed brake power = 34.27 kW, a drop of 14.31 %.

Exam traps

  • Quoting an observed power as the engine's rating. Test standards correct to reference temperature, pressure and humidity, so always say whether a figure is observed or corrected.
  • Reversing the equivalence ratio. φ > 1 is rich, not lean; φ < 1 is lean, not rich.
  • Thinking best efficiency and best power occur at the same air-fuel ratio. Best power is slightly rich; best efficiency is lean.
  • Retarding timing only to reduce NOx. Too much retard lowers efficiency, raises exhaust temperature and can raise HC and CO.
  • Blaming the engine when a hot day or high altitude lowers observed power. Intake air density has fallen; the engine may be unchanged.

5.7 Methods to improve engine performance

Power and efficiency come from four levers: more trapped air, better air-fuel mixing, less friction, and better control. Engine makers rarely find one large gain; they stack small gains. A turbocharger, a high-pressure injector, low-friction oil and a closed-loop ECU each move brake power, brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) or both. Road cars, trucks and gensets use these levers, then prove the result on a standard test.

You need: the variables in the chapter on variables affecting performance; the turbocharger hardware is covered under Supercharging: objectives, types and effects.

The four levers

Each lever changes a measurable variable. More air raises trapped mass. Better mixing shortens and shapes the burn. Less friction raises brake power for the same indicated power. Better control keeps the engine near its best point.

  • More air: turbocharging or supercharging raises intake density; an intercooler lowers charge temperature and raises density again. Four valves and good ports cut pumping loss; variable valve timing (VVT) moves the torque band.
  • Better mixture: high-pressure common rail direct injection (CRDI) gives a finer spray; electronic injection timing places the burn near top dead centre (TDC); chamber shape adds swirl, squish or tumble.
  • Less friction: low-viscosity oil with friction modifiers, surface coatings, lighter reciprocating parts, and better rings and bearings cut friction mean effective pressure (FMEP).
  • Better control: a closed-loop engine control unit (ECU) uses a lambda sensor to hold air-fuel ratio and a knock sensor to protect spark advance.

The levers interact: more air needs more fuel, and control keeps the extra air useful.

Cycle changes

Changing the cycle can raise efficiency more than any single part. Miller or Atkinson operation closes the intake valve early or late, so the expansion stroke is longer than the effective compression stroke. The engine extracts more work from the same fuel. A higher compression ratio does the same, but it is limited by knock in spark-ignition (SI) engines and by peak pressure in compression-ignition (CI) engines. In a turbocharged engine, Miller timing also lowers knock and turbine inlet temperature, so boost can rise. Cylinder deactivation and stop-start are control changes: they cut pumping and idle losses when demand is low.

ηOtto = 1 − 1/rγ−1

ηOtto is the ideal Otto cycle efficiency; r is the compression ratio; γ is the ratio of specific heats, 1.4 for air. With Miller or Atkinson timing the effective compression ratio, set by intake valve closing, is lower than the geometric one, while the expansion ratio stays geometric; that difference is where the gain comes from, so do not put the geometric ratio straight into this formula.

The ideal gain is set by the compression ratio. These changes must keep combustion stable; too much Miller timing hurts combustion and torque. In practice, the valve event is tuned with the boost level.

The trade-off and proof

Every step that raises power or efficiency usually raises oxides of nitrogen (NOx) or cost. More air raises peak pressure and temperature; advancing the injection timing raises NOx; thinner oil cuts friction but can let metal touch and wear at high load; aggressive Miller timing can hurt combustion. A boost increase must be matched by fuel, or the mixture leans and power falls; the knock limit or turbine inlet temperature sets the ceiling, not the boost number. A change that wins on one point may lose on another. The engine must be proved on a standard test, not a single best point. A performance claim is brake power, BSFC and emissions at defined speed-load points. Test procedure and in-cylinder pressure are in the chapter on testing standards and pressure measurement.

Use a dynamometer and repeat the same test conditions before and after. The trade-off is why a production engine is a compromise: driveability, fuel quality, durability and emission limits all pull against the peak number.

Worked example

Question. A 2.0 L naturally aspirated petrol engine gives 80 kW. A turbocharger with an intercooler raises intake air density to 1.4 times ambient. Assume the same volumetric efficiency and air-fuel ratio. The BSFC is 240 g/kWh. Find the new power and the new fuel flow.

  1. Air density ratio = 1.4. At the same air-fuel ratio, power scales with trapped air mass: Pnew = Pold × 1.4.
  2. Pnew = 80 kW × 1.4 = 112 kW.
  3. Gain = 112 − 80 = 32 kW = 40 %.
  4. Fuel flow = BSFC × brake power. New: 240 g/kWh × 112 kW = 26,880 g/h = 26.88 kg/h.
  5. Old: 240 g/kWh × 80 kW = 19,200 g/h = 19.2 kg/h.

Answer. The new power is 112 kW, a 40 % gain, and the new fuel flow is 26.88 kg/h against 19.2 kg/h before.

Worked example 2

Question. A petrol engine of compression ratio 9.5 gives 60 kW brake power with an indicated power of 72 kW. A new head raises the compression ratio to 11, and low-friction oil cuts the friction power by 15 %. Take γ = 1.4 and assume the indicated thermal efficiency rises in the same ratio as the Otto efficiency, at the same fuel flow. Find the new brake power.

  1. Old Otto efficiency: 1 − 1/9.50.4 = 1 − 1/2.4609 = 0.5936.
  2. New Otto efficiency: 1 − 1/110.4 = 1 − 1/2.6095 = 0.6168.
  3. Ratio: 0.6168 ÷ 0.5936 = 1.0390, so the new indicated power is 72 × 1.0390 = 74.81 kW.
  4. Old friction power: 72 − 60 = 12 kW. New: 12 × 0.85 = 10.2 kW.
  5. New brake power: 74.81 − 10.2 = 64.61 kW, a gain of 4.61 kW (7.7 %) for the same fuel.

Exam traps

  • Boost alone does not raise power: fuel must rise by the same ratio, and the knock limit or turbine inlet temperature sets the ceiling.
  • Miller or Atkinson timing is not just a longer expansion stroke; valve timing must keep combustion stable.
  • Low-viscosity oil is not always better; it must protect the bearing film at high load.
  • A higher compression ratio raises ideal efficiency but can cause knock or exceed peak-pressure limits.

5.8 Testing standards and in-cylinder pressure measurement

Two labs test the same engine and report two different powers because their procedures differ. A test standard fixes the procedure: what is fitted to the engine, the running-in, the speed, the air conditions and the correction to a reference. The in-cylinder pressure record is the other half: imep, peak pressure, heat release and cycle-to-cycle variation are all derived from it.

You need: imep and indicated power from the chapter on measuring indicated, brake and friction power, the test cell from the chapter on dynamometers, and the stages of combustion (see Stages of combustion and flame propagation in SI engines).

A rated power is a procedure, not a property

The same engine can be sold as 60 kW or 52 kW. The difference is what was bolted on during the test. A fan, an alternator, an air cleaner and a full exhaust system each take power that the bare engine never sees.

  • Gross power: bare engine, only the pumps needed for its own operation, no fan, no alternator, no silencer.
  • Net power: with the production fan, alternator, air cleaner and exhaust system fitted, as installed in the vehicle.
  • Running-in: the standard fixes how long the engine runs before measurement, and at what load.
  • Steady state: power is quoted at a fixed speed, with coolant and oil at declared temperatures, after readings stop drifting.

ISO 1585 covers net power of automotive engines. ISO 3046 covers power ratings of reciprocating engines for industrial and marine use with continuous and overload ratings. SAE J1349 is the American net-power procedure. Stationary engines in India are tested to IS 10000, the Indian standard methods of test for internal combustion engines. Quote the convention, the speed and the fuel along with the power, or the number means nothing.

Reference air and the driving cycle

Air density decides how much fuel can be burnt, so a cold January morning in Delhi and a hot May afternoon give different power from the same engine. Standards correct measured power to reference air, 25 °C and 99 kPa dry air pressure, by multiplying the measured value by a correction factor.

α = (99/pd)1.2 × (T/298)0.6

α is the correction factor for a spark-ignition engine in ISO 1585; pd is the dry air pressure at the inlet in kPa (barometric minus vapour pressure); T is the inlet air temperature in K; 99 kPa and 298 K are the reference values. Corrected power P₀ = α × P. Diesel engines use other exponents. The formula is used only while α stays between 0.93 and 1.07.

  • NEDC: 1180 s, about 11.0 km, two phases (urban, extra-urban), gentle accelerations.
  • WLTC: 1800 s, about 23.3 km, four phases, higher speeds and harder accelerations.
  • MIDC: the modified Indian driving cycle, adapted for Indian road speeds; it is the type-approval cycle for BS VI cars and light-duty vehicles, while two-wheelers are certified on WMTC.
  • BS VI: the current Indian stage, with separate petrol and diesel limits in g/km; for a petrol car, CO is capped near 1.0 g/km and NOx near 0.06 g/km.

The cycle sets the speed and load history, so the cycle decides the number. A CO figure from NEDC cannot be compared with a CO figure from WLTC.

The pressure measurement chain

In a spark-ignition engine the cylinder pressure swings from about 1 bar to 80 bar within 40° of crank angle, thousands of times a minute; in a turbocharged diesel the peak can reach about 150 bar or more. The chain that records it has four jobs: sense, convert, time-stamp, store.

  1. Transducer: a piezoelectric quartz crystal with its diaphragm flush with the combustion chamber wall, so no passage fills with carbon; hot engines use a water-cooled body.
  2. Charge to voltage: the crystal releases a small charge, of the order of picocoulombs per bar; a charge amplifier turns it into a voltage proportional to pressure. Cable movement and moisture also make charge, so low-noise cable is used.
  3. Crank-angle timing: an encoder on the crankshaft gives one pulse per degree plus an index pulse once per revolution.
  4. Logging: the data acquisition system samples pressure at every encoder pulse for 100 or more consecutive cycles and stores each sample against its crank angle.
  5. TDC reference and pegging: the encoder index is tied to true TDC with a TDC sensor or a motoring run. The relative piezo record is then shifted by one constant, either so that the pressure near bottom dead centre of the intake stroke equals the intake manifold pressure, or so that the compression line follows pVn = constant. That gives absolute pressure.

Without pegging the trace floats at the wrong absolute level: peak pressure and the heat-release curve come out wrong, but the imep taken as the closed-cycle integral ∮p dV is largely unaffected, because a constant offset cancels in the integral. A TDC (angle) error does change imep, because every pressure is paired with the wrong volume; this is why TDC is located carefully.

What the record gives you

  • p-θ plot: peak pressure and the crank angle where it occurs; the slope gives the maximum rate of pressure rise.
  • p-V plot: the enclosed area of the closed cycle is indicated work; divide by displacement per cycle to get imep. A log p versus log V plot gives the polytropic index.
  • Heat release rate: from the first law applied to the cylinder contents, using p-θ and V-θ; the crank angles for 10 % and 90 % burnt define the combustion duration.
  • Cycle-to-cycle variation: compute imep for each cycle separately and take the coefficient of variation.

COV = (σ / μ) × 100 %

σ is the standard deviation of imep over the recorded cycles (bar); μ is the mean imep (bar); COV is the coefficient of variation in percent. Below about 5 % is taken as stable combustion; a highly diluted engine that drifts past it is close to misfire.

Worked example

Question. A piezoelectric transducer and a crank-angle encoder record 100 cycles of a 1.5 L four-stroke engine running at 2000 rpm. Mean imep over the 100 cycles is 8.2 bar, with a standard deviation of 0.21 bar; peak pressure is 62 bar at 14° ATDC. Find the indicated power and the coefficient of variation of imep. Is the combustion stable?

  1. Convert imep to SI: 8.2 bar = 8.2 × 10⁵ Pa.
  2. Displacement volume: Vd = 1.5 L = 1.5 × 10⁻³ m³.
  3. A four-stroke engine fires once per two revolutions, so cycles per second = N/120 = 2000/120 = 16.67 s⁻¹.
  4. Indicated power IP = imep × Vd × (N/120) = 8.2 × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ × 16.67 = 20 500 W = 20.5 kW.
  5. COV = (σ/μ) × 100 = (0.21 / 8.2) × 100 = 2.56 %.
  6. The usual limit for stable combustion is 5 %, so 2.56 % is well inside it.

Answer. Indicated power is 20.5 kW and the COV of imep is 2.56 %, so combustion is stable.

Worked example 2

Question. A petrol engine gives 52.0 kW on a day when the inlet air is at 35 °C and the dry air pressure is 96.0 kPa. Find its corrected power by the spark-ignition formula.

  1. Pressure term: (99 ÷ 96.0)1.2 = 1.031251.2 = 1.0376.
  2. Temperature term: T = 35 + 273 = 308 K, so (308 ÷ 298)0.6 = 1.033560.6 = 1.0200.
  3. α = 1.0376 × 1.0200 = 1.0584, inside 0.93 to 1.07, so the formula may be used.
  4. Corrected power: P₀ = 1.0584 × 52.0 = 55.0 kW.

Answer. α = 1.058 and the corrected power is 55.0 kW, about 6 % above the observed 52.0 kW.

Exam traps

  • Using the piezo signal as absolute pressure. It is relative until pegged: an unpegged trace gives wrong peak pressure and heat release, although the loop area (imep) hardly changes.
  • Comparing a gross rating with a net rating, or an NEDC emission figure with a WLTC one. Different procedures, different numbers.
  • Forgetting the factor 2 for a four-stroke: using N/60 instead of N/120 in the imep-to-power step doubles the answer.
  • Ignoring TDC offset. A one-degree error shifts the peak-pressure angle and changes imep and heat release, because each pressure is paired with the wrong volume.
  • Quoting COV as a fraction when the question asks for a percentage, or computing COV of peak pressure while calling it COV of imep.

Takeaways

  1. Every performance number is built from three measurements only: torque, speed and fuel flow.
  2. Brake power is measured; indicated power is inferred; friction power is the difference between them.
  3. A Morse test separates the cylinders' indicated powers by removing one working cylinder at a time.
  4. Fuel flow and air flow must be logged with the load, because efficiency is meaningless without both.
  5. A dynamometer is a calibrated brake; a test cell makes its reading repeatable and safe.
  6. The heat balance sheet proves that the measured outputs add up to the fuel energy supplied.
  7. Load, speed, AFR, ignition or injection timing, compression ratio and ambient conditions move power and efficiency together.
  8. Performance improves through more air, better mixture, less friction, better control and cycle changes such as a higher compression ratio.
  9. A result is valid only if the test standard, the running-in state and steady or transient condition are stated.
  10. The in-cylinder pressure trace is the richest single record of what happens inside the engine.

Understand Define brake power, indicated power and friction power, and derive the relation between them. Show how each is expressed in terms of torque, speed and mean effective pressure. 12 marks

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Brake power BP is the power delivered at the crankshaft, where the dynamometer (the "brake") measures it. One revolution turns the shaft through 2π radians, so with torque T in N·m and speed N in rev/min, BP = 2πNT/60 in W.
  2. Indicated power IP is the rate at which the gas does work on the pistons. The area of the p-V loop is the work per cycle; dividing it by the swept volume Vd gives the indicated mean effective pressure imep, so the work per cycle is imep × Vd. A four-stroke completes one cycle in two revolutions (nR = 2), a two-stroke in one (nR = 1). Hence IP = imep × Vd × N/(60 nR) per cylinder, multiplied by the number of cylinders.
  3. Friction power FP is the part of IP that never reaches the shaft: FP = IP − BP. It has three parts: rubbing friction (piston rings and skirt, bearings, valve train), pumping work of the exhaust and intake strokes, and the drive of auxiliaries such as the oil pump, water pump and alternator.
  4. Mechanical efficiency is the share that survives: ηmech = BP/IP = 1 − FP/IP. It is about 0.80 to 0.90 at full load. FP changes little with load at fixed speed, so ηmech falls at part load and is zero at idle, where all the indicated power goes on friction.
  5. Mean effective pressure form. Each power can be written with its own mean effective pressure: BP = bmep × Vd × N/(60 nR), IP = imep × Vd × N/(60 nR), FP = fmep × Vd × N/(60 nR). Subtracting, fmep = imep − bmep. Equating BP = 2πNT/60 with the bmep form gives the torque relation:

Understand Explain the Morse test for a four-cylinder engine. State its assumptions, the procedure, and how indicated and friction power are calculated. 10 marks

Points to cover. All cylinders firing at a set speed: note BP; Cut one cylinder, reduce the load to restore the same speed, note the new BP; The drop is the IP of the cut cylinder; repeat for every cylinder; Assumptions: FP unchanged, other cylinders unchanged, same speed; Total IP = sum of drops; FP = IP − BP; ηmech = BP/IP; Limitations.

Model answer.

2. Cut cylinder 1. The engine slows; reduce the dynamometer load until the speed is back
   at the test value. Note BP1.
3. Restore cylinder 1, let the reading settle, then cut cylinder 2 and repeat.

Understand Describe the instruments used to measure fuel consumption and air consumption of an engine on a test bed, with the principle of each. 10 marks

Points to cover. Burette with three-way cock and stopwatch; density correction; Gravimetric method; rotameter, turbine and Coriolis meters; Air box with sharp-edged orifice and U-tube manometer, Cd about 0.6; Viscous-flow meter and hot-wire meter; Both flows logged together with load and speed.

Model answer.

  1. Why mass flow: combustion needs a fixed air mass per fuel mass, so both flows are wanted as mass flows, in kg/h or kg/s, logged at the same steady speed and load.
  2. Burette (volumetric) method: the engine draws fuel from a glass burette between two marks of known volume V. A three-way cock lets the engine run from the tank while the burette fills, then switches it to the burette; a stopwatch times the fall between the marks. ṁf = V × ρ × 3600 / t (V in L, ρ in kg/L, t in s, answer in kg/h). Example: 50 mL of diesel (0.83 kg/L) in 25 s gives 0.0415 × 3600 ÷ 25 = 5.98 kg/h.
  3. Gravimetric method: the fuel tank stands on a balance and the time to use a fixed mass, say 100 g, is measured. No density is needed, so it is more accurate and is used to calibrate other meters.
  4. Continuous fuel meters: a rotameter (a float rises in a tapered tube; needs density correction, steady flow only), a turbine meter (rotor speed gives volume flow as pulses) and a Coriolis meter (vibrating tube measures mass flow directly; the most accurate and the most costly).
  5. Air box with orifice: the engine breathes from a large box; air enters it through a sharp-edged orifice. The box damps the intake pulses so the orifice sees steady flow. A U-tube manometer reads the pressure drop as a water column h.
    Δp = 9.81 × h   (h in mm of water, Δp in Pa)
ṁa = Cd A √(2 ρa Δp)   (Cd ≈ 0.6 sharp-edged orifice)
  6. Example: A = 1.2 × 10⁻³ m², Cd = 0.62, h = 50 mm, ρa = 1.18 kg/m³: Δp = 490.5 Pa, √(2 × 1.18 × 490.5) = 34.0, ṁa = 0.62 × 1.2 × 10⁻³ × 34.0 = 0.0253 kg/s.
  7. Other air meters: the viscous-flow (Alcock) meter passes the air through fine passages where the flow is laminar, so the pressure drop is proportional to flow and pulsations average correctly; a venturi has a higher Cd (about 0.98) and a smaller permanent loss; a hot-wire or hot-film meter senses the cooling of a heated wire and responds fast enough for pulsating flow, which is why cars use it.

Understand Explain the construction and working of absorption and eddy-current dynamometers. Why is a transient dynamometer needed for emission testing? 10 marks

Points to cover. Absorption types: Prony, rope, hydraulic, eddy-current; Torque from reaction arm and load cell: T = W × L; BP = 2πNT/60; Eddy-current: rotor in a magnetic field, no mechanical contact, load set by coil current, fast control, water cooled; Transient dynamometer: motors and absorbs, follows a driving cycle second by second.

Model answer.

  1. An absorption dynamometer turns the engine's power into heat and measures the torque that does it. The types are the Prony brake and rope brake (friction), the hydraulic brake (water) and the eddy-current brake (electromagnetic).
  2. Torque measurement: the absorbing element (brake block, water casing or eddy-current stator) is mounted so that it is free to turn, on trunnions. The engine tries to drag it round; an arm of length L holds it against a load cell or spring balance reading W. Torque T = W × L, and BP = 2πNT/60.
  3. Hydraulic dynamometer: a rotor with vanes or cups turns inside a casing filled partly with water. The water is thrown outwards and drags on the casing; the water level sets the load. The water carries away the heat, so a continuous flow is supplied. It suits large powers at modest cost.
  4. Eddy-current dynamometer construction: a toothed steel rotor on the engine shaft turns between the poles of field coils fed with direct current; the stator (casing) carries water passages. Working: as the teeth pass, the flux in the stator changes and induces eddy currents in it. These currents and the field oppose the motion, so the rotor is braked and the casing is dragged round; the arm and load cell read the torque. The heat from the eddy currents is removed by cooling water.
  5. Features of the eddy-current type: no rubbing contact, so no wear and steady torque; the load is set by the coil current, so it can be changed quickly and smoothly and controlled electronically; it is compact. It can only absorb: it cannot drive the engine.
  6. Numerical example (hydraulic dynamometer of the dynamometers chapter):
    W = 120 N on an arm L = 0.5 m:   T = 120 × 0.5 = 60 N·m
N = 1500 rev/min:   BP = 2π × 1500 × 60 / 60 = 9425 W = 9.42 kW
  7. Why a transient dynamometer is needed for emission testing: emissions are certified over a driving cycle with rapid accelerations, decelerations, idling and engine braking. The dynamometer must change speed and torque second by second and must drive the engine during overrun. An AC dynamometer (a vector-controlled induction or synchronous machine of low inertia) works in all four quadrants, motoring and generating in both directions, so it can reproduce the cycle. Absorption dynamometers cannot motor the engine, and water and friction brakes respond too slowly.
  8. Check: 9.42 kW at 60 N·m from a 1.0 L four-stroke gives bmep = 2π × 2 × 60 ÷ 10⁻³ = 7.54 bar, a normal value for a petrol engine at part load.

Evaluate Evaluate the methods available to improve engine performance and rank them by their effect on brake thermal efficiency and emissions. 14 marks

Points to cover. Four levers: more air, better mixture, less friction, better control; Turbocharging and intercooling for trapped air mass; Injection pressure, spray and direct injection; Friction reduction; Control: VVT, electronic timing, closed-loop AFR, cylinder deactivation; Cycle changes: compression ratio, Miller/Atkinson; Trade-off with NOx and particulates; Justified ranking.

Model answer.

  1. Basis for ranking: brake thermal efficiency ηbth = ηith × ηmech, so a method helps by raising indicated efficiency (more work from the same heat) or mechanical efficiency (less friction), or by letting a smaller engine do the same job at a better load point. Emissions are judged on NOx, particulates, CO and HC.
  2. More air (turbocharging with intercooling): raises trapped air mass, so power rises roughly with density. Example: density × 1.4 on an 80 kW engine gives 112 kW at the same BSFC of 240 g/kWh. Its efficiency gain comes mainly from downsizing: a smaller engine runs at higher load, where friction is a smaller share. Emissions: higher temperatures raise NOx; intercooling limits it.
  3. Higher compression ratio and Miller/Atkinson timing: raise indicated efficiency directly. Example: r from 9.5 to 11 raises the Otto efficiency from 59.4 % to 61.7 %, a factor of 1.039. Limited by knock (SI) and peak pressure (CI). Miller timing lowers charge temperature, helping knock and NOx, but needs boost to keep power.
  4. Better mixture (high-pressure common rail, direct injection, swirl and tumble): faster, more complete burning raises indicated efficiency and lowers smoke and HC; advancing injection raises NOx, so it is traded against after-treatment.
  5. Less friction (low-viscosity oil, low-tension rings, coatings, variable oil pump): raises mechanical efficiency with no emission penalty. Example: cutting FP from 12 kW to 10.2 kW adds 1.8 kW of brake power for the same fuel. Limited by wear at high load.
  6. Better control (closed-loop lambda, knock sensing, electronic timing, VVT, cylinder deactivation, stop-start): keeps the engine at its best point across the map, cuts pumping loss at part load and enables three-way catalysts (large emission benefit).
  7. Ranking (for a road vehicle engine):
    RankMethodEffect on ηbthEffect on emissions
    1Better control (lambda, VVT, deactivation)medium, over the whole maplarge benefit (enables catalyst)
    2Turbocharging with downsizingmedium to largeNOx risk, managed by intercooling
    3Compression ratio / Miller cyclemedium (about 4 % in the example)neutral to small NOx gain
    4Injection and mixture qualitysmall to mediumlower smoke, HC; NOx trade-off
    5Friction reductionsmall (3 % of BP in the example)none
  8. Justification: control ranks first because it improves efficiency and emissions together at every operating point. Boosting ranks next because the downsizing it allows gives the largest real-world fuel saving, but it must be managed for NOx. Cycle changes give a sure thermodynamic gain but are capped by knock. Friction work is the cheapest and safest, but each step is small.
  9. Check with the combined example: compression ratio 11 plus 15 % less friction lift brake power from 60 kW to 64.61 kW at the same fuel, a 7.7 % rise in power and a 7.1 % fall in BSFC (60 ÷ 64.61 = 0.929); the gains add because one acts on indicated power and the other on friction.
Evaluate Discuss why an emission or power figure is meaningless unless the test standard, driving cycle and running-in condition are stated. Include the difference between gross and net power and between steady-state and transient testing. 14 marks

Points to cover. Agreed procedure: ambient correction, fuel, auxiliaries fitted; Gross power (essential auxiliaries only) against net power (as installed); Running-in: friction falls and power settles; Correction to reference air with an example; Steady-state test cells against transient tests on driving cycles; Driving cycles: MIDC, NEDC, WLTC; cycle-to-cycle variation and logging.

Model answer.

  1. A measured power or emission depends on how the test was run as much as on the engine. A standard fixes the procedure so that two laboratories get the same answer: what is fitted to the engine, how long it was run in, the speeds, the fuel, the air conditions and the correction to reference air, and for emissions the driving cycle.
  2. Gross and net power: gross power is measured on a bare engine with only the auxiliaries it needs to run; net power is measured with the production fan, alternator, air cleaner and exhaust system fitted, as installed. The fan, alternator and silencer each take power, so the same engine can be rated, say, 60 kW gross and 52 kW net. ISO 1585 and SAE J1349 define net power for road vehicles; ISO 3046 covers industrial and marine engines; IS 10000 gives the Indian test methods for stationary engines.
  3. Running-in: a new engine's rings and bearings bed in over the first hours, so friction falls and power rises; the standard fixes how long and at what load the engine runs before measurement.
  4. Reference air: power depends on air density, so a standard corrects the measured power to 25 °C and 99 kPa dry pressure. For a spark-ignition engine:
    α = (99/pd)1.2 × (T/298)0.6
Example: 52.0 kW at 35 °C and 96.0 kPa:
α = (99/96.0)1.2 × (308/298)0.6 = 1.0376 × 1.0200 = 1.058
corrected power = 1.058 × 52.0 = 55.0 kW
  5. The same engine therefore has an observed 52.0 kW and a corrected 55.0 kW, 6 % apart; quoting one without saying which is meaningless. The correction is applied only while α stays between 0.93 and 1.07.
  6. Steady-state and transient testing: a steady-state test holds speed and load until readings stop drifting, good for maps of BSFC and power. Emissions in real use come from transients: accelerations, cold start, gear changes. Transient tests use an AC dynamometer (engine) or a chassis dynamometer (vehicle) that follows a speed-time schedule second by second.
  7. Driving cycles decide the emission number:
    CycleDurationDistanceCharacter
    NEDC1180 sabout 11.0 kmtwo phases, gentle accelerations
    WLTC1800 sabout 23.3 kmfour phases, faster and harder
    MIDCIndian cycleset by the rulesBS VI cars and light vehicles
  8. A CO figure in g/km from NEDC cannot be compared with one from WLTC, because the speed and load history differ. Limits such as BS VI petrol car CO of 1.0 g/km have meaning only on the cycle they are written for.
  9. Data validity: in-cylinder results such as imep need 100 or more cycles because of cycle-to-cycle variation; a COV of imep of 2.56 % (0.21 bar on 8.2 bar) is stable, but a single cycle could be several per cent off the mean.
  10. Conclusion: a valid figure states the standard (gross or net, ISO 1585 or SAE J1349), the running-in, observed or corrected air, the fuel, steady or transient, and for emissions the cycle. Check: in the example the correction alone moved the power by 3.0 kW, larger than many design improvements, which shows why the procedure must be stated.

Glossary

Absorption dynamometer
A dynamometer that converts the absorbed engine power into heat, as in water-brake and eddy-current types.
Air box
A large settling chamber fitted with an orifice or nozzle, used to measure the air drawn by an engine smoothly and steadily.
BMEP
Brake mean effective pressure, the constant pressure that would produce the measured brake work in one cycle.
BS VI
The Indian Bharat Stage VI emission norm, applied to new vehicles from 2020, with tight limits on NOx, particulate matter and sulphur in fuel.
BSFC
Brake specific fuel consumption, the fuel mass needed per kilowatt-hour of brake work.
Charge amplifier
An electronic unit that converts the charge from a piezoelectric sensor into a proportional voltage.
Correction factor
The multiplier that converts power measured in test-cell air to power at the standard's reference air conditions.
COV of imep
Standard deviation of imep over many cycles divided by the mean imep, in per cent; a measure of combustion stability.
Cycle-to-cycle variation
The scatter in pressure and work between successive cycles of the same cylinder under identical settings, usually given as the COV of imep.
Eddy-current dynamometer
A dynamometer that loads the engine by electromagnetic drag with no mechanical contact, allowing fast control.
Equivalence ratio
Actual fuel-air ratio divided by the stoichiometric fuel-air ratio; above 1 is rich, below 1 is lean.
Gross power
Power measured with only the auxiliaries essential for running; net power is measured with the full installation.
Heat balance sheet
A statement showing how the fuel energy supplied is divided between brake power, cooling, exhaust and unaccounted losses.
Heat release rate
The rate of chemical energy release during combustion, calculated from the cylinder pressure trace.
MIDC
The Modified Indian Driving Cycle, the speed–time schedule used in India for chassis dynamometer emission tests.
Motoring test
A test in which an external motor drives the engine without fuel to measure its friction and pumping losses.
NEDC
The New European Driving Cycle, a speed–time schedule for vehicle emission testing consisting of urban and extra-urban phases.
Pegging
Shifting a relative piezoelectric pressure record by a constant so that it reads absolute pressure.
Prony brake
The simplest absorption dynamometer, using friction on a drum and a measured reaction arm.
Running-in
The initial period of engine operation in which surfaces bed in, friction falls and performance stabilises.
Steady state
A test condition in which speed, load and temperatures are held constant for long enough for all readings to settle.
Stoichiometric air–fuel ratio
The minimum air mass per unit fuel mass needed for complete combustion; about 14.7 for petrol and 14.5 for diesel.
Test cell
A controlled room with a dynamometer, cooling, ventilation, fuel supply and instrumentation for repeatable engine testing.
Transmission dynamometer
A dynamometer in the drive line that measures torque as the power passes through, from shaft twist or strain gauges, absorbing almost none of it.
Volumetric efficiency
The ratio of the air actually trapped per cycle to the swept volume of air at ambient conditions.
Willans line
A straight line of fuel flow against brake power at constant speed, extended to zero fuel flow to estimate friction power.
