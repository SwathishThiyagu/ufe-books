5.1 Engine performance parameters and efficiencies

An engine test gives three raw measurements: torque at the shaft, speed of the shaft, and the rate at which fuel disappears. Every performance number in this chapter is built from those three. Torque and speed give brake power, fuel flow and calorific value give fuel power, and efficiency is one divided by the other. Mean effective pressure rescales torque by engine size, so a 100 cc scooter engine and a 10-litre truck engine can be compared honestly. Datasheets, test beds and the fuel-consumption maps manufacturers publish all use these same definitions.

You need: bore, stroke, swept volume and the difference between a four-stroke and a two-stroke cycle (see Unit 1, Engine Fundamentals, Components and Cycles).

Power is torque times speed

On the test bed only the shaft carries work out of the engine, so two quantities are measured there: torque T, the turning moment in newton metres (N·m), and speed N, the rotation rate in revolutions per second (rev/s). One revolution turns the shaft through 2π radians, so each revolution delivers work of 2πT joules.

BP = 2πNT

BP is brake power in watts (W); N is shaft speed in rev/s; T is torque in N·m. If the tachometer reads rpm, use BP = 2πNT/60 with N in rpm, or divide rpm by 60 first.

Brake refers to the brake that loads the engine, so BP is the power handed to the outside world. The gas inside the cylinder pushes harder than the shaft delivers. The rate at which the gas does work on the piston is the indicated power IP, taken from an indicator diagram (chapter 5.2). The shortfall is friction power FP = IP − BP: rings on the bore, bearings, and the oil and water pumps. Mechanical efficiency is the fraction that survives, η_mech = BP/IP = 1 − FP/IP.

Mean effective pressure: the size-independent number

Torque alone cannot compare two engines of different size. Mean effective pressure puts them on a common footing. It is a fictitious constant pressure that, acting on the piston through the whole stroke, would produce the same work per cycle as the real, constantly changing pressure.

BP = bmep × V_d × N / n_R

bmep is brake mean effective pressure in pascals (N/m²); V_d is the swept volume in m³; N is speed in rev/s; n_R = 2 for a four-stroke (one cycle in two revolutions) and n_R = 1 for a two-stroke. Equating this to BP = 2πNT gives bmep = 2π n_R T / V_d.

Put imep in place of bmep, and indicated torque in place of T, to get indicated mean effective pressure. Then fmep = imep − bmep. All three are quoted in bar, where 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa. A naturally aspirated petrol engine reaches roughly 9–12 bar at peak torque; a turbocharged diesel reaches 18–25 bar. The factor n_R stays visible in every formula, because forgetting it is the commonest error in this chapter.

Thermal efficiency and air breathing

Fuel power is the rate at which chemical energy enters the engine: fuel power = mf × CV, where mf is fuel mass flow in kg/s and CV is the calorific value in J/kg (about 43 MJ/kg for petrol and diesel).

η_bth = BP / (mf × CV)    and    η_ith = IP / (mf × CV)

η_bth is brake thermal efficiency, η_ith is indicated thermal efficiency, both pure fractions (multiply by 100 for per cent). They are linked by η_bth = η_ith × η_mech.

Volumetric efficiency measures breathing: η_vol = mass of air actually trapped per cycle ÷ (ρ_a × V_d), where ρ_a is the density of air outside the engine in kg/m³. A naturally aspirated engine at full throttle manages 0.80–0.90; throttling at part load drops it far below. Relative efficiency compares the real engine with the ideal one, η_rel = η_ith / η_air-standard, the air-standard efficiency at the same compression ratio (Unit 1, Engine Fundamentals, Components and Cycles). It isolates losses the ideal cycle ignores: heat transfer, slow combustion, imperfect valve timing. Equivalence ratio is φ = (F/A) / (F/A)_stoich, actual fuel-air ratio over stoichiometric fuel-air ratio; petrol needs about 14.7 kg air per kg fuel, diesel about 14.5, so φ < 1 is lean and φ > 1 is rich.

Specific fuel consumption, specific power and the curves

Brake specific fuel consumption is the fuel bill per unit of work: BSFC = mf / BP, with mf in kg/h and BP in kW, giving kg/kWh; multiply by 1000 for g/kWh. Indicated specific fuel consumption is ISFC = mf / IP, and BSFC = ISFC / η_mech. A good petrol engine reaches about 250 g/kWh near its best point, a diesel about 200 g/kWh because of its higher compression ratio. Specific power compares engines by size: BP / V_d in kW per litre, and BP / mass in kW per kg. Turbocharging lifts both (see Unit 4, Supercharging).

Three curves carry most of the marks. At fixed throttle, BP climbs with speed because more cycles run each second, then falls when friction and breathing losses win; bmep peaks at a lower speed than BP. BSFC is a U against speed: high at low speed, lowest where η_bth is highest, rising again at high speed. Against load at fixed speed, BSFC is poor at light load, because friction is a nearly fixed tax on a small output, and best around three-quarter load.

Worked example

Question. A single-cylinder four-stroke engine has bore 100 mm and stroke 120 mm. At 1500 rpm it holds a bmep of 6 bar and burns 2.4 kg/h of fuel of calorific value 43 MJ/kg. Mechanical efficiency is 85 %. Find the swept volume, brake power, torque, brake thermal efficiency, BSFC, indicated power and friction power.

  1. Swept volume: V_d = (π/4) × D² × L = (π/4) × (0.100 m)² × (0.120 m) = 9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³, that is 0.9425 litre.
  2. Speed in rev/s: N = 1500/60 = 25 rev/s. The engine is a four-stroke, so n_R = 2.
  3. Brake power: BP = bmep × V_d × N/n_R = (6 × 10⁵ Pa) × (9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³) × (25/2) = 7069 W = 7.07 kW.
  4. Torque: T = BP/(2πN) = 7069 W / (2π × 25) = 45.0 N·m.
  5. Fuel flow: mf = 2.4/3600 = 6.667 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s. Fuel power = mf × CV = 6.667 × 10⁻⁴ × 43 × 10⁶ = 28 667 W = 28.67 kW.
  6. Brake thermal efficiency: η_bth = BP/fuel power = 7.069/28.67 = 0.2466 = 24.7 %.
  7. Brake specific fuel consumption: BSFC = mf/BP = 2.4/7.069 = 0.3395 kg/kWh = 339.5 g/kWh.
  8. Indicated power: IP = BP/η_mech = 7.069/0.85 = 8.32 kW.
  9. Friction power: FP = IP − BP = 8.32 − 7.07 = 1.25 kW.

Answer. V_d = 9.425 × 10⁻⁴ m³, BP = 7.07 kW, T = 45.0 N·m, brake thermal efficiency = 24.7 %, BSFC = 339.5 g/kWh, IP = 8.32 kW and FP = 1.25 kW.

Exam traps

  • A four-stroke completes one cycle in two revolutions. Using BP = bmep × V_d × N instead of × N/2 doubles the answer.
  • The formula 2πNT wants rev/s. Feeding 1500 (rpm) straight in without dividing by 60 inflates the power 60 times.
  • Bar and pascal are 10⁵ apart. Convert bmep to Pa before combining it with V_d in m³, or the power comes out 10⁵ times too small.
  • BSFC is usually quoted in g/kWh but the mass flow in mf/BP must be in kg/h. A factor of 1000 slips in silently.
  • bmep is a fictitious pressure that gives the work per cycle; it is not the peak cylinder pressure, and volumetric efficiency is about air trapped, not about power surviving friction.

Understand Why is bmep a fairer basis for comparing two engines than torque? 3 marks

Torque and bmep rise and fall together with speed, so flatness is not the point. Torque of a big engine is large simply because its swept volume is large. bmep divides the work per cycle by V_d, so engines of 100 cc and 10 L are reduced to the same scale and their breathing and combustion quality can be compared directly.

Apply A four-stroke engine of swept volume 0.5 L runs at 3000 rpm and delivers 60 N·m. Find BP and bmep. 4 marks

Apply An engine has brake thermal efficiency 32 % and mechanical efficiency 0.85. Find the indicated thermal efficiency. 4 marks

Analyze At constant speed, why does BSFC rise steeply as the load is reduced? 5 marks

Friction power is nearly constant while brake power falls with load, so FP/BP grows and η_mech drops. Since η_bth = η_ith × η_mech, brake thermal efficiency falls, and BSFC = 1/(η_bth × CV) rises sharply. Light load also means a weaker mixture and poorer combustion.

Analyze A two-stroke and a four-stroke have the same swept volume, the same bmep and the same speed. Compare their torques and powers. 5 marks

T = bmep × V_d / (2π n_R), so at the same bmep, swept volume and speed the two-stroke (n_R = 1) makes twice the torque of the four-stroke (n_R = 2), because it completes one cycle every revolution instead of every second revolution. BP = bmep × V_d × N / n_R, so the two-stroke also makes twice the power at the same speed.

5.2 Measuring indicated, brake and friction power

An engine makes two powers at once: indicated power at the piston face and brake power at the shaft. Only brake power can be measured directly with a brake. Indicated power comes from the p-V loop or from a Morse test. Friction power comes from the motoring test or Willan's line, or by subtraction once IP and BP are known. Test cells use these routes every day to report mechanical efficiency.

You need: 5.1 for the power and efficiency definitions, and Unit 1 for the working cycle and swept volume.

Three powers and one subtraction

Power leaves an engine down the shaft. The gas first spends part of its work on the engine's own friction — bearings, rings, valve train, oil and water pumps — and only the remainder reaches the shaft. Three numbers therefore exist.

Indicated power IP is the rate of work done by the gas on the piston. Brake power BP is the rate of work at the shaft. Friction power FP is the difference, and mechanical efficiency is the ratio of the two.

FP = IP − BP η_mech = BP / IP

IP, BP and FP are in the same unit, usually kW; η_mech is a pure number, often given as a percentage.

Brake power is computed from two measured quantities, torque and speed.

BP = 2πNT / 60

BP is brake power in W; T is the torque absorbed by the dynamometer in N·m; N is engine speed in rev/min; the factor 2π/60 converts rev/min to rad/s. Divide by 1000 for kW.

Torque and speed are read directly, so BP is never in doubt. IP needs a pressure record. FP is not measured at all — it is what remains. See 5.4 for the dynamometer and 5.1 for the rest of the performance parameters.

Indicated power from the p-V loop

  1. Record cylinder pressure against volume for one full cycle of one cylinder.
  2. The area inside the closed loop is the net work per cycle, to the pressure and volume scales.
  3. Measure the area in mm² with a planimeter, or count squares.
  4. Divide the area by the diagram length in mm to get the mean height in mm.
  5. Multiply the mean height by the indicator spring constant in bar/mm or kPa/mm. That is the imep.

IP = imep × V_d × N / (60 × n_R)

imep is indicated mean effective pressure in kPa; V_d is swept volume of one cylinder in m³; N is speed in rev/min; n_R is 2 for a four-stroke and 1 for a two-stroke; IP is in kW for one cylinder. Multiply by the number of cylinders.

n_R is the number of revolutions per power stroke. A four-stroke fires once in two revolutions, so at the same speed and imep it gives half the power of a two-stroke.

The old mechanical indicator scratched a diagram on a card; a modern engine uses a piezoelectric transducer and crank-angle encoder to draw the same loop in software (see 5.8). On a four-stroke the pumping loop already lies inside the closed area, so the imep obtained is the net imep.

Morse test: cut one cylinder at a time

The Morse test works on engines with three or more cylinders. Hold speed and load fixed and note BP with all cylinders firing. Cut the ignition (SI) or fuel (CI) of cylinder 1 only, and restore load to bring speed back to the same value. Note the new BP. Repeat for every cylinder.

IP = Σ (BP − BP_i), i = 1 … k

BP is brake power with all k cylinders firing in kW; BP_i is brake power with cylinder i cut in kW; the drop BP − BP_i is the indicated power of that one cylinder.

  • Assumption 1: friction power is the same whether a cylinder fires or not.
  • Assumption 2: the firing cylinders deliver the same power as before the cut.
  • Assumption 3: speed and load are held constant.
  • Then FP = IP − BP and η_mech = BP/IP. FP must come out small and positive.
  • Weakness: it needs a multi-cylinder engine, and the cut cylinder cools and loses its oil film.

Motoring test and Willan's line

The motoring test measures friction directly. Couple the engine to a dynamometer, switch off fuel and ignition, and drive it at the test speed. The dynamometer then reads the power needed to turn it over, which is the friction power. It is quick and works on a single-cylinder engine. Weakness: with no combustion the walls, piston and oil are cooler and no gas pressure pushes the rings out, so the friction measured is not the friction at running temperature.

Willan's line uses fuel flow. Hold speed constant and vary the load. Plot fuel consumption rate in kg/h against BP in kW. At moderate and heavy load the points lie on a straight line. Extend it to zero fuel flow: it meets the BP axis at BP = −FP, so the size of that negative intercept is FP. The assumptions are a straight line and friction power independent of load. Near zero load the line curves because combustion becomes poor, so only the straight part may be extrapolated. See 5.3 for the fuel-flow measurement.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine runs at constant speed. BP with all four cylinders firing is 20.0 kW. Cutting each cylinder in turn and restoring the same speed gives brake powers 14.2, 14.0, 14.3 and 14.1 kW. Find IP, FP and mechanical efficiency.

  1. Cylinder 1 cut: drop = BP − BP₁ = 20.0 − 14.2 = 5.8 kW, so cylinder 1 indicates 5.8 kW.
  2. Cylinder 2 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.0 = 6.0 kW.
  3. Cylinder 3 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.3 = 5.7 kW.
  4. Cylinder 4 cut: drop = 20.0 − 14.1 = 5.9 kW.
  5. Total indicated power IP = 5.8 + 6.0 + 5.7 + 5.9 = 23.4 kW.
  6. Friction power FP = IP − BP = 23.4 − 20.0 = 3.4 kW.
  7. Mechanical efficiency η_mech = BP / IP = 20.0 / 23.4 = 0.855 = 85.5 %.

Answer. IP = 23.4 kW, FP = 3.4 kW and η_mech = 85.5 %.

Exam traps

  • Adding the four cut readings (56.6 kW) instead of the four drops. A cut reading still contains the brake power of the other cylinders.
  • Reporting a negative friction power. FP is small and positive; a negative value means a cut reading was copied wrongly or BP was noted with the wrong number of cylinders firing.
  • Using N instead of N/2 in the indicated power formula for a four-stroke. That doubles IP, so the efficiency you calculate is half the true value: the 85.5 % example would come out near 43 %.
  • Mixing units in IP = imep × V_d × N/(60 n_R). Use kPa with m³, or convert bar to kPa (×100) and litres to m³ (×10⁻³).
  • Confusing the three routes: the motoring test gives FP directly, the Morse test gives IP, and Willan's line gives FP by extrapolation of a fuel-flow plot.

Understand Why can friction power not be measured directly on a firing engine? 3 marks

Apply A single-cylinder four-stroke engine has a swept volume of 0.0006 m³ and runs at 2400 rev/min. The imep from its indicator diagram is 700 kPa. Find the indicated power. 4 marks

n_R = 2, so power strokes per minute = 2400/2 = 1200. IP = imep × V_d × N/(60 n_R) = 700 × 0.0006 × 2400/(60×2) = 700 × 0.0006 × 20 = 8.4 kW.

Apply A six-cylinder engine develops 90.0 kW with all cylinders firing. Cutting cylinders in turn gives 71.0, 70.5, 71.5, 70.8, 71.2 and 71.0 kW. Find IP, FP and mechanical efficiency. 5 marks

Drops: 19.0, 19.5, 18.5, 19.2, 18.8, 19.0 kW; sum = 114.0 kW = IP. FP = 114.0 − 90.0 = 24.0 kW. η_mech = 90.0/114.0 = 0.789 = 78.9 %.

Analyze A Willan's line at constant speed meets the brake power axis at −2.6 kW. What does this tell you, and why does the line curve near zero load? 4 marks

The magnitude of the negative intercept is the friction power, so FP = 2.6 kW. At very light load the mixture is poor and a large share of the fuel goes on losses that do not scale with power, so the straight-line law fails.

Analyze The motoring test on a cold engine gives a higher friction power than the Morse test on the same warm engine. Give two reasons. 4 marks

Cold oil is more viscous, so bearing, piston and ring friction is larger. Cooler walls also keep the oil film colder and thicker, raising friction further. Note that the missing gas pressure behind the rings works the other way and tends to lower friction — that is why a motoring test on a warm engine usually reads slightly less than the true running friction.

Understand State the three assumptions of the Morse test. 3 marks

Friction power is unchanged when a cylinder is cut; the firing cylinders deliver the same indicated power as before the cut; speed and load are held constant throughout.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Engine plants run an automated motoring test at the end of the line: the dynamometer spins the engine without fuel and compares the torque trace with a stored signature, catching a tight bearing or wrong cam timing before dispatch. 5.3 Measuring fuel and air consumption

A power reading alone does not show how good an engine is. Judge it with the fuel and air that produced the power, measured while load is steady. Fuel is timed or weighed; air is metered by orifice, venturi or hot wire. Their mass ratio is air-fuel ratio; air flow against swept volume gives volumetric efficiency. Every test bed, type-approval run and workshop diagnosis starts here.

You need 5.1 for power and efficiency, and Unit 2 for fuel supply.

Fuel flow: burette, balance and meters

Fuel flow is a mass flow in kg/h, because combustion needs a fixed air mass per fuel mass. Three methods are common.

Volumetric method: the engine draws fuel from a glass burette with two marks setting volume V. A stopwatch times the fall between marks.

ṁf = (V × ρ × 3600) ÷ t

ṁf is fuel mass flow in kg/h; V is burette volume in L; ρ is fuel density in kg/L at fuel temperature (about 0.83 diesel, 0.74 petrol); t is time in s; 3600 converts s to h.

  1. Fill the burette above the top mark while the engine runs from the main tank.
  2. Turn the three-way cock so the engine draws only from the burette.
  3. Start the stopwatch at the top mark; stop at the bottom mark; note t.
  4. Repeat three times and average; correct ρ to fuel temperature.

The three-way cock lets the engine run from the tank while the burette refills, so filling time is never timed.

Small volume and short time increase error. A 50 mL burette read to ±1 mL is ±2 %; 25 s timed to ±0.2 s is ±0.8 %. A 5 s test makes timing error ±4 %. Use the largest volume and longest steady time.

Gravimetric method weighs fuel from a can on a balance and clocks the time to lose a set mass, say 100 g. No density is needed; accuracy is better, so it calibrates others.

  • Rotameter: float rises with flow; cheap, steady flow only, needs density correction.
  • Turbine meter: rotor speed gives volume as pulses.
  • Coriolis meter: measures mass directly; best accuracy, highest cost.
  • Gas fuels: gas meter gives volume at meter pressure and temperature; convert with ρ = p/(R T).

Air flow: air box, orifice and hot wire

Air is harder to meter because intake flow pulses every stroke, so a settling volume smooths it.

Air box: a large tank the engine breathes from, with a sharp-edged orifice open to the room. The box damps pulses; the orifice sees steady flow. A U-tube manometer reads pressure drop Δp across the orifice, usually as water column h in mm.

ṁa = C_d A √(2 ρ Δp)

ṁa is air mass flow in kg/s; C_d is discharge coefficient (about 0.6 sharp-edged orifice, 0.98 venturi); A is orifice area in m²; ρ is air density in kg/m³; Δp is pressure drop in Pa, equal to 9.81 × h for h in mm of water.

Air density comes from the test room: ρ = p ÷ (R T), where p is absolute room pressure in Pa, T is room temperature in K, and R = 287 J/(kg·K) for air. A hot Chennai afternoon changes ρ by several per cent, moving air flow and volumetric efficiency.

A venturi meter uses the same relation with higher C_d and smaller permanent loss; flow benches use it. A hot-wire anemometer heats a thin wire and measures current to keep it hot; flow cools the wire, so current reads velocity. It responds fast enough for pulsating intake and in-cylinder work.

Engine-as-compressor method: motor the engine with fuel cut off, collect and measure pumped air, for example in a gas meter. This gives breathing alone, without combustion disturbing flow.

Air-fuel ratio and volumetric efficiency

Both flows must be in the same units before dividing. With ṁa and ṁf in kg/s, air-fuel ratio is AFR = ṁa ÷ ṁf, always by mass, never by volume.

Stoichiometric values are about 14.7 for petrol and 14.5 for diesel. A light-load diesel may run at 30 or more; a petrol engine at full power runs near 12.

Volumetric efficiency compares air actually trapped per cycle with the mass that would fill swept volume at intake density.

η_vol = ṁa ÷ (ρ_a × V_s × (N ÷ 60) ÷ 2)

η_vol is volumetric efficiency; ṁa is measured air flow in kg/s; ρ_a is intake air density in kg/m³; V_s is total swept volume in m³; N is engine speed in rev/min. For a four-stroke, ÷ 2 gives one intake stroke in two revolutions; a two-stroke uses (N ÷ 60) with no halving.

Values near 85 to 95 % are normal for a good four-stroke at full throttle. Far below points to blocked filter, bad cam or hot intake; see 5.6.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke engine of 2.0 L swept volume runs at 3000 rpm. Air box orifice gives 0.052 kg/s; intake air density is 1.18 kg/m³. A burette test uses 50 mL diesel of density 830 kg/m³ in 25 s. Find fuel flow in kg/h and kg/s, air-fuel ratio by mass, and volumetric efficiency.

  1. Fuel mass: m = V × ρ = 0.050 L × 0.83 kg/L = 0.0415 kg. (830 kg/m³ = 0.83 kg/L.)
  2. Fuel flow: ṁf = m × 3600 ÷ t = 0.0415 × 3600 ÷ 25 = 5.976 kg/h, i.e. 5.98 kg/h.
  3. In kg/s: 5.976 ÷ 3600 = 1.66 × 10⁻³ kg/s.
  4. AFR = ṁa ÷ ṁf = 0.052 ÷ 1.66 × 10⁻³ = 31.3 (light-load diesel).
  5. Ideal air flow for four-stroke: ṁa,ideal = ρ_a × V_s × (N ÷ 60) ÷ 2 = 1.18 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ × (3000 ÷ 60) ÷ 2.
  6. Numbers: 1.18 × 0.002 = 0.00236; × 50 = 0.118; ÷ 2 = 0.0590 kg/s.
  7. η_vol = 0.052 ÷ 0.0590 = 0.881 = 88.1 %.

Answer. Fuel flow 5.98 kg/h (1.66 × 10⁻³ kg/s), air-fuel ratio 31.3 by mass, volumetric efficiency 88.1 %.

Exam traps

  • Quoting AFR by volume. AFR is always a mass ratio; a volume ratio near 31 is meaningless.
  • Skipping air density correction. ρ = p ÷ (R T) changes AFR and volumetric efficiency by several per cent.
  • Timing burette refill. The three-way cock must switch supply so the engine never drinks during filling.
  • Forgetting ÷ 2 for a four-stroke in ideal air flow; it doubles swept volume and halves efficiency.
  • Mixing units: mL with L, mm water with Pa, kg/h with kg/s. Convert and check answer size.

5.4 Dynamometers and engine test cells

A dynamometer is a brake that measures the torque it absorbs. The engine turns, the dynamometer resists, and a torque arm and load cell turn that resistance into a number. A test cell is the room built around that brake: rigid bed, guarded coupling, fuel and coolant lines, exhaust extraction, fire safety and a panel that logs speed, torque, temperatures and flows. Together they make an engine measurement repeatable and safe. Every brake power figure in 5.1 and 5.2 comes from a dynamometer.

You need: brake power from torque and speed in 5.2, and bmep in 5.1.

Absorption dynamometers load the engine

A dynamometer must do two things: absorb the engine's power as heat or electricity, and measure the reaction torque on its casing. The simplest absorption brake is the Prony brake. A wooden block presses on the flywheel, a lever arm of length L carries a spring balance, and the friction force at the rim is read as a weight. For a rope brake, a rope passes over the flywheel and two balances read the tight and slack sides.

Torque is force times perpendicular distance: T = W × L, where T is torque in N·m, W is the net force on the arm in N and L is the arm length in m. If the balance has a dead weight S with no load, use W = (reading − S). Brake power follows from torque and speed.

BP = 2πNT/60

BP is brake power in W; N is engine speed in rev/min; T is torque in N·m. For a torque arm, T = W × L, with W in N and L in m.

  • Prony and rope brakes: cheap, no external power, but friction fades with heat, poor at high speed, needs water cooling and a long arm.
  • Hydraulic (water) brake: rotor and stator with water between; load set by water quantity; torque arm measures reaction; good for high power.
  • Eddy-current brake: electromagnet drags a steel disc; load set by coil current; heat removed by water jacket; fast control.
  • DC machine: as a generator it absorbs power into a resistor bank (this is the absorption use); the same machine can also motor, driving the engine without fuel; heavier and slower than an eddy-current brake.

Motoring and transient dynamometers

An absorption brake can only load the engine. A motoring dynamometer is an electric machine that can also drive the engine. A DC machine motoring at controlled speed gives the motoring test: the engine is turned without fuel, and the shaft power needed equals friction power (5.2). The same machine can generate, so one unit covers both directions.

A transient dynamometer goes further. It changes speed and torque fast enough to follow a driving cycle second by second. An AC dynamometer uses a vector-controlled induction or synchronous machine with low inertia. It works in four quadrants: forward motoring, forward generating, reverse motoring, reverse generating. That lets it reproduce a stop-start city pattern, a cold start, or a turbo lag event.

An absorption dynamometer cannot do this. It cannot motor the engine, and it cannot hold a prescribed speed-torque trace second by second, even though an eddy-current unit changes load faster than a water brake. It only holds a steady speed while the operator sets load.

The test cell makes the measurement repeatable

A test cell is a room designed around the dynamometer. The engine sits on a rigid bed, often on anti-vibration mounts, aligned to the dynamometer shaft. The coupling between engine and dynamometer carries a fixed guard: a spinning shaft at 5000 rev/min must never be exposed.

Services are fixed: fuel supply with a measured tank or flow meter (5.3), coolant circuit with a heat exchanger and temperature control, ventilation to supply combustion air and remove fumes, exhaust extraction to carry gases outside, and fire detection with a clean-agent extinguisher.

The instrument panel logs speed, torque, temperatures and flows at the same time. Repeatability comes from keeping all of these the same from run to run.

  1. Warm the engine to a steady coolant temperature.
  2. Set the speed and load on the dynamometer controller.
  3. Log torque, speed, fuel flow and temperatures together.
  4. Repeat the point once to check drift.

Worked example

5.5 Heat balance

The engine takes chemical energy in with the fuel and turns part of it into work at the shaft. The rest leaves as heat, and it must go somewhere: into the cooling water, up the exhaust pipe, or out through the block by radiation and convection. A heat balance is a sheet that lists these streams in kW and as a percentage of the fuel power, and shows that they add up. It is how a test cell answers the question 'where did the fuel money go?', and it is how radiators and exhaust heat recovery equipment are sized.

You need: fuel power and brake thermal efficiency from 5.1, and brake power and fuel flow measurement from 5.3.

The sheet, and why it must close

Chemical energy enters the engine at one rate and leaves at several. The rate at which the fuel brings energy in is the fuel power:

P_f = m_f × CV / 3.6

P_f is the fuel power in kW, m_f is the fuel mass flow in kg/h, CV is the calorific value of the fuel in MJ/kg, and 3.6 converts kg/h × MJ/kg into kW.

On the output side there are four streams: brake power P_b at the shaft, heat to the cooling water P_c, heat carried away by the exhaust gas P_e, and the rest P_u, which covers radiation, convection, heat to the lubricating oil and any fuel that did not burn completely. Energy is conserved, so:

P_f = P_b + P_c + P_e + P_u

P_f is fuel power, P_b is brake power, P_c is heat to cooling water, P_e is heat in the exhaust gas, and P_u is the unaccounted remainder, all in kW.

Only a few universities teach the heat balance as a full topic, but industry draws one for every new engine. Each term is then also written as a percentage of P_f.

Measuring each stream

Brake power comes from the dynamometer (see 5.2). The cooling water term comes from the water itself:

P_c = m_w × c_pw × ΔT_w

m_w is the cooling water mass flow in kg/s, c_pw is the specific heat of water, 4.18 kJ/kgK, and ΔT_w is the rise in water temperature across the jacket in K.

The exhaust term is the sensible heat in the gas above ambient:

P_e = m_g × c_pg × ΔT_g

m_g is the exhaust gas mass flow in kg/s, c_pg is about 1.05 kJ/kgK for exhaust gas, and ΔT_g is the exhaust temperature minus the ambient air temperature in K.

  • Water flow by rotameter, or by filling a bucket for a measured time; water temperatures by thermometers at the jacket inlet and outlet.
  • Exhaust gas flow is air flow plus fuel flow, m_g = m_a + m_f, both measured as in 5.3. Using air alone understates P_e.
  • A gas calorimeter checks the exhaust figure: it cools the gas and measures the heat given up, which includes the latent heat of the water vapour. That is why it reads slightly higher than m_g × c_pg × ΔT_g.
  • P_u is not measured. It is P_f minus the other three terms.

Reading and drawing the sheet

Two rules keep the sheet honest. First, every percentage is taken on P_f, never on P_b. Second, all four terms must be in the same unit before they are added; kW throughout is easiest, so a figure in kJ/h must be divided by 3600 first.

Draw the sheet as a bar or a divided block: one long bar of 100 % cut into four pieces, largest first. A pie chart with four thin slices is hard to read at a glance; a bar shows at once that the exhaust carries more than half the fuel energy in the worked example below.

The unaccounted term shrinks as the engine gets hotter. A cold engine stores heat in its metal and oil, and that stored heat lands in P_u. Once the engine holds a steady temperature, the storage stops and P_u falls to a small radiation and convection figure.

Worked example

Question. An engine burns 3.0 kg/h of fuel of calorific value 42 MJ/kg and delivers 10 kW at the brake. Cooling water flows at 6 kg/min and rises 12 °C across the jacket. Exhaust gas flows at 0.045 kg/s with c_p = 1.05 kJ/kgK and leaves 400 K above ambient. Draw up the heat balance in kW and in percent.

  1. Fuel power: P_f = m_f × CV / 3.6 = 3.0 × 42 / 3.6 = 35.0 kW.
  2. Cooling water flow: m_w = 6 / 60 = 0.1 kg/s.
  3. Cooling water heat: P_c = m_w × c_pw × ΔT_w = 0.1 × 4.18 × 12 = 5.02 kW.
  4. Exhaust heat: P_e = m_g × c_pg × ΔT_g = 0.045 × 1.05 × 400 = 18.9 kW.
  5. Unaccounted: P_u = P_f − P_b − P_c − P_e = 35.0 − 10 − 5.02 − 18.9 = 1.08 kW.
  6. Percentages of fuel power: brake 10/35.0 = 28.6 %; cooling 5.02/35.0 = 14.3 %; exhaust 18.9/35.0 = 54.0 %; unaccounted 1.08/35.0 = 3.1 %.
  7. Check: 28.6 + 14.3 + 54.0 + 3.1 = 100.0 %, so the sheet closes on fuel power.

Answer. Fuel power 35.0 kW splits into 10 kW brake (28.6 %), 5.02 kW to cooling water (14.3 %), 18.9 kW in the exhaust (54.0 %) and 1.08 kW unaccounted (3.1 %).

Exam traps

  • The sheet closes on fuel power, not on brake power. Percentages taken on 10 kW would give 100 % for the brake and nonsense for the rest.
  • Exhaust mass flow is air plus fuel, m_a + m_f, not air alone; using air alone loses the fuel mass and understates the exhaust heat.
  • Never mix units on one sheet: kW, kJ/h and kcal/min must all be converted to a single unit before adding.
  • Use the temperature rise, not the absolute temperature: ΔT_g is exhaust minus ambient, and ΔT_w is outlet minus inlet. A rise of 12 K is the same as a rise of 12 °C.
  • Mass flows must be in kg/s before multiplying by c_p × ΔT: divide kg/h by 3600 and kg/min by 60.

5.6 Variables affecting engine performance

Any variable that changes the trapped air mass, the mixture strength or the timing moves brake power, brake thermal efficiency and emissions together. Engine ratings are quoted at agreed reference air conditions so that a result measured on a hot day or in a different test cell can be compared with the maker's figure. In a test cell, on a dynamometer and during certification, the observed result is corrected to the same reference conditions. Tuning a vehicle for altitude, humidity or fuel quality uses the same ideas. The aim is the shape of each curve, not just a list.

You need: 5.1 to 5.3 for the measured quantities; Unit 2 for ignition and injection timing; Unit 3 for knock.

Speed, load and the shape of BSFC

Speed changes three things at once: the trapped air mass per cycle, friction and pumping loss. Indicated power rises with speed until breathing becomes the limit, then falls. Friction power rises faster than indicated power at high speed. The gap between indicated and brake power therefore grows. Brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) is fuel mass flow divided by brake power. BSFC has a minimum at mid-speed. At low speed, heat loss and friction are a large share of each cycle. At high speed, friction and pumping are large. Brake thermal efficiency is the reciprocal idea: ηbth = 3600 / (BSFC × LCV) with BSFC in kg/kWh and LCV in kJ/kg, so the speed of minimum BSFC is also the speed of maximum brake thermal efficiency. The best efficiency is near the BSFC minimum. Load has a similar curve. At low load, friction and auxiliary losses dominate, so BSFC is high. It falls as load rises and is lowest around three-quarter load. At full load, SI engines enrich the mixture and CI engines reach the smoke limit, so BSFC rises again. The figure shows speed curves for correct and retarded timing.

BSFC = m<sub>f</sub> / P<sub>b</sub>;  η<sub>bth</sub> = P<sub>b</sub> / (m<sub>f</sub> × LCV)

mf is fuel mass flow in kg/s; Pb is brake power in kW; LCV is lower calorific value in kJ/kg; ηbth is brake thermal efficiency.

Mixture strength, equivalence ratio and timing

Mixture strength is best described by the equivalence ratio φ. It compares the actual fuel-air ratio with the stoichiometric fuel-air ratio: φ = (F/A)/(F/A)stoich = (A/F)stoich/(A/F). Here F/A is fuel-to-air mass ratio and A/F is air-to-fuel mass ratio. φ = 1 is stoichiometric, φ > 1 is rich and φ < 1 is lean. For SI engines, maximum brake power comes from a slightly rich mixture, with φ just above 1. The extra fuel improves flame speed and ensures that all air is used. Best brake thermal efficiency comes from a slightly lean mixture, φ around 0.85 to 0.95, because combustion is more complete, the throttle is more open (lower pumping loss) and no extra fuel is supplied beyond what the air can burn. Very rich mixtures raise CO and HC. Very lean mixtures slow combustion and can misfire. In CI engines the overall mixture is always lean; power is limited by smoke. Spark timing or injection timing sets where combustion occurs relative to top dead centre. Maximum brake torque (MBT) timing gives the highest brake torque at a given speed and mixture. Too much advance raises peak pressure, causes knock and raises NOx. Too much retard loses expansion work, raises exhaust temperature and can raise HC. The figure's retarded curve peaks earlier and lower. Timing and mixture together set CO, HC and NOx; see Unit 6 for the formation chemistry.

Air, temperature, deposits and fuel

Intake air density sets the trapped air mass. Density is ρ = p/(R T), where p is absolute pressure in Pa, T is absolute temperature in K and R is the gas constant for air, about 287 J/(kg·K). A hot day or a high-altitude site lowers density, so brake power falls unless the engine is corrected or boosted. Humidity adds water vapour, which displaces some air and gives a small loss. The standard corrects measured power to reference air temperature, pressure and humidity. Observed power and corrected power are therefore different numbers. Always state which one you mean. Coolant and oil temperature matter. Cold coolant and cold oil raise friction and make fuel vaporisation poorer, so BSFC rises. Very hot coolant can cause knock and thin the oil. Carbon deposits raise the compression ratio, create hot spots and restrict breathing, so they can cause knock and raise NOx. Valve clearance that is too tight leaks compression and can burn valves; too loose reduces lift and makes noise. Exhaust back pressure raises pumping work, especially on a turbocharged engine, and reduces scavenging. Fuel quality matters. Octane number controls SI knock; cetane number controls CI ignition delay; calorific value sets energy per kg; density sets injected mass for a given volume. Compression ratio raises ideal efficiency but is limited by knock in SI engines and by peak pressure and NOx in diesels. Correction procedures are in 5.8.

5.7 Methods to improve engine performance

Power and efficiency come from four levers: more trapped air, better air-fuel mixing, less friction, and better control. Engine makers rarely find one large gain; they stack small gains. A turbocharger, a high-pressure injector, low-friction oil and a closed-loop ECU each move brake power, brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) or both. Road cars, trucks and gensets use these levers, then prove the result on a standard test. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic.

You need: 5.6 for the variables being pushed, and see Unit 4 for supercharging and turbocharging.

The four levers

Each lever changes a measurable variable. More air raises trapped mass. Better mixing shortens and shapes the burn. Less friction raises brake power for the same indicated power. Better control keeps the engine near its best point.

  • More air: turbocharging or supercharging raises intake density; an intercooler lowers charge temperature and raises density again. Four valves and good ports cut pumping loss; variable valve timing (VVT) moves the torque band.
  • Better mixture: high-pressure common rail direct injection (CRDI) gives a finer spray; electronic injection timing places the burn near top dead centre (TDC); chamber shape adds swirl, squish or tumble.
  • Less friction: low-viscosity oil with friction modifiers, surface coatings, lighter reciprocating parts, and better rings and bearings cut friction mean effective pressure (FMEP).
  • Better control: a closed-loop engine control unit (ECU) uses a lambda sensor to hold air-fuel ratio and a knock sensor to protect spark advance.

The levers interact: more air needs more fuel, and control keeps the extra air useful.

Cycle changes

Changing the cycle can raise efficiency more than any single part. Miller or Atkinson operation closes the intake valve early or late, so the expansion stroke is longer than the effective compression stroke. The engine extracts more work from the same fuel. A higher compression ratio does the same, but it is limited by knock in spark-ignition (SI) engines and by peak pressure in compression-ignition (CI) engines. In a turbocharged engine, Miller timing also lowers knock and turbine inlet temperature, so boost can rise. Cylinder deactivation and stop-start are control changes: they cut pumping and idle losses when demand is low.

ηOtto = 1 − 1/rγ−1

ηOtto is ideal Otto cycle efficiency; r is compression ratio; γ is specific heat ratio.

The ideal gain is set by the compression ratio. These changes must keep combustion stable; too much Miller timing hurts combustion and torque. In practice, the valve event is tuned with the boost level.

The trade-off and proof

Every step that raises power or efficiency usually raises oxides of nitrogen (NOx) or cost. More air raises peak pressure and temperature; advanced injection raises NOx; less friction can raise wear; aggressive Miller timing can hurt combustion. A boost increase must be matched by fuel, or the mixture leans and power falls; the knock limit or turbine inlet temperature sets the ceiling, not the boost number. A change that wins on one point may lose on another. The engine must be proved on a standard test, not a single best point. A performance claim is brake power, BSFC and emissions at defined speed-load points. Test procedure and in-cylinder pressure are in 5.8.

Use a dynamometer and repeat the same test conditions before and after. The trade-off is why a production engine is a compromise: driveability, fuel quality, durability and emission limits all pull against the peak number.

Worked example

  1. Air density ratio = 1.4. At the same air-fuel ratio, power scales with trapped air mass: P_new = P_old × 1.4.
  2. P_new = 80 kW × 1.4 = 112 kW.
  3. Gain = 112 − 80 = 32 kW = 40 %.
  4. Fuel flow = BSFC × brake power. New: 240 g/kWh × 112 kW = 26 880 g/h = 26.88 kg/h.
  5. Old: 240 g/kWh × 80 kW = 19 200 g/h = 19.2 kg/h.

Answer. The new power is 112 kW, a 40 % gain, and the new fuel flow is 26.88 kg/h against 19.2 kg/h before.

Exam traps

  • Boost alone does not raise power: fuel must rise by the same ratio, and the knock limit or turbine inlet temperature sets the ceiling.
  • Miller or Atkinson timing is not just a longer expansion stroke; valve timing must keep combustion stable.
  • Low-viscosity oil is not always better; it must protect the bearing film at high load.
  • A higher compression ratio raises ideal efficiency but can cause knock or exceed peak-pressure limits.

5.8 Testing standards and in-cylinder pressure measurement

Two labs test the same engine and report two different powers. Nothing is wrong with either engine; the procedures differ. A test standard fixes the procedure — what is fitted to the engine, the running-in, the speed, the air conditions and the correction to a reference — so that numbers can be compared. The in-cylinder pressure record is the other half: it is the most detailed evidence an engine test can give, and every combustion number — imep, peak pressure, heat release, cycle-to-cycle variation — is derived from it.

You need: 5.2 for imep to indicated power, 5.4 for the test cell, Unit 3 for combustion stages, and Unit 6 for emission measurement.

A rated power is a procedure, not a property

The same engine can be sold as 60 kW or 52 kW. The difference is what was bolted on during the test. A fan, an alternator, an air cleaner and a full exhaust system each take power that the bare engine never sees.

  • Gross power: bare engine, only the pumps needed for its own operation, no fan, no alternator, no silencer.
  • Net power: with the production fan, alternator, air cleaner and exhaust system fitted, as installed in the vehicle.
  • Running-in: the standard fixes how long the engine runs before measurement, and at what load.
  • Steady state: power is quoted at a fixed speed, with coolant and oil at declared temperatures, after readings stop drifting.

ISO 1585 covers net power of automotive engines. ISO 3046 covers power ratings of reciprocating engines for industrial and marine use and defines several rating classes (continuous, overload, intermittent) with different permitted durations. SAE J1349 is the American net-power procedure. The Bureau of Indian Standards publishes an Indian standard adopted from ISO 1585, so an Indian net rating carries the same meaning. Quote the convention, the speed and the fuel along with the power, or the number means nothing.

Reference air and the driving cycle

Air density decides how much fuel can be burnt, so a cold January morning in Delhi and a hot May afternoon give different power from the same engine. Standards correct measured power to reference air, typically 25 °C, about 100 kPa dry air pressure and a stated humidity, by multiplying the measured value by a correction factor.

P₀ = α · P,   α = (p_ref / p_air)^a × (T / T_ref)^b

P₀ is corrected power (kW); P measured power (kW); p_air measured dry air pressure (kPa); T air temperature (K); p_ref, T_ref reference values set by the standard; a and b exponents fixed by the standard, with b positive so hot air corrects upwards. Most standards refuse to apply α outside roughly 0.93 to 1.07.

  • NEDC: 1180 s, about 11.0 km, two phases (urban, extra-urban), gentle accelerations.
  • WLTC: 1800 s, about 23.3 km, four phases, higher speeds and harder accelerations.
  • MIDC: the modified Indian driving cycle, adapted for Indian road speeds; it is the type-approval cycle for BS VI cars and light-duty vehicles, while two-wheelers are certified on WMTC.
  • BS VI: the current Indian stage, with separate petrol and diesel limits in g/km; for a petrol car, CO is capped near 1.0 g/km and NOx near 0.06 g/km.

The cycle sets the speed and load history, so the cycle decides the number. A CO figure from NEDC cannot be compared with a CO figure from WLTC.

The pressure measurement chain

In a spark-ignition engine the cylinder pressure swings from about 1 bar to 80 bar within 40° of crank angle, thousands of times a minute; in a turbocharged diesel the peak can reach about 150 bar or more. The chain that records it has four jobs: sense, convert, time-stamp, store.

  1. Transducer: a piezoelectric quartz crystal with its diaphragm flush with the combustion chamber wall, so no passage fills with carbon; hot engines use a water-cooled body.
  2. Charge to voltage: the crystal releases a small charge, of the order of picocoulombs per bar; a charge amplifier turns it into a voltage proportional to pressure. Cable movement and moisture also make charge, so low-noise cable is used.
  3. Crank-angle timing: an encoder on the crankshaft gives one pulse per degree plus an index pulse once per revolution.
  4. Logging: the data acquisition system samples pressure at every encoder pulse for 100 or more consecutive cycles and stores each sample against its crank angle.
  5. TDC reference and pegging: the encoder index is tied to true TDC using a motoring run or a TDC sensor; the relative piezo record is then shifted by a constant so that the compression line matches pVn = constant during motoring, which gives absolute pressure.

Without pegging the trace floats at the wrong absolute level: peak pressure and the heat-release curve come out wrong, but the imep taken as the closed-cycle integral ∮p dV is largely unaffected, because a constant offset cancels in the integral. A TDC (angle) error does move imep, but only slightly.

What the record gives you

  • p-θ plot: peak pressure and the crank angle where it occurs; the slope gives the maximum rate of pressure rise.
  • p-V plot: the enclosed area of the closed cycle is indicated work; divide by displacement per cycle to get imep. A log p versus log V plot gives the polytropic index.
  • Heat release rate: from the first law applied to the cylinder contents, using p-θ and V-θ; the crank angles for 10 % and 90 % burnt define the combustion duration.
  • Cycle-to-cycle variation: compute imep for each cycle separately and take the coefficient of variation.

COV = (σ / μ) × 100 %

σ is the standard deviation of imep over the recorded cycles (bar); μ is the mean imep (bar); COV is the coefficient of variation in percent. Below about 5 % is taken as stable combustion; a highly diluted engine that drifts past it is close to misfire.

Worked example

Question. A piezoelectric transducer and a crank-angle encoder record 100 cycles of a 1.5 L four-stroke engine running at 2000 rpm. Mean imep over the 100 cycles is 8.2 bar, with a standard deviation of 0.21 bar; peak pressure is 78 bar at 8° ATDC. Find the indicated power and the coefficient of variation of imep. Is the combustion stable?

  1. Convert imep to SI: 8.2 bar = 8.2 × 10⁵ Pa.
  2. Displacement volume: V_d = 1.5 L = 1.5 × 10⁻³ m³.
  3. A four-stroke engine fires once per two revolutions, so cycles per second = N/120 = 2000/120 = 16.67 s⁻¹.
  4. Indicated power IP = imep × V_d × (N/120) = 8.2 × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ × 16.67 = 20 500 W = 20.5 kW.
  5. COV = (σ/μ) × 100 = (0.21 / 8.2) × 100 = 2.56 %.
  6. The usual limit for stable combustion is 5 %, so 2.56 % is well inside it.

Answer. Indicated power is 20.5 kW and the COV of imep is 2.56 %, so combustion is stable.

Exam traps

  • Using the piezo voltage directly as pressure. The crystal gives only a relative signal. Without pegging the p-θ curve floats at the wrong absolute level, so peak pressure and the heat-release curve are wrong; the imep taken as the closed-cycle integral ∮p dV is largely unaffected because a constant offset cancels. A TDC (angle) error does move imep, but only slightly.
  • Comparing a gross rating with a net rating, or an NEDC emission figure with a WLTC one. Different procedures, different numbers.
  • Forgetting the factor 2 for a four-stroke: using N/60 instead of N/120 in the imep-to-power step doubles the answer.
  • Ignoring TDC offset. One degree of error shifts peak pressure and its crank angle, and changes imep at the same time.
  • Quoting COV as a fraction when the question asks for a percentage, or computing COV of peak pressure while calling it COV of imep.

Understand Why does a piezoelectric pressure signal need pegging? 3 marks

The crystal produces charge only when pressure changes, so the output is relative, not absolute, and slowly drifts. During a motoring run the compression line follows pVn = constant, so the whole record is shifted by one constant until it matches, fixing absolute pressure.

Analyze COV of imep rises from 2 % at stoichiometric to 7 % at a very lean mixture. What does that tell you about the engine? 4 marks

The lean mixture burns slowly and the flame may not establish every cycle, so imep scatters cycle by cycle. A COV above 5 % means partial misfires and rising unburnt hydrocarbon emissions, so that lean limit is not usable without charge stratification or faster combustion.

Understand Why can an emission figure from NEDC not be compared with one from WLTC? 3 marks

Each cycle is a different speed and load trace, with different accelerations, top speeds and durations. The engine's temperature history and transient fuelling differ, so the measured g/km belongs to that cycle only.

Analyze A 1° error in TDC reference is suspected. Which measured quantities move, and why? 4 marks

Peak pressure and its crank angle move, because the whole p-θ curve is shifted in angle; imep changes slightly because the cycle integration window and the V-θ pairing are wrong, and heat release rate changes more strongly because it depends on dp/dθ.

Takeaways

  1. Every performance number is built from three measurements only: torque, speed and fuel flow.
  2. Brake power is measured; indicated power is inferred; friction power is the difference between them.
  3. A Morse test separates the cylinders' indicated powers by removing one working cylinder at a time.
  4. Fuel flow and air flow must be logged with the load, because efficiency is meaningless without both.
  5. A dynamometer is a calibrated brake; a test cell makes its reading repeatable and safe.
  6. The heat balance sheet proves that the measured outputs add up to the fuel energy supplied.
  7. Load, speed, AFR, ignition or injection timing, compression ratio and ambient conditions move power and efficiency together.
  8. Performance improves only through more air, better mixture, less friction and better control.
  9. A result is valid only if the test standard, the running-in state and steady or transient condition are stated.
  10. The in-cylinder pressure trace is the richest single record of what happens inside the engine.

Glossary

Absorption dynamometer
A dynamometer that converts the absorbed engine power into heat, as in water-brake and eddy-current types.
Air box
A large settling chamber fitted with an orifice or nozzle, used to measure the air drawn by an engine smoothly and steadily.
BMEP
Brake mean effective pressure, the constant pressure that would produce the measured brake work in one cycle.
BS VI
The Indian Bharat Stage VI emission norm, applied to new vehicles from 2020, with tight limits on NOx, particulate matter and sulphur in fuel.
BSFC
Brake specific fuel consumption, the fuel mass needed per kilowatt-hour of brake work.
Charge amplifier
An electronic unit that converts the charge from a piezoelectric sensor into a proportional voltage.
Chemiluminescent analyzer
An emission instrument that measures oxides of nitrogen by the light emitted when NO reacts with ozone.
Cycle-to-cycle variation
The scatter in peak pressure and work between successive cycles of the same cylinder under identical settings.
Eddy-current dynamometer
A dynamometer that loads the engine by electromagnetic drag with no mechanical contact, allowing fast control.
Flame ionisation detector
An instrument that measures hydrocarbon concentration by ionising the sample in a hydrogen flame.
Gross power
Power measured with only the auxiliaries essential for running; net power is measured with the full installation.
Heat balance sheet
A statement showing how the fuel energy supplied is divided between brake power, cooling, exhaust and unaccounted losses.
Heat release rate
The rate of chemical energy release during combustion, calculated from the cylinder pressure trace.
Logging
Recording several channels of test data, such as pressure, crank angle, flow and temperature, together with a common time base.
MIDC
The Modified Indian Driving Cycle, the speed–time schedule used in India for chassis dynamometer emission tests.
Motoring test
A test in which an external motor drives the engine without fuel to measure its friction and pumping losses.
NEDC
The New European Driving Cycle, a speed–time schedule for vehicle emission testing consisting of urban and extra-urban phases.
Non-dispersive infrared analyzer
An instrument that measures carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide from their absorption of infrared light.
Prony brake
The simplest absorption dynamometer, using friction on a drum and a measured reaction arm.
Running-in
The initial period of engine operation in which surfaces bed in, friction falls and performance stabilises.
Smoke meter
An instrument that measures exhaust opacity or filter blackening to estimate soot from a diesel engine.
Steady state
A test condition in which speed, load and temperatures are held constant for long enough for all readings to settle.
Stoichiometric air–fuel ratio
The minimum air mass per unit fuel mass needed for complete combustion; about 14.7 for petrol and 14.5 for diesel.
Test cell
A controlled room with a dynamometer, cooling, ventilation, fuel supply and instrumentation for repeatable engine testing.
Volumetric efficiency
The ratio of the air actually trapped per cycle to the swept volume of air at ambient conditions.
