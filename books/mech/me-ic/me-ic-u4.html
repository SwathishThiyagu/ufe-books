4.1 Need for cooling; air and liquid cooling systems

Only about 30 percent of the fuel energy reaches the crankshaft as work. Much of the rest soaks into the metal and must leave, or the piston rings lose their oil film and the liner scores. Cooling is the path that heat takes from the cylinder walls to the outside air. Air cooling hands that heat from fins straight to moving air; liquid cooling carries it in a coolant to a radiator. Motorcycles use the first; cars, tractors and most gensets the second.

You need: the engine components and the four-stroke cycle, and the meaning of indicated and brake power from engine testing and performance.

Why heat must leave the engine

Fuel energy does not all become work. In a typical diesel engine, of every 100 units of heat released by burning fuel, about 30 leave as brake power at the crankshaft, about 30 go out with the exhaust gas, about 30 pass into the coolant, and about 10 are lost to friction and radiation. The coolant share must be carried away all the time the engine runs.

Metal sets one limit: aluminium alloy pistons lose strength quickly when they run hot. Lubricating oil sets a tighter limit: above roughly 150 °C the oil at the piston rings oxidises, turns to varnish and coke, and its film breaks. A broken film means metal touching metal, and the liner scores. The oil system that keeps this film alive is in Lubrication systems and engine friction.

Temperatures inside a running engine are far from even. The burning gas briefly passes 2000 °C, yet the cylinder wall stays near 150 to 200 °C because coolant sits behind it. The exhaust valve head runs hottest, roughly 650 to 750 °C, so the jacket passages are packed closest round the exhaust port and valve seats. Uneven cooling is as harmful as no cooling: if one side of the head runs hotter, it expands more, and the head can warp or blow its gasket. Too much cooling also hurts: a cold engine vaporises petrol badly, wears faster and wastes fuel.

Q = ṁ × c × ΔT

Q is the heat carried away per second in kW; ṁ is the coolant mass flow in kg/s; c is the specific heat of the coolant in kJ/kg·K (4.18 for water); ΔT is the temperature rise of the coolant in K.

Air cooling: fins, baffles and a blower

An air-cooled engine carries no coolant. Thin fins cast on the barrel and head add surface, so the same wall can hand its heat to a much larger area of moving air.

Fin spacing is a compromise. Fins packed close give more area on paper, but the air trapped between them stagnates and stops removing heat. Fins are kept thin to save weight and space, yet thick enough to conduct heat out to the tip; the real bottleneck is the air side, which removes heat far more slowly than the metal conducts it.

The air must be made to flow. On a motorcycle road speed does the job. A stationary pump set or a scooter with an enclosed engine has no useful road air, so a fan pushes air and sheet-metal cowls guide it between the fins instead of letting it slip around the outside.

Air cooling suits motorcycles, scooters, small pump sets and aircraft piston engines. It has no radiator, no pump and no coolant to freeze or leak, and it warms up fast. Its weakness is the small heat transfer coefficient of air, so the metal runs hotter and power per litre is limited.

Liquid cooling: jacket, radiator, pump, thermostat

A liquid-cooled engine wraps the hot parts in a jacket: a hollow space cast into the block and head, running close to the liner, the valve seats and the exhaust port. Coolant in the jacket picks up heat, a pump pushes it to the radiator, and air flowing over thin tubes and fins takes the heat out of it. Some syllabi call this indirect cooling, because the air never touches the hot metal.

The circuit has four parts. The water pump is a small belt-driven centrifugal pump. The radiator is the heat exchanger. The fan pulls air through it when the vehicle is slow or standing. The thermostat is a valve filled with wax: it stays shut while the engine is cold, so the engine warms up quickly, then opens at its set temperature and lets coolant through the radiator.

Liquid removes far more heat from a given area of metal than air does, so the jacket stays compact and all cylinders run at nearly the same temperature. The price is weight, cost, pump power and parts that can leak. Circulation by pump, by density difference, and under a pressure cap that raises the boiling point is covered in Thermosyphon, forced circulation and pressure cooling.

Worked example

Question. A genset engine burns 8 kg of diesel per hour. The calorific value of the diesel is 42 000 kJ/kg. Thirty percent of the fuel energy leaves through the coolant. The water may rise 6 K; c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K. Find (a) the fuel power, (b) the heat rate carried by the coolant, (c) the coolant mass flow in kg/s and in kg/min.

  1. Convert the fuel flow to kg/s: ṁf = 8 kg/h ÷ 3600 s/h = 2.222 × 10⁻³ kg/s.
  2. Fuel power Pf = 2.222 × 10⁻³ kg/s × 42 000 kJ/kg = 93.33 kW.
  3. Heat to coolant Q = 0.30 × 93.33 kW = 28.0 kW.
  4. Rearrange Q = ṁ c ΔT for the flow: ṁ = Q ÷ (c ΔT) = 28.0 kW ÷ (4.18 kJ/kg·K × 6 K) = 28.0 ÷ 25.08 = 1.12 kg/s.
  5. Convert to per minute: 1.12 kg/s × 60 s/min = 67 kg/min.

Answer. Fuel power is 93.3 kW, the coolant carries 28 kW, and the required coolant flow is about 1.12 kg/s, that is 67 kg per minute.

Worked example 2

Question. A 150 cc air-cooled engine must reject 3.0 kW through its fins at full load, with a heat transfer coefficient of 50 W/m²·K, mean fin temperature 160 °C and air at 35 °C. (a) Find the fin area needed. (b) Without fins the barrel and head offer only 0.08 m² of surface; what surface temperature rise would that need? (c) The same engine works a pump set in a closed shed where the air is 55 °C. Find the mean fin temperature for the same 3.0 kW.

  1. Convection from the fins: Q = h A (Ts − Tair), where h is the heat transfer coefficient in W/m²·K, A the fin area in m² and Ts the surface temperature.
  2. (a) Temperature difference = 160 − 35 = 125 K. A = Q ÷ (h ΔT) = 3000 W ÷ (50 W/m²·K × 125 K) = 3000 ÷ 6250 = 0.48 m².
  3. (b) With A = 0.08 m²: ΔT = Q ÷ (h A) = 3000 ÷ (50 × 0.08) = 3000 ÷ 4 = 750 K. The surface would have to sit about 750 K above the air, near 785 °C, which no aluminium head survives. The fins multiply the area six times (0.48 ÷ 0.08 = 6).
  4. (c) Q, h and A are unchanged, so ΔT stays 125 K. Mean fin temperature = 55 + 125 = 180 °C, 20 K hotter than outdoors, and the oil at the rings moves closer to its limit.

Answer. (a) 0.48 m² of fin area; (b) a 750 K rise, which is impossible, so fins are essential; (c) the fins settle at about 180 °C in the 55 °C shed.

Exam traps

  • Starting the coolant calculation from the brake power on the nameplate. The coolant takes a share of the fuel energy, so start from fuel flow times calorific value.
  • Calling the thermostat a temperature sensor. It is a wax-filled valve that opens and closes the coolant path; the fan and the gauge are separate.
  • Mixing units in Q = ṁ c ΔT. With c in kJ/kg·K the answer is in kW; using c = 4180 J/kg·K with kJ elsewhere gives a factor of 1000 error.
  • Saying the radiator cools the engine. The radiator cools the coolant; the jacket is what touches the hot metal.

4.2 Thermosyphon, forced circulation and pressure cooling; coolants

A liquid cooling loop moves heat from the engine jacket to the radiator. It can run on density difference alone (thermosyphon), on a pump (forced circulation), or under a pressure cap that lifts the boiling point. The coolant—water or a glycol mix—sets the safe upper temperature. Only a few universities teach all three arrangements at full depth. Road engines use forced circulation with a pressure cap; thermosyphon survives on small stationary engines.

You need: the jacket, pump, radiator and thermostat from Need for cooling; air and liquid cooling systems, and saturation temperature from thermodynamics.

Thermosyphon: flow by density difference

Hot water is lighter than cold water. In a closed loop, the column from the jacket to the radiator is hot and light; the downcomer is cold and heavy. Gravity pulls the heavy column down and pushes the light column up. Flow continues without a pump.

Δp = (ρc − ρh) g h

Δp is the driving pressure in Pa; ρc is cold-water density in kg/m³; ρh is hot-water density in kg/m³; g is 9.81 m/s²; h is the height between jacket centre and radiator centre in m.

The head is small, tens of pascals in a car-sized loop, so pipes are short and wide and the radiator sits well above the engine. Flow only starts once the water is hot, and it rises with the temperature difference. A small stationary engine at steady load copes; a vehicle engine at high load needs a pump.

Forced circulation and pressure cooling

A pump, usually belt-driven from the crankshaft, pushes coolant through the jacket, radiator and heater. A thermostat blocks flow to the radiator until the coolant reaches about 80–90 °C, then opens; a bypass route keeps the pump circulating during warm-up. An expansion tank gives the coolant room to expand and keeps the pump inlet flooded. Forced circulation gives a predictable flow at all speeds, so the radiator can be smaller.

A pressure cap is a spring-loaded valve. It holds the system above atmospheric pressure and raises the boiling point, because saturation temperature rises with pressure: a cap set 100 kPa above atmosphere lifts the boiling point of water from 100 °C to about 120 °C. The cap removes no heat itself. What it buys is margin: the coolant can run hotter without boiling, the larger coolant-to-air temperature difference lets a smaller radiator reject the same heat, and the system does not boil away on a hill climb or at altitude. The cap holds two valves: the pressure valve opens to the overflow tank when the set pressure is passed, and a small vacuum valve lets coolant flow back as the engine cools. Never open a hot cap: the sudden drop to atmospheric pressure flashes coolant to steam and scalds. A faulty cap lets the engine boil at a lower temperature;

Coolants and additives

A good coolant has a high specific heat and thermal conductivity, a low freezing point, a high boiling point, low viscosity so the pump moves it easily, no attack on iron, aluminium, copper and rubber hoses, no foaming, chemical stability over long service, and low cost and toxicity. Water has the best specific heat (about 4.18 kJ/kg·K) and is cheap, but it freezes at 0 °C, corrodes iron and aluminium, and hard water leaves calcium and magnesium scale on hot walls. Scale insulates and can block narrow passages.

Ethylene glycol (EG) and propylene glycol (PG) lower the freezing point and raise the boiling point. A 50 percent glycol mix is common: it gives freeze protection near −37 °C (EG) and a few degrees more boiling margin, while its specific heat, roughly 3.3 to 3.6 kJ/kg·K, stays acceptable. Above about 60–70 percent glycol, freeze protection worsens and heat transfer falls. Corrosion inhibitors—silicates, phosphates, borates or organic acids—protect metals; they are consumed over time, so coolant has a service life. Use soft or distilled water with glycol concentrate, not hard tap water.

Worked example

Question. A thermosyphon system has 0.4 m between jacket centre and radiator centre. The hot riser carries water at 80 °C and the cold downcomer water at 40 °C. Take the densities from the water tables, 971.8 kg/m³ at 80 °C and 992.2 kg/m³ at 40 °C, and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the driving pressure. Explain why a pump is added on the same engine at higher load.

  1. Identify h = 0.4 m, ρc = 992.2 kg/m³ (40 °C), ρh = 971.8 kg/m³ (80 °C), g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. Density difference: 992.2 − 971.8 = 20.4 kg/m³.
  3. Δp = (ρc − ρh) g h = 20.4 × 9.81 × 0.4.
  4. 20.4 × 9.81 = 200.1; 200.1 × 0.4 = 80.0 Pa.
  5. For comparison, 80 Pa is the pressure of an 8 mm column of water. It can push only a gentle flow through wide pipes. At higher load the heat to be removed rises, the flow needed by Q = ṁ c ΔT rises with it, and a pump that adds tens of kPa becomes necessary.

Answer. Δp ≈ 80 Pa, about 8 mm of water head, which is why thermosyphon loops need tall radiators and wide pipes.

Worked example 2

Question. A radiator cap opens at 100 kPa gauge. Using the water tables, find the boiling point of the coolant (taken as water) (a) with the cap, at sea level where the air is at 101.3 kPa; (b) with an open system (no cap) at a hill town 2000 m up where the air is at 79.5 kPa; (c) with the same cap at that town.

  1. A cap gauge setting adds to the local atmospheric pressure: pabs = patm + pcap.
  2. (a) p = 101.3 + 100 = 201.3 kPa. The saturation temperature at 201.3 kPa is 120.4 °C.
  3. (b) p = 79.5 kPa. The saturation temperature at 79.5 kPa is 93.3 °C, so an open system would boil with a 90 °C thermostat barely open.
  4. (c) p = 79.5 + 100 = 179.5 kPa. The saturation temperature is 116.8 °C, still about 20 K above a 95 °C running temperature.

Answer. (a) 120.4 °C; (b) 93.3 °C; (c) 116.8 °C. The cap keeps a safe boiling margin even at altitude.

Exam traps

  • A pressure cap raises the boiling point; it does not remove heat itself. The radiator gets smaller only because the hotter coolant gives a larger temperature difference to the air.
  • Thermosyphon flow needs height and a density difference. A horizontal loop or zero temperature difference gives no flow.
  • Hard water scale insulates hot walls and blocks passages; do not treat it as harmless water.
  • More glycol is not always better. Above about 60–70 percent, freeze protection worsens and heat transfer falls.
  • Opening a hot pressure cap causes flash steam and scalds; wait for the engine to cool.

4.3 Lubrication systems and engine friction

Friction takes a share of the power the burning fuel produces at every load, and at idle it takes nearly all of it. The lubrication system exists to keep that share small: it stores oil, cleans it, pumps it at the right pressure and delivers it to every sliding pair, rings, bearings and valve gear. This chapter lists where friction comes from, explains how an oil film carries load, and describes the circuits (mist, splash, pressure feed, wet sump and dry sump) that place oil on the right surface at the right moment.

You need: the rings, bearings and crank train from engine components, and indicated and brake power from engine testing and performance.

Where engine friction comes from

Lubrication has more jobs than reducing friction. An engine oil must:

  • Reduce friction and wear between sliding parts.
  • Cool: oil carries heat away from the piston underside, bearings and turbo shaft.
  • Seal: the film between rings and liner helps hold compression.
  • Clean: oil washes wear debris and soot to the filter.
  • Cushion shock loads in the bearings, and protect metal against rust and acids.

Not all the power that the burning charge pushes onto the piston reaches the flywheel. Some is spent overcoming friction between moving parts, and some is spent pushing gas in and out of the cylinder. Together they form the friction power, FP. It is defined as the gap between the power developed inside the cylinder and the power delivered at the crankshaft.

FP = IP − BP

FP is friction power (kW); IP is indicated power, the power developed on the piston (kW); BP is brake power, the power measured at the crankshaft (kW).

Rubbing friction comes from four places. Piston rings press on the cylinder wall and carry the gas load; the skirt rubs sideways as the rod swings. Main and big-end bearings shear their oil film. The valve train — cam, tappets, pushrods, rockers — rubs on every cycle. Pumping loss is separate work: pulling air past a nearly closed throttle and pushing exhaust out. At light load in an SI engine, pumping loss grows large.

Parameters that influence friction: engine speed (friction rises roughly with speed), load (ring pressure rises with cylinder pressure), oil viscosity and its temperature, bore-to-stroke ratio, number of rings, and surface finish. Two quantities summarise the loss: a ratio, the mechanical efficiency, and a pressure, the friction mean effective pressure. Because friction power changes little with load at a fixed speed, mechanical efficiency is highest at full load (often 80 to 90 percent) and falls to zero at idle, where all the indicated power is spent on friction.

ηm = BP / IP

ηm is mechanical efficiency (no unit, often written as a percentage); BP is brake power (kW); IP is indicated power (kW).

fmep = FP / (Vd × N/120)

fmep is friction mean effective pressure (Pa or bar), a definition only; FP is friction power (W); Vd is total swept volume (m³); N is speed (rev/min); N/120 is the number of cycles each cylinder completes per second in a four-stroke engine; with Vd taken as the swept volume of all cylinders together, the product is the swept volume handled per second by the whole engine.

How an oil film carries load

An oil film carries load in three ways, and a running engine passes through all three.

Hydrodynamic lubrication. The journal turns inside the bearing with a small clearance. Viscous drag pulls oil into the narrowing gap ahead of the closest point. That oil cannot escape fast enough, so its pressure rises and forms a wedge, and the journal floats on the wedge. Metal never touches metal. This needs enough speed, enough viscosity and the correct clearance.

Boundary lubrication. The film is a few molecules thick, held on the surface by the oil's own polarity. Load is carried by surface contact, not by fluid pressure, so wear is real. Mixed lubrication lies between boundary and hydrodynamic.

At start-up the shaft rests on the bearing and only a boundary film exists until the pump builds pressure and speed rises. Those first seconds cause most bearing wear, which is why oil must still flow on a cold morning; viscosity behaviour is covered in Lubricants: properties and additives.

Lubrication methods: mist, splash and pressure feed

Systems are classified by how the oil reaches the surface.

  • Mist (petroil): oil is mixed into the petrol at 2 to 5 percent of the petrol volume (1:50 to 1:20). The fuel-air-oil mist wets the bearings and cylinder, and the rest burns.
  • Splash: a dipper on the big end scoops oil from a trough and throws it around the crankcase.
  • Pressure feed: a pump sends oil through drillings to the bearings, and the overflow splashes the walls and valve gear.

Mist lubrication suits two-strokes, which have no sump, and works at any tilt angle. It is cheap, but it fouls spark plugs and smokes. A four-stroke cannot use it: its oil must stay in a sump and be used again, and its crankcase never carries the fuel mixture, so there is no mist to carry the oil.

Splash lubrication is simple and served small stationary engines and old motorcycles. The oil level is critical: too low starves the dipper, too high makes the oil froth. Pressure feed is used on almost all car and truck engines, usually combined with splash for the cylinder walls.

Wet sump and dry sump circuits

A wet sump engine keeps its oil in a pan under the crankcase. Oil flows from the sump through a strainer to the pump, then to the filter, along the main gallery to the bearings, and drains back by gravity.

Each part has a job. The strainer is coarse mesh that stops large debris before the pump. The pump is usually a gear or rotor type driven from the crankshaft. A relief valve beside the pump opens at a set pressure and returns surplus oil to the sump, so the filter and hoses survive cold thick oil. The filter carries a bypass that opens if it clogs. The main gallery is a drilled passage along the block feeding the main bearings, from which drillings lead to the big ends, cam and rockers.

A dry sump engine keeps oil in a separate tank. A scavenge pump pulls oil from a shallow pan into the tank, and a pressure pump feeds the engine from the tank. It costs more and adds plumbing, but oil cannot surge away from the pick-up in a hard corner, the engine can sit lower, and the tank holds more oil that cools better. Racing cars, aircraft and some heavy diesels use it.

Worked example

Question. A 1.6 litre four-cylinder four-stroke engine is motored at 3000 rev/min with the fuel cut off, and the dynamometer reads a torque of 24 N·m. In a firing test at the same speed the indicated power is 30 kW. Find the friction power, the brake power, the mechanical efficiency and the friction mean effective pressure.

  1. While motoring, no fuel burns, so the torque the dynamometer absorbs is the torque needed to overcome friction plus pumping. Speed N = 3000 rev/min, torque T = 24 N·m.
  2. Friction power FP = 2πNT/60 = 2π × 3000 × 24 / 60 = 7540 W = 7.54 kW.
  3. Brake power BP = IP − FP = 30 − 7.54 = 22.46 kW.
  4. Mechanical efficiency ηm = BP / IP = 22.46 / 30 = 0.7487, that is 74.9 percent.
  5. fmep = FP ÷ (Vd × N/120) = 7540 W ÷ (1.6 × 10⁻³ m³ × 3000/120 s⁻¹) = 7540 ÷ 0.040 = 188 500 Pa ≈ 1.89 bar. This is the pressure that, acting on the pistons, would do just the friction and pumping work.

Answer. Friction power 7.54 kW, brake power 22.46 kW, mechanical efficiency 74.9 percent, fmep about 1.89 bar.

Exam traps

  • Mechanical efficiency is brake power divided by indicated power, never the other way round. A value above 1 is always a sign of this slip.
  • Motoring torque gives friction plus pumping loss. It is not pure rubbing friction, so do not call it 'bearing friction only'.
  • Friction power is IP minus BP. If the question gives brake power and efficiency, first find IP = BP / ηm, then subtract.
  • Mist lubrication belongs to crankcase-scavenged two-strokes, where the fuel mixture passes through the crankcase. Writing it for a four-stroke loses marks.

4.4 Lubricants: properties and additives

A lubricant is not chosen by feel. It is chosen by measured properties: how its viscosity changes with temperature, how cold it can pour, how hot it can safely be, how it resists oxidation and what additives it carries. These properties decide whether oil reaches the bearings at a cold start, stays on the cylinder wall at full load, and keeps sludge and wear low over thousands of kilometres. This chapter lists those properties with units and explains how additives turn a base oil into an engine oil.

You need Lubrication systems and engine friction for where the oil goes and what it must do at each sliding pair.

Viscosity, grade and temperature limits

Viscosity is the oil's resistance to shear. Dynamic viscosity μ (Greek mu) is shear stress divided by shear rate; its SI unit is the pascal second (Pa·s), and engine oils are quoted in millipascal seconds (mPa·s). Kinematic viscosity ν (Greek nu) is μ divided by density; its unit is mm²/s, also called the centistoke (cSt). A grade such as 10W-40 is set by the SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) J300 limits, not by one number. The W means winter. The 10W part fixes low-temperature limits, a maximum cold-cranking viscosity and a maximum pumping viscosity at stated sub-zero temperatures, plus a minimum kinematic viscosity at 100 °C. The 40 part fixes a kinematic-viscosity band at 100 °C, 12.5 to below 16.3 mm²/s, and a minimum high-temperature high-shear viscosity at 150 °C. A multigrade oil meets both sets of limits. Its viscosity index (VI) is a dimensionless number showing how flat the viscosity-temperature curve is: a high VI means little thinning when hot. Pour point is the lowest temperature at which the oil still moves, in °C. Cloud point is the temperature at which wax first makes the oil cloudy, in °C. Flash point is the lowest temperature at which vapour above the oil ignites under test, in °C. Fire point is the higher temperature at which the vapour keeps burning for about five seconds, usually 10–20 °C above flash point.

Chemical properties and ageing

Carbon residue is the mass percent of coke left when oil is heated without air; it warns about deposits in hot zones. Acidity is measured as total acid number (TAN) in mg KOH per gram of oil. The base reserve is total base number (TBN), also in mg KOH/g. TAN rises as oil oxidises; TBN falls as it neutralises acids. Oxidation stability is the time or temperature needed to reach a chosen TAN, sludge or viscosity rise; it has no single unit. Oiliness is the ability to lower friction in boundary lubrication, where surfaces touch through a very thin film; it is judged on friction rigs, not by a simple number. Emulsification is the tendency to hold water; demulsibility is the reverse, stated as time to separate or percent water separated. Foaming is air held in the oil; it is reported as foam volume in mL and collapse time in seconds. Foam reduces oil flow and can cause cavitation in the pump.

Additives: small mass, large effect

Additives are 1–20 percent by mass of the finished oil. A viscosity index improver is a polymer that coils when cold and expands when hot, so it raises hot viscosity more than cold viscosity; this is how a 10W base oil becomes a 10W-40. Antioxidants break the chain reaction that forms acids and sludge. Detergents keep hot metal clean and neutralise acids. Dispersants keep soot and sludge suspended so they drain out instead of sticking. Anti-wear agents form a film on loaded surfaces; extreme-pressure (EP) agents react under high pressure to stop seizure. Corrosion and rust inhibitors coat metal with a thin film. Pour point depressants stop wax crystals from joining into a gel. Antifoam agents, often silicone, break bubbles. The base oil still carries the load. Most additives change its chemistry and its surface behaviour; the VI improver and the pour point depressant are the two that change how it flows.

Oil consumption and its fraction of fuel

Oil leaves an engine by four main routes. Ring blow-by pushes oil mist past the rings. Worn valve guides pull oil into the inlet. Turbo seals and crankcase ventilation can carry oil vapour. Volatility boils light oil fractions at high temperature. Consumption is best stated as a fraction of fuel used: f = Voil ÷ Vfuel, where Voil is the oil added in litres and Vfuel is the fuel burnt in litres. If distance D is in km and fuel economy FE is in km per litre, Vfuel = D ÷ FE. A good road engine stays below 1 percent; worn or racing engines can reach about 2 percent.

f = Voil ÷ Vfuel = Voil ÷ (D ÷ FE)

f is the oil consumption fraction, dimensionless; Voil is the oil added in litres; Vfuel is the fuel burnt in litres; D is distance in km; FE is fuel economy in km per litre.

  • Pour point is not the lowest safe operating temperature. Oil can still pour but be far too thick to pump or protect bearings.

4.5 Crankcase ventilation

No piston ring seals perfectly. On every compression and power stroke a little gas slips past the rings into the crankcase; this gas is called blow-by. Left there, it pressurises the crankcase, pushes oil out of the seals, and fills the oil with water, acid and fuel. Crankcase ventilation is the system that sweeps blow-by out of the crankcase. Old engines let it out to the air; every modern petrol car sends it back into the intake through a positive crankcase ventilation (PCV) valve so that it is burnt in the cylinders.

You need: rings and the sump from Lubrication systems and engine friction, and oil ageing (TAN, sludge) from Lubricants: properties and additives.

Blow-by: what gets into the crankcase

Blow-by leaks through the ring end gaps and past the ring faces when cylinder pressure is high. In a petrol engine it is partly unburnt mixture, pushed down during compression, and partly burnt gas, pushed down during combustion. It carries carbon dioxide, water vapour, carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, petrol vapour, soot and a fine oil mist. Worn rings or a scored liner make much more of it, so blow-by flow is a standard measure of engine wear.

An unventilated crankcase suffers four ways. Pressure builds and pushes oil out past the seals and gaskets. Water vapour condenses on cold walls and, with oil and soot, forms a creamy sludge that blocks the pick-up strainer. Nitrogen and sulphur oxides dissolve in that water as acids that corrode bearings, so the oil TAN climbs. Petrol vapour dissolves in the oil and thins it. A steady flow of air through the crankcase cures all four.

Blow-by fraction = V̇bb ÷ V̇air × 100 percent

V̇bb is the blow-by flow measured at the breather, in L/min; V̇air is the air flow into the engine, in L/min. For a four-stroke, V̇air = Vd × (N/2) × ηv, with Vd the swept volume in litres, N the speed in rev/min and ηv the volumetric efficiency.

Open breathers and the closed PCV system

Early engines used an open breather: a road draft tube ran from the crankcase to just below the car, and air rushing past at road speed sucked the gas out, with fresh air entering through a breather cap. It worked only when the car moved, and it emptied fuel vapour and oil mist into the air. Emission rules ended it on petrol cars from the early 1960s, beginning in California.

Positive crankcase ventilation closes the loop. A hose brings filtered fresh air from the clean side of the air cleaner into the rocker cover. The air sweeps down through the crankcase, mixes with the blow-by, and the mixture is drawn by manifold vacuum through the PCV valve into the intake manifold, below the throttle. The cylinders then burn it. An oil separator, a baffle box or a small cyclone in the rocker cover, throws the oil droplets out of the gas and drains them back to the sump before the gas reaches the valve.

Some syllabi separate a semi-closed system, whose filler cap still breathes to the air, from a fully closed one. In the fully closed system, blow-by beyond what the valve can pass flows back up the fresh-air hose into the air cleaner and is still burnt.

The PCV valve: flow set by manifold vacuum

The PCV valve is a tapered plunger in a small housing, held by a light spring. Manifold vacuum pulls the plunger towards the manifold end; the spring pushes it back. The gap left around the taper sets the flow, so the valve meters blow-by to match the engine condition.

A valve stuck shut or clogged with sludge stops the sweep, and the crankcase pressure symptoms return: oil leaks, oil in the air cleaner and sludge under the filler cap.

Turbocharged and diesel engines

A turbocharged petrol engine has vacuum in the manifold only at part load; under boost the manifold is above atmospheric, and gas would be forced backwards into the crankcase. So it has two paths, each with a check valve. At part load blow-by goes through the PCV valve into the intake manifold. Under boost that path shuts and blow-by is drawn into the compressor inlet, where the pressure is a little below atmospheric. Oil carried over this way coats the compressor and intercooler, so separation matters more on turbo engines.

A diesel has no throttle, so there is little manifold vacuum to drive a PCV valve, and its blow-by carries much more soot. Diesels use closed crankcase ventilation (CCV): blow-by passes through a coalescing filter that catches oil and soot, and a pressure-regulating valve holds the crankcase slightly below atmospheric while the cleaned gas is drawn into the compressor inlet.

Worked example

Question. A 1.5 litre four-stroke petrol engine runs at 3000 rev/min with a volumetric efficiency of 0.85. A blow-by meter on the breather reads 25 L/min. Taking the blow-by density as 1.1 kg/m³ and its unburnt fuel content as 3 percent by mass, find (a) the air flow into the engine in L/min, (b) the blow-by as a percentage of that air, (c) the blow-by mass per hour, and (d) the fuel vapour an open breather would release in a two-hour drive at this condition.

  1. Intake strokes per minute for a four-stroke: N/2 = 3000/2 = 1500.

  3. (b) Blow-by fraction = 25 ÷ 1912.5 × 100 = 1.31 percent.
  4. (c) 25 L/min × 60 min/h = 1500 L/h = 1.5 m³/h. Mass = 1.5 m³/h × 1.1 kg/m³ = 1.65 kg/h.
  5. (d) Gas in two hours = 1.65 × 2 = 3.3 kg. Fuel vapour = 0.03 × 3.3 kg = 0.099 kg, about 99 g, released to the air by an open breather and burnt in the cylinders by a PCV system.

Answer. (a) 1912.5 L/min; (b) 1.31 percent; (c) 1.65 kg/h; (d) about 99 g of fuel vapour in two hours.

4.6 Supercharging: objectives, types and effects

Supercharging forces more air mass into the same swept volume. More trapped air allows more fuel, so a smaller engine can give the power of a larger one. The blower is driven from the crankshaft, so it costs engine power; the useful figure is the net gain. Superchargers appear in performance petrol cars, aircraft at altitude, and some industrial diesels. This chapter covers the objectives, the main mechanical types, the density ratio, and the effects on knock, thermal load and exhaust. Exhaust-driven boosting follows in Turbocharging methods and turbo lag.

You need: swept volume and volumetric efficiency from engine terminology, and knock from abnormal combustion in SI engines; charge cooling is in Variable geometry turbochargers and intercooling.

Objectives and the density ratio

A naturally aspirated engine fills the cylinder with air near ambient pressure, about 1.0 bar. Its torque is limited by the air mass trapped each cycle. A supercharger raises the intake pressure above ambient; the ratio p₂/p₁ is the pressure ratio, using absolute pressures. The mass trapped also depends on temperature: hot air is less dense. For a perfect gas, density ratio is pressure ratio times the inverse temperature ratio. A boost to 1.6 bar sounds like 60 percent more air. But compression heats the air: even a perfect (isentropic) compressor takes 300 K air to T₂ = T₁ (p₂/p₁)(γ−1)/γ = 300 × 1.60.286 = 343 K, and a real one with isentropic efficiency ηc = 0.70 reaches T₂ = T₁ + (343 − 300)/ηc = 362 K. The density ratio is then only (1.6/1.0) × (300/362) = 1.33, about 33 percent more air.

ρ₂/ρ₁ = (p₂/p₁)(T₁/T₂)

ρ is density in kg/m³; p is absolute pressure; T is absolute temperature in K; subscript 1 is ambient before the blower, subscript 2 is after the blower.

  • More power for the same swept volume and better power-to-weight ratio.
  • Altitude compensation: a blower restores sea-level air density when ambient pressure falls.
  • Sometimes lower specific fuel consumption, because friction and engine weight are spread over more kW.

Mechanical supercharger types

All mechanical superchargers are driven by a belt or gears from the crankshaft. The roots blower has two rotors, each with two or three lobes, turning in opposite directions inside a close-fitting casing. Each lobe traps a pocket of air at inlet pressure and carries it round the outside to the delivery side. There is no compression inside the blower: the pressure rises only when the pocket opens to the higher-pressure manifold and air flows back into it, which makes the roots blower hotter and less efficient at high pressure ratio than a compressing machine. It is a positive-displacement machine: flow per revolution is nearly constant, so the air delivered rises with engine speed. The sliding-vane blower uses vanes in a rotor to trap and move air; it is simple but the vanes wear. The centrifugal blower uses a fast impeller to throw air outward; pressure rises roughly with tip speed squared. It is efficient at high speed but gives little boost at low speed.

Positive-displacement blowers give strong low-speed response. Centrifugal blowers suit high-speed power. The figure follows the worked example through a belt-driven roots blower: the pressure rises 1.6 times, but the temperature rises too, so the density rises only 1.33 times.

Drive power and the net gain

The blower takes power from the crank. The honest output is net brake power: Pnet = Pgross − Pdrive, where Pgross is the power from the extra trapped air and Pdrive is the compressor work plus belt and gear losses. Pdrive often reaches 10 to 20 percent of gross power at high boost. The drive power is the compressor work plus the drive losses: Pdrive = ṁa cp (T₂ − T₁) ÷ ηdrive, where ṁa is the air mass flow in kg/s, cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K for air and ηdrive the belt and gear efficiency. Drive power grows with mass flow and with pressure ratio, and so does charge heating. That sets the limits of supercharging: the net gain is always smaller than the density ratio suggests, and knock and thermal load grow with boost.

Effects on the engine

  • Peak cylinder pressure and temperature rise, so pistons, valves, head gasket and bearings face higher thermal and mechanical load. Cooling and lubrication must be upgraded.
  • In SI engines, boost raises end-gas pressure and temperature, so knock tendency rises. Common fixes are a lower compression ratio, higher-octane fuel, retarded spark and charge cooling; see Variable geometry turbochargers and intercooling.
  • In CI engines, more air mass reduces smoke at a given fuel rate, but higher temperature can raise NOx; injection timing may need adjustment.
  • Exhaust mass flow rises with boost. Exhaust temperature does not always rise: in a CI engine the extra air usually lowers it at the same fuel rate, while in an SI engine it depends on the mixture and timing. The exhaust manifold must withstand the greater heat flow, and back pressure must stay low enough for good scavenging.
  • Modifications for supercharging include lower compression ratio, stronger pistons and gasket, larger cooling and oil capacity, fuel enrichment and intercooling. A blower is not a simple bolt-on for a standard engine.
  • Emissions change: more power per litre, but higher NOx is possible; after-treatment and calibration must match the boost.

Worked example

Question. A 1.5 litre petrol engine gives 75 kW naturally aspirated with intake air at 1.0 bar and 300 K. A belt-driven supercharger with isentropic efficiency 0.70 raises the manifold to 1.6 bar absolute; take γ = 1.4 for air. At this speed 0.104 kg/s of air passes through the blower, cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K, and the belt drive is 90 percent efficient. Find the delivery temperature, the density ratio, the gross power if power follows trapped air mass, the blower drive power, the net power and the percentage gain.

  1. Isentropic delivery temperature: (γ − 1)/γ = 0.4/1.4 = 0.286, so T₂ₛ = 300 × 1.60.286 = 300 × 1.1437 = 343.1 K. No real blower can deliver cooler air than this.
  2. Actual delivery temperature: T₂ = T₁ + (T₂ₛ − T₁)/ηc = 300 + 43.1/0.70 = 300 + 61.6 = 361.6 K.
  3. Density ratio = (p₂/p₁)(T₁/T₂) = 1.6 × 300/361.6 = 1.327.
  4. Gross power = 75 kW × 1.327 = 99.6 kW.
  5. Compressor work rate = ṁa cp (T₂ − T₁) = 0.104 × 1.005 × 61.6 = 6.44 kW. Drive power from the crank = 6.44 ÷ 0.90 = 7.15 kW.
  6. Net power = 99.6 − 7.15 = 92.4 kW. Gain = 92.4 − 75 = 17.4 kW, that is 17.4/75 × 100 = 23.2 percent.

Answer. T₂ = 361.6 K; density ratio 1.33; gross 99.6 kW; drive 7.15 kW; net 92.4 kW, a gain of about 23 percent, far less than the 60 percent the pressure ratio suggests.

Exam traps

  • Using gauge pressure in the pressure ratio. p₂ and p₁ must be absolute; a gauge reading of 0.6 bar is 1.6 bar absolute if ambient is 1.0 bar.
  • Forgetting charge heating. 1.6 bar is not 60 percent extra mass; with a 0.70 efficient blower the air leaves at 362 K and the density ratio is 1.33. A delivery temperature below the isentropic value (343 K here) is impossible.
  • Quoting gross power. The belt-driven blower takes crank power, so the exam answer is net power.
  • Assuming supercharging always lowers specific fuel consumption. It can, but at high boost the drive loss, fuel enrichment and knock control can worsen it.
  • Mixing knock effects. Boost raises knock tendency in SI engines, so compression ratio is lowered or fuel octane raised; it is not increased.

4.7 Turbocharging methods and turbo lag

A turbocharger is a turbine driven by exhaust gas that spins a compressor and pushes more air into the engine. The exhaust energy is otherwise wasted. The catch is time: the rotor has mass and takes a moment to speed up, and that delay is turbo lag. Turbochargers are common on small petrol and diesel cars, tractors and trucks, where more air means more fuel burnt and more power from a smaller engine.

You need: Supercharging for pressure ratio, delivery temperature and density ratio.

Layout and the two turbocharging methods

A turbocharger has a turbine wheel and a compressor wheel on one shaft, held in an oil-fed bearing housing. Exhaust gas drives the turbine wheel; the shaft spins the compressor wheel, which draws air through the air filter and pushes it into the intake manifold at higher pressure and density. A wastegate valve lets some exhaust bypass the turbine, and its actuator sets the boost.

The energy comes from exhaust enthalpy, which the turbine extracts as the gas cools and expands; a turbocharger is an exhaust-driven supercharger.

Two manifold methods are common. In constant-pressure turbocharging, all cylinders feed one exhaust manifold. The pulses mix and the turbine sees an almost steady pressure. This suits steady high-load running, but at low load response is slow. In pulse turbocharging, narrow pipes keep the exhaust pulses separate and aim them at the turbine. Boost builds earlier, so most road vehicles use pulse turbocharging.

A single turbo must work from low to rated speed, so its size is a compromise. Twin turbos can be parallel: two small, quick-spooling turbos, each fed by half the cylinders. Twin turbos can also be in series: a small turbo makes boost at low speed, a large turbo takes over at high speed, and valves switch the flow. A crank-driven blower, covered in Supercharging, gives boost as soon as the engine turns, but it takes power from the crankshaft.

Boost threshold is not turbo lag

Boost threshold is the lowest engine speed, at a given load, where the exhaust flow can spin the turbine fast enough for the compressor to exceed atmospheric pressure. Below the threshold the turbo is a restriction, not a boost source. A large turbine housing raises the threshold; a small one lowers it. The threshold is a speed.

Turbo lag is a time. Open the throttle at a speed above the threshold and the boost does not appear at once. The exhaust gas must first accelerate the turbine wheel, shaft and compressor wheel; the intake pipes and intercooler must then fill with denser air. A typical small petrol turbo takes about one to two seconds to reach full boost after a throttle step. The rotor stores kinetic energy ½Jω², where J is its moment of inertia in kg·m² and ω its speed in rad/s, and the turbine can only supply that energy at the rate its surplus power allows. The figure plots the second worked example: the rotor climbs from 30 000 to 150 000 rev/min in about 1.3 s.

Lag is felt as a pause followed by a surge, worst after a gear change or a spell of light-load cruising. The cure is not more boost; it is less rotating mass, a smaller turbine housing, or split exhaust pulses.

Matching, wastegate and lag reduction

Matching a turbocharger means choosing a compressor and turbine that work well together over the engine range. On the compressor side, the operating point at cruise should sit inside the high-efficiency island, typically 70 to 75 percent, away from surge at low flow and choke at high flow. On the turbine side, the shaft speed must stay below its limit at rated power. A wastegate protects the turbine: when boost reaches the set value, the actuator opens the wastegate and bypasses exhaust around the turbine. Less exhaust through the turbine means less turbine power, so boost stops rising.

Lag-reduction hardware attacks inertia and exhaust volume. A small turbine housing raises gas velocity at low flow, at the cost of exhaust backpressure at high flow. A twin-scroll turbine housing keeps the pulses from cylinders that would otherwise interfere; Lightweight turbine and compressor wheels, and ball bearings instead of journal bearings, cut the rotating mass and friction. Variable geometry, covered in Variable geometry turbochargers and intercooling, changes the turbine nozzle area with speed. The term anti-lag system (ALS) means something else: a rally-car trick that retards the spark and burns fuel in the exhaust manifold to keep the turbine spinning when the driver lifts off. Road cars do not use it.

Bearings, oil and heat soak

The bearing housing gets pressurised oil from the engine; the film supports the shaft and carries heat away from the hot turbine end. After a hard run the turbine housing is very hot. If the engine stops at once, oil flow stops and the oil left in the bearing housing can cook into carbon, damaging the bearing and seal; this is heat soak. Idling for a minute or two lets oil carry the heat away. Water-cooled bearing housings reduce the risk.

Worked example 2

Question. A small turbo rotor (turbine wheel, shaft and compressor wheel) has a moment of inertia J = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ kg·m². At a throttle step it must spin up from 30 000 to 150 000 rev/min, and the turbine has, on average, 1.8 kW more power than the compressor and bearings absorb. Estimate the lag. How much shorter is it with a lighter rotor of 30 percent less inertia?

Exam traps

  • Lag and boost threshold get mixed up. Threshold is a speed below which no boost is possible; lag is a time delay at a speed where boost is possible.
  • The wastegate bypasses exhaust around the turbine. It does not dump intake air, and it does not open the compressor side.
  • A large turbine housing lowers exhaust backpressure at high power but raises the boost threshold. A small housing spools quickly but can choke the top end.
  • Boost is often given in bar gauge while pressure ratio is absolute. At sea level, 1.0 bar gauge means pressure ratio about 2.0, not 1.0.

4.8 Variable geometry turbochargers and intercooling

A turbocharger that cannot change its turbine area is a compromise: small enough for low-speed response, or large enough for high-speed power, never both. A variable geometry turbocharger moves vanes or a nozzle ring to change the area while running. An intercooler then removes the heat that compression added, so the charge is denser at the same boost. Together they give strong low-speed torque, controlled high-speed boost and lower NOx. They appear in diesel cars, trucks, tractors and some petrol engines.

You need: Turbocharging methods and turbo lag for pressure ratio, compressor heating and the turbine side; Supercharging for why boost adds air mass.

A nozzle that changes size

A fixed turbine has one inlet area. At 1200 rpm the exhaust flow is small, gas speed through the nozzle is low, and the turbine extracts little work. The compressor therefore makes little boost. At 5000 rpm the same area is too small. Exhaust backs up in the cylinder, pumping loss rises, and boost may exceed the limit. A variable geometry turbocharger (VGT) changes the turbine inlet area while the engine runs.

  • Moving vanes: small vanes pivot at the nozzle throat. Closed vanes give a narrow throat and high gas speed at low flow.
  • Sliding nozzle ring: an axial ring moves to cover or open the throat. It gives the same area change with fewer moving parts.
  • Actuator: a vacuum capsule or electric motor moves the vanes or ring. The ECU uses engine speed, fuel quantity, boost pressure and vane position to set the area.
  • Low speed: small area, high exhaust velocity, fast spool. High speed: large area, low back pressure, boost held at the map limit.

The result is useful boost at 1200 rpm that a fixed turbine sized for rated power cannot reach. The figure shows the vanes nearly closed at low flow and open at high flow. The actuator is the weak point in cost and reliability: it lives near hot exhaust, so position feedback and self-calibration matter.

A wastegate is a valve that bypasses exhaust around the turbine to cap boost. It cannot make the turbine smaller at low speed, so it does not fix lag. VGT controls boost by area, so it often needs no wastegate. Closing the vanes also raises the exhaust manifold pressure above the intake manifold pressure, and that pressure difference is what pushes exhaust gas through the EGR (exhaust gas recirculation) valve into the intake. Closed further, the vanes create enough back pressure to act as an exhaust brake on a downhill run.

Intercooling: density after the compressor

Compression heats the charge. For a perfect gas, density ρ = p/(R T). The compressor raises p by the pressure ratio rp, but it also raises T, so density rises by less than rp. At pressure ratio 1.6 and compressor efficiency 0.70, the air leaves at about 362 K instead of 300 K, so the density ratio is 1.33, not 1.60, as in the supercharging worked example. An intercooler is a heat exchanger between the compressor and the intake manifold. It removes heat at nearly constant pressure.

ε = (T₂ − T₃) / (T₂ − Tc,in)

ε is intercooler effectiveness (0 to 1); T2 is compressor outlet temperature in K; T3 is intercooler outlet temperature in K; Tc,in is cooling air or water inlet temperature in K. A value near 1 means the outlet is close to the coolant inlet temperature.

Air-to-air intercooler uses ram air through a front-mounted core. It is simple and needs no extra pump, but long pipes add volume and throttle lag. Air-to-water intercooler uses a coolant loop and a small radiator. It is compact and holds outlet temperature steady in traffic, but adds mass and a second cooling circuit. In a spark ignition engine the cooler sits before the throttle so only air passes through it. Placing it after the throttle is possible, but the extra volume hurts throttle response.

Intercooling lowers the charge temperature after the compressor. In an SI engine this reduces knock, so the compression ratio or boost can be raised. Volumetric efficiency improves because cold air is denser. Lower peak combustion temperature cuts NOx. Fuel consumption falls at the same power because the ECU can use more air and less fuel enrichment. In a diesel engine the gain is density and NOx control; very cold air can raise ignition delay and particulates, so the cooler is not made larger than needed.

Question. A turbocharged engine takes air at 300 K and 1.0 bar. The compressor pressure ratio is 1.6 and its isentropic efficiency is 0.70; take γ = 1.4. Exam traps

  • Do not say the intercooler increases boost pressure. It increases density at the same pressure; the pressure ratio is set by the compressor.
  • VGT vanes close at low engine speed to speed the gas, and open at high speed. Reversing this gives lag and overboost.
  • A wastegate and a VGT are not the same. A wastegate bypasses exhaust; a VGT changes nozzle area.
  • Compressor efficiency is in the denominator of the temperature rise term. A lower efficiency gives a hotter outlet, not a cooler one.
  • Intercooler effectiveness uses the inlet temperature of the cooling medium (ambient air for an air-to-air core, the cooler loop water for an air-to-water core), not the engine coolant temperature.

4.9 Scavenging in two-stroke engines

A two-stroke engine has no separate exhaust stroke. The piston must clear burnt gas and fill the cylinder while the ports are open near bottom dead centre. Fresh charge is pushed in by the crankcase pump in small engines or by a blower in large ones. The port layout decides how much charge stays and how much escapes with the exhaust. This chapter defines delivery ratio, trapping efficiency and scavenging efficiency, then compares cross, loop and uniflow scavenging. These ideas appear in scooters, chainsaws, outboard motors and large marine diesels.

You need: two-stroke engines and port timing diagrams, and the reason a blower raises intake density from Supercharging.

Why two-stroke engines need scavenging

In a four-stroke engine a separate exhaust stroke removes burnt gas. A two-stroke engine has only a short overlap near bottom dead centre. Scavenging is the process of replacing burnt gas with fresh charge during that overlap. The fresh charge is air in a CI engine and air-fuel mixture in an SI engine.

Burnt gas starts to leave when the exhaust port opens because cylinder pressure is above atmospheric. The rest must be pushed out by fresh charge at a slightly higher pressure. Small engines use the crankcase as a pump: the piston downstroke compresses charge in the crankcase, and a reed valve or piston-port skirt controls entry. Larger engines use a separate blower, often a Roots blower.

D = Vdel / Vs

D is delivery ratio; Vdel is fresh charge delivered per cycle, in cm³; Vs is swept volume, in cm³.

ηt = Vtr / Vdel

ηt is trapping efficiency; Vtr is fresh charge trapped in the cylinder, in cm³.

ηs = Vtr / Vec

ηs is scavenging efficiency; Vec is the total cylinder volume at exhaust closure, in cm³.

Delivery ratio measures how much charge the blower supplies relative to the swept volume. Trapping efficiency measures how much of that delivered charge stays. Scavenging efficiency measures how much of the cylinder contents at exhaust closure is fresh charge. The three are linked by ηs = ηt × D × Vs ÷ Vec. When Vec is close to Vs, ηs ≈ ηt × D. The product ηt × D is also called the charging efficiency: trapped fresh charge per swept volume. Strictly, all of these are mass ratios. Volumes can stand in for masses only when every volume is referred to the same pressure and temperature, usually the outside air, and the worked example does exactly that.

Cross flow and loop scavenging

Cross scavenging uses transfer and exhaust ports on opposite sides. The piston crown carries a raised deflector beside the transfer port that turns the incoming charge upward, so it rises along the transfer side, crosses under the head and comes down the exhaust side, pushing burnt gas ahead of it. The layout is simple and cheap. But part of the charge still takes the short path straight across: it short-circuits to the exhaust port before it mixes with burnt gas, and the heavy deflector crown runs hot. Short-circuiting wastes air and, in an SI engine, fuel and oil.

Loop scavenging, often called Schnuerle scavenging, angles two transfer ports toward the back wall. The two streams meet, rise along the rear wall, turn across the cylinder head, and sweep down toward the exhaust port. The loop gives better mixing and trapping than cross flow. It uses less air for the same output and has lower short-circuiting. Many small motorcycle and scooter engines use loop scavenging with piston-controlled ports.

A reed valve at the crankcase inlet opens when crankcase pressure falls below atmospheric and closes when pressure rises. A piston-port inlet uses the piston skirt to uncover the inlet. Both act as one-way valves for crankcase pumping.

Uniflow scavenging and comparison

Uniflow scavenging sends fresh charge in one direction. Inlet ports at one end of the cylinder are uncovered by the piston. Exhaust leaves at the other end through a poppet valve in the head or through ports uncovered by an opposed piston. The flow does not reverse or cross itself. Mixing with burnt gas is low and short-circuiting is small. Uniflow gives the best scavenging efficiency and the highest power per litre of the three layouts.

The hardware is the price. A uniflow engine needs a blower to supply charge above exhaust pressure, plus valve gear or a second crankshaft for opposed pistons. Large marine two-stroke diesels use uniflow with an exhaust valve and a blower. Small cheap engines stay with loop or cross scavenging because the extra hardware costs more than the fuel saved.

Worked example

Question. A two-stroke cylinder has a swept volume of 300 cm³. The blower delivers 240 cm³ of fresh charge per cycle measured at outside conditions. Trapping efficiency is 0.80. At exhaust port closure the cylinder contents, referred to the same outside conditions, are 300 cm³. Find the trapped fresh charge, the scavenging efficiency and the delivery ratio.

  1. Given Vs = 300 cm³, Vdel = 240 cm³, ηt = 0.80, and Vec = 300 cm³.
  2. Trapped fresh charge Vtr = ηt × Vdel = 0.80 × 240 cm³ = 192 cm³.
  3. Scavenging efficiency ηs = Vtr / Vec = 192 cm³ / 300 cm³ = 0.64.
  4. Delivery ratio D = Vdel / Vs = 240 cm³ / 300 cm³ = 0.80.
  5. Check: ηt × D = 0.80 × 0.80 = 0.64, which equals ηs because Vec = Vs.

Answer. The trapped fresh charge is 192 cm³, the scavenging efficiency is 0.64 (64 percent), and the delivery ratio is 0.80.

Worked example 2

Question. Two simple models bracket real scavenging. Perfect displacement: fresh charge pushes burnt gas out like a piston, with no mixing, so ηs = D until D reaches 1. Perfect mixing: fresh charge mixes at once with the whole cylinder contents, giving ηs = 1 − e−D.   2. Perfect mixing: ηs = 1 − e−0.80 = 1 − 0.449 = 0.551.
  3. The cylinder achieved ηs = 0.64, between the two: better than full mixing, worse than ideal displacement.

Exam traps

  • Delivery ratio, trapping efficiency and scavenging efficiency are three different numbers. Delivered volume is not trapped volume, because part of the fresh charge leaves with the exhaust.
  • Scavenging efficiency uses the cylinder contents at exhaust closure, not the swept volume, unless the question says the two volumes are equal.
  • Cross scavenging is not loop scavenging. Cross flow has opposite ports and a straight path, so short-circuiting is high; loop flow uses angled transfer streams that meet and turn around the cylinder wall.
  • Uniflow does not remove the need for a pressure difference. It still needs a blower or pump to drive fresh charge from the inlet end to the exhaust end.
  • In a crankcase-scavenged SI engine, short-circuited charge carries fuel and lubricating oil, so the lost charge is not just lost air.

Takeaways

  1. Only about a quarter to a third of the fuel energy leaves an engine as brake power; the rest must be taken away as heat by coolant, oil and exhaust.
  2. Cooling must be controlled, not maximised: too little causes knock, pre-ignition and oil breakdown, while too much raises friction, wear and deposits and wastes fuel.
  3. A thermosyphon loop drives only tens of pascals, so road engines use a pump; a pressure cap adds boiling margin (100 kPa gauge gives about 120 °C) but removes no heat itself.
  4. Friction power changes little with load at a given speed, so mechanical efficiency is highest at full load and zero at idle; the right oil at the right pressure returns real brake power.
  5. A lubricant is selected by its viscosity grade, viscosity index and additive package; the SAE 40 band is 12.5 to below 16.3 mm²/s at 100 °C.
  6. Blow-by must be swept out of the crankcase and burnt: the PCV valve passes little at idle and most at full load.
  7. Boost adds less air than its pressure ratio suggests, because compression heats the charge: 1.6 bar with a 0.70 efficient compressor gives only 1.33 times the density.
  8. A turbocharger runs on exhaust energy that would otherwise be wasted, but its rotor must store ½Jω² before boost arrives; that delay is turbo lag.
  9. Variable geometry fixes low-speed boost and the intercooler recovers the density lost to compressor heating, about 15 percent at 1.6 bar.
  10. In a two-stroke the port layout decides how much fresh charge stays in the cylinder; uniflow and loop scavenging lose far less charge than simple cross flow.

Understand Draw the heat balance of a petrol engine and explain why cooling is necessary. State what happens if the engine is overcooled. 10 marks

Points to cover. Typical split: 25–30% brake power, about 30% to coolant, about 35% out with exhaust, rest friction and radiation; Overheating effects: knock and pre-ignition, loss of volumetric efficiency, oil thinning and oxidation, thermal stress and warping of the head; Overcooling effects: higher friction and wear, poor fuel vaporisation, sludge and acid formation, lower thermal efficiency; Conclusion: cooling is a control problem, the jacket temperature must be held within a band.

Model answer.

  1. Define the heat balance: an account, per second, of where the fuel energy goes. Fuel power = ṁf × CV = brake power + heat to coolant + heat to exhaust + friction and radiation.
  2. Take a petrol engine burning 5 kg/h of fuel with CV = 44 000 kJ/kg. Fuel power = 5 × 44 000 ÷ 3600 = 61.1 kW. A typical split for a petrol engine:
    SharePercentkW
    Brake power2817.1
    Heat to coolant3018.3
    Heat to exhaust3420.8
    Friction, radiation, unaccounted84.9
    Total10061.1
  3. Draw the balance as a bar or a Sankey strip: one wide arrow of 61.1 kW splitting into the four streams above, the coolant stream labelled 18.3 kW. Figure 4.1.1 is the same picture for a diesel genset.
  4. Why cooling is necessary (overheating): the gas passes 2000 °C while the cylinder wall must stay near 150 to 200 °C. Without cooling, (a) the oil film on the liner oxidises and cokes above about 150 °C at the rings, so rings stick and the liner scores; (b) aluminium pistons and heads lose strength, and the exhaust valve, already near 700 °C, burns; (c) hot spots cause pre-ignition and the hot charge raises knock in SI engines; (d) hot inlet walls heat the charge and lower volumetric efficiency; (e) uneven temperatures warp the head and blow the gasket.
  5. Why it must not be overdone (overcooling): (a) petrol vaporises poorly on cold walls, so mixture and combustion worsen; (b) cold, thick oil raises friction and slows oil delivery, so wear rises; (c) water from combustion condenses in the crankcase with acids and forms sludge; (d) more heat goes to the coolant, so thermal efficiency and fuel economy fall; (e) emissions of unburnt HC rise.
  6. The control that holds the band: the thermostat keeps coolant away from the radiator until about 80 to 90 °C, and the fan switches on only when needed.
  7. Check: 17.1 + 18.3 + 20.8 + 4.9 = 61.1 kW, the whole fuel power. Conclusion: cooling removes roughly a third of the fuel energy and must hold the metal in a temperature band, neither hot nor cold.
Understand Compare air cooling and liquid cooling of IC engines with sketches, giving advantages, disadvantages and typical applications. 12 marks

Points to cover. Air cooling: fins on barrel and head, direct convection, no radiator or pump; needs a fan and ducting on multicylinder engines; local hot spots; Liquid cooling: water jacket, radiator, pump, thermostat, fan; uniform temperature, quieter, compact bore spacing; Comparison table on weight, cost, maintenance, cooling uniformity, warm-up, freezing risk; Applications: air for scooters, motorcycles, small gensets and aircraft; liquid for cars, trucks, tractors and large stationary engines.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose of both: carry heat from the cylinder walls and head to the outside air. They differ in how the heat reaches the air.
  2. Sketches: an air-cooled barrel with fins, and a liquid-cooled block with jacket, pump, thermostat and radiator (the full liquid loop is Figure 4.1.1).
  3. Air cooling: fins cast on the barrel and head add surface; air flows between them by road speed or a fan, guided by cowls and baffles; heat leaves by convection, Q = h A ΔT. Because h for air is small, a lot of area is needed: to reject 3.0 kW at h = 50 W/m²·K with fins at 160 °C in 35 °C air, A = 3000 ÷ (50 × 125) = 0.48 m², six times the 0.08 m² of the bare barrel and head.
  4. Liquid cooling: coolant in a jacket picks up heat from the liner, valve seats and exhaust port; a belt-driven centrifugal pump sends it through the thermostat to the radiator; the fan pulls air through the radiator. Heat flow Q = ṁ c ΔT: a genset putting 28 kW into water that rises 6 K needs 28.0 ÷ (4.18 × 6) = 1.12 kg/s.
  5. Comparison:
    PointAir coolingLiquid cooling
    Heat removallimited, hot spots at the headhigh and even round all cylinders
    Partsfins, cowls, fanjacket, pump, radiator, thermostat, fan, hoses
    Weight and costlowhigher
    Warm-upfastslower until the thermostat opens
    Freezing, leaks, boilingnoneneeds antifreeze, sealed pressure system

    Maintenancekeep fins cleancoolant, hoses, pump seal
  6. Advantages of air cooling: simple, light, cheap, no coolant to freeze or leak, quick warm-up. Disadvantages: lower power per litre, uneven temperatures, noise, poor cooling in slow traffic or a closed room (in a 55 °C shed the same fins settle at 180 °C).
  7. Advantages of liquid cooling: uniform temperature, higher compression ratio and power per litre possible, quieter, compact cylinder spacing, cabin heater from the coolant. Disadvantages: weight, cost, pump power, leaks and corrosion, more maintenance.
  8. Applications: air for motorcycles, scooters, small pump sets and aircraft piston engines; liquid for cars, trucks, tractors, most gensets and large stationary engines.
  9. Conclusion: air cooling trades power and temperature control for simplicity; liquid cooling is chosen wherever power per litre and even temperature matter.
Understand Explain the thermosyphon, forced circulation and pressure cooling systems. Why do modern engines use pressurised forced circulation? 12 marks

Points to cover. Thermosyphon: density difference drives flow, no pump, needs tall radiator, flow depends on temperature difference, slow warm-up circulation; Forced circulation: pump output, thermostat control, bypass during warm-up, higher flow and smaller radiator; Pressure cooling: sealed cap raises boiling point, a 100 kPa gauge cap lifts water from 100 °C to about 120 °C, so a higher jacket temperature is allowed with a smaller radiator and less fan power; Why: stable flow at all speeds, smaller and lighter radiator, no boiling at high altitude or high load.

Model answer.

  1. All three are liquid cooling loops: jacket, radiator and connecting hoses. They differ in what drives the flow and at what pressure.
  2. Thermosyphon: hot coolant in the jacket is lighter than cold coolant in the radiator. The hot riser rises to the radiator top tank, the cooled water falls through the downcomer to the jacket bottom. The driving pressure is Δp = (ρc − ρh) g h. Sketch: jacket low, radiator high, as Figure 4.2.1.
  3. Size of that pressure, with water at 80 °C in the riser (971.8 kg/m³), 40 °C in the downcomer (992.2 kg/m³) and h = 0.4 m:
    Δp = (992.2 − 971.8) × 9.81 × 0.4
   = 20.4 × 9.81 × 0.4 = 80 Pa  (about 8 mm of water)
  4. Consequences of so small a head: wide short pipes, a tall radiator well above the engine, no flow until the water is hot, and flow that falls exactly when the engine is cold or the radiator is clogged. Advantages: no pump, no power, simple. Used on small stationary engines.
  5. Forced circulation: a belt-driven centrifugal pump adds tens of kPa, so flow is set by pump speed, not by temperature. A wax thermostat blocks the radiator until about 80 to 90 °C; a bypass lets the pump circulate through the jacket during warm-up; an expansion tank takes up volume changes. Flow is predictable at every speed, so the radiator is smaller.
  6. Pressure cooling: a spring-loaded cap seals the system and holds it above atmospheric pressure. Saturation temperature rises with pressure, so the coolant can run hotter without boiling. The cap also carries a vacuum valve that lets coolant return from the overflow tank as the engine cools.
  7. Boiling points from the water tables, cap at 100 kPa gauge:
    CaseAbsolute pressureBoils at
    Sea level, cap101.3 + 100 = 201.3 kPa120.4 °C
    2000 m, no cap79.5 kPa93.3 °C
    2000 m, cap79.5 + 100 = 179.5 kPa116.8 °C
  8. Why modern engines use pressurised forced circulation: (a) flow is steady at all speeds and loads; (b) the coolant can run hotter, so the larger coolant-to-air temperature difference lets a smaller, lighter radiator and less fan power reject the same heat; (c) boiling is prevented on hill climbs, in traffic and at altitude, where an open system would boil at 93 °C; (d) coolant is not lost as steam.
  9. Caution to state: never open a hot cap; the drop to atmospheric pressure flashes the coolant to steam.
  10. Check: the cap does not remove heat; it buys boiling margin, here about 20 K above a 95 °C running temperature even at 2000 m.
Understand Explain the functions of engine lubrication and describe wet sump, dry sump and mist lubrication systems with sketches. 12 marks

Points to cover. Functions: reduce friction and wear, carry away heat, remove wear debris, seal piston rings, cushion shock loads, resist corrosion; Wet sump: strainer, gear pump, relief valve, filter, main gallery, drillings to mains, big ends, camshaft; oil returns by gravity; Dry sump: scavenge pump, remote tank, pressure pump; advantages in racing, aircraft and off-road vehicles; Mist: oil mixed with fuel, no pump or filter, total loss, used in small two-strokes.

Model answer.

  1. Functions of lubrication: (1) reduce friction and wear by keeping a film between sliding parts; (2) cool the piston underside, bearings and turbo shaft; (3) seal the gap between rings and liner; (4) clean, carrying wear debris and soot to the filter; (5) cushion shock loads in bearings; (6) protect against rust and acid corrosion.
  2. Classification by how the oil reaches the surface: mist (petroil), splash, and pressure feed; pressure-feed systems are wet sump or dry sump.
  3. Wet sump, with a sketch as Figure 4.3.1: oil is stored in the sump under the crankcase. The pump (gear or rotor, driven from the crankshaft) draws it through the strainer, sends it through the full-flow filter (with a bypass valve if it clogs) to the main gallery, a drilled passage along the block. Drillings feed the main bearings, then through the crankshaft to the big ends, and to the camshaft bearings and rockers. Oil drains back to the sump by gravity. A relief valve next to the pump returns surplus oil to the sump so pressure cannot rise too high with cold, thick oil. Splash from the crank throws oil onto the liner and piston pin.
  4. Advantages of wet sump: simple, cheap, compact; used in almost all cars and trucks. Limits: oil can surge away from the pick-up in hard cornering or on steep slopes, the deep sump raises engine height, and a small sump runs hot.
  5. Dry sump, with a sketch:
  6. Dry sump working: oil drains to a shallow pan; a scavenge pump, larger than the pressure pump, lifts it to a separate tank; a pressure pump feeds the engine from the tank through the filter and gallery. Advantages: no oil surge in corners or on slopes, lower engine mounting and centre of gravity, larger and better-cooled oil volume. Disadvantages: two pumps, a tank and more piping, higher cost. Used in racing cars, aircraft and some off-road and heavy engines.
  7. Mist (petroil) lubrication: oil mixed into the petrol at 1:50 to 1:20 (2 to 5 percent of the petrol volume) in crankcase-scavenged two-strokes. The mixture passes through the crankcase, the oil mist wets the bearings and cylinder, and the oil then burns. Advantages: no pump, filter or sump, works at any angle. Disadvantages: total loss of oil, smoke, plug fouling, oil supply tied to throttle and not to need. A four-stroke cannot use it because its crankcase never carries the fuel mixture.
  8. Conclusion: pressure feed with a wet sump is the default; dry sump where oil surge or engine height matters; mist only in small two-strokes.

Apply Explain why an engine crankcase must be ventilated and describe the PCV system with a sketch. A 1.6 litre four-stroke engine runs at 2500 rev/min with volumetric efficiency 0.85 and its blow-by meter reads 20 L/min; find the blow-by as a percentage of intake air. 12 marks

Points to cover. Blow-by: what it is and what it contains; Effects of an unventilated crankcase: pressure, sludge, acid, oil dilution, pollution; Open road draft tube versus closed PCV system, with sketch; PCV valve operation at idle, part load, full load and backfire; Calculation: 1700 L/min of air, blow-by 1.18 percent; Turbo and diesel variants and common faults.

Model answer.

  1. Blow-by: gas that leaks past the piston rings into the crankcase during compression and combustion. In a petrol engine it is unburnt mixture plus burnt gas: CO₂, H₂O, CO, NOx, fuel vapour, soot and oil mist.
  2. Why ventilate: without a sweep of air, (a) crankcase pressure rises and pushes oil past seals and gaskets; (b) water vapour condenses on cold walls and forms sludge with oil and soot; (c) nitrogen and sulphur oxides form acids that corrode bearings; (d) fuel vapour dilutes and thins the oil; (e) if vented to the air, the hydrocarbons pollute.
  3. Open system (road draft tube): a pipe from the crankcase to below the car, sucked by the air stream at road speed, with a breather cap for fresh air. It does nothing at idle and vents fuel vapour and oil mist to the air; emission rules ended it on petrol cars from the early 1960s.
  4. Closed PCV system, with a sketch as Figure 4.5.1: filtered air from the clean side of the air cleaner enters the rocker cover, sweeps the crankcase, and the mixture of air and blow-by is drawn by manifold vacuum through an oil separator and the PCV valve into the intake manifold below the throttle, to be burnt. Surplus blow-by at full load flows back up the fresh-air hose to the air cleaner and is still burnt.
  5. PCV valve operation (tapered plunger, spring against manifold vacuum):
    ConditionVacuumValveFlow
    Off or backfirenone or pressureclosednone
    Idle, decelerationhighsmall gapsmall
    Part loadmoderatemidwaymedium
    Full loadlowwide openlargest
  6. Calculation: a 1.6 litre four-stroke at 2500 rev/min, ηv = 0.85, blow-by meter 20 L/min.
    V̇air = Vd × N/2 × ηv = 1.6 × 1250 × 0.85 = 1700 L/min
Blow-by fraction = 20 / 1700 × 100 = 1.18 %
  7. Turbocharged and diesel engines: under boost the manifold is above atmospheric, so a check valve shuts the PCV path and blow-by goes to the compressor inlet instead. Diesels, with no throttle vacuum, use a closed crankcase ventilation filter that removes oil and soot and sends the gas to the compressor inlet.
  8. Faults: valve stuck open or split hose, unmetered air and a lean rough idle (10 kg/h measured plus 1.5 kg/h leak gives λ = 1.15); valve blocked, crankcase pressure, oil leaks, oil in the air cleaner and sludge.
  9. Check: 1.18 percent is in the range of a healthy engine; a rising blow-by fraction at the same speed and load points to worn rings or liner.
Apply A four-stroke engine of 1.5 litre swept volume runs at 3000 rpm and gives indicated power 55 kW against brake power 40 kW. Find the friction power, the mechanical efficiency and the friction mean effective pressure. 12 marks

Points to cover. FP = IP − BP = 15 kW; Mechanical efficiency = BP/IP = 40/55 = 72.7%; Cycles per second = (3000/60)/2 = 25; fmep = FP/(Vs × cycles per second) = 15000/(0.0015 × 25) = 4 × 10⁵ Pa = 4 bar; Comment on how fmep varies with speed and oil viscosity.

Model answer.

  1. Given: Vd = 1.5 L = 1.5 × 10⁻³ m³ (whole engine), N = 3000 rev/min, IP = 55 kW, BP = 40 kW, four-stroke.
  2. Friction power:
    FP = IP − BP = 55 − 40 = 15 kW
  3. Mechanical efficiency:
    ηm = BP / IP = 40 / 55 = 0.727 = 72.7 %
  4. Cycles per second per cylinder for a four-stroke, and the swept volume handled per second:
    N/120 = 3000/120 = 25 cycles per second
Vd × N/120 = 1.5 × 10⁻³ × 25 = 0.0375 m³/s
  5. Friction mean effective pressure:
    fmep = FP / (Vd × N/120) = 15 000 / 0.0375
     = 400 000 Pa = 4.0 bar
  6. Check by the mean-effective-pressure route: imep = IP/(Vd N/120) = 55 000/0.0375 = 14.7 bar and bmep = 40 000/0.0375 = 10.7 bar; imep − bmep = 4.0 bar = fmep, and bmep/imep = 0.727 = ηm.
  7. Comment on the value: 4 bar is high for a modern car engine at 3000 rev/min (motoring tests give nearer 1.5 to 2 bar, as in the 1.6 litre example of the lubrication chapter, 1.89 bar). It suggests the data includes pumping at part throttle or a worn engine.
  8. How fmep varies: it rises with speed (bearing and ring shear grow with sliding speed, and pumping loss grows with flow), rises with oil viscosity (cold oil), and changes little with load at a fixed speed. That is why ηm rises with load: FP stays near 15 kW while IP grows.
  9. What a lower fmep would be worth: if better oil and lower ring tension brought fmep down to 2.0 bar at the same speed and indicated power,
    FP = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.0375 = 7500 W = 7.5 kW
BP = 55 − 7.5 = 47.5 kW
ηm = 47.5 / 55 = 86.4 %
  10. How FP is measured in practice (named only; the methods belong to engine testing): motoring the engine with the fuel off, Willan's line (fuel rate against BP extended to zero load), the Morse test on a multi-cylinder engine, or IP from an indicator diagram minus BP from the dynamometer.
  11. Why fmep is the better comparison: FP in kW grows with engine size and speed, while fmep is a pressure, so a 1.5 litre car engine and a 10 litre truck engine can be compared on the same scale.
  12. Answer: FP = 15 kW, ηm = 72.7 %, fmep = 4.0 bar.
Apply In a test, 30% of the fuel energy goes to the coolant. Fuel flow is 5 kg/h of calorific value 44 MJ/kg and the coolant water rises 8 °C. Find the coolant flow required in kg/s. 10 marks

  1. Given: fuel 5 kg/h, CV = 44 MJ/kg = 44 000 kJ/kg, 30 % of the fuel energy to the coolant, coolant rise ΔT = 8 °C (8 K), water with c = 4.18 kJ/kg·K.

Apply An intercooler cools the compressor delivery from 343 K to 313 K at almost constant pressure. Find the percentage rise in air density and explain the effect on engine power. 12 marks

Points to cover. At constant pressure, density ratio = T₂/T₃ = 343/313 = 1.096; About 9.6% more air mass in the same volume; With the same air–fuel ratio, about 9.6% more fuel can be burnt, so roughly the same rise in power; Also lower intake temperature reduces knock tendency, allowing a higher compression ratio or more boost; Note the pressure drop across the intercooler and its effectiveness.

Model answer.

  1. Given: compressor delivery T₂ = 343 K, intercooler outlet T₃ = 313 K, pressure nearly constant through the cooler.
  2. At constant pressure ρ ∝ 1/T, so:
    ρ₃/ρ₂ = T₂/T₃ = 343/313 = 1.096
Rise in density = 9.6 %
  3. Effectiveness, if the cooling air enters at 303 K:
    ε = (T₂ − T₃)/(T₂ − Tc,in) = (343 − 313)/(343 − 303) = 30/40 = 0.75
  4. Effect on air mass: the cylinder fills with 9.6 percent more air mass at the same boost and volumetric efficiency. With the same air-fuel ratio, 9.6 percent more fuel can be burnt, so power rises by roughly the same amount.
  5. With the 1.6 bar boost from 300 K that gave 343 K, the overall density ratio becomes 1.6 × 300/313 = 1.53 instead of 1.40.
  6. Further effects: (a) lower charge temperature lowers end-gas temperature, so knock falls in SI engines and more boost or a higher compression ratio can be used; (b) lower peak combustion temperature cuts NOx; (c) lower exhaust and component temperatures reduce thermal load on pistons and valves; (d) less fuel enrichment is needed for cooling at full load, so fuel consumption falls.
  7. Penalties: the core loses some pressure, so the true gain is a little smaller; the extra volume adds to throttle lag; in a diesel too cold a charge lengthens the ignition delay.
  8. Heat the intercooler must reject, for an air flow of 0.10 kg/s:
    q = cp ΔT = 1.005 × (343 − 313) = 30.2 kJ/kg
Q = 0.10 × 30.2 = 3.0 kW to the cooling air
  9. Knock benefit in an SI engine, estimating the end-gas temperature after isentropic compression at compression ratio 9 (90.4 = 2.408):
    Without intercooler: 343 × 2.408 = 826 K
With intercooler:    313 × 2.408 = 754 K, about 72 K cooler
  10. Power example: an engine making 90 kW without the intercooler would make about 90 × 1.096 = 98.6 kW with it, at the same boost and air-fuel ratio, before allowing for the small pressure loss.
  11. Answer: density rises 9.6 percent, giving about 9.6 percent more power at the same boost, plus a knock and NOx benefit.

Analyze Explain turbo lag, its causes and the methods used to reduce it. Include variable geometry and intercooling in your answer. 14 marks

Points to cover. Cause: turbo speed depends on exhaust energy, which depends on fuel burnt; time constant of rotor inertia and gas build-up; Worse at low engine speed and after a gearshift; worse with a large turbo chosen for peak power; Remedies: small turbo, twin-scroll and divided turbine housing, lightweight compressor and turbine wheels, ball bearings, blow-off and wastegate control, VGT; VGT: vanes close at low flow to raise gas velocity and turbine work, open at high flow to avoid over-boost; Intercooling does not cut lag but raises the density benefit of the boost that does arrive.

Model answer.

  1. Definition: turbo lag is the time between opening the throttle and getting full boost, at an engine speed where boost is possible. It differs from the boost threshold, which is the lowest engine speed at which the turbo can make any boost.
  2. Causes: (1) the rotor (turbine wheel, shaft, compressor wheel) must store kinetic energy ½Jω² before it reaches boost speed; (2) exhaust energy rises only after more fuel is burnt, and more fuel needs more air, a loop that builds slowly; (3) the exhaust manifold and turbine housing volume must fill and pressurise; (4) the intake pipes and intercooler must fill with denser air; (5) a large turbine chosen for peak power gives low gas velocity at low flow.
  3. Numbers, from the turbocharging worked example: J = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ kg·m², spool from 30 000 to 150 000 rev/min with 1.8 kW of surplus turbine power.
    ω₁ = 3142 rad/s, ω₂ = 15 708 rad/s
ΔE = ½ J (ω₂² − ω₁²) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ × (2.467 − 0.099) × 10⁸ = 2369 J
t  = ΔE / P = 2369 / 1800 = 1.32 s
Rotor 30 % lighter: t = 0.7 × 1.32 = 0.92 s
  4. When it is worst: at low engine speed, after a gear change, and after light-load cruising, when exhaust energy is low and the rotor has slowed.
  5. Remedies: (a) smaller turbine housing (higher gas velocity at low flow, but more back pressure at high flow); (b) twin-scroll or divided housings that keep exhaust pulses apart, and pulse rather than constant-pressure manifolds; (c) light wheels (titanium aluminide turbine, small compressor) and ball bearings instead of journal bearings to cut J and friction; (d) parallel twin turbos, each smaller, or sequential turbos with a small one for low speed; (e) electric assist on the shaft or an electric compressor; (f) variable geometry.
  6. Variable geometry turbine: vanes round the turbine wheel close at low flow to narrow the throat and raise gas velocity, so the turbine makes more power early and spools fast; at high flow they open to lower back pressure and hold boost at the limit without a wastegate. It addresses lag and threshold together.
  7. Intercooling: it does not cut lag, and its volume can add a little. It raises the density benefit of the boost that arrives: at 1.6 bar, cooling 361.6 K air to 314.7 K lifts the density ratio from 1.33 to 1.53.
  8. Wastegate: it limits boost at high speed; it does not reduce lag, but it lets a smaller, quicker turbine be used without overboosting at the top end.
  9. Conclusion: lag is an energy-storage delay; cut the energy to store (lighter rotor) or supply it faster (higher gas velocity, VGT, twin-scroll, electric assist).
Evaluate Compare supercharging and turbocharging. Discuss the effects of supercharging on engine power, knock, thermal loading and the modifications needed in the engine. 14 marks

Points to cover. Drive: mechanical (belt/gear) versus exhaust turbine; parasitic loss versus waste energy recovery; Response: immediate versus lag; boost curve against engine speed; Effect on power: power roughly proportional to air mass flow; volumetric efficiency and trapping efficiency change; Knock: higher intake temperature and pressure raise knock in SI engines; intercooling and retarded timing or lower compression ratio used; Thermal loading: higher gas temperature and pressure, hotter exhaust valve and piston; need for richer mixture at full load to cool the charge; Modifications: lower compression ratio, stronger pistons, connecting rods and bearings, better cooling and lubrication, sodium-cooled or better valve material, intercooler, boost control.

  3. Effect on power, with the supercharging worked example: 75 kW engine, 1.6 bar, 0.70 efficient blower.

  4. A turbocharger at the same boost takes its 5 kW or so of compressor power from the exhaust, so nearly all of the 99.6 kW reaches the crank, less a small rise in pumping work from turbine back pressure.
  5. Effect on knock: boost raises both pressure and temperature of the end gas in SI engines, so knock tendency rises. Controls: intercooling, retarded spark, richer full-load mixture, lower compression ratio, higher-octane fuel, knock sensor.
  6. Thermal loading: peak pressure and gas temperature rise, so pistons, rings, exhaust valves, head gasket and bearings carry more heat and load; more heat goes to the coolant and oil. In a CI engine the extra air usually lowers exhaust temperature at the same fuel rate.

Analyze Compare cross-flow, loop and uniflow scavenging in two-stroke engines. Which gives the best scavenging efficiency and why? 12 marks

Points to cover. Cross: transfer and exhaust ports opposite, charge short-circuits to exhaust, poor scavenging efficiency, but simple; Loop (Schnürle): transfer ports angled to loop the charge up the back wall and over the head, pushing burnt gas out, used in most small petrol two-strokes; Uniflow: inlet ports at one end and exhaust valves or ports at the other, charge moves in one direction; best scavenging, needs a valve or opposed pistons; Compare scavenging efficiency, charging efficiency, charge loss and specific output; Also mention crankcase-scavenged versus blower-scavenged and the effect of exhaust tuning.

Model answer.

  1. Scavenging replaces burnt gas with fresh charge while the ports are open near bottom dead centre. Measures: delivery ratio D = Vdel/Vs, trapping efficiency ηt = Vtr/Vdel, scavenging efficiency ηs = Vtr/Vec and charging efficiency ηt × D, all with volumes referred to the same pressure and temperature (they are really mass ratios).
  2. Cross scavenging (sketch as Figure 4.9.1): transfer and exhaust ports on opposite walls; a deflector on the piston crown turns the charge up the transfer side, across under the head and down to the exhaust. Simple and cheap, but part of the charge short-circuits straight across, and the heavy deflector crown runs hot.
  3. Loop (Schnürle) scavenging: transfer ports flank the exhaust and aim the charge at the far wall; the streams rise up that wall, turn over the head and sweep down to the exhaust. Flat piston crown, less short-circuiting, better trapping. Used in most small petrol two-strokes.
  4. Uniflow scavenging: inlet ports round the bottom of the cylinder and an exhaust valve in the head (or ports uncovered by an opposed piston); the charge moves one way, inlet to exhaust, like a piston of air, with little mixing. Best scavenging, but needs a blower and valve gear or two crankshafts. Used in large marine and locomotive diesels.
  5. Worked comparison, D = 0.80, Vec = Vs = 300 cm³, measured ηt = 0.80:

  7. Comparison:

  8. Crankcase-scavenged engines (delivery ratio usually below 1, charge carries fuel and oil) lose fuel when charge short-circuits; blower-scavenged diesels lose only air. Tuned exhaust pipes return a pressure wave that pushes escaping charge back before the port closes.
  9. Answer: uniflow gives the best scavenging efficiency because the flow never reverses or crosses itself, so fresh charge displaces burnt gas with least mixing and least short-circuiting.
Evaluate A small turbocharged petrol car is used mostly in stop-and-go Indian city traffic. Discuss the cooling, lubricant and boost-control choices you would make and why. 14 marks

Points to cover. Cooling: pressurised forced circulation with thermostat, electric fan that runs after shutdown to protect the turbo bearing, coolant with glycol and corrosion inhibitors; Lubrication: fully synthetic or semi-synthetic multigrade with high viscosity index, anti-wear and anti-oxidant additives, oil-cooled turbo bearing, short oil change interval for short trips; Turbo protection: idling before shutdown, oil feed from the main gallery with a restrictor and drain to the sump; Boost control: small turbo or VGT for early boost, intercooler to reduce knock at high ambient temperature, wastegate or electronic boost control; Trade-offs: fuel quality and knock, cost of synthetic oil against turbo life, heat soak in traffic.

Model answer.

  1. The duty: short trips, long idling, low road speed and high ambient temperature (often 35 to 45 °C), occasional full-load bursts. The choices must handle heat soak, cold short-trip running, knock, and lag at low speed.
  2. Cooling: pressurised forced circulation with a wax or map-controlled thermostat so the engine warms quickly on short trips; an electric fan that runs on after shutdown to cool the turbo and head; a 100 kPa cap so the coolant can run hot without boiling (120.4 °C at sea level); a 50 percent glycol coolant with corrosion inhibitors and distilled water, since scale from hard water cuts heat transfer; a water-cooled turbo bearing housing.
  3. Lubrication: a low-viscosity, high-VI synthetic or semi-synthetic multigrade (for example 0W-20 or 5W-30 as the maker specifies) that flows at once on cold starts yet keeps its high-shear viscosity at the turbo bearing; strong antioxidant, detergent and anti-wear additives; shorter drain intervals than highway use, because short trips leave fuel and water in the oil. A turbo oil feed with a restrictor and a free drain back to the sump.
  4. Crankcase ventilation: a closed PCV system with a good oil separator, routing blow-by to the compressor inlet under boost, so oil mist does not coat the compressor and intercooler. Short trips condense water in the crankcase; a working PCV sweep keeps sludge down.
  5. Boost control: a small turbo, twin-scroll or VGT for boost from low speed, so the car pulls in traffic without lag; an electronically controlled wastegate; a knock sensor with spark retard.
  6. Intercooler at high ambient, 1.6 bar, ηc = 0.70, air at 313 K (40 °C), ε = 0.80:
    T₂ = 313 × [1 + (1.60.286 − 1)/0.70] = 313 × 1.205 = 377.3 K
T₃ = 377.3 − 0.80 × (377.3 − 313) = 325.9 K
Density ratio over the 40 °C air = 1.6 × 313/325.9 = 1.54
Uncooled charge at 377 K would be 104 °C: a knock risk
  7. Driver habits for the turbo: let it idle 30 to 60 seconds after a hard run before switching off, so oil carries heat away from the bearing housing.
  8. Trade-offs: synthetic oil and short drain intervals cost more but protect the turbo bearing; a smaller turbo gives quick response but limits top-end power; running hotter coolant improves efficiency but narrows the knock margin, so the knock sensor and octane rating matter. Fuel quality varies, so the calibration must tolerate lower-octane petrol.
  9. Conclusion: choose for heat and short trips first (pressurised cooling with after-run fan, high-VI synthetic oil, good PCV separation), then for low-speed boost with a small or variable geometry turbo and an intercooler sized for 40 °C air.

Glossary

Additive
A chemical blended into a base oil to give a property the base oil lacks, such as anti-wear or detergency.
Blow-by
Gas that leaks past the piston rings into the crankcase during compression and combustion.
Blowdown
The first rush of high-pressure burnt gas out of the exhaust port when it opens, before fresh charge arrives.
Boost pressure
The amount by which intake pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure, usually quoted in bar.
Coolant
The liquid used in a cooling system, normally water with ethylene glycol and corrosion inhibitors.
Dry sump
A lubrication layout in which oil is scavenged to a separate tank and pumped back under pressure to the engine.
Fin
A thin extended metal surface on an air-cooled engine that increases the area available for convection to air.
Flash point
The temperature at which oil vapour gives a momentary flash when a flame is applied; a low value warns of fuel dilution.
Forced circulation
Coolant circulation produced by a pump, so the flow rate is set by pump speed and not by temperature alone.
Friction power
The difference between indicated power and brake power, spent on rubbing contacts and on pumping gas in and out of the cylinder.
Heat balance
An account of where the chemical energy of the fuel goes: brake power, coolant, exhaust, friction and radiation.
Intercooler
A heat exchanger that cools air leaving the compressor before it enters the intake manifold.
Mechanical efficiency
The ratio of brake power to indicated power, ηm = BP/IP.
Mist lubrication
Lubrication of small two-strokes by oil premixed with fuel, with no pump, filter or sump.
PCV valve
A spring-loaded plunger valve that meters crankcase gas into the intake manifold according to manifold vacuum.
Pour point
The lowest temperature at which the oil will still flow under standard test conditions.
Radiator
A tube-and-fin heat exchanger that transfers heat from the coolant to the air stream.
Scavenging
The removal of burnt gas from a two-stroke cylinder and its replacement by fresh charge during the port overlap.
Supercharger
A mechanically driven compressor that raises the pressure and density of the air entering the engine.
Thermostat
A temperature-operated valve that keeps coolant away from the radiator until the engine reaches its working temperature.
Thermosyphon
Coolant circulation driven only by the density difference between hot and cold coolant, with no pump.
Turbo lag
The delay between a demand for power and the delivery of boost, set by rotor inertia and the time needed to build exhaust energy.
Turbocharger
A turbine–compressor pair on a common shaft, where exhaust gas drives the turbine and the compressor boosts intake air.
Variable geometry turbocharger
A turbocharger whose turbine inlet vanes move to match gas flow, giving high boost at low speed and safe boost at high speed.
Viscosity
The internal resistance of a fluid to flow, quoted in centistokes (cSt) at a stated temperature or as an SAE grade.
Viscosity index
A scale showing how much an oil's viscosity falls as temperature rises; higher index means less change.
Wastegate
A valve that lets part of the exhaust bypass the turbine so that boost cannot rise above its set value.
Water jacket
The set of cast passages in the block and head through which liquid coolant flows around hot parts.
Wet sump
A lubrication layout in which the oil supply sits in a pan under the crankshaft and drains back to it by gravity.
