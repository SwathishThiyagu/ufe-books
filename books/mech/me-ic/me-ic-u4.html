4.1 Need for cooling; air and liquid cooling systems

Burning fuel releases heat, and only about 30 percent of it reaches the crankshaft as useful work. The rest sits in the metal and must leave, or the piston rings lose their oil film and the liner scores. Cooling is the path that heat takes from the cylinder walls to the outside air. Two families do this job: air cooling, where fins on the outside hand heat straight to a moving air stream, and liquid cooling, where a coolant carries heat to a radiator. Motorcycles and small gensets use the first; cars and tractors, and larger gensets, use the second.

You need: engine components and the four-stroke cycle from Unit 1, and the meaning of brake power from Unit 5.

Why heat must leave the engine

Fuel energy does not all become work. In a typical diesel engine, of every 100 units of heat released by burning fuel, about 30 leave as brake power at the crankshaft, about 30 go out with the exhaust gas, about 30 pass into the coolant, and about 10 are lost to friction and radiation. The coolant share is a fixed tax the engine pays, and something must carry it away.

Metal sets the upper limit. Aluminium alloy pistons lose strength quickly a few hundred degrees above normal running temperature, so the crown must stay well below that. Lubricating oil sets a tighter limit: above roughly 150 °C the oil at the piston rings oxidises, turns to varnish and coke, and its film breaks. A broken film means metal touching metal, and the liner scores. The oil system that keeps this film alive is in 4.3.

Uneven cooling is as harmful as no cooling. If one side of the head runs hot and the other cold, the two sides expand by different amounts, thermal stress builds up, and the head can warp or blow its gasket. Too much cooling also hurts: a cold engine vaporises petrol badly, wears faster and gives lower efficiency. The aim is a target metal temperature, not the coldest possible engine.

Q = ṁ × c × ΔT

Q is the heat carried away per second in kW; ṁ is the coolant mass flow in kg/s; c is the specific heat of the coolant in kJ/kg·K (4.18 for water); ΔT is the temperature rise of the coolant in K.

Air cooling: fins, baffles and a blower

An air-cooled engine carries no coolant. Thin metal fins are cast or machined on the barrel and head. Each fin adds surface, so the same metal wall can hand its heat to a much larger area of air. Heat passes from the hot metal into the air that flows between the fins, and the moving air carries it away.

Fin spacing is a compromise. Fins packed close together give more area on paper, but the air trapped between them becomes stagnant and stops removing heat. Spacing is chosen so that fresh air keeps touching metal along the whole gap. Fin thickness is kept small to save weight and space, but thick enough that heat can still conduct out to the tip; the real bottleneck is the air side, which removes heat far more slowly than the metal conducts it.

The air must be made to flow. On a motorcycle road speed does the job. A stationary genset or pump set has no road speed, so a blower or fan pushes air and sheet-metal baffles guide it between the fins instead of letting it slip around the outside of the engine. Baffles also stop the same air from being reused.

Air cooling suits motorcycles, scooters, small gensets, pump sets and aircraft piston engines. It has no radiator, no pump and no coolant to freeze or leak, and it warms up fast. Its weakness is that air carries little heat per kilogram, so the metal runs hotter, the head runs hotter than the barrel, and power per litre is limited.

Liquid cooling: jacket, radiator, pump, thermostat

A liquid-cooled engine wraps the hot parts in a jacket: a hollow space cast into the block and head, running close to the liner, the valve seats and the exhaust port. Coolant in the jacket picks up heat, a pump pushes it to the radiator, and air flowing over thin tubes and fins takes the heat out of it. Some syllabi call this indirect cooling, because the air never touches the hot metal.

The circuit has four parts. The water pump is a small centrifugal pump driven by a belt or gears. The radiator is the heat exchanger. The fan pulls air through the radiator when the vehicle is slow or standing. The thermostat is a valve filled with wax: it stays shut while the engine is cold, so the engine warms up quickly, then opens at its set temperature and lets coolant through the radiator.

Liquid removes far more heat from a given area of metal than air does, so the jacket stays compact and all cylinders run at nearly the same temperature. The price is weight, cost, pump power and parts that can leak. Circulation by pump, by density difference, and under a pressure cap that raises the boiling point is covered in 4.2.

Worked example

Question. A genset engine burns 8 kg of diesel per hour. The calorific value of the diesel is 42 000 kJ/kg. Thirty percent of the fuel energy leaves through the coolant. The cooling water is allowed to rise 6 K. Take the specific heat of water as 4.18 kJ/kg·K. Find (a) the fuel power, (b) the heat rate carried by the coolant, (c) the coolant mass flow in kg/s and in kg/min.

  1. Convert the fuel flow to kg/s: ṁf = 8 kg/h ÷ 3600 s/h = 2.222 × 10⁻³ kg/s.
  2. Fuel power is fuel flow times calorific value: Pf = 2.222 × 10⁻³ kg/s × 42 000 kJ/kg = 93.33 kW. This is the fuel power, not the nameplate rating.
  3. The coolant takes 30 percent of the fuel power: Q = 0.30 × 93.33 kW = 28.0 kW.
  4. Rearrange Q = ṁ c ΔT for the flow: ṁ = Q ÷ (c ΔT) = 28.0 kW ÷ (4.18 kJ/kg·K × 6 K) = 28.0 ÷ 25.08 = 1.12 kg/s.
  5. Convert to per minute: 1.12 kg/s × 60 s/min = 67 kg/min.

Answer. Fuel power is 93.3 kW, the coolant carries 28 kW, and the required coolant flow is about 1.12 kg/s, that is 67 kg per minute.

Exam traps

  • Starting the coolant calculation from the brake power on the nameplate. The coolant takes a share of the fuel energy, so start from fuel flow times calorific value.
  • Calling the thermostat a temperature sensor. It is a wax-filled valve that opens and closes the coolant path; the fan and the gauge are separate.
  • Saying an air-cooled engine has no cooling medium. Air is the medium; the heat still leaves by convection, only through fins instead of a jacket.
  • Mixing units in Q = ṁ c ΔT. With c in kJ/kg·K the answer is in kW; using c = 4180 J/kg·K with kJ elsewhere gives a factor of 1000 error.
  • Saying the radiator cools the engine. The radiator cools the coolant; the jacket is what touches the hot metal.

4.2 Thermosyphon, forced circulation and pressure cooling; coolants

A liquid cooling loop moves heat from the engine jacket to the radiator. It can run on density difference alone (thermosyphon), on a pump (forced circulation), or under a pressure cap that lifts the boiling point. The coolant—water or a glycol mix—sets the safe upper temperature. Only a few universities teach all three arrangements at full depth. Road engines use forced circulation with a pressure cap; thermosyphon survives on small stationary engines.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the heat path and radiator layout.

Thermosyphon: flow by density difference

Hot water is lighter than cold water. In a closed loop, the column from the jacket to the radiator is hot and light; the downcomer is cold and heavy. Gravity pulls the heavy column down and pushes the light column up. Flow continues without a pump.

Δp = (ρc − ρh) g h

Δp is the driving pressure in Pa; ρc is cold-water density in kg/m³; ρh is hot-water density in kg/m³; g is 9.81 m/s²; h is the height between jacket centre and radiator centre in m.

The head is small—tens of pascals in a car-sized loop—so pipes are short and wide. At low engine speed the heat rate is low and the loop copes; at high speed the same engine needs a pump.

Forced circulation and pressure cooling

A pump, usually belt-driven from the crankshaft, pushes coolant through the jacket, radiator and heater. A thermostat blocks flow to the radiator until the coolant reaches about 80–90 °C, then opens; a bypass route keeps the pump circulating during warm-up. An expansion tank gives the coolant room to expand and keeps the pump inlet flooded. Forced circulation gives a predictable flow at all speeds, so the radiator can be smaller.

A pressure cap is a spring-loaded valve. It holds the system above atmospheric pressure and raises the boiling point. Saturation temperature rises with pressure: a cap set about 100 kPa above atmosphere lifts the boiling point of water to roughly 120 °C. Hotter coolant carries more heat for the same flow, and the radiator can be smaller. A release valve also lets air or steam out and admits air on cooling. Never open a hot cap: the sudden drop to atmospheric pressure flashes coolant to steam and scalds. A faulty cap lets the engine boil at a lower temperature; it does not add cooling.

Coolants and additives

Water has high specific heat (about 4.18 kJ/kg·K), is cheap and transfers heat well, but it freezes at 0 °C, corrodes iron and aluminium, and hard water leaves calcium and magnesium scale on hot walls. Scale acts as an insulator and can block narrow passages.

Ethylene glycol (EG) and propylene glycol (PG) lower the freezing point and raise the boiling point. A 50 percent glycol mix is common: it gives freeze protection near −37 °C (EG) and enough boiling-point margin, while keeping heat capacity and viscosity acceptable. Above about 60–70 percent glycol, freeze protection worsens and heat transfer falls. Corrosion inhibitors—silicates, phosphates, borates or organic acids—protect metals; they are consumed over time, so coolant has a service life. Use soft or distilled water with glycol concentrate, not hard tap water.

Worked example

Question. A thermosyphon system has 0.4 m between jacket centre and radiator centre. Water density is 995 kg/m³ at 40 °C and 977 kg/m³ at 80 °C. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the driving pressure head. Explain why a pump is added on the same engine at higher speed.

  1. Identify h = 0.4 m, ρc = 995 kg/m³, ρh = 977 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. Density difference = 995 − 977 = 18 kg/m³.
  3. Δp = 18 × 9.81 × 0.4.
  4. 18 × 9.81 = 176.58; then 176.58 × 0.4 = 70.632 Pa.
  5. This small head gives gentle flow; at high speed heat rejection rises, so a pump is needed.

Answer. Δp = 70.632 Pa (about 70.6 Pa).

Exam traps

  • A pressure cap raises the boiling point; it does not increase cooling capacity.
  • Thermosyphon flow needs height and a density difference. A horizontal loop or zero temperature difference gives no flow.
  • Hard water scale insulates hot walls and blocks passages; do not treat it as harmless water.
  • More glycol is not always better. Above about 60–70 percent, freeze protection worsens and heat transfer falls.
  • Opening a hot pressure cap causes flash steam and scalds; wait for the engine to cool.

4.3 Lubrication systems and engine friction

Friction takes a fixed slice of the power that the burning fuel produces, and no engine escapes it. The lubrication system exists to keep that slice small: it stores oil, cleans it, pumps it at the right pressure, and delivers it to every sliding pair — rings, bearings and valve gear. This chapter lists where friction comes from, explains how an oil film carries load, and describes the circuits — mist, splash, pressure feed, wet sump and dry sump — that place oil on the right surface at the right moment.

You need: Unit 1 for rings, bearings and the crank train, and see Unit 5, Engine Testing and Performance, for the definitions of indicated and brake power.

Where engine friction comes from

Not all the power that the burning charge pushes onto the piston reaches the flywheel. Some is spent overcoming friction between moving parts, and some is spent pushing gas in and out of the cylinder. Together they form the friction power, FP. It is defined as the gap between the power developed inside the cylinder and the power delivered at the crankshaft.

FP = IP − BP

FP is friction power (kW); IP is indicated power, the power developed on the piston (kW); BP is brake power, the power measured at the crankshaft (kW).

Rubbing friction comes from four places. Piston rings press on the cylinder wall and carry the gas load; the skirt rubs sideways as the rod swings. Main and big-end bearings shear their oil film. The valve train — cam, tappets, pushrods, rockers — rubs on every cycle. Pumping loss is separate work: pulling air past a nearly closed throttle and pushing exhaust out. At light load in an SI engine, pumping loss grows large.

Parameters that influence friction: engine speed (friction rises roughly with speed), load (ring pressure rises with cylinder pressure), oil viscosity and its temperature, bore-to-stroke ratio, number of rings, and surface finish. Two ratios summarise the loss.

η_m = BP / IP

η_m is mechanical efficiency (no unit, often written as a percentage); BP is brake power (kW); IP is indicated power (kW).

fmep = FP / (V_d × N/120)

fmep is friction mean effective pressure (Pa or bar), a definition only; FP is friction power (W); V_d is total swept volume (m³); N is speed (rev/min); N/120 is the number of cycles per second of a four-stroke engine.

How an oil film carries load

An oil film carries load in three ways, and a running engine passes through all three.

Hydrodynamic lubrication. The journal turns inside the bearing with a small clearance. Viscous drag pulls oil into the narrowing gap ahead of the closest point. That oil cannot escape fast enough, so its pressure rises and forms a wedge, and the journal floats on the wedge. Metal never touches metal. This needs enough speed, enough viscosity and the correct clearance.

Boundary lubrication. The film is a few molecules thick, held on the surface by the oil's own polarity. Load is carried by surface contact, not by fluid pressure, so wear is real. Mixed lubrication lies between boundary and hydrodynamic.

At start-up the shaft rests on the bearing and only a boundary film exists until the pump builds pressure and speed rises. Those first seconds cause most bearing wear, which is why oil must still flow on a cold morning; viscosity behaviour is covered in 4.4.

Lubrication methods: mist, splash and pressure feed

Systems are classified by how the oil reaches the surface.

  • Mist (petroil): oil is mixed with petrol, commonly 2 to 5 percent by volume. The fuel-air-oil mist wets the bearings and cylinder, and the rest burns.
  • Splash: a dipper on the big end scoops oil from a trough and throws it around the crankcase.
  • Pressure feed: a pump sends oil through drillings to the bearings, and the overflow splashes the walls and valve gear.

Mist lubrication suits two-strokes, which have no sump, and works at any tilt angle. It is cheap, but it fouls spark plugs and smokes. A four-stroke cannot use it, because petrol in the sump would wash oil off the cylinder wall.

Splash lubrication is simple and served small stationary engines and old motorcycles. The oil level is critical: too low starves the dipper, too high makes the oil froth. Pressure feed is used on almost all car and truck engines, usually combined with splash for the cylinder walls.

Wet sump and dry sump circuits

A wet sump engine keeps its oil in a pan under the crankcase. Oil flows from the sump through a strainer to the pump, then to the filter, along the main gallery to the bearings, and drains back by gravity.

Each part has a job. The strainer is coarse mesh that stops large debris before the pump. The pump is usually a gear or rotor type driven from the crankshaft. A relief valve beside the pump opens at a set pressure and returns surplus oil to the sump, so the filter and hoses survive cold thick oil. The filter carries a bypass that opens if it clogs. The main gallery is a drilled passage along the block feeding the main bearings, from which drillings lead to the big ends, cam and rockers.

A dry sump engine keeps oil in a separate tank. A scavenge pump pulls oil from a shallow pan into the tank, and a pressure pump feeds the engine from the tank. It costs more and adds plumbing, but oil cannot surge away from the pick-up in a hard corner, the engine can sit lower, and the tank holds more oil that cools better. Racing cars, aircraft and some heavy diesels use it.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke engine is motored at 3000 rev/min with the fuel cut off, and the dynamometer reads a torque of 24 N·m. In a firing test at the same speed the indicated power is 30 kW. Find the friction power, the brake power and the mechanical efficiency.

  1. While motoring, no fuel burns, so the torque the dynamometer absorbs is the torque needed to overcome friction plus pumping. Speed N = 3000 rev/min, torque T = 24 N·m.
  2. Friction power FP = 2πNT/60 = 2π × 3000 × 24 / 60 = 7540 W = 7.54 kW.
  3. Brake power BP = IP − FP = 30 − 7.54 = 22.46 kW.
  4. Mechanical efficiency η_m = BP / IP = 22.46 / 30 = 0.7487, that is 74.9 percent.

Answer. Friction power 7.54 kW, brake power 22.46 kW, mechanical efficiency 74.9 percent.

Exam traps

  • Mechanical efficiency is brake power divided by indicated power, never the other way round. A value above 1 is always a sign of this slip.
  • Motoring torque gives friction plus pumping loss. It is not pure rubbing friction, so do not call it 'bearing friction only'.
  • Friction power is IP minus BP. If the question gives brake power and efficiency, first find IP = BP / η_m, then subtract.
  • Mist lubrication belongs to two-strokes. Writing it for a four-stroke loses marks, because petrol would dilute the sump oil.

4.4 Lubricants: properties and additives

A lubricant is not chosen by feel. It is chosen by measured properties: how its viscosity changes with temperature, how cold it can pour, how hot it can safely be, how it resists oxidation and what additives it carries. These properties decide whether oil reaches the bearings at a cold start, stays on the cylinder wall at full load, and keeps sludge and wear low over thousands of kilometres. This chapter lists those properties with units and explains how additives turn a base oil into an engine oil.

You need Chapter 4.3 for where the oil goes and what it must do at each sliding pair.

Viscosity, grade and temperature limits

Viscosity is the oil's resistance to shear. Dynamic viscosity μ (Greek mu) is shear stress divided by shear rate; its SI unit is the pascal second (Pa·s), and engine oils are quoted in millipascal seconds (mPa·s). Kinematic viscosity ν (Greek nu) is μ divided by density; its unit is mm²/s, also called the centistoke (cSt). A grade such as 10W-40 is set by SAE (Society of Automotive Engineers) limits, not by one number. The W means winter. The 10W part is a cold-cranking limit at low temperature; the 40 part is a kinematic-viscosity band at 100 °C. A multigrade oil meets both. Its viscosity index (VI) is a dimensionless number showing how flat the viscosity-temperature curve is: a high VI means little thinning when hot. Pour point is the lowest temperature at which the oil still moves, in °C. Cloud point is the temperature at which wax first makes the oil cloudy, in °C. Flash point is the lowest temperature at which vapour above the oil ignites under test, in °C. Fire point is the higher temperature at which the vapour keeps burning for about five seconds, usually 10–20 °C above flash point.

Chemical properties and ageing

Carbon residue is the mass percent of coke left when oil is heated without air; it warns about deposits in hot zones. Acidity is measured as total acid number (TAN) in mg KOH per gram of oil. The base reserve is total base number (TBN), also in mg KOH/g. TAN rises as oil oxidises; TBN falls as it neutralises acids. Oxidation stability is the time or temperature needed to reach a chosen TAN, sludge or viscosity rise; it has no single unit. Oiliness is the ability to lower friction in boundary lubrication, where surfaces touch through a very thin film; it is judged on friction rigs, not by a simple number. Emulsification is the tendency to hold water; demulsibility is the reverse, stated as time to separate or percent water separated. Foaming is air held in the oil; it is reported as foam volume in mL and collapse time in seconds. Foam reduces oil flow and can cause cavitation in the pump.

Additives: small mass, large effect

Additives are 1–20 percent by mass of the finished oil. A viscosity index improver is a polymer that coils when cold and expands when hot, so it raises hot viscosity more than cold viscosity; this is how a 10W base oil becomes a 10W-40. Antioxidants break the chain reaction that forms acids and sludge. Detergents keep hot metal clean and neutralise acids. Dispersants keep soot and sludge suspended so they drain out instead of sticking. Anti-wear agents form a film on loaded surfaces; extreme-pressure (EP) agents react under high pressure to stop seizure. Corrosion and rust inhibitors coat metal with a thin film. Pour point depressants stop wax crystals from joining into a gel. Antifoam agents, often silicone, break bubbles. The base oil still carries the load; additives change its behaviour, not its basic viscosity.

Oil consumption and its fraction of fuel

Oil leaves an engine by four main routes. Ring blow-by pushes oil mist past the rings. Worn valve guides pull oil into the inlet. Turbo seals and crankcase ventilation can carry oil vapour. Volatility boils light oil fractions at high temperature. Consumption is best stated as a fraction of fuel used: f = Voil ÷ Vfuel, where Voil is the oil added in litres and Vfuel is the fuel burnt in litres. If distance D is in km and fuel economy FE is in km per litre, Vfuel = D ÷ FE. A good road engine stays below 1 percent; worn or racing engines can reach about 2 percent.

f = V<sub>oil</sub> ÷ V<sub>fuel</sub> = V<sub>oil</sub> ÷ (D ÷ FE)

f is the oil consumption fraction, dimensionless; Voil is the oil added in litres; Vfuel is the fuel burnt in litres; D is distance in km; FE is fuel economy in km per litre.

Worked example

Question. A car covers 1000 km and its owner tops up 0.4 litre of engine oil. Fuel average is 15 km per litre. Find the fuel used, the oil consumption as a fraction of fuel, and the same value as a percentage.

  1. Fuel used Vfuel = D ÷ FE = 1000 km ÷ 15 km/L = 66.7 L.
  2. Oil fraction f = Voil ÷ Vfuel = 0.4 L ÷ 66.7 L = 0.006.
  3. Percentage = f × 100 = 0.006 × 100 = 0.6 percent.

Answer. Fuel used is 66.7 L; oil consumption is 0.006 of fuel, that is 0.6 percent.

Exam traps

  • A higher viscosity grade is not automatically better. Too thick an oil starves narrow passages at cold start and raises churning loss; match the grade to ambient temperature and engine design.
  • The W in 10W-40 means winter, not weight. 10W-40 is one oil that meets a cold 10W limit and a hot 40 band, not a mixture of grade 10 and grade 40.
  • Flash point is not fire point and neither is autoignition temperature. Fire point is higher, and a high flash point alone does not prove good lubrication.
  • TAN and TBN use the same unit, mg KOH/g, but move in opposite directions. Rising TAN and falling TBN both mean the oil is ageing.
  • Pour point is not the lowest safe operating temperature. Oil can still pour but be far too thick to pump or protect bearings.

4.5 Supercharging: objectives, types and effects

Supercharging forces more air mass into the same swept volume. More trapped air allows more fuel, so a smaller engine can give the power of a larger one. The blower is driven from the crankshaft, so it costs engine power; the useful figure is the net gain. Superchargers appear in performance petrol cars, aircraft at altitude, and some industrial diesels. This chapter covers the objectives, the main mechanical types, the density ratio, and the effects on knock, thermal load and exhaust. Exhaust-driven boosting follows in 4.6.

You need: swept volume and volumetric efficiency from Unit 1, and knock from Unit 3; charge cooling is in 4.7.

Objectives and the density ratio

A naturally aspirated engine fills the cylinder with air near ambient pressure, about 1.0 bar. Its torque is limited by the air mass trapped each cycle. A supercharger raises the intake pressure above ambient; the ratio p₂/p₁ is the pressure ratio, using absolute pressures. The mass trapped also depends on temperature: hot air is less dense. For a perfect gas, density ratio is pressure ratio times the inverse temperature ratio. A boost to 1.6 bar sounds like 60 percent more air. If compression heats the air from 300 K to 340 K, the density ratio is only (1.6/1.0) × (300/340) = 1.41, so about 41 percent more air.

ρ₂/ρ₁ = (p₂/p₁)(T₁/T₂)

ρ is density in kg/m³; p is absolute pressure; T is absolute temperature in K; subscript 1 is ambient before the blower, subscript 2 is after the blower.

  • More power for the same swept volume and better power-to-weight ratio.
  • Altitude compensation: a blower restores sea-level air density when ambient pressure falls.
  • Sometimes lower specific fuel consumption, because friction and engine weight are spread over more kW.

Mechanical supercharger types

All mechanical superchargers are driven by a belt or gears from the crankshaft. The roots blower has two meshing lobes that trap a fixed volume of air and push it to the intake. It is a positive-displacement machine: flow per revolution is nearly constant, so boost rises with engine speed. The sliding-vane blower uses vanes in a rotor to trap and move air; it is simple but the vanes wear. The centrifugal blower uses a fast impeller to throw air outward; pressure rises roughly with tip speed squared. It is efficient at high speed but gives little boost at low speed.

Positive-displacement blowers give strong low-speed response. Centrifugal blowers suit high-speed power. The figure shows the air path through a belt-driven supercharger; the pressure rises, but temperature also rises.

Drive power and the net gain

The blower takes power from the crank. The honest output is net brake power: P_net = P_gross − P_drive, where P_gross is the power from the extra trapped air and P_drive is the compressor work plus belt and gear losses. P_drive often reaches 10 to 20 percent of gross power at high boost. This limits boost: drive power grows with mass flow and pressure ratio, and compression also heats the charge. The necessity is clear: more power density, altitude compensation and a smaller package. The limitations are also clear: the net gain is always smaller than the density ratio suggests, and knock and thermal load grow.

Effects on the engine

  • Peak cylinder pressure and temperature rise, so pistons, valves, head gasket and bearings face higher thermal and mechanical load. Cooling and lubrication must be upgraded.
  • In SI engines, boost raises end-gas pressure and temperature, so knock tendency rises. Common fixes are a lower compression ratio, higher-octane fuel, retarded spark and charge cooling; see 4.7.
  • In CI engines, more air mass reduces smoke at a given fuel rate, but higher temperature can raise NOx; injection timing may need adjustment.
  • Exhaust mass flow and exhaust temperature rise. The exhaust manifold must withstand thermal cycling, and back pressure must stay low enough for good scavenging.
  • Modifications for supercharging include lower compression ratio, stronger pistons and gasket, larger cooling and oil capacity, fuel enrichment and intercooling. A blower is not a simple bolt-on for a standard engine.
  • Emissions change: more power per litre, but higher NOx is possible; after-treatment and calibration must match the boost.

Worked example

Question. A 1.5 litre petrol engine gives 75 kW naturally aspirated with intake air at 1.0 bar and 300 K. A belt-driven supercharger raises the intake manifold to 1.6 bar absolute and 340 K. The blower absorbs 11 kW from the crank at this speed. Find the density ratio, the gross power if power follows trapped air mass, the net power, and the percentage gain over the naturally aspirated engine.

  1. Use absolute pressures and temperatures: p₁ = 1.0 bar, T₁ = 300 K, p₂ = 1.6 bar, T₂ = 340 K.
  2. Density ratio = (p₂/p₁)(T₁/T₂) = (1.6/1.0)(300/340) = 1.4118.
  3. Gross power = 75 kW × 1.4118 = 105.88 kW.
  4. Net power = gross power − blower drive power = 105.88 − 11 = 94.88 kW.
  5. Gain = 94.88 − 75 = 19.88 kW. Percentage gain = (19.88/75) × 100 = 26.51 percent.

Answer. Density ratio 1.41; gross power 105.88 kW; net power 94.88 kW; gain 19.88 kW, about 26.51 percent.

Exam traps

  • Using gauge pressure in the pressure ratio. p₂ and p₁ must be absolute; a gauge reading of 0.6 bar is 1.6 bar absolute if ambient is 1.0 bar.
  • Forgetting charge heating. 1.6 bar at 340 K is not 60 percent extra mass; the density ratio is 1.41.
  • Quoting gross power. The belt-driven blower takes crank power, so the exam answer is net power.
  • Assuming supercharging always lowers specific fuel consumption. It can, but at high boost the drive loss, fuel enrichment and knock control can worsen it.
  • Mixing knock effects. Boost raises knock tendency in SI engines, so compression ratio is lowered or fuel octane raised; it is not increased.

4.6 Turbocharging methods and turbo lag

A turbocharger is a turbine driven by exhaust gas that spins a compressor and pushes more air into the engine. The exhaust energy is otherwise wasted, so the power seems free. The catch is time: the turbine and compressor have mass, and they take a moment to speed up. That delay is turbo lag. Turbochargers are common on small petrol and diesel cars, tractors and trucks, where more air means more fuel burnt and more power from a smaller engine.

You need: Chapter 4.5 for pressure ratio and density ratio, and Unit 5 for air flow measurement.

Layout and the two turbocharging methods

A turbocharger has a turbine wheel and a compressor wheel on one shaft, held in a bearing housing. Hot exhaust gas enters the turbine housing, pushes the turbine wheel, and leaves through the exhaust pipe. The shaft spins the compressor wheel, which draws air through the air filter and pushes it into the intake manifold at higher pressure and density. The bearing housing gets pressurised oil from the engine; the oil film supports the shaft and carries heat away from the hot turbine end. A wastegate is a valve that lets some exhaust gas bypass the turbine, and its actuator sets how much boost the engine makes.

The energy comes from exhaust enthalpy, not from the air. Exhaust at high pressure and temperature still has work to give, and the turbine extracts it. That is why a turbocharger is called an exhaust-driven supercharger.

Two manifold methods are common. In constant-pressure turbocharging, all cylinders feed one exhaust manifold. The pulses mix and the turbine sees an almost steady pressure. This suits steady high-load running, but at low load the pulses are lost in the volume and response is slow. In pulse turbocharging, narrow pipes keep the exhaust pulses separate and aim them at the turbine. The pulse energy arrives quickly, so boost builds earlier. Most road vehicles use pulse turbocharging.

A single turbo must work from low to rated speed, so its size is a compromise. Twin turbos can be parallel: two small turbos, each feeding half the cylinders. Smaller wheels have less inertia, so they spin up faster. Twin turbos can also be in series: a small turbo makes boost at low speed, a large turbo takes over at high speed, and valves switch the flow. A crank-driven blower, covered in Chapter 4.5, gives boost as soon as the engine turns, but it takes power from the crankshaft.

Boost threshold is not turbo lag

Boost threshold is the lowest engine speed, at a given load, where the exhaust flow can spin the turbine fast enough for the compressor to exceed atmospheric pressure. Below the threshold the turbo is a restriction, not a boost source. A large turbine housing raises the threshold; a small one lowers it. The threshold is a speed.

Turbo lag is a time. Open the throttle at a speed above the threshold and the boost does not appear at once. The exhaust gas must first accelerate the turbine wheel, shaft and compressor wheel; the intake pipes and intercooler must then fill with denser air. A typical small petrol turbo takes about one to two seconds to reach full boost after a throttle step. The figure shows this: throttle moves in the first 0.3 s, the turbine spools from 0.2 s to 1.5 s, and full boost arrives at about 1.6 s.

Lag is felt as a pause followed by a surge. It is worst after a gear change or when the engine has been cruising at low load. The cure is not more boost; it is less rotating mass, a smaller turbine housing, or split exhaust pulses.

Matching, wastegate and anti-lag

Matching a turbocharger means choosing a compressor and turbine that work well together over the engine range. On the compressor side, the operating point at cruise should sit inside the high-efficiency island, typically 70 to 75 percent, away from surge at low flow and choke at high flow. On the turbine side, the shaft speed must stay below its limit at rated power. A wastegate protects the turbine: when boost reaches the set value, the actuator opens the wastegate and bypasses exhaust around the turbine. Less exhaust through the turbine means less turbine power, so boost stops rising. The actuator can be a spring-and-diaphragm can or an electronic motor.

Anti-lag measures attack inertia and exhaust volume. A small turbine housing raises gas velocity at low flow, at the cost of exhaust backpressure at high flow. A twin-scroll turbine housing keeps the pulses from cylinders that would otherwise interfere; pairing cylinders so their exhaust events are evenly spaced gives each pulse a clear path to the wheel. Lightweight turbine and compressor wheels, and ball bearings instead of journal bearings, cut the rotating mass and friction. Variable geometry, covered in Chapter 4.7, changes the turbine nozzle area with speed.

Heat soak is the other habit. After a hard run, the turbine housing is hot. If the engine stops at once, oil flow stops, and the oil left in the bearing housing can cook into carbon, damaging the bearing and shaft seal. Idling for a minute or two lets oil carry heat away. Modern water-cooled bearing housings reduce this risk, but the habit is still useful.

Worked example

Question. A 2 litre four-stroke engine runs at 3000 rpm. Intake air density is 1.2 kg/m³. The compressor raises the air temperature by 62 K. Take cp for air as 1.005 kJ/kg·K. Exhaust mass flow is 0.062 kg/s, cp for exhaust gas is 1.15 kJ/kg·K, and the turbocharger mechanical efficiency is 0.65. Find the compressor power and the required exhaust temperature drop across the turbine.

  1. Swept volume per intake stroke = 2 × 10⁻³ m³. A four-stroke engine has one intake stroke per two revolutions, so at 3000 rpm there are 3000/2 = 1500 intake strokes per minute.
  2. Volume flow of air Q = 2 × 10⁻³ m³ × 1500 / 60 = 0.050 m³/s.
  3. Air mass flow m_a = density × Q = 1.2 kg/m³ × 0.050 m³/s = 0.060 kg/s.
  4. Compressor power P_c = m_a cp_a ΔT_a = 0.060 kg/s × 1.005 kJ/kg·K × 62 K = 3.74 kW.
  5. Turbine must supply more than this because of mechanical loss: P_t = P_c / η_m = 3.74 kW / 0.65 = 5.75 kW.
  6. Turbine power also equals m_exh cp_g ΔT_t. So ΔT_t = P_t / (m_exh cp_g) = 5.75 kW / (0.062 kg/s × 1.15 kJ/kg·K) = 80.7 K.

Answer. Compressor power is 3.74 kW, and the exhaust gas must drop by about 81 K across the turbine.

Exam traps

  • Lag and boost threshold get mixed up. Threshold is a speed below which no boost is possible; lag is a time delay at a speed where boost is possible.
  • The turbo does not run on air. It runs on exhaust enthalpy, which includes both pressure and temperature.
  • The wastegate bypasses exhaust around the turbine. It does not dump intake air, and it does not open the compressor side.
  • A large turbine housing lowers exhaust backpressure at high power but raises the boost threshold. A small housing spools quickly but can choke the top end.
  • Boost is often given in bar gauge while pressure ratio is absolute. At sea level, 1.0 bar gauge means pressure ratio about 2.0, not 1.0.

4.7 Variable geometry turbochargers and intercooling

A turbocharger that cannot change its turbine area is a compromise: small enough for low-speed response, or large enough for high-speed power, never both. A variable geometry turbocharger moves vanes or a nozzle ring to change the area while running. An intercooler then removes the heat that compression added, so the charge is denser at the same boost. Together they give strong low-speed torque, controlled high-speed boost and lower NOx. They appear in diesel cars, trucks, tractors and some petrol engines.

You need: Chapter 4.6 for pressure ratio, compressor heating and the turbine side; Chapter 4.5 for why boost adds air mass.

A nozzle that changes size

A fixed turbine has one inlet area. At 1200 rpm the exhaust flow is small, gas speed through the nozzle is low, and the turbine extracts little work. The compressor therefore makes little boost. At 5000 rpm the same area is too small. Exhaust backs up in the cylinder, pumping loss rises, and boost may exceed the limit. A variable geometry turbocharger (VGT) changes the turbine inlet area while the engine runs.

  • Moving vanes: small vanes pivot at the nozzle throat. Closed vanes give a narrow throat and high gas speed at low flow.
  • Sliding nozzle ring: an axial ring moves to cover or open the throat. It gives the same area change with fewer moving parts.
  • Actuator: a vacuum capsule or electric motor moves the vanes or ring. The ECU uses engine speed, fuel quantity, boost pressure and vane position to set the area.
  • Low speed: small area, high exhaust velocity, fast spool. High speed: large area, low back pressure, boost held at the map limit.

The result is boost at 1200 rpm that a fixed turbine cannot reach. In the figure the fixed unit is near 0.2 bar there, while the VGT is near 0.9 bar. The actuator is the weak point in cost and reliability. It lives near hot exhaust, so position feedback and self-calibration matter.

A wastegate is a valve that bypasses exhaust around the turbine to cap boost. It cannot make the turbine smaller at low speed, so it does not fix lag. VGT controls boost by area, so it often needs no wastegate. It also raises exhaust temperature and flow for exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), and it can close the vanes to create back pressure for an exhaust brake on a downhill run.

Intercooling: density after the compressor

Compression heats the charge. For a perfect gas, density ρ = p/(R T). The compressor raises p by the pressure ratio rp, but it also raises T, so density rises by less than rp. At pressure ratio 1.6 and compressor efficiency 0.70, the air leaves at about 362 K instead of 300 K. The density ratio is 1.33, not 1.60. An intercooler is a heat exchanger between the compressor and the intake manifold. It removes heat at nearly constant pressure.

ε = (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>3</sub>) / (T<sub>2</sub> − T<sub>coolant,in</sub>)

ε is intercooler effectiveness (0 to 1); T2 is compressor outlet temperature in K; T3 is intercooler outlet temperature in K; Tcoolant,in is cooling air or water inlet temperature in K. A value near 1 means the outlet is close to the coolant inlet temperature.

Air-to-air intercooler uses ram air through a front-mounted core. It is simple and needs no extra pump, but long pipes add volume and throttle lag. Air-to-water intercooler uses a coolant loop and a small radiator. It is compact and holds outlet temperature steady in traffic, but adds mass and a second cooling circuit. In a spark ignition engine the cooler sits before the throttle so only air passes through it. Placing it after the throttle is possible, but the extra volume hurts throttle response.

Intercooling lowers the charge temperature after the compressor. In an SI engine this reduces knock, so the compression ratio or boost can be raised. Volumetric efficiency improves because cold air is denser. Lower peak combustion temperature cuts NOx. Fuel consumption falls at the same power because the ECU can use more air and less fuel enrichment. In a diesel engine the gain is density and NOx control; very cold air can raise ignition delay and particulates, so the cooler is not made larger than needed.

4.8 Scavenging in two-stroke engines

A two-stroke engine has no separate exhaust stroke. The piston must clear burnt gas and fill the cylinder while the ports are open near bottom dead centre. Fresh charge is pushed in by the crankcase pump in small engines or by a blower in large ones. The port layout decides how much charge stays and how much escapes with the exhaust. This chapter defines delivery ratio, trapping efficiency and scavenging efficiency, then compares cross, loop and uniflow scavenging. These ideas appear in scooters, chainsaws, outboard motors and large marine diesels.

You need: two-stroke port timing from Unit 1 and the reason a blower raises intake density from Chapter 4.5.

Why two-stroke engines need scavenging

In a four-stroke engine a separate exhaust stroke removes burnt gas. A two-stroke engine has only a short overlap near bottom dead centre. Scavenging is the process of replacing burnt gas with fresh charge during that overlap. The fresh charge is air in a CI engine and air-fuel mixture in an SI engine.

Burnt gas starts to leave when the exhaust port opens because cylinder pressure is above atmospheric. The rest must be pushed out by fresh charge at a slightly higher pressure. Small engines use the crankcase as a pump: the piston downstroke compresses charge in the crankcase, and a reed valve or piston-port skirt controls entry. Larger engines use a separate blower, often a Roots blower.

D = Vdel / Vs

D is delivery ratio; Vdel is fresh charge delivered per cycle, in cm³; Vs is swept volume, in cm³.

η<sub>t</sub> = V<sub>tr</sub> / V<sub>del</sub>    and    η<sub>s</sub> = V<sub>tr</sub> / V<sub>ec</sub>

ηt is trapping efficiency; ηs is scavenging efficiency; Vtr is fresh charge trapped in the cylinder, in cm³; Vec is total cylinder volume at exhaust closure, in cm³.

Delivery ratio measures how much charge the blower supplies relative to the swept volume. Trapping efficiency measures how much of that delivered charge stays. Scavenging efficiency measures how much of the cylinder contents at exhaust closure is fresh charge. The three are linked by ηs = ηt × D × Vs ÷ Vec. When Vec is close to Vs, ηs ≈ ηt × D. Delivered volume is not trapped volume, because part of the fresh charge leaves with the exhaust.

Cross flow and loop scavenging

Cross scavenging uses transfer and exhaust ports on opposite sides. The transfer port faces the exhaust port. Fresh charge enters as a jet across the piston crown and pushes burnt gas ahead of it. The shape is simple and cheap. The same straight path lets fresh charge short-circuit: it can reach the exhaust port before it mixes with burnt gas. Short-circuiting wastes air and, in an SI engine, fuel and oil.

Loop scavenging, often called Schnuerle scavenging, angles two transfer ports toward the back wall. The two streams meet, rise along the rear wall, turn across the cylinder head, and sweep down toward the exhaust port. The loop gives better mixing and trapping than cross flow. It uses less air for the same output and has lower short-circuiting. Many small motorcycle and scooter engines use loop scavenging with piston-controlled ports.

A reed valve at the crankcase inlet opens when crankcase pressure falls below atmospheric and closes when pressure rises. A piston-port inlet uses the piston skirt to uncover the inlet. Both act as one-way valves for crankcase pumping.

Uniflow scavenging and comparison

Uniflow scavenging sends fresh charge in one direction. Inlet ports at one end of the cylinder are uncovered by the piston. Exhaust leaves at the other end through a poppet valve in the head or through ports uncovered by an opposed piston. The flow does not reverse or cross itself. Mixing with burnt gas is low and short-circuiting is small. Uniflow gives the best scavenging efficiency and the highest power per litre of the three layouts.

The hardware is the price. A uniflow engine needs a blower to supply charge above exhaust pressure, plus valve gear or a second crankshaft for opposed pistons. Large marine two-stroke diesels use uniflow with an exhaust valve and a blower. Small cheap engines stay with loop or cross scavenging because the extra hardware costs more than the fuel saved.

Worked example

Question. A two-stroke cylinder has a swept volume of 300 cm³. The blower delivers 240 cm³ of fresh charge per cycle measured at outside conditions. Trapping efficiency is 0.80. At exhaust port closure the cylinder holds 300 cm³ of mixture. Find the trapped fresh charge, the scavenging efficiency and the delivery ratio.

  1. Given Vs = 300 cm³, Vdel = 240 cm³, ηt = 0.80, and Vec = 300 cm³.
  2. Trapped fresh charge Vtr = ηt × Vdel = 0.80 × 240 cm³ = 192 cm³.
  3. Scavenging efficiency ηs = Vtr / Vec = 192 cm³ / 300 cm³ = 0.64.
  4. Delivery ratio D = Vdel / Vs = 240 cm³ / 300 cm³ = 0.80.
  5. Check: ηt × D = 0.80 × 0.80 = 0.64, which equals ηs because Vec = Vs.

Answer. The trapped fresh charge is 192 cm³, the scavenging efficiency is 0.64 (64 percent), and the delivery ratio is 0.80.

Exam traps

  • Delivery ratio, trapping efficiency and scavenging efficiency are three different numbers. Delivered volume is not trapped volume, because part of the fresh charge leaves with the exhaust.
  • Scavenging efficiency uses the cylinder contents at exhaust closure, not the swept volume, unless the question says the two volumes are equal.
  • Cross scavenging is not loop scavenging. Cross flow has opposite ports and a straight path, so short-circuiting is high; loop flow uses angled transfer streams that meet and turn around the cylinder wall.
  • Uniflow does not remove the need for a pressure difference. It still needs a blower or pump to drive fresh charge from the inlet end to the exhaust end.
  • In a crankcase-scavenged SI engine, short-circuited charge carries fuel and lubricating oil, so the lost charge is not just lost air.

Check yourself

Remember Define delivery ratio and write its formula. 2 marks

Delivery ratio is the fresh charge delivered per cycle divided by the swept volume: D = Vdel / Vs.

Remember Name the two hardware arrangements used to obtain uniflow scavenging. 2 marks

An exhaust poppet valve in the cylinder head, or an opposed piston that uncovers exhaust ports at the other end.

Understand Why does cross scavenging short-circuit more than loop scavenging? 3 marks

In cross scavenging the transfer port faces the exhaust port, so fresh charge can travel straight across and reach the exhaust before it mixes. In loop scavenging the transfer streams meet and turn along the rear wall, giving a longer path and better mixing.

Understand Why does a crankcase-scavenged two-stroke need a reed valve or a piston-port valve? 3 marks

The crankcase acts as a pump. The valve lets fresh charge enter the crankcase when pressure is low and closes when the piston compresses the crankcase charge, so charge goes to the cylinder instead of back out of the inlet.

Apply A 250 cm³ two-stroke engine delivers 200 cm³ of fresh charge per cycle. Trapping efficiency is 0.75 and the cylinder volume at exhaust closure is 250 cm³. Find the trapped fresh charge, the scavenging efficiency and the delivery ratio. 4 marks

Vtr = 0.75 × 200 = 150 cm³. ηs = 150 / 250 = 0.60. D = 200 / 250 = 0.80.

Analyze Compare a small scooter engine using crankcase scavenging with a large marine diesel using uniflow scavenging. Consider short-circuiting, air used and hardware. 5 marks

The scooter has simple piston ports or a reed valve and no separate blower, but short-circuiting is higher and some fuel-air charge is lost. The marine diesel needs a blower, an exhaust valve and more hardware, but uniflow flow gives low short-circuiting, low air waste and high power per litre.

Analyze Explain why uniflow gives the best scavenging but is not common in cheap small engines. 4 marks

One-direction flow reduces mixing and short-circuiting, so trapping is high. But uniflow needs a blower plus valve gear or opposed pistons and two crankshafts. That cost is too high for a cheap scooter or chainsaw, where loop scavenging is good enough.

Takeaways

  1. Only about a quarter to a third of the fuel energy leaves an engine as brake power; the rest must be taken away as heat by coolant, oil and exhaust.
  2. Cooling must be controlled, not maximised: too little causes knock, pre-ignition and oil breakdown, while too much raises friction, wear and deposits and lowers efficiency.
  3. Liquid cooling ranges from a thermosyphon loop with no pump to a pressurised pump-driven system, and pressure is the cheapest way to lift the safe upper temperature.
  4. Friction power is a near-fixed tax on indicated power, so clean oil at the right pressure and the right viscosity returns real brake power.
  5. A lubricant is selected by its viscosity grade, viscosity index and additive package, not by how thick it feels between the fingers.
  6. Supercharging raises the air mass trapped per cycle, so a smaller engine can make the same power, at the cost of higher thermal and mechanical loading.
  7. A turbocharger runs on exhaust energy that would otherwise be wasted, so it is more efficient than a belt-driven blower, but that energy arrives late — that delay is turbo lag.
  8. Variable geometry and intercooling attack the turbo's weaknesses from two sides: the vanes fix low-speed boost, the intercooler recovers the density lost to compressor heating.
  9. In a two-stroke the port layout decides how much fresh charge stays in the cylinder; uniflow and loop scavenging lose far less charge than simple cross-flow.

Apply

Apply A four-stroke engine of 1.5 litre swept volume runs at 3000 rpm and gives indicated power 55 kW against brake power 40 kW. Find the friction power, the mechanical efficiency and the friction mean effective pressure. 12 marks

Apply In a test, 30% of the fuel energy goes to the coolant. Fuel flow is 5 kg/h of calorific value 44 MJ/kg and the coolant water rises 8 °C. Find the coolant flow required in kg/s. 10 marks

Apply A supercharger raises intake pressure from 1.0 bar to 1.6 bar absolute from 300 K. Treating compression as isentropic with γ = 1.4, find the delivery temperature and the density ratio. 12 marks

At constant pressure, density ratio = T₂/T₃ = 343/313 = 1.096; About 9.6% more air mass in the same volume; With the same air–fuel ratio, about 9.6% more fuel can be burnt, so roughly the same rise in power; Also lower intake temperature reduces knock tendency, allowing a higher compression ratio or more boost; Note the pressure drop across the intercooler and its effectiveness.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Explain turbo lag, its causes and the methods used to reduce it. Include variable geometry and intercooling in your answer. 14 marks

Analyze Compare cross-flow, loop and uniflow scavenging in two-stroke engines. Which gives the best scavenging efficiency and why? 12 marks

Glossary

Additive
A chemical blended into a base oil to give a property the base oil lacks, such as anti-wear or detergency.
Blowdown
The first rush of high-pressure burnt gas out of the exhaust port when it opens, before fresh charge arrives.
Boost pressure
The amount by which intake pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure, usually quoted in bar.
Coolant
The liquid used in a cooling system, normally water with ethylene glycol and corrosion inhibitors.
Dry sump
A lubrication layout in which oil is scavenged to a separate tank and pumped back under pressure to the engine.
Fin
A thin extended metal surface on an air-cooled engine that increases the area available for convection to air.
Flash point
The temperature at which oil vapour gives a momentary flash when a flame is applied; a low value warns of fuel dilution.
Forced circulation
Coolant circulation produced by a pump, so the flow rate is set by pump speed and not by temperature alone.
Friction power
The difference between indicated power and brake power, spent on rubbing contacts and on driving auxiliaries.
Heat balance
An account of where the chemical energy of the fuel goes: brake power, coolant, exhaust, friction and radiation.
Intercooler
A heat exchanger that cools air leaving the compressor before it enters the intake manifold.
Mechanical efficiency
The ratio of brake power to indicated power, η_mech = BP/IP.
Mist lubrication
Lubrication of small two-strokes by oil premixed with fuel, with no pump, filter or sump.
Pour point
The lowest temperature at which the oil will still flow under standard test conditions.
Radiator
A tube-and-fin heat exchanger that transfers heat from the coolant to the air stream.
Scavenging
The removal of burnt gas from a two-stroke cylinder and its replacement by fresh charge during the port overlap.
Supercharger
A mechanically driven compressor that raises the pressure and density of the air entering the engine.
Thermostat
A temperature-operated valve that keeps coolant away from the radiator until the engine reaches its working temperature.
Thermosyphon
Coolant circulation driven only by the density difference between hot and cold coolant, with no pump.
Turbo lag
The delay between a demand for power and the delivery of boost, set by rotor inertia and the time needed to build exhaust energy.
Turbocharger
A turbine–compressor pair on a common shaft, where exhaust gas drives the turbine and the compressor boosts intake air.
Variable geometry turbocharger
A turbocharger whose turbine inlet vanes move to match gas flow, giving high boost at low speed and safe boost at high speed.
Viscosity
The internal resistance of a fluid to flow, quoted in centistokes (cSt) at a stated temperature or as an SAE grade.
Viscosity index
A scale showing how much an oil's viscosity falls as temperature rises; higher index means less change.
Water jacket
The set of cast passages in the block and head through which liquid coolant flows around hot parts.
Wet sump
A lubrication layout in which the oil supply sits in a pan under the crankshaft and drains back to it by gravity.
