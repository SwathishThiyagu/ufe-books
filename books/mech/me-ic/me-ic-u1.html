1.1 History and classification of IC engines

An internal combustion engine (IC engine) burns its fuel inside its own cylinder. The hot gas pushes a piston, and a crank carries the work out to a shaft. Burning the fuel where the work is done removes the boiler and furnace of a steam plant, so the engine is small, light and starts in seconds; that is why it sits in scooters, cars, gensets and pump sets. Engines differ in a handful of independent choices: how the charge is ignited, how many strokes make a cycle, which ideal cycle it follows, how it is cooled, what it burns, how air is supplied and how the cylinders are arranged. This chapter gives the short history of those choices and the labels that follow from them.

You need nothing earlier in this book; school physics of pressure, volume and the crank is enough.

From steam to the IC engine: the names to remember

In a steam engine, coal burns in a separate furnace and the heat travels through boiler tubes into steam. Much of it escapes up the chimney, and the boiler is heavy. An IC engine puts the flame next to the moving part, so almost all the heat is released in the gas that does the pushing. An engine of this kind is light enough for a two-wheeler.

Examiners ask for the following people and dates, in this order:

  • Étienne Lenoir, 1860: the first IC engine sold in numbers. It burned coal gas, was lit by an electric spark, and did not compress the charge, so its efficiency was only a few per cent.
  • Alphonse Beau de Rochas, 1862: stated on paper that the charge should be compressed before ignition, in a four-stroke sequence.
  • Nikolaus Otto, 1876: built the first working four-stroke engine with compression. Almost every petrol engine still follows his sequence of intake, compression, power and exhaust; see four-stroke and two-stroke engines.
  • Dugald Clerk, about 1880: built a working two-stroke engine, with a separate pump cylinder that filled and swept the working cylinder, giving one power stroke every revolution.
  • Rudolf Diesel, patent 1892, working engine 1897: compressed air so strongly that it became hot enough to ignite fuel sprayed into it, so no spark was needed.
  • Felix Wankel, 1950s: the rotary engine, with a triangular rotor in place of a piston.

Compression is the step that made the IC engine efficient: Lenoir had none, Otto and Diesel built their engines round it.

Reciprocating and rotary

A reciprocating engine has a piston sliding in a cylinder, a connecting rod, and a crank that turns the back-and-forth motion into rotation. Nearly every road vehicle uses this layout, and the rest of this unit is about it. The part names are in engine terminology and components.

A rotary (Wankel) engine has a triangular rotor turning inside a curved housing. Each face of the rotor traps a pocket of gas and carries it through suction, compression, expansion and exhaust in one continuous turn. It is smooth and compact, but sealing the rotor tips against the housing is hard, so few cars use it now.

Some syllabi add more bases: the fuel supply (carburettor, port injection, direct injection), the valve location (side valve or overhead valve), the speed (low, medium, high) and the use (automotive, stationary, marine, aircraft). Each is again a separate question.

  • Ignition. In an SI engine a spark plug lights a fuel-air mixture prepared earlier. In a CI engine, fuel is sprayed into air that compression has made hot, and it ignites by itself; see working principle of SI and CI engines.
  • Strokes. A four-stroke engine needs two crankshaft revolutions per cycle; a two-stroke needs one. Small two-strokes use ports in the cylinder wall; large two-stroke diesels use an exhaust valve in the head with inlet ports in the wall.
  • Air supply. A naturally aspirated engine fills its cylinder at about atmospheric pressure. A supercharger (driven by the crankshaft) or a turbocharger (driven by an exhaust turbine) pushes in denser air, so more fuel burns in the same swept volume. This is taught with cooling and lubrication, in the unit on supercharging.
  • Fuel. Petrol, diesel, CNG, LPG and alcohol blends are all in mass production. Hydrogen IC engines are in trials and small-series production; most hydrogen vehicles on the road use fuel cells instead.

Where each type sits

Petrol with spark ignition: scooters, motorcycles, cars and small gensets. Petrol mixes with air easily and the spark fixes the moment of burning. Diesel with compression ignition: trucks, buses, tractors and irrigation pump sets, where the engine runs for long hours and fuel cost per kilometre matters. CNG runs city buses and autorickshaws; its high octane rating lets an SI engine use a higher compression ratio.

Two-strokes give one power stroke per turn with few parts, so they are cheap and lively, but some fresh charge escapes with the exhaust. Stricter emission rules moved Indian two-wheelers to four-strokes; two-strokes survive in chainsaws, outboard motors and very large ship engines. Air cooling suits motorcycles and small gensets; water cooling suits cars, trucks and any large engine that must hold a steady temperature under load.

Worked example

Question. A scooter engine carries these particulars: fuel petrol, spark plug, two-stroke, cooling fins and no radiator, no blower or turbocharger, one cylinder of 150 cm³. (a) Classify it under seven headings. (b) How many power strokes does it make in 1000 revolutions of the crankshaft, and how many would a four-stroke cylinder make?

  1. Ignition: a spark plug lights a prepared mixture, so the engine is spark ignition (SI).
  2. Strokes: stated as two-stroke, so one power stroke per crankshaft revolution.
  3. Cycle: an SI engine adds heat almost at constant volume, so it follows the Otto cycle.
  4. Fuel: petrol. Cooling: fins and no radiator, so air-cooled.
  5. Air supply: no blower or turbocharger, so naturally aspirated. Cylinders: single.
  6. Two-stroke power strokes: 1000 rev × 1 power stroke per rev = 1000 power strokes.
  7. Four-stroke power strokes: one per 2 revolutions, so 1000 ÷ 2 = 500 power strokes.
  8. The 150 cm³ fixes the size only; it changes none of the seven labels.

Answer. SI, two-stroke, Otto cycle, petrol, air-cooled, naturally aspirated, single cylinder; 1000 power strokes in 1000 revolutions against 500 for a four-stroke cylinder.

Exam traps

  • Two-stroke is not two-cylinder. The label counts piston strokes per cycle, not cylinders; a single-cylinder two-stroke is common.
  • Diesel is a fuel and a cycle name, while compression ignition is a method. An ignition question wants the words SI or CI.
  • The history order is Lenoir (1860), Beau de Rochas (1862), Otto (1876), Clerk (about 1880), Diesel (1892 to 1897). Putting Diesel before Clerk, or crediting Otto with the two-stroke, loses the mark.
  • Supercharged and turbocharged both mean forced induction. A turbocharger is driven by exhaust gas; a supercharger is driven by the crankshaft through a belt or gears.
  • Air-cooled does not mean uncooled: fins, a fan or the rush of air past a moving motorcycle carry the heat away.

1.2 Engine terminology and components: functions and materials

Every engine is described with a fixed set of words: bore, stroke, crank radius, top and bottom dead centre, swept volume, clearance volume, compression ratio, piston speed and mean effective pressure. Every later calculation of power and efficiency is built from them. The same engine has a fixed set of parts, each with one job and a material chosen for that job. This chapter gives both lists, so later chapters can use the words and the parts without defining them again.

You need the reciprocating layout from history and classification of IC engines.

The geometry words

These terms describe one cylinder. Figure 1.2.1 marks each of them on the engine of the worked example.

  • Bore (d): the inside diameter of the cylinder, in mm.
  • Stroke (L): the distance the piston travels between its two end positions. The crank pin moves on a circle of radius a, the crank radius, so L = 2a.
  • Top dead centre (TDC): the piston position nearest the cylinder head, where the cylinder volume is smallest. Bottom dead centre (BDC): the position farthest from it, where the volume is largest.
  • Swept volume (Vs): the volume the piston sweeps from TDC to BDC. Engine displacement is Vs times the number of cylinders.
  • Clearance volume (Vc): the volume left above the piston at TDC; it holds the combustion chamber.
  • Compression ratio (r): the cylinder volume at BDC divided by the volume at TDC.
  • Connecting rod ratio: rod length l divided by crank radius a, usually 3.5 to 4.5 in car engines.

Vs = (π/4) d² L

d is the bore and L the stroke in the same unit; centimetres give cm³.

r = (Vs + Vc)/Vc. Here r is a pure number: the two volumes are in the same unit, so the unit cancels.

mean piston speed S̄p = 2LN

L is the stroke in m and N the speed in rev/s; the piston covers two strokes per revolution. Car engines run at 8 to 15 m/s at rated speed.

Mean effective pressure (mep) is the steady pressure that, acting on the piston over one stroke of every cycle, would give the same net work per cycle as the real, changing pressure: mep = Wnet/Vs, with W in J and Vs in m³ giving Pa. Because it divides out the size, it compares a small fast engine with a large slow one on a fair basis.

Why these materials

  • The piston reverses direction twice per revolution, so it must be light. Aluminium alloy is about one third as dense as cast iron, so the reversing force stays small; it also carries heat away from the crown quickly.
  • The crankshaft is twisted by each cylinder in turn and bent by the gas load, millions of times. Forged steel resists this fatigue, and the journals are ground and hardened for the bearings.
  • The block must be rigid, cheap and good at damping vibration. Cast iron does all three; an aluminium block saves weight but needs iron liners where the piston slides.
  • The exhaust valve runs red hot. Steel alloyed with chromium and silicon keeps its strength and resists scaling at that temperature.
  • Rings must press outward on the bore and still slide. Cast iron keeps its springiness when hot, and its graphite helps it slide.

The gap between piston and bore is set for the hot running state, because the aluminium piston expands more than the iron bore. A cold engine on a winter morning therefore makes a little piston slap that disappears once it warms up.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder engine has a bore of 80 mm, a stroke of 90 mm and a clearance volume of 62.8 cm³ per cylinder. Find the swept volume of one cylinder, the displacement, the compression ratio and the crank radius.

  1. Work in centimetres so the volume comes out in cm³: d = 8.0 cm, L = 9.0 cm.
  2. Swept volume: Vs = (π/4) d² L = 0.7854 × 64.0 × 9.0 = 452.4 cm³.
  3. Displacement: 4 × 452.4 = 1809.6 cm³, about 1.8 litre, the figure a brochure prints.
  4. Compression ratio: r = (452.4 + 62.8)/62.8 = 515.2/62.8 = 8.20.
  5. Crank radius: a = L/2 = 90/2 = 45 mm.

Answer. Vs = 452.4 cm³ per cylinder, displacement 1809.6 cm³ (1.8 L), r = 8.20, crank radius 45 mm.

Worked example 2

Question. The same engine runs at 3000 rev/min with an indicated mean effective pressure of 8 bar. Find the mean piston speed and the indicated power of the engine.

  1. Speed: N = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. Mean piston speed: S̄p = 2LN = 2 × 0.090 × 50 = 9.0 m/s.
  3. A four-stroke cylinder completes N/2 = 25 cycles per second.
  4. Work per cycle per cylinder: mep × Vs = 8 × 10⁵ Pa × 452.4 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 361.9 J.
  5. Indicated power: 4 cylinders × 361.9 J × 25 per s = 36 190 W = 36.2 kW.

Answer. Mean piston speed 9.0 m/s; indicated power 36.2 kW.

Exam traps

  • Compression ratio is not Vs/Vc. The charge starts in the whole volume at BDC, Vs + Vc, and ends in Vc; the slip gives 7.20 instead of 8.20 in the example.
  • A bore of 80 mm is 8.0 cm, not 80 cm. Put the bore and the stroke in the same unit before you square and multiply.
  • Stroke is twice the crank radius, not the crank radius.
  • Swept volume is one cylinder; displacement is all cylinders. Read which one the question asks for.
  • mep needs Vs in m³ to give pascals. Feed it cm³ and the answer is a million times too small.

1.3 Four-stroke and two-stroke engines

A four-stroke engine gives the piston four separate jobs (intake, compression, power, exhaust) and needs two crankshaft revolutions for one cycle. A two-stroke engine does the gas exchange near the bottom of the stroke, through ports in the cylinder wall, and finishes a cycle in one revolution. The choice decides how often each cylinder fires, how much fuel escapes unburnt and how the engine is built. Both SI and CI engines are made in both forms, so this chapter covers four engines: four-stroke SI and CI, and two-stroke SI and CI.

You need TDC, BDC and the part names from engine terminology and components.

Four strokes, two revolutions

A stroke is one travel of the piston between TDC and BDC, which is half a crankshaft revolution, or 180° of crank angle (°CA). Four strokes take 4 × 180° = 720°CA, two revolutions.

  1. Intake (suction): the piston moves TDC → BDC with the inlet valve open; the falling pressure draws in fuel-air mixture (SI) or air alone (CI).
  2. Compression: both valves closed, BDC → TDC. Pressure and temperature rise. Near the end, the spark fires (SI) or fuel is injected (CI).
  3. Power (expansion): the burning gas drives the piston TDC → BDC; this is the only stroke that gives work to the crank.
  4. Exhaust: the exhaust valve open, BDC → TDC; the rising piston pushes the burnt gas out.

The four-stroke SI and CI engines differ only in what enters in the intake stroke and how burning starts. Each cylinder gives one power stroke per 720°CA, and the flywheel carries the crank through the three idle strokes. In a real engine the valves open before and close after the dead centres; see valve and port timing diagrams.

Two strokes, one revolution: the crankcase-scavenged SI engine

The small two-stroke petrol engine has no valves. Three ports in the cylinder wall are opened and closed by the moving piston: the inlet port into the sealed crankcase, the transfer port from the crankcase to the cylinder, and the exhaust port from the cylinder to the silencer. The crankcase is the pump. Figure 1.3.1 shows the piston near BDC.

  1. Upward stroke (BDC → TDC): the piston first covers the transfer port and then the exhaust port, and compresses the charge above it. Below, the crankcase volume grows, its pressure falls below atmospheric, and fresh mixture flows in through the inlet port (or a reed valve). The spark fires just before TDC.
  2. Downward stroke (TDC → BDC): the burning gas drives the piston down, the power stroke. Below, the piston compresses the mixture in the crankcase. Near the end the piston edge uncovers the exhaust port first, and most burnt gas rushes out (blowdown). Then it uncovers the transfer port, and the compressed mixture flows from the crankcase into the cylinder and pushes out the rest of the burnt gas. This sweeping is scavenging.

Because exhaust and transfer ports are open together, some fresh mixture flows straight out of the exhaust (short-circuiting). That raw petrol is why the old two-stroke scooter was smoky and thirsty. A deflector on the piston crown (cross scavenging) or ports angled to loop the charge up and round the cylinder (loop scavenging) reduce the loss.

Two-stroke CI engines

A two-stroke diesel scavenges with air only, so short-circuited air wastes no fuel; fuel is injected after the ports close. It does not use crankcase compression, because the crankcase must hold the lubricating oil. A scavenge blower (often a Roots blower) or a turbocharger supplies air above atmospheric pressure to an air box round the cylinder.

In uniflow scavenging, air enters through ports all round the bottom of the liner and leaves through exhaust valves in the head, so it sweeps the cylinder in one direction. Large marine and power-station engines, the biggest IC engines built, work this way, at around 100 rev/min. Scavenging methods and their efficiencies are taught with supercharging, in the unit on cooling, lubrication and supercharging.

Which one, and why

At the same speed a two-stroke cylinder fires twice as often as a four-stroke cylinder: once per revolution against once per two revolutions. In theory it gives twice the power from the same size; in practice part of the stroke is lost to the ports, scavenging is imperfect and some charge escapes, so the real gain is well short of double.

Worked example

Question. A four-stroke engine runs at 3000 rev/min. Find the power strokes per second per cylinder and the time for one cycle. Repeat for a two-stroke engine at the same speed.

  1. Speed: N = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. Four-stroke: one cycle per two revolutions, so 50/2 = 25 cycles per second, that is 25 power strokes per second per cylinder.
  3. Four-stroke cycle time: 1/25 = 0.040 s = 40 ms.
  4. Two-stroke: one cycle per revolution, so 50 power strokes per second per cylinder.
  5. Two-stroke cycle time: 1/50 = 0.020 s = 20 ms.

Answer. Four-stroke: 25 power strokes/s, cycle 40 ms. Two-stroke: 50 power strokes/s, cycle 20 ms.

Worked example 2

Question. Two single-cylinder engines of 150 cm³ run at 5000 rev/min. The four-stroke has an indicated mep of 6 bar. The two-stroke, because of its ports and short-circuiting, has 4.5 bar. Find the indicated power of each.

  1. Work per cycle = mep × Vs. Four-stroke: 6 × 10⁵ × 150 × 10⁻⁶ = 90 J. Two-stroke: 4.5 × 10⁵ × 150 × 10⁻⁶ = 67.5 J.
  2. Cycles per second: four-stroke 5000/120 = 41.67; two-stroke 5000/60 = 83.33.
  3. Four-stroke power: 90 × 41.67 = 3750 W = 3.75 kW.
  4. Two-stroke power: 67.5 × 83.33 = 5625 W = 5.63 kW.
  5. Ratio: 5.63/3.75 = 1.5. With equal mep it would have been 7.5 kW, exactly double.

Answer. Four-stroke 3.75 kW; two-stroke 5.63 kW, 1.5 times as much, not twice.

Exam traps

  • A two-stroke cycle does have exactly two strokes, one up and one down. The point is that each stroke does two jobs: compression above and induction below on the way up, power and gas exchange on the way down.
  • A two-stroke fires once per revolution and a four-stroke once per two revolutions. “Twice as often” is per second at the same speed, not “twice per revolution”.
  • Do not double the two-stroke power blindly; port losses and scavenging losses keep the real gain well short of double (1.5 times in the second worked example).
  • Scavenging is not supercharging. Scavenging sweeps burnt gas out with fresh charge; supercharging raises the density of the charge.
  • Two-stroke diesels do not use crankcase scavenging; they use a blower, and the big ones use uniflow with exhaust valves.

1.4 Working principle of SI and CI engines

SI and CI engines run the same four strokes but start the burning differently. An SI (spark ignition) engine mixes petrol with air first, compresses the mixture and lights it with a spark. A CI (compression ignition) engine compresses air alone until it is hot, then sprays diesel into it, and the fuel ignites by itself. That one difference sets the compression ratio, the way load is controlled, the weight, the efficiency and the exhaust, and so decides why scooters and cars burn petrol while trucks, tractors and gensets burn diesel.

You need the four strokes from four-stroke and two-stroke engines and the compression ratio from engine terminology and components.

SI engine: the mixture is made before it is compressed

Petrol is volatile, so it is mixed with air before it reaches the cylinder: in a carburettor, by an injector in the inlet port, or (in direct-injection engines) early in the intake stroke. The cylinder therefore holds a ready mixture when compression starts.

  1. Intake: the piston draws in mixture; a throttle valve in the inlet sets how much.
  2. Compression to a ratio of about 8 to 12; pressure and temperature rise, but the mixture must not ignite by itself.
  3. Ignition: the spark plug fires 10° to 40° before TDC, because the flame front needs a few milliseconds to cross the chamber. The pressure peaks just after TDC.
  4. Power, then exhaust.

If the compression ratio is too high, the last part of the mixture (the end gas) is heated by compression and by the advancing flame until it ignites all at once before the flame reaches it. This is knock; it limits the compression ratio of an SI engine, and petrol is rated by its octane number, its resistance to knock. Knock is taught in the unit on combustion.

Load is controlled by the throttle, that is by the quantity of mixture, whose strength stays near the chemically correct ratio. At part load the nearly closed throttle makes the piston suck against a partial vacuum, and this pumping loss is why a petrol car is thirsty in slow city traffic.

CI engine: air is compressed, fuel is injected at the end

There is no spark plug and, usually, no throttle. The intake draws in air only, and a compression ratio of about 16 to 22 heats it. With ideal compression from 300 K, a ratio of 18 gives about 950 K; diesel fuel ignites by itself in air above roughly 250 °C (about 520 K), so the air is far hotter than it needs to be.

Near the end of compression an injector sprays diesel at high pressure as fine droplets. They evaporate, mix with the hot air and, after a short ignition delay of about a millisecond, ignite at many points at once; the rest burns as fast as it mixes. Injection starts about 5° to 25° before TDC.

Load is controlled by the amount of fuel injected, with the air flow almost the same at every load. This is quality control: the mixture strength changes, and there is almost no throttling loss. Diesel is rated by its cetane number, its readiness to ignite, the opposite of what petrol needs.

The high compression ratio gives a CI engine its better efficiency, but also peak pressures far above those of an SI engine (modern diesels exceed 150 bar), so the block, head, rods and bearings are heavier, and the engine runs at lower speed. Ideal cycles for both engines are compared in ideal air-standard cycles.

1.5 Valve and port timing diagrams

A valve timing diagram shows when the inlet and exhaust valves of a four-stroke engine open and close, in crank angle degrees measured from TDC and BDC. Real engines do not open the valves exactly at the dead centres, because the gas has inertia and needs time to start and stop moving; opening early and closing late fills and empties the cylinder better. A port timing diagram shows the same thing for the ports of a two-stroke engine. Both are drawn as a circle that the crank sweeps, and both are standard exam sketches.

You need the four strokes and the three ports from four-stroke and two-stroke engines.

Reading a valve timing diagram

Draw a circle with TDC at the top and BDC at the bottom, and let the crank turn clockwise. A four-stroke cycle needs two turns, 720°CA, so the diagram is a spiral of two turns: the intake stroke runs TDC → BDC on the first turn, compression BDC → TDC, power TDC → BDC on the second turn and exhaust BDC → TDC. Angles are crank degrees, not cam degrees: the camshaft turns at half crank speed.

  • Inlet valve opens (IVO) before TDC, by the inlet lead, so it is already open when the piston starts down.
  • Inlet valve closes (IVC) after BDC, by the inlet lag: the moving column of charge keeps flowing in, the ram effect, even though the piston has begun to rise.
  • Exhaust valve opens (EVO) before BDC, by the exhaust lead: the burnt gas, still at 3 to 5 bar, rushes out (blowdown), so the piston need not push against high pressure on the exhaust stroke.
  • Exhaust valve closes (EVC) after TDC, by the exhaust lag: the outgoing gas keeps flowing and drags burnt gas out of the clearance space.

Inlet open period = inlet lead + 180° + inlet lag. Exhaust open period = exhaust lead + 180° + exhaust lag. Valve overlap = inlet lead + exhaust lag: the few degrees around TDC when both valves are open, so the outgoing exhaust helps draw in the fresh charge. The spark (SI) or the start of injection (CI) is marked on the same diagram near the end of compression, but it is not a valve event.

SI and CI valve timing compared

Both engines use the same four events in the same order; only the size of the angles and the reasons differ. Typical values (they vary with design and speed):

An SI engine is throttled. At idle the inlet manifold is at low pressure while the exhaust is at about atmospheric, so a large overlap lets burnt gas flow back into the inlet and the idle becomes rough. A high-speed SI engine still uses a fair overlap and a late inlet closing, because at high speed the gas inertia is large and they improve breathing.

A naturally aspirated CI engine has no throttle, but its high compression ratio leaves very little space between the piston crown and the valve heads at TDC, so the valves cannot stand far open there, and the inlet pressure is not above the exhaust pressure. It therefore uses a small overlap. A turbocharged diesel, whose inlet pressure is above its exhaust pressure, uses a larger overlap so that clean air scavenges the clearance volume and cools the exhaust valve.

Port timing in a two-stroke engine

In a piston-ported engine the piston edge is the valve, so each port opens and closes at angles fixed by the height of the port in the cylinder wall. The exhaust and transfer ports are uncovered as the piston nears BDC and covered as it rises, so their events are symmetric about BDC: the exhaust port opens, say, 60° before BDC and closes 60° after BDC. The exhaust port sits higher than the transfer port, so it opens first and closes last.

A piston-controlled inlet port is uncovered by the piston skirt as the piston nears TDC, so its events are symmetric about TDC. Reed valves and rotary valves make the inlet timing unsymmetric, which helps at low speed.

Two periods matter. The blowdown period, from exhaust opening to transfer opening, lets the cylinder pressure fall below the crankcase pressure, so fresh charge does not flow back into the crankcase. The period from transfer closing to exhaust closing is the price of symmetric ports: the exhaust stays open with no fresh charge entering, so some charge escapes. Port timing cannot be changed on a running engine; tuners change it by raising or lowering the port edges.

Worked example

Question. A four-stroke SI engine has the inlet valve opening 10° before TDC and closing 40° after BDC, and the exhaust valve opening 45° before BDC and closing 10° after TDC. Find the inlet open period, the exhaust open period and the overlap, and the duration of the overlap at 3000 rev/min.

  1. Inlet open period = lead + 180° + lag = 10° + 180° + 40° = 230°CA.
  2. Exhaust open period = 45° + 180° + 10° = 235°CA.
  3. Overlap = inlet lead + exhaust lag = 10° + 10° = 20°CA, from 10° before to 10° after TDC.
  4. At 3000 rev/min the crank turns 50 × 360 = 18 000° per second, so 1° takes 1/18 000 s = 0.0556 ms.
  5. Overlap time = 20 × 0.0556 = 1.11 ms.

Answer. Inlet 230°CA, exhaust 235°CA, overlap 20°CA, which lasts 1.11 ms at 3000 rev/min.

Worked example 2

Question.   • Overlap is already inside the inlet and exhaust periods; do not add it again.
  • Inlet lead is measured from TDC but inlet lag from BDC; likewise exhaust lead from BDC and lag from TDC. Mixing the reference points gives wrong periods.
  • Do not write that CI engines never have overlap. Naturally aspirated diesels use a small one; turbocharged diesels a larger one.
  • A piston-ported two-stroke diagram is symmetric about BDC for exhaust and transfer. Do not draw it with unequal lead and lag as for a four-stroke.

1.6 Firing order, valve layouts and valve actuation

A multi-cylinder engine fires its cylinders one after another in a fixed sequence, the firing order, so that the power strokes come at equal intervals and the crankshaft is not hammered from one end. Two related choices decide how the gas gets in and out: the valve layout, which says where the valves sit, and the valve actuation, which says how the cam pushes them open. Together they set how smoothly an engine runs, how well it breathes and how much service it needs.

You need the four strokes and the 720°CA cycle from four-stroke and two-stroke engines.

Firing interval and firing order

The cylinders are numbered from the front (timing-gear or fan end), and cylinder 1 is the reference. In a four-stroke engine each cylinder fires once in 720°CA, so for equal spacing the angle between successive firings, the firing interval, is:

θf = 720°/n

n is the number of cylinders and θf is in crank degrees. A two-stroke cycle lasts 360°, so there θf = 360°/n.

An inline four has θf = 180°. Its crankshaft is flat: throws 1 and 4 point one way and throws 2 and 3 the opposite way, so pistons 1 and 4 reach TDC together, and 180° later pistons 2 and 3 do. Of each pair, one is at the end of compression and fires, while the other is at the end of exhaust.

After cylinder 1 fires, the next TDC belongs to pair 2-3, so either 2 or 3 fires next; then 4 must fire, because 1 has just finished its power stroke and is on exhaust. Only two orders are possible: 1-3-4-2 (the usual one) and 1-2-4-3. The pair 1 and 4 fires in the same order in both; only the pair 2 and 3 changes places. The order 1-2-3-4 is impossible on this crankshaft: the third firing comes at 360°, when only pistons 1 and 4 are at TDC, so it must be cylinder 4.

An inline six (θf = 120°) usually fires 1-5-3-6-2-4, which alternates between the front and rear halves of the engine.

Why the order matters

  • Balance and torque: equal intervals give a power pulse every 180° in a four, so the torque on the shaft is steadier and the flywheel can be lighter.
  • Bearing load: consecutive firings are kept apart along the shaft (1 then 3, not 1 then 2), so neighbouring main bearings are not loaded by two successive power strokes.
  • Torsional vibration: alternating between the two ends of the shaft reduces the twisting of a long crankshaft.
  • Breathing: the order decides which exhaust pulses meet in the manifold, and so how well each cylinder empties and fills.

If the spark plug leads or injector pipes are fitted in the wrong order, some cylinders fire at the end of exhaust instead of compression, and the engine runs badly or will not start.

The layout says where the valves sit. Four lettered layouts are named after the shape of the cylinder and chamber in section.

Valve actuation

Each valve opens once per cycle, that is once in two crank revolutions, so the camshaft of a four-stroke engine turns at half crank speed: Ncam = Ncrank/2. It is driven by gears, a roller chain or a toothed belt.

  1. A cam lobe lifts a follower (tappet).
  2. In an OHV engine the follower lifts a pushrod, which tilts a rocker arm, which pushes the valve open.
  3. In an OHC engine the lobe presses on a bucket tappet over the valve, or on a finger follower.
  4. A valve spring closes the valve and keeps the follower on the cam.

A small tappet clearance is left in the train so the valve still closes fully when the stem grows with heat; a hydraulic lifter, an oil-filled plunger, takes up that clearance automatically. Pushrods add moving mass, which limits engine speed; overhead camshafts remove them and allow higher speed. A broken timing belt usually lets the pistons strike the open valves.

1.7 Ideal air-standard cycles and their comparison

An air-standard cycle replaces the burning of fuel by heat added from outside, and the exhaust by heat rejected, to a fixed mass of air treated as an ideal gas. It gives the highest efficiency an engine following that sequence of processes could reach, and lets us compare engine types on equal terms. The Otto cycle adds heat at constant volume (the SI engine), the Diesel cycle at constant pressure (the slow CI engine) and the dual cycle partly at each (the fast CI engine). Which cycle comes out best depends on what is held equal, and that comparison is the favourite long question of this chapter.

You need the compression ratio from engine terminology and components and the SI and CI processes from working principle of SI and CI engines.

Air-standard assumptions

  • The working fluid is a fixed mass of air, an ideal gas (pv = RT, R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K), in a closed cycle.
  • Specific heats are constant: cp = 1.005 and cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K, γ = cp/cv = 1.4.
  • Combustion is replaced by heat added from a hot source, and the exhaust and intake by heat rejected at constant volume.
  • Compression and expansion are isentropic: no friction and no heat transfer to the walls.

The efficiency so found is called the air-standard efficiency. A real engine always reaches less; fuel-air cycles and actual cycle analysis explains how much less and why.

Otto cycle: heat at constant volume

  • 1→2 isentropic compression from v₁ to v₂ = v₁/r;
  • 2→3 heat added at constant volume, qin = cv(T₃ − T₂);
  • 3→4 isentropic expansion back to v₁;
  • 4→1 heat rejected at constant volume, qout = cv(T₄ − T₁).

Since T₂/T₁ = T₃/T₄ = rγ−1, the efficiency 1 − qout/qin simplifies to:

ηOtto = 1 − 1/rγ−1

r is the compression ratio and γ = 1.4 for air. Only r appears: the heat added and the temperatures cancel.

With the pressure ratio α = p₃/p₂, the mean effective pressure is mep = p₁ r (rγ−1 − 1)(α − 1)/[(γ − 1)(r − 1)]. Some syllabi call this the constant-volume cycle.

Diesel and dual cycles

The Diesel cycle adds heat at constant pressure, 2→3, while the piston moves out; heat addition stops at the cut-off ratio ρ = v₃/v₂. Then qin = cp(T₃ − T₂) and

ηDiesel = 1 − (1/rγ−1) × (ργ − 1)/[γ(ρ − 1)]

The bracket (ργ − 1)/[γ(ρ − 1)] is greater than 1 for every ρ > 1, so at the same r the Diesel cycle is less efficient than the Otto cycle, and it gets worse as the cut-off ratio grows (more fuel, more load).

The dual (limited-pressure) cycle adds heat first at constant volume, 2→3, with α = p₃/p₂, and then at constant pressure, 3→4, with the cut-off ratio ρ = v₄/v₃; expansion is 4→5 and rejection 5→1. Its efficiency is

ηdual = 1 − (1/rγ−1) × (αργ − 1)/[(α − 1) + γα(ρ − 1)]

Put ρ = 1 and it becomes the Otto formula; put α = 1 and it becomes the Diesel formula.

Comparing the three cycles

Draw the three cycles on a T-s diagram from the same state 1. Each adds heat qin and rejects heat qout along the same constant-volume line from v₁, and η = 1 − qout/qin. The cycle that rejects least heat for the same heat in is the most efficient.

Held equalEfficiency orderWhy
Compression ratio and heat inputOtto > dual > DieselOtto adds all its heat at the smallest volume, so it expands through the largest ratio and rejects least heat
Maximum pressure and heat inputDiesel > dual > Ottothe pressure limit lets the Diesel cycle use a much higher compression ratio
Maximum pressure and maximum temperatureDiesel > dual > Ottosame reason; this is the limit set by the strength and the materials of the engine

The second and third rows are the fair comparison of real engines, because an engine is limited by the peak pressure its parts can carry, not by its compression ratio. That is why a CI engine, though it follows the “less efficient” cycle at equal r, is the more efficient engine. The two worked examples show it with numbers: the Diesel cycle at r = 16 reaches almost the same peak pressure as the Otto cycle at r = 8, and is about 5 points more efficient.

Worked example

Question. An Otto cycle has a compression ratio of 8. At the start of compression the air is at 1 bar and 300 K, and the maximum temperature is 1800 K. Take γ = 1.4, cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K and R = 0.287 kJ/kg·K. Find the pressure and temperature at each corner, the heat added, the net work, the efficiency and the mean effective pressure.

  1. State 2: T₂ = T₁ r0.4 = 300 × 2.297 = 689.2 K; p₂ = p₁ r1.4 = 1 × 18.38 = 18.38 bar.
  2. State 3: constant volume, so p₃ = p₂ T₃/T₂ = 18.38 × 1800/689.2 = 48.0 bar.
  3. State 4: T₄ = T₃/r0.4 = 1800/2.297 = 783.5 K; p₄ = p₃/r1.4 = 48.0/18.38 = 2.61 bar.
  4. Heat added: qin = 0.718 × (1800 − 689.2) = 797.5 kJ/kg. Heat rejected: qout = 0.718 × (783.5 − 300) = 347.1 kJ/kg.
  5. Net work: w = 797.5 − 347.1 = 450.4 kJ/kg. Efficiency: 450.4/797.5 = 0.565, the same as 1 − 1/80.4 = 1 − 0.4353 = 56.5 %.
  6. Volumes: v₁ = RT₁/p₁ = 0.287 × 300/100 = 0.861 m³/kg; v₂ = 0.861/8 = 0.1076 m³/kg.
  7. mep = w/(v₁ − v₂) = 450.4/0.7534 = 597.8 kPa = 5.98 bar.

Answer. p₂ = 18.4 bar, T₂ = 689 K; p₃ = 48.0 bar; T₄ = 784 K, p₄ = 2.61 bar; qin = 797.5 kJ/kg, w = 450.4 kJ/kg, η = 56.5 %, mep = 5.98 bar.

Worked example 2

Question. A Diesel cycle has a compression ratio of 16 and a cut-off ratio of 2, starting from the same 1 bar and 300 K (cp = 1.005 kJ/kg·K). Find the peak pressure, the corner temperatures, the efficiency and the mep, and compare with the Otto cycle of the first example.

  1. State 2: 160.4 = 3.031, so T₂ = 909.4 K; 161.4 = 48.50, so p₂ = p₃ = 48.5 bar. This is the peak pressure.
  2. State 3: T₃ = ρ T₂ = 2 × 909.4 = 1818.9 K.
  3. State 4: T₄ = T₃ (ρ/r)0.4 = 1818.9 × (2/16)0.4 = 1818.9 × 0.4353 = 791.7 K.

Exam traps

  • Write the exponent: 80.4 = 2.297, not 8 × 0.4. Writing “80.4 = 2.297” loses the mark.
  • “Otto is more efficient than Diesel” is true only at the same compression ratio. At the same peak pressure the order reverses.
  • The cut-off ratio is v₃/v₂ in the Diesel cycle but v₄/v₃ in the dual cycle, because the dual cycle has an extra corner.
  • mep is net work divided by swept volume (v₁ − v₂), not by v₁; with kJ/kg and m³/kg the answer is in kPa.
  • Air-standard efficiency is not brake efficiency; it leaves out heat loss, friction and gas exchange.

1.8 Fuel-air cycles and actual cycle analysis

The air-standard cycle is a clean upper limit, but a real engine burns a fuel-air mixture whose specific heats rise with temperature and whose hot products dissociate. The fuel-air cycle keeps the ideal processes and adds these real properties of the working fluid; it explains why a real engine can never reach the air-standard figure even with perfect hardware. The actual cycle, read from an indicator diagram of a running engine, then adds the losses of the machine: finite burning time, heat loss to the walls, early exhaust opening, pumping and friction. Engineers use the three levels to see where the gap between the textbook efficiency and the measured one comes from.

You need the Otto cycle and its worked example from ideal air-standard cycles, and the valve events from valve and port timing diagrams.

Fuel-air cycle: what it adds

The fuel-air cycle keeps the air-standard processes (isentropic compression and expansion, instantaneous constant-volume burning in the Otto form, no heat loss, no friction) but uses the real working fluid. Four effects change the result:

  1. Variable specific heats. cp and cv of gases rise with temperature, and γ falls. The same heat then raises the temperature less, so the peak temperature and pressure are lower, and the lower γ reduces the work of expansion.
  2. Dissociation. Above about 1600 K some CO₂ and H₂O break back into CO, H₂, O₂ and OH, absorbing heat. Part of it comes back as the gas cools during expansion, but too late in the stroke to give full work. Dissociation cuts the peak temperature most near the chemically correct mixture.
  3. Change in the number of molecules. Burning changes the number of moles; for petrol the products have a few per cent more moles than the reactants, which raises the pressure slightly.
  4. Composition of the charge. The fresh charge mixes with residual burnt gas left from the previous cycle, and its fuel content (the mixture strength) sets how much heat is released.

The result is that the fuel-air cycle efficiency is well below the air-standard value at the same compression ratio; for a chemically correct petrol mixture it is typically about four fifths of it. Values for each compression ratio and mixture strength are read from fuel-air charts.

Effect of mixture strength and compression ratio

The mixture strength is measured by the equivalence ratio φ, the actual fuel-air ratio divided by the chemically correct one (φ = 1 correct, φ > 1 rich, φ < 1 lean).

  • Efficiency rises as the mixture is made leaner: a lean mixture burns cooler, so specific heats stay lower and there is little dissociation. A rich mixture wastes fuel that finds no oxygen.
  • Power is highest with a slightly rich mixture, about φ = 1.1 to 1.2: all the air is used, and the excess fuel cools the charge.
  • Raising the compression ratio raises the fuel-air efficiency, as in the air-standard cycle, and the ratio of fuel-air to air-standard efficiency stays roughly the same.

This is why an SI engine is set slightly rich for full power and leaner for cruising economy.

Actual cycle: the losses read from the indicator diagram

An indicator diagram records cylinder pressure against volume in a running engine. Figure 1.8.1 lays it over the ideal Otto cycle; the gap between them is made of these losses:

  • Time loss: burning takes 40° to 60° of crank angle, not zero, so the spark is advanced and part of the pressure rise happens while the piston is still compressing and part after it has started down. The top of the diagram is rounded and lower.
  • Heat loss to the cylinder walls, head and piston during combustion and expansion lowers the expansion line. Cooling water carries roughly a quarter to a third of the fuel energy away.
  • Exhaust blowdown loss: the exhaust valve opens before BDC, so the corner at the end of expansion is cut off; some work is lost to cut the pumping work of the exhaust stroke.
  • Pumping loss: drawing in the charge below atmospheric pressure and pushing out the exhaust above it makes a small negative loop at the bottom of the diagram. It is largest at part throttle.
  • Incomplete combustion and leakage: some fuel leaves unburnt and some gas leaks past the rings.
  • Rubbing friction in rings, bearings and the valve train, and the power to drive the pumps, take work after the indicator diagram: they separate indicated from brake work.

With indicated work Wi (net area of the diagram), brake work Wb and fuel energy Qf per cycle: ηi = Wi/Qf, ηm = Wb/Wi and ηb = Wb/Qf = ηi ηm. The measurement of these powers is taught in the unit on engine testing and performance.

Worked example

Question. The Otto cycle of the air-standard worked example (r = 8, 1 bar and 300 K, qin = 797.5 kJ/kg) reached 1800 K with constant cv = 0.718 kJ/kg·K. Repeat the heat addition with a specific heat that rises with temperature, cv = 0.71 + 0.00019 T kJ/kg·K, keeping T₂ = 689.2 K and p₂ = 18.38 bar. Find the new peak temperature and pressure.

  1. Heat added at constant volume: qin = ∫cv dT = 0.71(T₃ − T₂) + 0.000095(T₃² − T₂²).
  2. Put in the numbers: 797.5 = 0.71(T₃ − 689.2) + 0.000095(T₃² − 475 000).
  3. Rearranged: 0.000095 T₃² + 0.71 T₃ − 1332.0 = 0, so T₃ = [−0.71 + √(0.5041 + 0.5062)]/0.00019 = (−0.71 + 1.0051)/0.00019 = 1553 K.
  4. Check: 0.71 × 864.0 = 613.4 and 0.000095 × (2 412 400 − 475 000) = 184.1; the sum is 797.5 kJ/kg.
  5. Peak pressure at constant volume: p₃ = 18.38 × 1553/689.2 = 41.4 bar.
  6. Compared with constant cv: 247 K cooler and 6.6 bar lower at the peak, because the mean cv over the heating is 797.5/864 = 0.923, not 0.718 kJ/kg·K.

Answer. T₃ = 1553 K and p₃ = 41.4 bar, against 1800 K and 48.0 bar with constant specific heat; the expansion work, and with it the efficiency, falls too.

Worked example 2

Question. A single-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine of 500 cm³ swept volume runs at 3000 rev/min. Its indicator diagram gives 380 J for the main loop and 18 J for the pumping loop per cycle. A brake test gives 290 J of shaft work per cycle, and it uses 0.0255 g of petrol (calorific value 44 MJ/kg) per cycle. Find the indicated mep, the indicated, mechanical and brake efficiencies, and the friction power.

  1. Net indicated work: Wi = 380 − 18 = 362 J (the pumping loop is negative work).
  2. Indicated mep = Wi/Vs = 362/(500 × 10⁻⁶) = 724 000 Pa = 7.24 bar.
  3. Fuel energy per cycle: Qf = 0.0255 × 10⁻³ kg × 44 × 10⁶ J/kg = 1122 J.
  4. ηi = 362/1122 = 32.3 %; ηm = 290/362 = 80.1 %; ηb = 290/1122 = 25.8 % (check: 0.323 × 0.801 = 0.258).
  5. Cycles per second: 3000/120 = 25. Indicated power 362 × 25 = 9.05 kW; brake power 290 × 25 = 7.25 kW; friction power 9.05 − 7.25 = 1.80 kW.
  6. Against the air-standard 56.5 % at r = 8, the engine reaches 32.3/56.5 = 0.57 of the ideal in indicated terms.

Answer. imep 7.24 bar; ηi 32.3 %, ηm 80.1 %, ηb 25.8 %; friction power 1.80 kW.

Exam traps

  • The fuel-air cycle is still ideal: it has no friction, no heat loss and no pumping. Those belong to the actual cycle.
  • Variable specific heat lowers the peak temperature for the same heat input; it does not raise it. The specific heat goes up, so ΔT = q/cv goes down.
  • Maximum power and maximum efficiency do not come at the same mixture: power slightly rich, efficiency lean.
  • The pumping loop is subtracted from the main loop to get net indicated work, not added.
  • Indicated efficiency is before friction; brake efficiency is ηi × ηm. Percentage points are not per cent: 56.5 % to 32.3 % is a fall of 24.2 points, or 43 per cent of the ideal value.

Takeaways

  1. An IC engine converts chemical energy of fuel into work by burning the fuel inside the cylinder, and its types follow from separate choices such as ignition, strokes, cooling and aspiration.
  2. Bore, stroke, TDC, BDC and compression ratio form the fixed vocabulary for every later calculation on power, efficiency and size.
  3. A four-stroke engine gives one power stroke in two revolutions; a two-stroke engine gives one per revolution but needs port-based scavenging.
  4. SI engines need a spark and a premixed charge; CI engines inject fuel into hot air and depend on autoignition and a high compression ratio.
  5. Valve and port timing diagrams fix the real opening and closing of gas passages, so lead, lag and overlap explain why an engine breathes better than the simple cycle suggests.
  6. Firing order and valve layout decide how smoothly power pulses reach the crankshaft and how the valves are driven.
  7. Otto, Diesel and dual air-standard cycles allow a fair comparison of efficiency at fixed conditions because combustion is replaced by heat addition.
  8. Fuel-air cycles and actual cycle analysis explain the gap between ideal efficiency and what the engine really delivers, through mixture strength, variable specific heats, gas exchange, heat loss and friction.

Glossary

Air-standard cycle
An ideal cycle in which air is the working fluid and combustion is replaced by heat addition from an external source.
Autoignition
Spontaneous ignition of the charge caused by high temperature and pressure rather than by a spark.
Bore
The inner diameter of the engine cylinder.
Brake power
Useful power measured at the crankshaft, after friction losses.
Clearance volume
The small volume left above the piston when it is at top dead centre.
Compression ratio
The ratio of total cylinder volume to clearance volume, written r.
Cut-off ratio
In the Diesel cycle, the ratio of volume after heat addition to volume before it, written ρ.
Dissociation
Breakdown of burnt gases at high temperature into simpler species, which absorbs heat and lowers peak temperature.
Firing order
The order in which the cylinders of a multi-cylinder engine fire.
Fuel-air cycle
An ideal cycle that accounts for a real fuel-air mixture and specific heats that vary with temperature.
IC engine
A heat engine in which fuel burns inside a closed cylinder and the expanding gases push a piston to produce work.
Indicated power
Power developed inside the cylinder, found from the indicator diagram.
Mean effective pressure
A fictitious constant pressure that, acting over one stroke, would give the same work as the real cycle.
Naturally aspirated engine
An engine that takes in air at atmospheric pressure without a compressor.
Port
An opening in the cylinder wall uncovered by the moving piston to admit or release gas.
Scavenging
The process of pushing out burnt gases and filling the cylinder with fresh charge.
Stroke
The travel of the piston between top dead centre and bottom dead centre.
Supercharger
A compressor driven by the engine that raises intake air density above atmospheric.
Swept volume
The volume displaced by the piston in one stroke, equal to (π/4)d²L for one cylinder.
Tappet clearance
The small gap in the valve train that allows for thermal expansion of valve parts.
Valve lag
The angle by which a valve closes after the piston passes TDC or BDC.
Valve lead
The angle by which a valve opens before the piston reaches TDC or BDC.
Valve overlap
The crank-angle interval during which both inlet and exhaust valves are open.
Volumetric efficiency
The ratio of actual air drawn in per cycle to the theoretical volume swept by the piston.
