1.1 History and classification of IC engines

An internal combustion engine burns its fuel inside its own cylinder. The hot gas pushes a piston or a rotor, and a shaft carries the work out. Burning inside avoids the pipe and chimney losses of a steam plant, so the engine is small, light and starts in seconds; that is why it sits in scooters, gensets and pump sets. No two engines are alike, so engineers sort them by a few independent choices: ignition, number of strokes, cooling, aspiration and fuel. This chapter gives the short history of those choices and the labels that follow from them.

You need nothing earlier in this book; school-level physics on pressure, volume and the crank is enough.

Four names that made the engine

In a steam engine, coal burns in a separate furnace and the heat travels through boiler tubes into steam. Much of it escapes up the chimney, and the boiler is heavy. An internal combustion engine (IC engine) puts the flame next to the moving part. Almost all the heat is released in the gas that does the pushing, so the engine is light enough for a two-wheeler.

Four people are asked for by name:

  • Étienne Lenoir, 1860: the first IC engine built in large numbers. It ran on coal gas, was lit by a flame, and did not compress the charge before burning, so its efficiency was very low.
  • Nikolaus Otto, 1876: compressed the charge first, then lit it, in four piston strokes. Compression is the step that makes an IC engine efficient, and almost every petrol engine still follows his sequence; see four-stroke operation.
  • Rudolf Diesel, patent 1892, working engine 1897: squeezed air so hard that it became hot enough to set fire to fuel sprayed into it, so no spark was needed.
  • Dugald Clerk, 1878: a two-stroke engine with a separate pump to fill and sweep the cylinder, giving one power stroke every revolution.

Reciprocating and rotary

A reciprocating engine has a piston sliding in a cylinder, a connecting rod, and a crank that turns the back-and-forth motion into rotation. Nearly every road vehicle uses this shape.

A rotary engine has no piston that stops and reverses. The Wankel engine has a triangular rotor turning inside a curved housing. Each face of the rotor traps a pocket of gas and carries it through suction, compression, expansion and exhaust in one continuous turn. It is smooth and compact, but sealing the rotor tips against the housing is difficult, so few cars use it now.

The two families are classified apart because the moving part, and the way the chamber volume changes, are different. Ignition, fuel and cooling labels apply inside both families. Part names are in Chapter 1.2.

Five independent labels

Classification is several lists, not one. An engine takes one label from each list, and the choices are independent: a two-stroke engine can be air-cooled or water-cooled, spark-ignited or compression-ignited. Changing one label does not force a change in another.

  • Ignition. In a spark-ignition (SI) engine a spark plug lights a mixture of fuel and air prepared earlier. In a compression-ignition (CI) engine, fuel is sprayed into air that compression has made hot, and it ignites by itself. See SI and CI working principle.
  • Strokes. A four-stroke engine uses four piston strokes and two shaft revolutions per cycle. A two-stroke engine uses ports and scavenging, and fires once per revolution. See Chapter 1.3.
  • Cooling. An air-cooled engine has fins on the cylinder and loses heat to air blown past them. A water-cooled engine has a coolant jacket around the cylinder and a radiator.
  • Aspiration. A naturally aspirated engine fills its cylinder at about atmospheric pressure. A supercharged or turbocharged engine uses a compressor to push in denser air, so more fuel burns in the same swept volume. See Unit 4, Cooling, Lubrication and Supercharging.
  • Fuel. Petrol, diesel, CNG, LPG, alcohol blends and hydrogen are all used in production engines.

Two more descriptions are often added: the number and layout of cylinders (single, inline, V, opposed) and the duty (steady genset, variable-speed vehicle). Neither is a stroke label. Two-stroke counts strokes per cycle; two-cylinder counts cylinders.

Where each type sits

Petrol with spark ignition: scooters, motorcycles, cars and small gensets. Petrol mixes with air easily and the spark fixes the moment of burning.

Diesel with compression ignition: trucks, buses, tractors and irrigation pump sets, where the engine runs for long hours and fuel cost per kilometre matters. CNG runs city buses and autorickshaws where a gas pipeline and filling stations exist; its high octane rating lets an SI engine use a higher compression ratio.

Two-stroke: small scooters, chainsaws, outboard motors and some gensets. Fewer parts and one power stroke per turn make it cheap and lively, but fresh charge escapes with the exhaust, so it is smoky; stricter emission rules moved most Indian two-wheelers to four-stroke.

Air cooling suits motorcycles, scooters and small portable gensets, where there is no radiator to service. Water cooling suits cars, trucks, tractors and any large engine that must hold a steady temperature under load.

Worked example

Question. A scooter engine carries these particulars: fuel petrol, spark plug, two-stroke, cooling by fins, no blower or turbocharger, displacement 150 cm³, one cylinder. (a) Classify it under five headings. (b) Name one vehicle use. (c) How many power strokes does this cylinder make in 1000 revolutions of the crankshaft, and how many would a four-stroke cylinder of the same size make?

  1. Fuel: the particulars say petrol.
  2. Ignition: a spark plug lights a prepared mixture, so the engine is spark-ignition (SI).
  3. Strokes: the engine is stated to be two-stroke, so it gives one power stroke per crankshaft revolution.
  4. Cooling: fins and no radiator or coolant pump, so it is air-cooled.
  5. Aspiration: no blower or turbocharger is fitted, so it is naturally aspirated.
  6. Use: a scooter, and equally a small motorcycle or a portable pump.
  7. Two-stroke power strokes: 1000 revolutions × 1 power stroke per revolution = 1000 power strokes.
  8. Four-stroke power strokes: one stroke per 2 revolutions, so 1000 ÷ 2 = 500 power strokes.
  9. The displacement of 150 cm³ and the single cylinder fix the size only; they change none of the five labels.

Answer. Petrol, SI, two-stroke, air-cooled, naturally aspirated; used in a scooter; 1000 power strokes in 1000 revolutions against 500 for a four-stroke of the same size.

Exam traps

  • Two-stroke is not two-cylinder. A single-cylinder two-stroke engine exists; the label counts piston strokes per cycle, not cylinders.
  • Diesel is a fuel and a cycle name, while compression ignition is a method. Most diesel engines are CI, but an ignition question wants the words SI or CI.
  • Air-cooled does not mean uncooled. The fins, a fan, or the rush of air past a moving motorcycle carry the heat away.
  • Supercharged and turbocharged both mean forced aspiration. In a turbocharger the compressor is driven by an exhaust turbine; in a supercharger it is driven by a belt or gears from the crankshaft.
  • A four-stroke cylinder fires once in two revolutions, not once per revolution. Halve the revolution count before you count power strokes.

1.2 Engine terminology and components: functions and materials

An engine is described with a small fixed set of words: bore, stroke, top dead centre, bottom dead centre, swept volume, clearance volume, compression ratio and mean effective pressure. Every later calculation — power, efficiency, torque — is built from these. The same engine has a fixed set of parts, each with one job and a material chosen for that job. This chapter gives both lists, so that later chapters can use the words and the parts without re-defining them.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for what an IC engine is, and school geometry for the area of a circle and the volume of a cylinder.

The geometry words every later formula uses

These six words describe the space inside one cylinder. Fix them now; later chapters assume them.

  • Bore (d): the inside diameter of the cylinder, in mm or cm.
  • Stroke (L): the distance the piston travels from its highest to its lowest position. Stroke equals twice the crank radius.
  • Top dead centre (TDC): the piston position closest to the cylinder head. Bottom dead centre (BDC): the position farthest from it.
  • Swept volume (Vs): the volume the piston sweeps in one stroke, from TDC to BDC.
  • Clearance volume (Vc): the small volume left above the piston when it is at TDC.
  • Compression ratio (r): the total cylinder volume at BDC divided by the total volume at TDC.

Vₛ = (π/4) d² L

Vₛ is the swept volume of one cylinder; d is the bore and L is the stroke, both in the same unit (cm gives cm³).

r = (Vₛ + Vᴄ) / Vᴄ

r is the compression ratio, a pure number with no unit; Vₛ is swept volume and Vᴄ is clearance volume, in the same unit.

MEP = Wₙₑₜ / Vₛ

MEP is mean effective pressure in Pa (divide by 10⁵ for bar); Wₙₑₜ is the net work per cycle in J; Vₛ is swept volume in m³. For a multi-cylinder engine use the total swept volume.

Mean effective pressure is a convenience. It pretends the varying gas pressure inside the cylinder is one steady pressure that would produce the same work in one stroke. It lets you compare a small fast engine with a large slow one on a fair basis, and it is the number an engine tester reports.

Why these materials and not others

Each material answers one hard demand.

  • The piston must be light, because it reverses direction twice per revolution. Aluminium alloy weighs about one third of cast iron for the same size, so the reversing forces stay small.
  • The crankshaft must be stiff and strong, because it carries the full torque of every cylinder at once. Forged alloy steel is used, and the journals are ground smooth for the bearings.
  • The block must be rigid, cheap and good at damping vibration. Cast iron does all three; aluminium alloy saves weight but needs steel liners where the piston slides.
  • The exhaust valve runs red hot and must not soften. Alloy steels with chromium and silicon keep their strength at high temperature.
  • Rings must press against the bore and still slide. Cast iron or steel keeps its springiness at working temperature.

The gap between the piston and the bore is set for the hottest running condition. A cold engine is therefore slightly noisy, and a Chennai engine started on a cool morning sounds different from the same engine after a highway run.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder engine has a bore of 80 mm, a stroke of 90 mm and a clearance volume of 62.8 cm³ per cylinder. Find the swept volume of one cylinder, the total displacement, and the compression ratio.

  1. Convert the lengths to centimetres so that the volume comes out in cm³: bore d = 80 mm = 8.0 cm, stroke L = 90 mm = 9.0 cm.
  2. Swept volume of one cylinder: Vₛ = (π/4) d² L = 0.7854 × (8.0)² × 9.0 = 0.7854 × 64.0 × 9.0 = 452.4 cm³.
  3. Total displacement for four cylinders: 4 × 452.4 = 1809.6 cm³, that is about 1.81 litre. This is the number a car brochure calls 1.8 L.
  4. Compression ratio: r = (Vₛ + Vᴄ) / Vᴄ = (452.4 + 62.8) / 62.8 = 515.2 / 62.8 = 8.20.
  5. Check the units: cm³ divided by cm³ leaves a pure number, so r = 8.20 has no unit.

Answer. Swept volume = 452.4 cm³ per cylinder, total displacement = 1809.6 cm³ (≈ 1.81 L), and compression ratio = 8.20.

Exam traps

  • Compression ratio is not Vₛ / Vᴄ. It is (Vₛ + Vᴄ) / Vᴄ, because the gas is squeezed into the clearance volume plus nothing else, but it starts from the whole cylinder volume.
  • Mixing millimetres and centimetres in (π/4) d² L. A bore of 80 mm is 8.0 cm, not 80. Square the bore in the unit you will use for the stroke.
  • Swept volume is one cylinder; total displacement is swept volume times the number of cylinders. The example gives both, and examiners often ask only one.
  • Stroke is twice the crank radius, not the crank radius. A common slip in balance and piston-speed sums.
  • Mean effective pressure needs swept volume in m³ to give pascals. Feed it cm³ and your answer is out by a factor of one million.

Analyze A student computes the compression ratio of the worked example as 452.4 / 62.8 = 7.20. State what went wrong, give the correct value, and say which engine runs hotter, r = 7.20 or r = 8.20. 5 marks

Analyze Explain why the clearance volume of a real engine can never be zero, and what would break if it were. 6 marks

The clearance space holds the valves, the spark plug or injector, and the gas at TDC. If it were zero, the piston would strike the head, the gas would be pushed out, and there would be no room for combustion to start. The compression ratio would be infinite and the pressure rise would destroy the parts. In practice a minimum clearance also covers thermal expansion of the piston and rod.

1.3 Four-stroke and two-stroke engines

A four-stroke engine gives the piston four separate jobs — intake, compression, power, exhaust — and needs two crankshaft turns for one cycle. A two-stroke engine does the same gas exchange with ports in the cylinder wall and finishes one cycle in a single turn. The difference decides how often each cylinder fires, how smooth the torque is, how much fuel it uses and how it is cooled. You meet it in a Chennai scooter, a generator set and a truck engine.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for engine classification, and Chapter 1.2 for TDC, BDC and stroke.

Four strokes, two turns

A stroke is the piston's travel from top dead centre (TDC) to bottom dead centre (BDC), or the reverse. In a four-stroke engine each stroke is one direction of piston travel, which is half a crankshaft turn, or 180°CA (crank angle degrees). Four strokes take 4 × 180° = 720°CA, which is two full crankshaft revolutions.

Intake stroke: the piston moves from TDC to BDC with the inlet valve open. Falling cylinder pressure lets fresh fuel-air mixture (spark-ignition, SI, engine) or air (compression-ignition, CI, engine) enter. Compression stroke: both valves are closed and the piston moves from BDC to TDC, squeezing the charge and raising its pressure and temperature.

Power stroke: near TDC a spark ignites the mixture in an SI engine, or a fuel injector sprays diesel in a CI engine. Hot gas pushes the piston to BDC and the crankshaft delivers work. Exhaust stroke: the exhaust valve opens and the rising piston pushes burnt gas out of the cylinder.

Each cylinder gives one power stroke per 720°CA. The separate exhaust stroke gives clean gas exchange, which is why four-stroke engines dominate cars and trucks. Valve opening is not exactly at TDC or BDC; see Chapter 1.5 for lead, lag and overlap.

One turn with ports

A two-stroke engine completes one cycle in one crankshaft revolution, 360°CA. It uses ports cut in the cylinder wall, opened and closed by the moving piston edge. A simple crankcase-scavenged engine has three sets of ports: intake (fresh charge into the crankcase), transfer (crankcase to cylinder) and exhaust (cylinder to atmosphere). The sealed crankcase acts as a pump.

Upward stroke: the piston rises from BDC. It first covers the transfer and exhaust ports, trapping the charge, then compresses it. At the same time the rising piston lowers the crankcase pressure, so fresh charge is drawn in through the intake port or a reed valve. The spark fires near TDC.

Downward stroke: the burning gas pushes the piston down, giving the power stroke. The exhaust port opens first and most burnt gas leaves. The transfer port opens next, and the compressed charge in the crankcase flows into the cylinder. This fresh charge sweeps the remaining burnt gas out; the process is called scavenging. The piston then covers the ports and compression begins again.

There is no separate intake or exhaust stroke. Scavenging arrangements include uniflow scavenging, where the fresh charge flows in one direction and an exhaust valve or port at the far end lets gas out, and loop scavenging, where the charge loops across the cylinder. Details are in Unit 4, Cooling, Lubrication and Supercharging. Port timing is drawn in Chapter 1.5.

Which one, and why

The central difference is the number of power strokes per revolution. A four-stroke cylinder fires once every two revolutions; a two-stroke cylinder fires once every revolution. At the same speed and displacement, the two-stroke has twice as many power pulses in theory, and the torque is smoother because pulses arrive more often.

f₄ = n / 120, f₂ = n / 60

f₄ and f₂ are power strokes per second per cylinder; n is engine speed in rev/min. Cycle times are t₄ = 120/n s and t₂ = 60/n s.

Real engines do not get double power. Two-stroke scavenging is imperfect: some fresh charge escapes with the exhaust, so fuel consumption and unburnt hydrocarbon emissions are higher, and thermal efficiency is lower. A four-stroke engine's separate strokes fill and empty the cylinder better, giving lower fuel use and lower emissions. The cost is more parts: valves, camshaft, and a heavier cylinder head.

Cooling demand is higher in a two-stroke because it fires twice as often at the same speed. Small two-strokes use air cooling with fins; car four-strokes use liquid cooling. Two-strokes suit mopeds, chainsaws and outboard motors, where low weight and simple construction matter. Four-strokes suit cars, trucks and generator sets, where fuel economy and emissions matter.

More than four strokes

A multi-stroke engine completes one cycle in k piston strokes, where k is greater than four. A six-stroke engine adds two strokes after exhaust: water is injected onto the hot cylinder to make steam, giving a second power stroke, and a final exhaust stroke clears the steam. The cycle angle is k × 180°CA; for six strokes it is 1080°CA, or three revolutions. The gain is recovery of exhaust heat. The cost is lower power per litre and extra hardware. More strokes per cycle mean fewer power strokes per revolution, so a multi-stroke engine is heavier and less powerful for its size unless heat recovery pays back. Other multi-stroke designs use extra strokes for better gas exchange. These engines remain in research and niche stationary use, not mass-produced cars.

Worked example

Question. A four-stroke engine runs at 3000 rpm. Find the number of power strokes per second per cylinder and the time for one complete cycle. Repeat for a two-stroke engine at the same speed.

  1. Convert speed: n = 3000 rev/min = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. Four-stroke: one cycle takes two revolutions, so cycle frequency = 50/2 = 25 cycles/s. Thus 25 power strokes/s per cylinder.
  3. Four-stroke cycle time = 1/25 = 0.040 s. Equivalently, 2/50 = 0.040 s.
  4. Two-stroke: one cycle takes one revolution, so cycle frequency = 50 cycles/s. Thus 50 power strokes/s per cylinder.
  5. Two-stroke cycle time = 1/50 = 0.020 s.

Answer. At 3000 rpm, the four-stroke cylinder gives 25 power strokes/s with a cycle time of 0.040 s; the two-stroke cylinder gives 50 power strokes/s with a cycle time of 0.020 s.

Exam traps

  • Two-stroke does not mean two strokes per cycle in the four-stroke sense. It means one power stroke every revolution, with ports and scavenging; calling it 'two strokes' hides the gas exchange.
  • Do not double the power of a two-stroke blindly. Twice the power strokes is an ideal count; scavenging losses and lower trapping efficiency reduce real output.
  • Four-stroke cycle = 720°CA = 2 revolutions. Two-stroke cycle = 360°CA = 1 revolution. Mixing these gives wrong cycle time and wrong power-stroke frequency.
  • Scavenging is not supercharging. Scavenging pushes burnt gas out with fresh charge; supercharging raises the incoming charge density. Supercharging is in Unit 4.
  • Ports are not valves. In a piston-ported two-stroke the piston edge opens and closes the ports; there is no camshaft or poppet valve for those ports, though uniflow may use an exhaust valve.

1.4 Working principle of SI and CI engines

Both engines burn fuel inside the cylinder, but ignite it differently. An SI (spark ignition) engine mixes petrol with air first, compresses the mixture, and lights it with a spark plug. A CI (compression ignition) engine compresses air alone until hot, then sprays diesel into it, and the fuel self-ignites. The four strokes stay the same. This difference sets compression ratio, load control, exhaust and typical use: petrol for scooters and small cars, diesel for trucks, tractors and generators.

You need Chapter 1.2 for bore, stroke, TDC, BDC and clearance volume, and Chapter 1.3 for the four-stroke sequence.

SI engine: the charge is mixed before it is compressed

SI means spark ignition. Petrol is the usual fuel. Air and fuel meet before the spark: in a carburettor, in the intake port by an injector, or early in the intake stroke. The cylinder therefore holds a premixed charge — fuel vapour spread through air — when compression starts. Hardware is in Unit 2.

Intake draws mixture; a throttle plate sets how much. Compression raises pressure and temperature. Near the end of compression the spark plug fires. A flame front starts at the plug and crosses the unburnt charge. Burning gas raises pressure, drives the piston down on the power stroke, and the exhaust stroke pushes gas out.

The flame takes milliseconds to cross the chamber, so spark is set slightly before top dead centre (TDC). This is spark timing. Too late and the burn finishes after the piston has dropped, wasting work.

Compression ratio is about 8:1 to 12:1. A higher ratio heats the end gas more. If it reaches autoignition temperature before the flame arrives, it burns suddenly and the engine knocks. The ratio is held below this knock limit (Unit 3).

Load is set by the throttle — the quantity of mixture admitted. At part load the plate is nearly closed and the piston works against a vacuum, wasting work as pumping loss. That is why a petrol car is thirsty in slow city traffic.

r = (V<sub>s</sub> + V<sub>c</sub>) ÷ V<sub>c</sub>

r is the compression ratio, a pure number; Vs is the swept volume in cm³ or m³; Vc is the clearance volume in the same unit.

CI engine: air is compressed, fuel is injected at the end

CI means compression ignition. There is no spark plug. Intake draws air only. Compression ratio is 16:1 to 22:1, about double SI. Compressing air heats it: starting near 300 K, a ratio of 18 gives roughly 900 to 950 K at the end of compression. Diesel self-ignites at about 250 °C, so this air is hot enough.

Near the end of compression the injector sprays diesel as a fine mist. Droplets evaporate and mix with air. After a short ignition delay the mixture autoignites at several points, and the rest burns as fast as it mixes. The injector delivers fuel; the spark plug delivers a spark. They are not interchangeable.

A CI engine has no throttle. Air flow stays almost the same at every load. Power is set by how much fuel is injected, so mixture strength changes. This is quality control against the SI engine's quantity control, and it avoids part-load pumping loss. That is why a diesel suits a bus or truck that stops and starts all day.

The high ratio raises ideal-cycle efficiency (Chapter 1.7), so a diesel usually beats a petrol engine of similar size on thermal efficiency. The price is high peak cylinder pressure, which needs heavier parts, plus nitrogen oxides (NOx) and smoke as the hard emissions (Unit 6). The fuels want opposite properties: diesel needs a high cetane number, meaning it ignites easily; petrol needs a high octane number, meaning it resists autoignition.

Exhaust differs too. An SI engine runs close to stoichiometric, so a three-way catalyst can clean carbon monoxide (CO), unburnt hydrocarbons and NOx together. A CI engine always runs lean, so CO and hydrocarbon levels are low but NOx and particulates are high, and a simple three-way catalyst does not work. That fact drives most diesel emission work.

Timing: spark and injection as events

Both timings are measured in crank angle degrees relative to TDC. Spark timing is where the plug fires, commonly 10° to 30° before TDC depending on speed and load. Injection timing is when the nozzle opens, commonly about 5° to 20° before TDC in a direct-injection diesel, and how long it stays open sets fuel quantity.

Spark too early gives high peak pressure and knock; too late gives low power and a hot exhaust. Injection too early lengthens ignition delay, so more fuel is ready when it finally burns and the engine runs rough with diesel knock; too late leaves unburnt fuel as smoke and wastes power. The maps and mechanisms belong to Unit 2.

Worked example

Question. An SI engine has a compression ratio of 9 and a CI engine has a compression ratio of 18. Both have a clearance volume of 50 cm³. Find the swept volume and the total cylinder volume of each engine.

  1. Write the definition: r = (Vs + Vc) ÷ Vc = 1 + Vs/Vc, so Vs = (r − 1) × Vc.
  2. SI engine: Vs = (9 − 1) × 50 cm³ = 8 × 50 = 400 cm³.
  3. CI engine: Vs = (18 − 1) × 50 cm³ = 17 × 50 = 850 cm³.
  4. Total cylinder volume at BDC is Vs + Vc. SI: 400 + 50 = 450 cm³.
  5. CI: 850 + 50 = 900 cm³.

Answer. The SI engine has a swept volume of 400 cm³ and a total cylinder volume of 450 cm³; the CI engine has a swept volume of 850 cm³ and a total cylinder volume of 900 cm³.

Worked example 2

Question. A four-cylinder CI engine has a bore of 10 cm, a stroke of 12 cm and a compression ratio of 19. Find the clearance volume of one cylinder in cm³. The number of cylinders does not enter this calculation, because bore, stroke and compression ratio are all per cylinder.

  1. Swept volume of one cylinder: Vs = (π/4) × d² × L, with d = 10 cm and L = 12 cm.
  2. Vs = 0.7854 × 100 cm² × 12 cm = 942.5 cm³.
  3. From r = 1 + Vs/Vc: Vc = Vs ÷ (r − 1) = 942.5 ÷ 18.
  4. Vc = 52.4 cm³.

Answer. The clearance volume of one cylinder is about 52.4 cm³.

Exam traps

  • A CI engine compresses air only. Writing that it compresses a fuel-air mixture is the commonest error.
  • Spark plug and injector are not interchangeable. One starts a flame in a ready mixture; the other creates the mixture by spraying fuel.
  • A diesel has no spark, but it does ignite. The fuel self-ignites in hot air.
  • Higher compression ratio is not automatically better. In SI it raises knock risk; in CI it raises peak pressure and NOx.
  • Keep load control straight: throttle controls air in SI (quantity control), injected fuel controls load in CI (quality control).

1.5 Valve and port timing diagrams

A valve timing diagram is a map of when the inlet and exhaust valves open and close, measured in crank angle degrees from top dead centre (TDC) and bottom dead centre (BDC). It exists because a four-stroke engine cannot breathe well if the valves open and close exactly at TDC and BDC. The same idea for a two-stroke engine is a port timing diagram. These diagrams guide cam design, tuning and fault finding on cars, motorcycles, gensets and pumps.

You need: four-stroke and two-stroke engines and working principle of SI and CI engines.

Reading a valve timing diagram

A four-stroke cycle takes 720° of crank rotation. In a valve timing diagram, 0° is TDC at the start of the intake stroke. The piston reaches BDC at 180°, TDC at 360°, BDC at 540° and TDC again at 720°. Angles are crank degrees (°CA), not cam degrees. The cam turns at half crank speed in a four-stroke engine.

The inlet valve does not open exactly at TDC. It opens before TDC by the inlet lead. It closes after BDC by the inlet lag. The exhaust valve opens before BDC by the exhaust lead and closes after TDC by the exhaust lag. Lead and lag use gas inertia: the exhaust needs a head start to blow down, and the incoming charge keeps moving after BDC.

Inlet open period = θ<sub>i,lead</sub> + 180° + θ<sub>i,lag</sub>; Exhaust open period = θ<sub>e,lead</sub> + 180° + θ<sub>e,lag</sub>; Valve overlap = θ<sub>i,lead</sub> + θ<sub>e,lag</sub>

θi,lead is inlet lead before TDC; θi,lag is inlet lag after BDC; θe,lead is exhaust lead before BDC; θe,lag is exhaust lag after TDC. All angles are in crank degrees. Overlap is the common open time around TDC.

Valve overlap is the short period when both valves are open. Near the end of exhaust, fresh charge can help push out burnt gas. Too much overlap at low speed can send fresh charge back into the inlet or let exhaust enter the cylinder. The diagram is used to check cam timing after assembly and to tune an engine.

SI and CI valve timing compared

Both SI and CI four-stroke engines use the same four events: inlet open, inlet close, exhaust open and exhaust close. The difference is in the size of the angles and the reason for them. An SI engine throttles the air-fuel mixture. At idle, the inlet pressure is low, so a large overlap can push exhaust gas back into the inlet. SI engines therefore use a small overlap at low speed. A CI engine has no throttle plate, so there is no air-fuel mixture to lose through overlap. But its high compression ratio leaves very little valve-to-piston clearance, and without a turbocharger the inlet pressure is not above the exhaust pressure. Naturally aspirated CI engines therefore use small overlap or none. Larger overlap is used in pressure-charged diesels, where boost pressure above exhaust pressure lets fresh air scavenge the clearance volume.

In an SI engine the inlet closes after BDC to use the ram effect of the moving mixture. In a CI engine the inlet also closes after BDC, but the trapped charge is only air; fuel is injected later. The exhaust valve opens before BDC in both engines. This blowdown, the early rush of burnt gas out of the cylinder, uses the remaining pressure to start the exhaust flow, so the piston does less pumping work on the upstroke.

Port timing in a two-stroke engine

A two-stroke engine often uses ports cut in the cylinder wall instead of valves. The piston itself acts as the valve. A port timing diagram shows the crank angle at which each port is uncovered and covered. The exhaust port opens first, about 60° before BDC in many small engines. The transfer port opens a little later. On the upstroke the transfer port closes first and the exhaust port closes last. This order lets burnt gas leave before fresh charge enters and keeps the exhaust open while the charge is transferred.

In a simple piston-port engine the exhaust and transfer ports open and close at equal angles around BDC, so their timing is symmetric about BDC. The inlet port, if it is piston-controlled, is near TDC and is symmetric about TDC. Reed valves and rotary valves can make the inlet timing asymmetric. A two-stroke CI engine uses air scavenging and a fuel injector, but its exhaust and transfer port timing is read the same way.

A port timing diagram is not adjustable like a camshaft. The port height and piston skirt set the timing. Changing a port height changes the open period and the compression ratio. For this reason, port timing is used in tuning small motorcycles, chainsaws and outboard motors; see Unit 4 for scavenging details.

Worked example

Question. A four-stroke SI engine has the inlet valve opening 10° before TDC and closing 40° after BDC. The exhaust valve opens 45° before BDC and closes 10° after TDC. Find the inlet open period, the exhaust open period and the valve overlap in crank degrees.

  1. Draw the 720° cycle. TDC at start of intake = 0°. BDC after intake = 180°. Use the inlet lead 10° and inlet lag 40°.
  2. Inlet open period = 10° + 180° + 40° = 230°CA.
  3. Exhaust opens 45° before BDC and closes 10° after TDC. Exhaust open period = 45° + 180° + 10° = 235°CA.
  4. Overlap is the common open time around TDC: inlet lead + exhaust lag = 10° + 10° = 20°CA. Count it once.

Answer. Inlet open period = 230°CA, exhaust open period = 235°CA, valve overlap = 20°CA.

Exam traps

  • Crank degrees, not cam degrees: in a four-stroke the cam turns 360° while the crank turns 720°. A 20°CA overlap is 10° of cam rotation.
  • Do not add overlap twice: it is the common open period around TDC, already inside the inlet and exhaust periods if you compute from lead and lag.
  • Before and after both add: inlet lead is before TDC and inlet lag is after BDC, so the inlet period is lead + 180° + lag.
  • Valve timing is not ignition timing: spark or injection near the end of compression is a separate event; do not mark it as valve overlap.
  • A simple two-stroke port diagram is symmetric about BDC for exhaust and transfer; do not copy the asymmetric cam lead and lag values into it.

1.6 Firing order, valve layouts and valve actuation

A four-cylinder engine does not fire all its cylinders at once. It fires them one after another in a fixed sequence, so the power pulses arrive at equal intervals and the crankshaft is not hammered to one side. That sequence is the firing order. Related choices fix where the valves sit — the valve layout — and how they are pushed open — the valve actuation. Together these three decide how smoothly an engine runs, how well it breathes, and how much service it needs.

You need the four-stroke cycle from Chapter 1.3 and the terms lead, lag and overlap from Chapter 1.5.

Firing order and firing interval

The firing order is the sequence in which the cylinders of a multi-cylinder engine take turns to produce a power stroke. Cylinders are numbered from the front of the engine, the radiator or fan end, and cylinder 1 is the reference. The order is not a free choice: in a four-stroke engine each cylinder needs two crankshaft revolutions to finish its cycle, the pistons share crank throws, and the intake and exhaust ports must be grouped sensibly.

The angle between two successive firings, measured at the crankshaft, is the firing interval. Splitting one full cycle equally among the cylinders gives:

θ_f = 720 / n

θ_f is the firing interval in crank degrees; 720 is the number of crank degrees in one four-stroke cycle (two revolutions); n is the number of cylinders. For a two-stroke the same reasoning gives 360 / n, because its cycle lasts one revolution.

For an inline four-cylinder engine θ_f = 720/4 = 180°. Two orders are common:

  • 1-3-4-2 — the usual order in inline four-cylinder car engines.
  • 1-2-4-3 — the alternative; the interval is still 180°, but the two cylinders of a piston pair fire in the opposite sequence.

Even spacing keeps the torque reaching the crankshaft steady. It spreads the load on the main bearings, so the shaft winds and unwinds less and the engine runs quieter. The order also decides which exhaust pulses meet inside the manifold, and so how well the cylinders scavenge. Fitting spark plug leads in the wrong sequence keeps the 180° interval but scrambles the order, and the engine runs rough or will not start.

Valve layouts

The valve layout says where the valves sit relative to the cylinder: in the block beside it, or in a head above it. Four lettered layouts name the classic arrangements.

In the L-head (flathead, or side-valve) both valves sit in the block on one side of the cylinder, and the combustion chamber extends sideways over them. One low camshaft drives them directly, so the engine is cheap and compact; this layout was common in early cars and small stationary engines. Its weakness is a long, thin chamber: the flame has far to travel, compression must stay low, and output is knock-limited.

In the T-head the valves sit one on each side of the cylinder and two camshafts are needed. In the F-head one valve is in the head and the other in the block. Both tried to improve breathing while keeping the drive simple; both are now only historical.

The I-head puts both valves in the head directly above the piston, giving a compact chamber, short flame travel and good gas flow. Almost every modern engine uses it. Its family names describe the drive: OHV (overhead valve) keeps the camshaft in the block and pushes through pushrods; OHC (overhead camshaft) puts the camshaft in the head; DOHC (double overhead camshaft) uses one camshaft for the intake valves and one for the exhaust valves, which is what makes four valves per cylinder practical.

Valve actuation

Valve actuation is the chain of parts that turns crankshaft rotation into valve lift at the correct instant. Each valve opens once in two crank revolutions, so the camshaft that operates it turns at half crank speed:

N_cam = N_crank / 2

N_cam is the camshaft speed in rev/min and N_crank is the crankshaft speed in rev/min, for a four-stroke engine. A two-stroke camshaft turns at crank speed.

  1. The crankshaft drives the camshaft through gears, a chain or a toothed belt.
  2. A cam lobe on the shaft lifts a follower as it rotates.
  3. In a pushrod engine the follower raises a pushrod, which swings a rocker arm, which opens the valve.
  4. In an overhead camshaft engine the lobe presses directly on a bucket over the valve.
  5. A spring returns the valve and keeps the follower pressed against the cam.

Gears suit engines where the camshaft sits close to the crankshaft and last longest. Chains are durable but noisier. Toothed belts are quiet and cheap but must be replaced at a stated interval, and a snapped belt usually bends valves. Pushrods add reciprocating mass, which limits safe speed, but keep the engine compact and cheap. Overhead camshafts remove the pushrods and allow higher speeds and multi-valve heads; a rocker arm may survive as a roller finger follower. A hydraulic lifter is a small oil-filled cylinder in the valvetrain that changes length to hold the clearance at zero, so no periodic tappet adjustment is needed. Variable valve timing, the recent addition, rotates the camshaft slightly relative to the crank or switches to another cam profile; all the parts above stay in place.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke engine fires in the order 1-3-4-2 and runs at 2400 rpm. Find the firing interval in crank degrees and the time between two successive firings.

  1. Firing interval: θ_f = 720 / n = 720 / 4 = 180° of crank rotation.
  2. Convert engine speed to revolutions per second: N = 2400 / 60 = 40 rev/s.
  3. 180° is half a revolution, so the time between firings is t = 0.5 rev / 40 rev/s.
  4. t = 0.0125 s = 12.5 ms.

Answer. The firing interval is 180° of crankshaft rotation, and successive firings are 12.5 ms apart.

Exam traps

  • For a four-stroke use 720/n for the firing interval, not 360/n. Writing 360/n is the commonest slip and loses the whole numerical mark.
  • Firing order and firing interval are different things. Changing the order does not change the interval for a given n; it changes balance, manifold grouping and crankshaft loading.
  • Cylinder numbering is a convention. State that cylinder 1 is at the front (radiator end) before using any order, because makers number differently.
  • L-head means both valves in the block on one side; I-head means both in the head. T-head has one on each side and F-head one in each place. Examiners test these letters.
  • The half-speed rule for the camshaft holds for a four-stroke only. In a two-stroke the camshaft (if fitted) turns at crank speed.

1.7 Ideal air-standard cycles and their comparison

An ideal air-standard cycle replaces combustion with heat addition from an external source and uses air as the working fluid. It exists to compare engine cycles on equal terms: same air mass, ideal gas, constant specific heats, closed cycle, no intake or exhaust. The Otto cycle adds heat at constant volume; the Diesel cycle adds it at constant pressure; the dual cycle does both. Engineers use these cycles to see how compression ratio, cut-off ratio and heat input set efficiency and mean effective pressure before real losses enter. See Chapter 1.4 for the real SI and CI processes.

You need: ideal-gas relations and the first law from thermodynamics; Chapter 1.4 for the real SI and CI working principles.

Air-standard assumptions

An air-standard cycle treats the cylinder as a closed system filled with a fixed mass of air. Combustion is replaced by heat Qin added from an external source. Exhaust is replaced by heat Qout rejected to a sink. The same air returns to the starting state each cycle.

  • Air is an ideal gas: pV = mRT.
  • Specific heats cp and cv are constant, so γ = cp/cv is constant. For air, γ = 1.4.
  • No intake or exhaust stroke, so no gas exchange.
  • No friction, no heat loss to walls, no chemical change.

These assumptions remove fuel chemistry, variable specific heats and heat transfer. That makes the Otto, Diesel and dual cycles comparable at the same compression ratio r and the same heat input Qin. The price is that air-standard efficiency is higher than a real engine's. Fuel-air cycles and actual losses are in Chapter 1.8.

Otto cycle: heat at constant volume

The Otto cycle has four processes:

  1. 1–2: isentropic compression from V1 to V2.
  2. 2–3: constant-volume heat addition Qin.
  3. 3–4: isentropic expansion.
  4. 4–1: constant-volume heat rejection Qout.

ηOtto = 1 − 1 / r(γ−1)

r = V1/V2 is the compression ratio; γ = cp/cv is the specific heat ratio (1.4 for air); ηOtto is thermal efficiency.

The formula contains no Qin and no temperature. For fixed γ, only r sets efficiency. Higher r raises efficiency. In a real SI engine, knock limits r; see Unit 3, Combustion in SI and CI Engines. Some syllabi call this the constant-volume cycle.

Diesel cycle: heat at constant pressure

The Diesel cycle replaces the constant-volume heat addition by constant-pressure heat addition. The piston moves while heat enters, so the volume grows from V2 to V3. The cut-off ratio is ρ = V3/V2. A Diesel cycle has ρ > 1.

η<sub>Diesel</sub> = 1 − (1 / r<sup>(γ−1)</sup>) × ((ρ<sup>γ</sup> − 1) / (γ(ρ − 1)))

r is the compression ratio; ρ is the cut-off ratio; γ = 1.4 for air; ηDiesel is thermal efficiency.

For ρ > 1 the bracket ((ργ − 1)/(γ(ρ − 1))) is greater than 1. So at the same r, Diesel efficiency is lower than Otto efficiency. Some syllabi call this the constant-pressure cycle.

Dual cycle, comparison and mean effective pressure

The dual cycle adds heat in two steps: first at constant volume, then at constant pressure. It uses a pressure ratio α = p3/p2 and a cut-off ratio ρ = V4/V3. Otto and Diesel are special cases: α = 1 gives the Diesel cycle; ρ = 1 gives the Otto cycle.

η<sub>dual</sub> = 1 − (1 / r<sup>(γ−1)</sup>) × ((α ρ<sup>γ</sup> − 1) / ((α − 1) + γ α (ρ − 1)))

α is the pressure ratio; ρ is the cut-off ratio; γ = 1.4 for air; r is the compression ratio.

For the same r and same Qin, the efficiency order is Otto > dual > Diesel. Mean effective pressure compares engines of different size:

MEP = W<sub>net</sub> / V<sub>s</sub>, with V<sub>s</sub> = V<sub>1</sub> − V<sub>2</sub>

Wnet is net work per cycle in J; Vs is swept volume in m3; MEP is in Pa (1 kPa = 103 Pa).

A high MEP means more work from a smaller engine. See Unit 5, Engine Testing and Performance for measured MEP.

Worked example

Question. An Otto cycle has compression ratio 8 and γ = 1.4. A Diesel cycle has the same compression ratio and cut-off ratio 2. Find both thermal efficiencies.

  1. Both cycles use r = 8 and γ = 1.4; the Diesel cycle also uses ρ = 2.
  2. Compute rγ−1 = 80.4 = 2.297, so 1/rγ−1 = 0.4353.
  3. Otto: η = 1 − 0.4353 = 0.5647 = 56.5%.
  4. Diesel: ργ = 21.4 = 2.639, so ργ − 1 = 1.639.
  5. γ(ρ − 1) = 1.4 × (2 − 1) = 1.4. The bracket is 1.639 / 1.4 = 1.171.
  6. Diesel: η = 1 − (0.4353 × 1.171) = 1 − 0.5097 = 0.4903 = 49.0%.

Answer. Otto efficiency is 56.5%; Diesel efficiency is 49.0% at the same compression ratio.

Exam traps

  • Diesel efficiency needs the cut-off ratio ρ; the Otto formula does not apply to a Diesel cycle.
  • At the same compression ratio, Otto is more efficient than Diesel. Do not compare at different r and claim a general rule.
  • For the Diesel cycle the cut-off ratio is ρ = V3/V2; for the dual cycle the numbering shifts and it is ρ = V4/V3. It is never the compression ratio r, and it equals 1 only in the Otto cycle.
  • MEP uses net work per cycle divided by swept volume; using piston displacement in litres without converting gives wrong units.
  • Air-standard efficiency is not brake efficiency; it ignores friction, heat loss and gas exchange (Chapter 1.8, Unit 5).

1.8 Fuel-air cycles and actual cycle analysis

An air-standard cycle is a clean estimate, but a real engine runs on a fuel-air mixture whose specific heats change with temperature. A fuel-air cycle keeps the ideal cycle shape and adds a real mixture, variable specific heats, dissociation and finite combustion. It still has no friction and no pumping loss. Actual cycle analysis then adds gas exchange, heat loss, blowdown, exhaust, friction and incomplete combustion, and reads these from an indicator diagram. Engineers use the three levels to see where the gap between textbook efficiency and measured efficiency comes from.

You need: ideal air-standard cycles from Chapter 1.7, valve timing from Chapter 1.5, and SI/CI working from Chapter 1.4.

Fuel-air cycle: real mixture, still ideal

An air-standard cycle uses air alone as the working fluid, constant specific heats, and heat added from outside. A fuel-air cycle keeps the same cycle outline (compression, heat release, expansion, heat rejection) but uses a mixture of fuel vapour and air. It also lets specific heat rise with temperature, and the heat release is not instantaneous, so the peak pressure is lower and the cycle is closer to a limited-pressure cycle.

The specific heat at constant volume, c_v (J/kg·K), is the heat needed to raise 1 kg of gas by 1 K at constant volume. In real gases c_v increases with temperature. Hot burned gas therefore needs more heat for the same temperature rise, so peak temperature and pressure are lower than the air-standard estimate. Dissociation at high temperature also absorbs energy when CO₂ and H₂O break into CO, H₂, O and OH; part of that energy returns later, but the peak is flattened.

Combustion is finite. The flame needs time to cross the chamber, so pressure rise is not instantaneous. Because heat release is spread over part of the expansion, the fuel-air cycle sits closer to a limited-pressure cycle than to a pure constant-volume Otto cycle. The fuel-air cycle still assumes ideal processes: no friction, no pumping loss, no heat loss to walls, no leakage. It is an ideal cycle with a real mixture.

η_fuel-air = W_net / Q_in

η_fuel-air is fuel-air cycle efficiency (dimensionless); W_net is net ideal work per cycle (J); Q_in is heat released by the fuel per cycle (J).

At the same compression ratio, fuel-air efficiency is lower than air-standard efficiency. The gap comes from the real mixture, variable c_v and dissociation.

Actual cycle: gas exchange and losses

The actual cycle is the real p–V or p–θ diagram from a running engine. Valve timing from Chapter 1.5 means intake opens before TDC and exhaust closes after TDC. The piston therefore pumps fresh charge and exhaust gas through finite pressure differences. In a naturally aspirated engine the intake line sits below atmospheric pressure and the exhaust line above it. The loop between them is the pumping loop; its area is pumping work, and it reduces net work.

Heat loss to cylinder walls, piston and head removes energy during compression and combustion. The exhaust valve opens before BDC. High-pressure gas blows down through the valve, so the exhaust stroke starts at lower pressure. This avoids pumping high-pressure gas and protects the valve, but some expansion work is lost. Friction in piston rings, bearings, valve train and auxiliaries converts indicated work into heat. Incomplete combustion leaves some fuel energy unburned.

η_i = W_i / Q_fuel

η_i is indicated efficiency; W_i is indicated work per cycle from the indicator diagram (J); Q_fuel is chemical energy supplied by fuel per cycle (J).

η_b = W_b / Q_fuel = η_i × η_m

η_b is brake efficiency; W_b is brake work per cycle at the shaft (J); η_m = W_b / W_i is mechanical efficiency (dimensionless).

The indicator diagram area gives W_i. The brake work W_b is smaller by friction. For a naturally aspirated engine the pumping loop usually reduces W_i, so its sign matters.

Comparing air-standard, fuel-air and actual

The three cycles form a ladder. Air-standard is the top estimate; fuel-air removes mixture and property errors; actual removes gas-exchange, heat-transfer and friction errors. At the same compression ratio the efficiencies fall in that order.

CycleEfficiencyMain extra effect
Air-standard Otto56.5%None: air, constant c_v
Fuel-air Otto48.0%Real mixture, variable c_v, dissociation
Actual indicated38.0%Pumping, heat loss, blowdown, incomplete combustion

Falls are compared in percentage points, not percent. A fall from 56.5% to 48.0% is 8.5 percentage points. It is not an 8.5% fall; the relative fall is 8.5/56.5 = 15.0%. Use 'percentage points' in exams.

The actual indicated value does not include friction; brake efficiency would be lower by η_m. Only a few universities teach the full loss breakdown. If your syllabus stops at fuel-air, learn the definitions, the direction of each loss, and the indicator diagram. Detailed loss accounting belongs to Unit 5, Engine Testing and Performance. Emission formation belongs to Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. An air-standard Otto cycle has efficiency 56.5%. A fuel-air cycle at the same compression ratio has efficiency 48.0%. The actual indicated efficiency is 38.0%. Find the fall from air-standard to fuel-air, from fuel-air to actual, and the overall fall.

  1. Write the three efficiencies: η_air = 56.5%, η_fuel-air = 48.0%, η_actual,i = 38.0%.
  2. Fall from air-standard to fuel-air: η_air − η_fuel-air = 56.5 − 48.0 = 8.5 percentage points.
  3. Fall from fuel-air to actual: η_fuel-air − η_actual,i = 48.0 − 38.0 = 10.0 percentage points.
  4. Overall fall: η_air − η_actual,i = 56.5 − 38.0 = 18.5 percentage points.
  5. Check: 8.5 + 10.0 = 18.5 percentage points.

Answer. The falls are 8.5 percentage points, 10.0 percentage points and 18.5 percentage points overall.

Exam traps

  • A fuel-air cycle is still ideal. Friction, pumping and wall heat loss belong to the actual cycle; do not subtract them in the fuel-air step.
  • Percentage points are not percent. A fall from 56.5% to 48.0% is 8.5 percentage points, not an 8.5% fall.
  • Indicated efficiency is before friction. Brake efficiency is η_i × η_m, so it is lower; do not call η_i the brake efficiency.
  • Valve timing means the actual cycle is not closed at TDC and BDC. Intake opens before TDC and exhaust closes after TDC; the pumping loop is part of the indicator diagram.
  • Blowdown before BDC loses expansion work but reduces pumping work. It is not free; state both effects.

Takeaways

  1. An IC engine converts chemical energy of fuel into work by burning the fuel inside the cylinder, and its types follow from separate choices such as ignition, strokes, cooling and aspiration.
  2. Bore, stroke, TDC, BDC and compression ratio form the fixed vocabulary for every later calculation on power, efficiency and size.
  3. A four-stroke engine gives one power stroke in two revolutions; a two-stroke engine gives one per revolution but needs port-based scavenging.
  4. SI engines need a spark and a premixed charge; CI engines inject fuel into hot air and depend on autoignition and a high compression ratio.
  5. Valve and port timing diagrams fix the real opening and closing of gas passages, so lead, lag and overlap explain why an engine breathes better than the simple cycle suggests.
  6. Firing order and valve layout decide how smoothly power pulses reach the crankshaft and how the valves are driven.
  7. Otto, Diesel and dual air-standard cycles allow a fair comparison of efficiency at fixed conditions because combustion is replaced by heat addition.
  8. Fuel-air cycles and actual cycle analysis explain the gap between ideal efficiency and what the engine really delivers, through mixture strength, variable specific heats, gas exchange, heat loss and friction.

Glossary

Air-standard cycle
An ideal cycle in which air is the working fluid and combustion is replaced by heat addition from an external source.
Autoignition
Spontaneous ignition of the charge caused by high temperature and pressure rather than by a spark.
Bore
The inner diameter of the engine cylinder.
Brake power
Useful power measured at the crankshaft, after friction losses.
Clearance volume
The small volume left above the piston when it is at top dead centre.
Compression ratio
The ratio of total cylinder volume to clearance volume, written r.
Cut-off ratio
In the Diesel cycle, the ratio of volume after heat addition to volume before it, written ρ.
Dissociation
Breakdown of burnt gases at high temperature into simpler species, which absorbs heat and lowers peak temperature.
Firing order
The order in which the cylinders of a multi-cylinder engine fire.
Fuel-air cycle
An ideal cycle that accounts for a real fuel-air mixture and specific heats that vary with temperature.
IC engine
A heat engine in which fuel burns inside a closed cylinder and the expanding gases push a piston to produce work.
Indicated power
Power developed inside the cylinder, found from the indicator diagram.
Mean effective pressure
A fictitious constant pressure that, acting over one stroke, would give the same work as the real cycle.
Naturally aspirated engine
An engine that takes in air at atmospheric pressure without a compressor.
Port
An opening in the cylinder wall uncovered by the moving piston to admit or release gas.
Scavenging
The process of pushing out burnt gases and filling the cylinder with fresh charge.
Stroke
The travel of the piston between top dead centre and bottom dead centre.
Supercharger
A compressor driven by the engine that raises intake air density above atmospheric.
Swept volume
The volume displaced by the piston in one stroke, equal to (π/4)d²L for one cylinder.
Tappet clearance
The small gap in the valve train that allows for thermal expansion of valve parts.
Valve lag
The angle by which a valve closes after the piston passes TDC or BDC.
Valve lead
The angle by which a valve opens before the piston reaches TDC or BDC.
Valve overlap
The crank-angle interval during which both inlet and exhaust valves are open.
Volumetric efficiency
The ratio of actual air drawn in per cycle to the theoretical volume swept by the piston.
