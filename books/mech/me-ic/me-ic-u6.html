6.1 Formation of CO, unburnt hydrocarbons and NOx

An engine never burns all its fuel perfectly. Carbon monoxide (CO) comes from too little oxygen, unburnt hydrocarbons (HC) from fuel that the flame never reaches, and oxides of nitrogen (NOx) from a flame that is too hot. These are the three engine-out gases, measured before any catalytic converter. Each comes from a different failure, so one fix cannot remove all three, and every emission control that follows is built on these three routes.

You need: the stages of combustion and flame quenching (Combustion in SI and CI engines) and the idea of air–fuel ratio.

CO: the gas of a rich mixture

Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless, poisonous gas. It forms when carbon in the fuel is only partly oxidised: C + ½O2 → CO. With enough oxygen, CO burns on to CO2, mainly through the reaction CO + OH → CO2 + H.

φ = (F/A)actual ÷ (F/A)stoich

φ is the equivalence ratio; F/A is the fuel–air ratio by mass. φ > 1 is rich, φ < 1 is lean. The air–fuel ratio is the inverse idea: for petrol the stoichiometric value is about 14.7, so φ = 0.9 means an air–fuel ratio of 14.7 ÷ 0.9 = 16.3. The excess air factor λ = 1/φ is used on oxygen sensors.

In a petrol engine CO depends almost only on φ. On the rich side there is not enough oxygen to finish the carbon, so CO climbs steeply: a few per cent by volume at φ = 1.2. At the high temperature of the burned gas some CO2 also dissociates (CO2 ⇌ CO + ½O2). During expansion the gas cools fast and the CO-burning reactions slow down and “freeze”, so even a stoichiometric or slightly lean engine leaves a small CO level, a few tenths of a per cent, above the low-temperature equilibrium value.

A diesel runs lean overall (φ about 0.3 to 0.8), so its engine-out CO is low. Some CO still comes from rich pockets in the spray and from lean regions too cool to finish burning.

Unburnt hydrocarbons: fuel that hides from the flame

Unburnt hydrocarbons are fuel molecules, and partly burnt pieces of them, that leave in the exhaust. An analyser counts them as carbon atoms, in ppm C1 (see the flame ionisation detector in emission measurement). In a petrol engine the main sources are:

  • Crevices: the narrow gap above the top piston ring (the top land), the ring grooves and the spark-plug thread. Mixture is pushed in during compression, the flame cannot enter a gap this narrow, and the mixture comes back out during expansion. Studies of petrol engines put crevices as the largest single source.
  • Oil film: fuel vapour dissolves in the oil on the liner while the pressure is high and comes out again after the flame has passed.
  • Wall quenching: the flame dies a short distance from a cold wall, typically about 0.1 mm. Most of this thin layer mixes with hot gas later and burns, so quenching adds less HC than crevices.
  • Liquid fuel and misfire: on a cold start fuel wets the port and the cylinder and evaporates too late; a cycle that misfires sends all its fuel out.
  • Valve overlap: when intake and exhaust valves are open together, a little fresh mixture can short-circuit to the exhaust.

Some of the HC that leaves the crevices and the oil film burns in the hot gas during expansion and in the exhaust port. That is why retarding the spark lowers HC: the exhaust leaves hotter, and more HC burns on the way out. It also lowers NOx, but costs fuel. HC falls as the mixture leans out, then rises again when the charge is so lean that flames die and cycles misfire. In a diesel, HC comes from over-lean edges of the spray and from fuel that dribbles out of the nozzle sac after injection ends.

NOx: the gas of a hot flame

NOx means nitric oxide (NO) plus nitrogen dioxide (NO2). In a petrol engine NO is almost all of it; in a diesel NO2 can be 10 to 30 per cent. Most NO is thermal NO, made from the nitrogen and oxygen of the air in the hot burned gas behind the flame.

Zeldovich mechanism: O + N2 → NO + N, then N + O2 → NO + O. Adding the third reaction N + OH → NO + H gives the extended Zeldovich mechanism. The first reaction must break the very strong N≡N bond, so it is slow below about 1800 K and speeds up sharply above it.

Thermal NO therefore needs three things together: high temperature, oxygen atoms, and time at that temperature. The peak sits slightly lean of stoichiometric, near φ = 0.9 (air–fuel ratio about 16): the burned gas is still very hot and some oxygen is left over. Richer, there is no spare oxygen; leaner, the extra air cools the flame. Anything that raises the peak burned gas temperature raises NO: advanced spark or injection, a higher compression ratio, high load and hot intake air. Exhaust gas recirculation, spark retard and charge cooling lower it. Small amounts also come from prompt NO in the flame front and from nitrogen bound in the fuel.

Worked example

Question. A petrol engine passes 0.05 m³/s of dry exhaust, measured at standard conditions (0 °C, 1 atm), where the density of CO is 1.25 kg/m³. The NDIR analyser reads 0.4 % CO by volume and the brake power is 60 kW. Find the CO mass flow and the brake-specific CO in g/kWh.

  1. CO volume fraction: 0.4 % = 0.4 ÷ 100 = 0.004.
  2. CO volume flow: 0.004 × 0.05 m³/s = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s.
  3. CO mass flow: 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s × 1.25 kg/m³ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s = 0.25 g/s.
  4. CO per hour: 0.25 g/s × 3600 s/h = 900 g/h.
  5. Brake-specific CO: 900 g/h ÷ 60 kW = 15 g/kWh.
  6. Check the density: 28.0 kg/kmol ÷ 22.41 m³/kmol = 1.25 kg/m³ at 0 °C and 1 atm, so the data agree.

Answer. CO mass flow is 0.25 g/s and brake-specific CO is 15 g/kWh.

Worked example 2

Question. A petrol engine burns 10 kg/h of fuel at φ = 0.9 (stoichiometric air–fuel ratio 14.7) and gives 40 kW. The analyser reads 2000 ppm NO in the wet exhaust, whose mean molar mass is 28.6 kg/kmol. Find the NOx in g/kWh. Emission rules count NOx as NO2 (46.0 kg/kmol).

  1. Air–fuel ratio: 14.7 ÷ 0.9 = 16.33. Air flow: 16.33 × 10 = 163.3 kg/h.
  2. Exhaust mass flow = air + fuel = 163.3 + 10 = 173.3 kg/h.
  3. Exhaust molar flow: 173.3 ÷ 28.6 = 6.061 kmol/h.
  4. ppm is a mole fraction: NO flow = 2000 × 10⁻⁶ × 6.061 = 0.01212 kmol/h.
  5. Mass as NO2: 0.01212 × 46.0 = 0.5576 kg/h = 557.6 g/h.
  6. Brake-specific NOx: 557.6 ÷ 40 = 13.9 g/kWh.

Answer. The engine emits about 558 g/h of NOx (as NO2), that is 13.9 g/kWh before any catalyst.

Exam traps

  • Saying NOx is highest in the richest, hottest-sounding mixture. NO peaks slightly lean, near φ = 0.9, because it needs spare oxygen as well as heat.
  • Writing “φ = 0.9 means an air–fuel ratio of 15”. For petrol, φ = 0.9 is 14.7 ÷ 0.9 = 16.3.
  • Calling three reactions “the Zeldovich mechanism”. The first two are the Zeldovich (thermal) mechanism; with N + OH → NO + H it is the extended Zeldovich mechanism.
  • Naming wall quenching as the main HC source. Crevices are the largest; the quench layer is only about 0.1 mm and much of it burns later.
  • Converting ppm to mass with the molar mass of NO when the rule asks for NOx as NO2: the answer comes out 30/46 of the right value.

6.2 Smoke and particulate matter

Diesel smoke is the visible part of particles born in the fuel-rich core of the spray. There the fuel has far more fuel than oxygen, so it cracks into carbon-rich pieces that grow into tiny spheres and chains. Most of this soot burns later in the stroke; what survives, with oil, unburnt fuel and sulphate stuck to it, is particulate matter (PM). This chapter defines PM, follows a particle from birth to tailpipe, and shows how PM is weighed and why it trades against NOx.

You need: diesel combustion and its stages (Combustion in SI and CI engines), and the NOx route in formation of CO, HC and NOx.

What counts as particulate matter

Particulate matter is defined by the way it is measured: everything that collects on a filter when exhaust, diluted with clean air, is drawn through it at 47 °C. The filter is weighed before and after, and the gain is the PM mass. PM is a mixture of four parts:

  • Soot (dry carbon): the black solid core. It gives black smoke.
  • Soluble organic fraction (SOF): unburnt fuel and lubricating oil that condense on the soot as the gas cools. At light load it can be half the mass or more.
  • Sulphate with bound water: from sulphur in the fuel, burnt to SO2 and partly to sulphate. Low-sulphur diesel (10 ppm sulphur) has made this part small.
  • Ash: metal compounds from lubricant additives and engine wear, a few per cent of the mass. Ash never burns.

PM = soot + SOF + sulphate + ash

each term is a mass in the same filter sample, in mg; the water bound to the sulphate is counted with it.

Smoke is not the same as PM. You see black smoke because of the soot. A clear exhaust can still carry SOF and sulphate, and the visible colour tells the cause: black smoke is soot from too much fuel for the air; blue smoke is burning lubricating oil (worn rings or valve guides); white smoke is unburnt fuel droplets or water vapour, common on a cold start.

From rich core to tailpipe: six steps

In the core of a diesel spray the local equivalence ratio is far above 1, often 2 to 4, and the temperature is 1600 to 2600 K. Soot forms only in these hot, oxygen-poor places, and grows through six steps:

  1. Pyrolysis: heat breaks fuel molecules into small pieces such as acetylene (C2H2) and builds ring molecules, the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH). No oxygen is involved.
  2. Nucleation: PAH molecules stick together into the first particles, about 1 to 2 nm across.
  3. Surface growth: acetylene adds to the particle surface; mass rises fast while the number of particles stays the same.
  4. Coagulation: small particles collide and merge into larger spheres, about 10 to 30 nm.
  5. Agglomeration: spheres stick into chains and clusters a few hundred nm long.
  6. Oxidation: when the burning jet mixes with air, OH radicals and oxygen burn the soot. Most of the soot formed is burnt here; exhaust PM is what survives.

So engine-out PM depends on two rates: how much soot forms in the core, and how much burns out before the gas cools in expansion. Anything that leaves the late gas cool or short of oxygen lets more soot survive.

What moves PM, and the NOx–PM trade-off

These changes lower PM by shrinking the rich core or helping burn-out:

  • Higher injection pressure and smaller nozzle holes: finer droplets and faster mixing.
  • More swirl or a better bowl shape: faster air–fuel mixing.
  • Low-sulphur fuel and low oil consumption: less sulphate and SOF.

These changes raise PM:

  • Higher load at the same air: more fuel per kilogram of air, a richer core.
  • Injection retard: lower peak temperature and less time for burn-out.
  • Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR): less oxygen and lower temperature for burn-out.

Retard and EGR are the two main NOx controls, which is the NOx–PM trade-off: the low temperature and low oxygen that stop NO forming also stop soot burning. A modern diesel escapes the trade-off by using EGR and retard for NOx and then trapping the extra soot in a particulate filter, or by moving to the low-temperature combustion of HCCI and RCCI.

Worked example

Question. A 40 kW diesel engine has a raw exhaust flow of 0.05 m³/s at standard conditions. A partial-flow system dilutes a sample of the exhaust 8 times (1 part exhaust to 7 parts filtered air) and draws 0.30 m³ of the diluted gas through a filter at 47 °C. The filter gains 0.90 mg. Find the raw PM concentration, the PM mass flow and the brake-specific PM.

  1. Concentration on the filter (diluted): 0.90 mg ÷ 0.30 m³ = 3.0 mg/m³.
  2. Undo the dilution: raw concentration = 3.0 × 8 = 24 mg/m³.
  3. PM mass flow: 24 mg/m³ × 0.05 m³/s = 1.2 mg/s.
  4. Per hour: 1.2 × 3600 = 4320 mg/h = 4.32 g/h.
  5. Brake-specific PM: 4.32 g/h ÷ 40 kW = 0.108 g/kWh.
  6. Compare with a heavy-engine limit of 0.01 g/kWh: a filter must catch 1 − 0.01/0.108 = 0.907, about 91 % of the mass.

Answer. Raw PM is 24 mg/m³, the PM flow 1.2 mg/s, and the brake-specific PM 0.108 g/kWh, so the engine needs a particulate filter of at least about 91 % efficiency to reach 0.01 g/kWh.

Worked example 2

Question. An opacimeter with an effective light path of 0.430 m reads 45 % opacity during a free-acceleration test. Find the light absorption coefficient k, which in-use smoke limits use.

  1. Opacity N is the share of light blocked: transmitted fraction = 1 − 0.45 = 0.55.
  2. Beer–Lambert law: transmitted fraction = e−kL, so k = −ln(1 − N) ÷ L.
  3. ln 0.55 = −0.598, so k = 0.598 ÷ 0.430 = 1.39 m⁻¹.

Answer. k = 1.39 m⁻¹. The coefficient is used instead of opacity because it does not depend on the path length of the meter.

Exam traps

  • Writing “smoke = particulate matter”. Smoke is the visible soot; PM also holds SOF, sulphate and ash, which can dominate at light load.
  • Treating the filter-sample volume as the engine’s exhaust flow. The filter sees diluted gas: multiply by the dilution ratio, then by the real exhaust flow.
  • Treating opacity, Bosch smoke number and filter smoke number as the same or as PM mass. They measure light extinction or paper blackening, mostly from soot, and use different scales.
  • Saying soot forms in the lean outer zone. It forms in the hot rich core and is burnt in the outer, oxygen-rich zone.
  • Assuming EGR always helps a diesel. It lowers NOx but raises PM.

6.3 Emission control: in-cylinder and after-treatment methods

Every emission control is a choice of place: stop the pollutant forming inside the cylinder, or convert and trap it in the exhaust. In-cylinder methods change mixing, timing and temperature. After-treatment adds catalysts, filters and urea. A petrol engine held at stoichiometric mixture can use one three-way catalyst; a lean diesel needs an oxidation catalyst, a particulate filter and SCR or a NOx trap. The skill is matching each device to the engine’s air–fuel ratio and exhaust temperature.

You need: how CO, HC, NOx and PM form (formation of CO, HC and NOx, smoke and particulate matter).

In-cylinder treatments

In-cylinder methods cut what the engine makes in the first place:

  • Spark or injection retard: the burn moves later, the peak temperature falls and NOx falls. It costs fuel; in a diesel it raises PM.
  • Injection strategy (CI): higher pressure (common rail) gives finer sprays and less soot; a pilot injection shortens the delay and softens the first burst, lowering noise and NOx; a post injection late in expansion burns soot or supplies heat for the filter; rate shaping starts the injection slowly.
  • Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR): exhaust sent back to the intake replaces some oxygen and adds CO2 and H2O, which absorb heat. The flame runs cooler, so NOx falls. Cooled EGR packs more mass into the cylinder; high-pressure EGR is taken before the turbine, low-pressure EGR after the particulate filter. Too much EGR raises PM, HC and fuel use.
  • Air–fuel control (SI): an oxygen (lambda) sensor and closed-loop injection hold λ = 1 for the catalyst.
  • Chamber and charge motion: a compact chamber with fewer crevices cuts HC; swirl and squish speed mixing and cut soot.
  • Crankcase ventilation and evaporative control: blow-by gas is sent back to the intake, and fuel vapour from the tank is held in a charcoal canister and burnt later.
EGR % = (CO₂ intake − CO₂ ambient) ÷ (CO₂ exhaust − CO₂ ambient) × 100

each CO₂ is a volume (mole) per cent on the same basis; the result is the share of exhaust in the intake charge.

After-treatment: the three-way catalyst and the diesel line

The three-way catalyst (TWC) is a ceramic honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium. At λ = 1 it does three jobs at once: CO + ½O2 → CO2; HC + O2 → CO2 + H2O; and NO is reduced by CO, H2 and HC, for example 2NO + 2CO → N2 + 2CO2. The reductions need the CO and HC that the oxidations use up, so the window is narrow: λ within about ±0.5 % of 1, roughly an air–fuel ratio of 14.6 to 14.8. Lean of it, spare oxygen takes the reductants and NOx passes; rich of it, CO and HC pass. A catalyst works only above its light-off temperature, about 250 to 300 °C, so most of a cycle’s emissions come in the first minute.

A diesel’s exhaust always holds spare oxygen, so it needs a different line:

  • Diesel oxidation catalyst (DOC): burns CO, HC and part of the SOF, and turns some NO into NO2, which helps the next two devices.
  • Diesel particulate filter (DPF): a wall-flow honeycomb whose channels are blocked at alternate ends, so the gas must pass through the porous walls and the soot stays behind. It is cleaned by regeneration: passively by NO2 at about 250 to 400 °C, or actively by raising the gas to about 600 °C with a post injection. Ash cannot burn and slowly fills the filter.
  • Selective catalytic reduction (SCR): a 32.5 % urea solution (AdBlue) is sprayed into the exhaust and breaks down to ammonia: (NH2)2CO + H2O → 2NH3 + CO2. The standard reaction is 4NH3 + 4NO + O2 → 4N2 + 6H2O, one ammonia per NO; the fast reaction uses equal NO and NO2. Unused ammonia (ammonia slip) is burnt in a small slip catalyst at the end.
  • Lean NOx trap (LNT): stores NO2 as barium nitrate while lean; a short rich pulse every minute or so releases it and reduces it to N2. It needs no urea but is poisoned by sulphur and costs fuel for the rich pulses.

Fuel sulphur must be low (10 ppm in BS-VI fuel), since sulphur poisons the DOC, LNT and SCR and adds sulphate PM.

Worked example

Question. An EGR valve opens on a diesel engine. CO2 is 0.04 % in the ambient air, 1.80 % in the intake manifold and 12.0 % in the exhaust. Find the EGR rate as a share of the intake charge, and the mass of exhaust recirculated per kg of fresh air.

  1. Rise of CO2 in the intake caused by EGR: 1.80 − 0.04 = 1.76 %.
  2. Rise it would have if the whole charge were exhaust: 12.0 − 0.04 = 11.96 %.
  3. EGR share x = 1.76 ÷ 11.96 = 0.147, that is 14.7 % of the intake charge.
  4. Fresh air is the rest: 1 − 0.147 = 0.853 of the charge.
  5. Exhaust per kg of fresh air: x ÷ (1 − x) = 0.147 ÷ 0.853 = 0.173 kg.

Answer. EGR is 14.7 % of the intake charge, which is 0.173 kg of exhaust per kg of fresh air (taking mole and mass fractions as equal, since the molar masses of air and diesel exhaust are close).

Worked example 2

Question. A 200 kW truck diesel makes 6.0 g/kWh of engine-out NOx (as NO2). The SCR must remove 90 % of it. Find the urea and AdBlue flow at full load and compare the AdBlue with the diesel flow at a brake specific fuel consumption of 210 g/kWh.

  1. NOx removed: 0.90 × 6.0 × 200 = 1080 g/h.
  2. In moles: 1080 ÷ 46.0 = 23.5 mol/h of NOx.
  3. Standard SCR uses one NH3 per NO, so 23.5 mol/h of NH3.
  4. Each urea molecule gives two NH3: urea = 23.5 ÷ 2 = 11.7 mol/h; × 60.06 g/mol = 705 g/h.
  5. AdBlue is 32.5 % urea: 705 ÷ 0.325 = 2169 g/h, about 2.17 kg/h.
  6. Diesel flow: 210 × 200 = 42 000 g/h = 42 kg/h; AdBlue ÷ diesel = 2.17 ÷ 42 = 0.052.
  7. Tailpipe NOx: 6.0 × (1 − 0.90) = 0.6 g/kWh.

Answer. The engine needs about 0.70 kg/h of urea, that is 2.17 kg/h of AdBlue, about 5 % of its diesel flow, and the tailpipe NOx falls to 0.6 g/kWh.

Exam traps

  • Fitting a three-way catalyst alone to a lean engine. With spare oxygen the NO reduction stops, so NOx passes unconverted.
  • Giving the TWC window as “λ ± 0.3”. That spans air–fuel ratios from about 10 to 19; the real window is about ±0.5 % of λ = 1.
  • Dividing by (CO2 exhaust − CO2 intake) and calling the answer the EGR share of the charge. That ratio is exhaust per unit of fresh air (17.3 % in the example), not the share of the charge (14.7 %).
  • Mixing up DOC and DPF: the DOC oxidises gases, the DPF traps solid soot.
  • Saying regeneration empties the filter. It burns soot; ash stays.

6.4 Emission measurement: NDIR, chemiluminescence, FID and smoke meters

An exhaust bench does not measure “pollution” as one number. It measures each gas by a physical effect that belongs to that gas: CO and CO₂ absorb infrared light, NO gives off light when it meets ozone, hydrocarbons make ions in a hydrogen flame, and smoke blocks light or blackens paper. Every engine change and every certificate is proved on such a bench, so its readings, and its mistakes, decide what a controller or an examiner believes.

You need: the gases and particles of formation of CO, HC and NOx and smoke and particulate matter.

Sampling decides the answer

Many wrong emission numbers are sampling errors, not analyser errors. For engine-out work the probe sits before the first catalyst; for a tailpipe test, after the last device. The line to the bench is heated to about 190 °C so water and heavy hydrocarbons stay as vapour, and a filter keeps soot out of the gas analysers. If the line runs cold, water condenses, dissolves NO2 and takes heavy HC with it, and the reading falls.

Each analyser wants its sample in a different state. The flame ionisation detector takes the hot, wet gas. The infrared and chemiluminescence analysers take gas that has passed a cooler or water trap, because water absorbs infrared and quenches the chemiluminescence light. The smoke meter takes raw, unfiltered exhaust, because soot is what it measures.

On a steady test bed the bench samples raw exhaust. For a vehicle on a driving cycle, constant-volume sampling (CVS) dilutes the whole exhaust with filtered air to a fixed total flow and fills bags with a proportional sample, which the bench reads after the test.

NDIR for CO and CO₂, and oxygen by magnetism

NDIR means non-dispersive infrared: a broad infrared source is used without a prism or grating. A chopper sends the beam alternately through a sample cell and a reference cell filled with a non-absorbing gas. The detector is itself filled with the gas to be measured (CO, for a CO analyser), so it responds only to the wavelengths that gas absorbs; that is how the analyser picks its gas without dispersing the light. CO absorbs near 4.7 µm and CO2 near 4.3 µm.

I = I₀ e^(−a c L)

I₀ and I are the light entering and leaving the cell; a is the absorption coefficient of the gas; c its concentration; L the cell length (the Beer–Lambert law). The analyser is calibrated with a zero gas (nitrogen) and a span gas of known concentration.

NDIR suits CO and CO2, whose bands are strong. It is poor for low-ppm NO and for hydrocarbons, whose absorption is weak and overlaps water. Oxygen does not absorb in the infrared; it is measured by its magnetism. O2 is paramagnetic (attracted into a magnetic field), and a paramagnetic analyser measures that pull.

Chemiluminescence for NO and NOx

The chemiluminescence detector (CLD) mixes the sample with ozone made inside the instrument: NO + O3 → NO2* + O2. The star marks an excited molecule; as it drops back it gives off light, which a photomultiplier counts. The light is proportional to NO. NO2 does not give this light, so for total NOx the sample first passes a heated converter that turns NO2 into NO.

  1. Bypass the converter: the reading is NO.
  2. Send the sample through the converter: the reading is NOx (apparent).
  3. Apparent NO2 = NOx (apparent) − NO.
  4. True NO2 = apparent NO2 ÷ η, where η is the converter efficiency (0 to 1).

The converter efficiency is checked by feeding it a gas of known NO2 content and dividing the NO2 it converts by the NO2 offered. Water and CO2 quench the excited molecules, so the sample is dried or the reading corrected.

FID for hydrocarbons, and smoke meters

The flame ionisation detector (FID) burns the sample in a small hydrogen flame. Organic carbon atoms form ions, and an electrode collects them. The current depends on the number of carbon atoms, not of molecules, so the reading is in ppmC: ppmC = ppm of the compound × its number of carbon atoms. One ppm of propane, C3H8, reads about 3 ppmC. The FID is blind to CO, CO2 and water, and gives low readings for oxygenated compounds such as methanol and formaldehyde. A heated FID at about 190 °C keeps heavy HC from condensing; a non-methane cutter burns every hydrocarbon except methane, so total HC minus the cutter reading gives non-methane HC.

Smoke is measured with light. An opacimeter (the Hartridge type is the classic) shines light across a fixed path of exhaust and reports opacity in per cent or the absorption coefficient k in m⁻¹. A filter-paper meter draws a fixed volume through white paper and reads its blackness as a Bosch smoke number from 0 to 10, or a filter smoke number. Neither gives PM mass; that needs a gravimetric filter weighed on a microbalance.

Worked example

Question. A chemiluminescence analyser reads 380 ppm with the NO2 converter bypassed and 420 ppm with the converter in line. The converter efficiency is 0.92. Find the true NO2, the true NOx and how much the uncorrected bench under-reports.

  1. Bypass reading: NO = 380 ppm.
  2. Converter in line: apparent NOx = 420 ppm.
  3. Apparent NO2 = 420 − 380 = 40 ppm.
  4. True NO2 = 40 ÷ 0.92 = 43.48 ppm, about 43.5 ppm.
  5. True NOx = 380 + 43.5 = 423.5 ppm.
  6. Under-report = 423.5 − 420 = 3.5 ppm, about 0.8 % of the reading.

Answer. True NO2 is 43.5 ppm and true NOx 423.5 ppm; the uncorrected bench reads 3.5 ppm low.

Worked example 2

Question. An NDIR CO analyser transmits 70 % of the light (I/I₀ = 0.70) with a 2.0 % CO span gas and 80 % with the engine sample. Assuming Beer–Lambert behaviour, find the CO in the sample.

  1. From I/I₀ = e−acL, ln(I/I₀) is proportional to c.
  2. Span: ln 0.70 = −0.3567. Sample: ln 0.80 = −0.2231.
  3. c = 2.0 % × (−0.2231) ÷ (−0.3567) = 2.0 × 0.6255 = 1.25 %.
  4. A straight-line guess from the transmissions, 2.0 × (1 − 0.80)/(1 − 0.70) = 1.33 %, would be 6.6 % high (1.333 ÷ 1.251): the response is not linear.

Answer. The sample holds 1.25 % CO. Real analysers store a curve made from several span gases for the same reason.

Exam traps

  • Comparing an FID reading in ppmC directly with a limit for one compound: divide by the carbon number first (90 ppmC of propane is 30 ppm).
  • Forgetting the converter efficiency: the apparent NO2 is always low and must be divided by η before adding it to NO.
  • Letting the sample line run cold: water condenses, takes NO2 and heavy HC, and the bench reads low.
  • Treating opacity or a Bosch number as PM mass: they measure light extinction or blackness; mass needs a filter and a balance.
  • Using NDIR for low-ppm NOx or HC: its absorption is weak and overlaps water; use the CLD and the FID.

6.5 Emission standards and driving cycles

An emission standard is a pair: a test procedure, which for a car is a fixed speed–time trace driven on rollers, and a limit for each pollutant in g/km (light vehicles) or g/kWh (heavy engines). The trace fixes the distance, speeds, accelerations, idle time and cold start, so two vehicles are compared on the same drive. India has used this system through the Bharat Stage (BS) norms, now BS-VI, for cars, two-wheelers, trucks and off-road engines.

You need: the bench of emission measurement.

A test, not a trip

A certification test is a script. A driver or robot follows the speed–time trace on a chassis dynamometer, rollers that load the wheels like the road. The car starts cold, after standing overnight at room temperature, because the first minute before the catalyst lights off gives much of the CO and HC. The exhaust is diluted in a CVS system, bag samples are read on the bench, and the mass of each pollutant over the cycle is divided by the distance.

e = m ÷ d; b = m ÷ W

e is the distance-specific emission in g/km; m the pollutant mass over the cycle in g; d the cycle distance in km; b the brake-specific emission in g/kWh; W the engine work over the cycle in kWh.

A bench reading in ppm is a concentration, a fraction of the gas. A limit in g/km is a mass per unit distance, and a limit in g/kWh is a mass per unit work. A ppm number cannot be compared with either until it is turned into a mass with the gas volume and density, as the second worked example does.

Cycles in use

Indian cars are certified on the Modified Indian Driving Cycle (MIDC), built on the European NEDC trace: four urban parts of 195 s with speeds up to 50 km/h, then an extra-urban part of 400 s up to 120 km/h, 1180 s and 11.0 km in all. Before that, small vehicles were tested on the slower Indian Driving Cycle. BS-VI two-wheelers use the Worldwide Motorcycle Test Cycle (WMTC). Europe has replaced the NEDC by the WLTC, with longer, faster and more dynamic phases; the United States uses the FTP-75, which weights a cold start and a hot start. Heavy engines use steady and transient engine cycles, such as the WHSC and WHTC.

The cycle matters because speed, acceleration and idle time decide how hot the flame and the catalyst become. A slow urban cycle with a cold start gives more CO and HC per km; a fast, hard cycle gives more NOx. The same car gives different g/km on different traces, which is why a result is meaningless without the name of its cycle.

India’s path and in-service checks

India went to BS-IV nationwide in April 2017 and skipped BS-V to reach BS-VI in April 2020. Fuel sulphur fell from 50 ppm to 10 ppm, which the catalysts and filters of BS-VI need. BS-VI added particle limits for diesel cars and on-board diagnostics; its second stage, from April 2023, added real driving emission (RDE) checks with portable equipment on public roads. RDE does not replace the laboratory cycle; it checks that the car stays clean away from the script.

Two other rules are often confused with emission limits. Corporate average fuel economy (CAFE) norms cap the average fuel consumption, and so the CO2 per km, of all the cars a maker sells; they are not a pollutant limit. The pollution under control (PUC) certificate is a quick in-use check: CO and HC at idle for petrol vehicles and free-acceleration smoke for diesels. It is not a type-approval test.

Worked example

Question. A petrol car is certified over the 11.0 km cycle. Its bags hold 0.66 g of NOx, and the BS-VI limit is 0.06 g/km. Find the result. The maker wants a 20 % margin below the limit: find the target in g/km, the NOx mass allowed per test and how much must be removed.

  4. Reduction needed: 0.66 − 0.528 = 0.132 g per test, that is 20 % of the present NOx.

Answer. The car emits 0.060 g/km, exactly at the limit; for a 20 % margin it must emit at most 0.528 g per test, 0.132 g less than now.

Question. In a CVS test the diluted exhaust totals 150 m³ at standard conditions. The bag reads 3.0 ppm NOx and the dilution-air bag 0.2 ppm.   • Forgetting the cold start, where much of a cycle’s CO and HC is made.
  • Treating a PUC certificate as a type-approval result, or CAFE as an emission limit.

6.6 Alternative fuels: CNG, hydrogen and others

An alternative fuel changes three things at once: how much carbon it carries per unit of energy, its octane or cetane number, and how it must be stored. India imports most of its crude oil, so energy security and city air push the search for substitutes. A fuel with more hydrogen and less carbon gives less CO₂ per megajoule; a high octane number lets a spark-ignition engine run a higher compression ratio; but a gas fuel may need a 200 bar cylinder. You cannot judge a fuel on one property.

You need: octane and cetane numbers and knock (Combustion in SI and CI engines).

CNG and LPG

CNG (compressed natural gas) is mostly methane, 85 to 95 per cent, stored at about 200 bar in steel or composite cylinders. Methane has a research octane number (RON) near 120 and a lower heating value (LHV) of 50 MJ/kg, so a dedicated CNG engine can run a higher compression ratio without knock. It enters as a gas, so it mixes well and makes almost no soot, and it has the lowest carbon per unit energy of any hydrocarbon. The unburnt HC from a CNG engine is mostly methane, which is hard to oxidise and needs a specially coated catalyst; methane is also a strong greenhouse gas, so leaks matter. Delhi’s buses and auto-rickshaws moved to CNG in the early 2000s.

LPG (liquefied petroleum gas) is propane and butane, liquid at a few bar (up to about 8 bar at room temperature), so it stores in a simple tank. Its RON is about 105 and it burns cleanly, with modestly lower CO2 per MJ than petrol. It comes from refineries and from natural gas processing.

CO₂ per MJ = (n × 44.0 ÷ M) ÷ LHV

n is the moles of CO₂ per mole of fuel (its carbon atoms); M the fuel’s molar mass in g/mol; 44.0 the molar mass of CO₂; LHV in MJ/kg. The result is in kg CO₂ per MJ.

Alcohols, biodiesel and other fuels

Ethanol has RON about 109 and LHV about 27 MJ/kg, so a litre carries about two-thirds of the energy of petrol. Its large heat of vaporisation cools the charge, which helps against knock but makes cold starting harder. India sells petrol blended with ethanol (E10 and E20); blends need compatible seals and hoses, because ethanol attacks some rubbers and corrodes some metals when water is present. Methanol is similar, but more toxic and corrosive.

Biodiesel is made by transesterification: vegetable oil or animal fat reacts with methanol over a catalyst to give fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) and glycerol. Its cetane number is about 50 to 60 and its LHV about 38 MJ/kg. The oxygen in the molecule cuts soot, CO and HC, but NOx often rises slightly. Raw vegetable oil is not biodiesel: it is far too viscous for an injector.

Other fuels are used in special cases. DME (dimethyl ether) is a gas at room conditions that liquefies at about 5 bar; its cetane number is about 55 and it burns almost without soot. Biogas is 50 to 70 per cent methane with CO2, cleaned before use. Producer gas from burning biomass in limited air is about half nitrogen, with CO and H2; its LHV is only about 4 to 6 MJ per normal cubic metre. Ammonia carries no carbon but ignites poorly and needs a pilot fuel.

Hydrogen, and the fuels side by side

Hydrogen has the highest LHV per kg, about 120 MJ/kg, and no carbon, so burning it gives no fuel CO2, CO, HC or soot. It burns in air from about 4 to 75 per cent by volume, needs a minimum ignition energy about a tenth that of petrol, and has a fast flame. So the design problems are pre-ignition, backfire into the intake and NOx: the flame is hot and the nitrogen comes from the air. Hydrogen engines run lean or use direct injection. It is stored as gas at 350 or 700 bar, or as liquid at about 20 K. Its climate benefit depends on how it is made: from natural gas (grey), with the CO2 captured (blue), or by electrolysis with renewable power (green).

6.7 Dual fuel and multi-fuel engines

A dual-fuel engine burns two fuels in the same cycle: a gas that carries most of the energy, and a small diesel pilot that lights it. Methane and most gas fuels have a cetane number near zero, so compression alone does not ignite them in the time the piston allows; the pilot supplies the ignition. A multi-fuel engine is something else: one engine that runs on any one of several fuels, one at a time. Dual fuel is used in CNG and biogas gensets, converted trucks and LNG ships; multi-fuel in military and farm engines.

You need: ignition delay and diesel knock (Combustion in SI and CI engines), and the gas fuels of alternative fuels.

In a dual-fuel engine the gas does most of the work and the diesel only lights it. A bi-fuel car carries two fuels but never burns them together. A multi-fuel engine promises fuel flexibility, not a mixture of fuels.

Metering the gas and lighting it

The gas is mixed with the air before the intake valve, which is called fumigation. A venturi mixer draws gas in with the pressure drop at its throat; a gas carburettor adds a zero-pressure regulator and a metering valve; timed port injection through solenoid gas injectors gives the best control and the least risk of backfire in the manifold. The cycle then runs:

  1. A lean gas–air charge enters and is compressed.
  2. At a compression ratio of about 17 the charge reaches roughly 800 to 860 K, near methane’s self-ignition temperature of about 810 K, but at that temperature methane takes far longer to ignite than the few milliseconds the piston stays near top dead centre.
  3. A small diesel pilot is injected near top dead centre. Diesel has a short delay, so it ignites first.
  4. The burning pilot sprays act like many spark plugs and start flames through the gas–air charge; expansion follows as in a diesel.

The pilot is quoted as its share of the fuel energy. In converted truck and genset diesels it is often 10 to 30 per cent at high load and more at light load; large purpose-built gas engines use a micro-pilot of about 1 per cent. A common-rail injector can meter such a small pilot accurately; a mechanical pump cannot go much below its idle delivery.

Substitution, knock and the light-load penalty

Two figures describe how much diesel the gas replaces. The diesel substitution is the share of the diesel mass saved: (ṁd − ṁdf) ÷ ṁd, where ṁd is the diesel flow on diesel alone and ṁdf the pilot flow in dual-fuel mode at the same power. The gas energy share is ṁgLHVg ÷ (ṁgLHVg + ṁdfLHVd), where ṁg is the gas flow. They differ because the efficiency of the engine changes when gas is used.

At high load the gas share and the premixed charge are largest. If the pilot is early, the intake hot or the compression ratio high, the end gas ignites before the flames arrive: knock. Methane’s high octane number delays it, but knock still caps the substitution at full load.

At light load the gas–air charge is very lean. Flames from the pilot travel slowly and die in the leanest parts, so some gas leaves unburnt: CO and HC rise and the efficiency falls. The unburnt methane is called methane slip. It comes mainly from this incomplete burning, from crevices and quench layers, and partly from fresh charge that short-circuits to the exhaust during valve overlap. Methane warms the climate far more per kg than CO2, so slip matters more than its HC number suggests.

Multi-fuel engines

A multi-fuel engine is a diesel built to accept fuels of different ignition quality. Its compression ratio is fixed at build, so it is a compromise: high enough (often above 20) for a low-cetane fuel to ignite, but limited by peak pressure. The injection pump must deliver the same energy from fuels of different density and viscosity; some military engines used a fuel-density compensator on the pump for this. Four properties set the fuel range: cetane number (ignition delay and knock), viscosity (spray and pump leakage), lubricity (pump and injector wear) and volatility (vapour lock in a hot pump).

Producer-gas dual fuel is the classic rural set: a biomass gasifier feeds a diesel genset. Producer gas has a low heating value and is about half nitrogen, so the engine is derated and the pilot share stays high, often a third of the energy or more.

Worked example

Question. A dual-fuel engine gives 40 kW. On diesel alone it burns 10 kg/h. At the same power in dual-fuel mode it burns 2.5 kg/h of diesel (LHV 42.5 MJ/kg) and 6.6 kg/h of CNG (LHV 50 MJ/kg). Find the diesel substitution, the gas energy share and the brake thermal efficiency in both modes.

  1. Diesel substitution: (10 − 2.5) ÷ 10 = 0.75, that is 75 %.
  2. Diesel-only fuel energy: 10 × 42.5 = 425 MJ/h.
  3. Dual-fuel energies: diesel 2.5 × 42.5 = 106.25 MJ/h; gas 6.6 × 50 = 330 MJ/h; total 436.25 MJ/h.
  4. Gas energy share: 330 ÷ 436.25 = 0.756, that is 75.6 %.
  5. Brake power in MJ/h: 40 kW × 3.6 = 144 MJ/h.
  6. Efficiency on diesel: 144 ÷ 425 = 0.339 (33.9 %); in dual fuel: 144 ÷ 436.25 = 0.330 (33.0 %).

Answer. The gas replaces 75 % of the diesel mass and supplies 75.6 % of the energy; efficiency falls slightly, from 33.9 % to 33.0 %.

Exam traps

  • Mixing up the definitions: diesel saved (75 % above), gas energy share (75.6 %) and gas mass share (6.6 ÷ 9.1 = 72.5 %) are three different numbers; say which one you give.
  • Calling a CNG-petrol car dual fuel: it is bi-fuel, the fuels are switched, never burnt together.
  • Treating the pilot as a fixed 10 %: it is set at each operating point, larger at light load.
  • Assuming more gas is always better: at light load it brings CO, HC and methane slip; at high load, knock.
  • Saying methane slip comes only from valve overlap: most of it is gas the lean flames never burnt.

6.8 Stratified charge and low heat rejection (LHR) engines

Two ideas from engine history are back in the exam: the stratified charge engine and the low heat rejection (LHR) engine. A stratified charge engine places an easy-to-ignite rich mixture at the spark plug and a lean mixture everywhere else, so a spark-ignition engine can run lean overall and control its load without a throttle. An LHR engine insulates the combustion chamber so less heat leaks to the coolant. Both aim at efficiency; each pays for it in emissions or reliability.

You need: carburettor mixture limits, gasoline direct injection (Fuel supply and ignition systems), and engine heat balance (Engine testing and performance).

Why stratify the charge

A homogeneous petrol mixture can be lit by a spark only between about φ = 0.7 and 1.3. To give part load, a normal petrol engine therefore keeps the mixture near stoichiometric and lowers the amount of it with a throttle. The throttle makes the piston pull against a partial vacuum on every intake stroke: this pumping loss is why petrol engines are inefficient at light load.

A stratified charge is deliberately non-uniform: rich (φ about 1 to 1.3) at the plug, very lean or even pure air elsewhere. The spark lights the rich zone easily, and its flame burns into the lean rest. Load is then controlled by the amount of fuel, as in a diesel (quality control), with the throttle wide open. The overall air–fuel ratio can be 20 to 40 or more. The gains:

  • Less pumping loss, because the throttle stays open.
  • A higher ratio of specific heats in the lean charge and less heat loss from the cooler gas near the walls, so a higher cycle efficiency.
  • Less tendency to knock in the lean end gas, so a higher compression ratio is possible.
  • Low CO, since oxygen is plentiful.

How the charge is stratified

Divided-chamber (pre-chamber) engines. A small pre-chamber in the head, holding the spark plug, gets a rich mixture; the main chamber gets a lean one. In the three-valve divided-chamber engines of the 1970s a third, small intake valve fed the pre-chamber from a separate carburettor circuit. The burning rich mixture shoots out as flame jets, a torch, and burns the lean main charge. Such engines met the strict US emission rules of 1975 without a catalytic converter. Modern racing and some production engines use a similar pre-chamber jet ignition.

Open-chamber, direct-injection engines. Fuel is injected straight into the cylinder late in compression so that a rich cloud reaches the plug at the moment of the spark. Early open-chamber systems of the 1970s used strong swirl to carry the spray past the plug. Modern gasoline direct injection (GDI) does it three ways: wall-guided, a shaped piston bowl deflects the spray to the plug; air-guided, tumble or swirl carries it; spray-guided, a precise piezo injector places the spray edge right at the plug. At high load these engines switch to a homogeneous λ = 1 charge for full power.

The costs: HC rises from the over-lean edges of the cloud, soot can form in the richest zone around the injector, the spark plug can foul, and, above all, a lean exhaust cannot use a three-way catalyst for NOx. Stratified GDI cars therefore needed a lean NOx trap and low-sulphur fuel; when particle and NOx limits tightened, most makers went back to λ = 1 direct injection.

Low heat rejection (LHR) engines

About a quarter to a third of the fuel energy in a diesel goes into the coolant. An LHR engine (also called an insulated or, in its extreme form, an “adiabatic” engine) reduces this by insulating the surfaces the hot gas touches: piston crown, cylinder head, valve faces and liner top. The usual method is a thermal barrier coating, typically 0.3 to 0.5 mm of yttria-stabilised zirconia (a ceramic of low conductivity, about 1 to 2 W/(m·K)) sprayed on a metallic bond coat. Other methods are ceramic inserts and air-gap pistons, which trap an insulating layer of air under a separate crown.

The expected gain is smaller than it looks. Heat kept from the coolant mostly leaves with the exhaust, not as piston work, so the extra brake efficiency is a few per cent at best unless the hot exhaust drives a turbocharger or a turbo-compound turbine. The hot walls also heat the incoming air, lowering volumetric efficiency, and raise the gas temperature, which raises NOx. The lubricating oil on the liner breaks down above about 250 °C, which limits how hot the walls may run.

LHR suits the diesel, which likes hot walls: the ignition delay shortens and diesel knock falls, and low-cetane fuels such as alcohols and vegetable oils ignite more easily. It does not suit the petrol engine, where hot walls cause knock.

ΔT = q t ÷ k

ΔT is the temperature drop across the coating in K; q the heat flux through it in W/m²; t its thickness in m; k its thermal conductivity in W/(m·K).

Worked example

Question. In a divided-chamber engine each cycle puts 0.05 g of air with fuel at an air–fuel ratio of 12 into the pre-chamber and 0.50 g of air at an air–fuel ratio of 22 into the main chamber. Find the fuel in each chamber, the overall air–fuel ratio and λ (stoichiometric 14.7).

  1. Pre-chamber fuel: 0.05 ÷ 12 = 0.00417 g.
  2. Main-chamber fuel: 0.50 ÷ 22 = 0.02273 g.
  3. Totals: air 0.55 g, fuel 0.00417 + 0.02273 = 0.02690 g.
  4. Overall air–fuel ratio: 0.55 ÷ 0.02690 = 20.4.
  5. λ = 20.4 ÷ 14.7 = 1.39; the pre-chamber alone has λ = 12 ÷ 14.7 = 0.82 (φ = 1.22).

Answer. The charge is lean overall, air–fuel ratio 20.4 (λ = 1.39), while the plug fires a rich mixture at λ = 0.82.

Worked example 2

Question. A 1.5 L four-stroke petrol engine at 2000 rev/min gives 15 kW at part load. Throttled, the intake manifold is at 0.50 bar and the exhaust at 1.05 bar; run stratified and unthrottled, the intake is at 0.95 bar. Estimate the pumping power each way and the saving as a share of the output.

  1. Pumping work per cycle ≈ (pexhaust − pintake) × swept volume.
  2. Throttled: (1.05 − 0.50) × 10⁵ Pa × 1.5 × 10⁻³ m³ = 82.5 J per cycle.
  3. Unthrottled: (1.05 − 0.95) × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 15.0 J per cycle.
  4. A four-stroke engine completes one cycle every two revolutions: 2000 ÷ 120 = 16.7 cycles per second.
  5. Pumping power: 82.5 × 16.7 = 1375 W throttled; 15.0 × 16.7 = 250 W unthrottled.
  6. Saving: 1375 − 250 = 1125 W, that is 1.125 ÷ 15 = 7.5 % of the output.

Answer. Removing the throttle saves about 1.1 kW of pumping power, 7.5 % of the 15 kW output, before any gain from the lean cycle itself.

Worked example 3

Question. A piston crown carries a mean heat flux of 0.5 MW/m². Find the temperature drop across (a) a 0.5 mm zirconia coating with k = 1.0 W/(m·K) and (b) 10 mm of aluminium crown with k = 150 W/(m·K), for the same flux.

  1. Coating: ΔT = q t ÷ k = 0.5 × 10⁶ × 0.0005 ÷ 1.0 = 250 K.
  2. Aluminium: ΔT = 0.5 × 10⁶ × 0.010 ÷ 150 = 33 K.
  3. A layer 20 times thinner holds almost eight times the temperature drop, because its conductivity is 150 times lower.

Answer. The coating holds about 250 K and the metal only 33 K, so the coated surface runs much hotter and less heat passes; in practice the flux itself falls too, so this is a first estimate.

Exam traps

  • Saying a stratified charge engine runs a rich mixture: it is rich only at the plug; overall it is lean.
  • Fitting only a three-way catalyst to a stratified lean engine: NOx needs a lean NOx trap or SCR.
  • Expecting the coolant heat saved by an LHR engine to appear as brake power: most of it leaves in the exhaust unless it is recovered.
  • Recommending LHR for a petrol engine: hot walls cause knock; LHR helps the diesel.
  • Forgetting that hot LHR walls raise NOx and lower volumetric efficiency.

6.9 Advanced combustion: HCCI, RCCI and low temperature combustion

Low-temperature combustion (LTC) is a way to burn fuel that avoids NOx and soot at the same time. It premixes and dilutes the charge so that no part of it is rich enough to make soot or hot enough to make NOx. The price is control: ignition is set by the chemistry of the charge, not by a spark or an injection pulse. HCCI and RCCI are the two forms named in exam syllabi; PCCI lies between them and the ordinary diesel.

You need: the soot and NOx routes (formation of CO, HC and NOx, smoke and particulate matter) and the dual-fuel idea (dual fuel and multi-fuel engines).

The φ–T map and the soot–NOx trade-off

Think of every small parcel of gas in the cylinder as a point on a map of local equivalence ratio φ against local temperature T. Soot forms in an island of rich, hot states, roughly φ above 2 at 1600 to 2600 K. NO forms in a band of hot states with oxygen to spare, roughly above 2200 K at φ near or below 1. Both regions are about local gas, not the average of the cylinder.

A conventional diesel spray crosses both. Its core passes through the soot island; its flame sheet, burning near stoichiometric, passes through the NOx band. Cooling the charge with EGR moves the flame out of the NOx band but leaves the core rich, so soot survives; adding air or retarding does the opposite. This is the soot–NOx trade-off. To beat it, the charge must be both lean (or well mixed, so no parcel is rich) and cool (peak below about 2000 K, held there by dilution). That region is the LTC window.

Efficiency can still be diesel-like: the engine runs unthrottled at a high compression ratio, and the lean, cool gas loses less heat. The difficulty is ignition, because a lean, cool mixture ignites slowly and its timing drifts with temperature.

HCCI: homogeneous charge compression ignition

HCCI mixes fuel and air before compression, like a petrol engine, and ignites it by compression heat, like a diesel. The charge is lean, φ about 0.2 to 0.5, or diluted with EGR. There is no spark and no flame front: the whole charge reaches self-ignition almost together and burns in a few crank degrees. Its timing follows the chemistry:

τ = A exp(E/RT)

τ is the ignition delay; A a constant for the fuel and pressure; E/R the activation temperature, about 15 000 K here; T the charge temperature in K. A small rise in T shortens τ sharply.

The control levers are all indirect: intake air temperature, the EGR rate, hot residual gas trapped by variable valve timing, the compression ratio and the fuel’s reactivity. None acts on a single cycle like a spark does.

  • Gains: near-zero NOx and soot; diesel-like efficiency; low pumping loss.
  • Costs: high HC and CO at light load, because the coolest parts of the charge, near the walls and in crevices, never finish burning; violent pressure rise (ringing) at high load; a narrow load range; hard cold starts.

No production car runs pure HCCI. The nearest is spark-controlled compression ignition (used in some production petrol engines since 2019), where a small spark-lit flame compresses the rest of a lean charge into self-ignition at the chosen moment.

PCCI and RCCI

PCCI (premixed charge compression ignition) uses one fuel injected early, so it has time to mix before it ignites. The charge is partly premixed: soot is lower than in a normal diesel because the rich core is smaller, but some φ gradient remains, and ignition is still set by chemistry.

RCCI (reactivity controlled compression ignition) uses two fuels of different reactivity. A low-reactivity fuel (petrol, ethanol or natural gas) is port-injected to make a lean premixed charge. A high-reactivity fuel (diesel) is injected directly, once or twice, early enough to mix partly. Ignition starts where the diesel share is highest and spreads through the gradient of reactivity, which spreads out the heat release. Changing the ratio of the two fuels from cycle to cycle, and the diesel timing, sets the burn: more port fuel makes the charge less reactive and later. This gives RCCI a wider load range than HCCI and a real control lever. RCCI is still a research and demonstration strategy, not yet in production.

RCCI looks like the dual-fuel engine but works differently: there the diesel pilot lights a flame that travels through the gas; in RCCI the whole charge self-ignites in sequence, from more to less reactive parcels, with no flame front. A useful single number is the global reactivity, the primary reference fuel (PRF) number of the blend in the cylinder; with iso-octane (PRF 100) and n-heptane (PRF 0) it equals the iso-octane mass share × 100.

Worked example

Question. Two HCCI runs have the same charge pressure. Run 1 has a charge temperature of 900 K, run 2 of 950 K. The ignition delay follows τ = A exp(E/RT) with E/R = 15 000 K. Find τ900 ÷ τ950 and say what it means for control.

  1. Ratio: τ900 ÷ τ950 = exp[(E/R)(1/900 − 1/950)], because A cancels.
  2. 1/900 = 1.1111 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; 1/950 = 1.0526 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; difference 5.848 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹.
  3. Exponent: 15 000 × 5.848 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.877.
  4. exp(0.877) = 2.40.
  5. A 50 K warmer charge ignites 2.4 times sooner, so a few kelvin of intake drift move the burn by crank degrees.

Answer. τ900 ÷ τ950 ≈ 2.4: HCCI needs intake temperature held within a few kelvin.

Worked example 2

Question. A research RCCI engine gets 24 mg of iso-octane (PRF 100) per cycle through the port and 6 mg of n-heptane (PRF 0) by direct injection. Find the premixed ratio and the global PRF. At higher load the controller moves to 27 mg and 3 mg: what happens to the global reactivity and the burn?

  1. Premixed ratio = port fuel ÷ total fuel = 24 ÷ (24 + 6) = 0.80.
  2. Global PRF = 100 × 0.80 + 0 × 0.20 = 80.
  3. At higher load: 27 ÷ 30 = 0.90, global PRF 90.
  4. A higher PRF means a less reactive charge: ignition comes later and the heat release is slower, which keeps the pressure rise rate down at the higher load. (The two fuels have nearly equal LHV, about 44 MJ/kg, so the total energy is unchanged.)

Answer. The premixed ratio is 0.80 and the global PRF 80; moving to PRF 90 at high load retards and slows the burn, which is how RCCI avoids the ringing that limits HCCI.

Exam traps

  • Calling HCCI a lean spark-ignition engine: there is no spark and no flame front; the charge self-ignites.
  • Saying cooling alone removes soot: a rich parcel still makes soot; LTC needs a lean, well-mixed charge as well as a low temperature.
  • Saying LTC makes NOx exactly zero: it keeps the peak below the fast NO range, so NOx is very low, not zero.
  • Calling ringing ordinary knock: it is a very fast pressure rise from the whole charge burning at once.
  • Describing RCCI as a pilot lighting a gas: its two fuels are chosen for their reactivity gradient, and the ratio is changed with load.

6.10 Combustion visualization: optical engines and laser diagnostics

Combustion is fast, hot and hidden inside metal. To see it, researchers build an engine with quartz or sapphire windows, light the inside with a laser, and film the light with a high-speed camera. Only a few syllabi teach this, because the hardware is costly. It is how spray shapes, flame speeds, soot clouds and the low-temperature strategies of HCCI and RCCI were understood.

You need: flame propagation (Combustion in SI and CI engines) and the soot steps of smoke and particulate matter.

An engine you can look into

Windows can sit in the head, the liner or the piston crown. Sapphire is stronger and passes more infrared; quartz is cheaper and passes ultraviolet, which laser methods need. In the Bowditch design the piston is extended and carries a window in its crown and a 45° mirror below it. Light from the chamber travels down the cylinder axis through the crown, and the mirror turns it sideways to a camera beside the engine, so the camera sees the whole bore from below.

An optical engine is not a production engine: its compression ratio is often lower, its walls are colder or hotter, the chamber is simplified and it is fired for short runs because soot and oil foul the windows. Those differences, not the short runs, change its cycle-to-cycle variation, so its images show mechanisms, not production numbers.

Light tuned to what you want to see

  • High-speed imaging of the flame’s own light shows flame growth and glowing soot.
  • Schlieren and shadowgraph turn density gradients into brightness, showing the flame front and the vapour edge of a spray.
  • Mie scattering from droplets is strong and shows the liquid spray; Rayleigh scattering from gas molecules is weak and gives density or temperature after calibration.
  • Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) excites one chosen molecule (fuel tracer, OH, NO, formaldehyde) and maps where it is.
  • Laser-induced incandescence and two-colour pyrometry give soot amount and soot temperature.
  • Particle image velocimetry (PIV) correlates two images of seed particles to map the flow field in a plane; laser Doppler anemometry gives the velocity at one point, so swirl or tumble needs the point moved across the cylinder.

A laser sheet, not a beam, lights a plane: cylindrical lenses spread the beam into a sheet under a millimetre thick. Common sources are the Nd:YAG laser, whose 1064 nm output is frequency-doubled to 532 nm (green) or quadrupled to 266 nm, and excimer lasers in the ultraviolet, such as XeCl at 308 nm. The flame expansion speed from two frames is S = Δr ÷ Δt, where Δr is the growth of the flame radius and Δt the time between frames. It includes the push of the expanding burned gas, so it is larger than the burning velocity of the mixture.

Worked example

Question. Two high-speed frames are 0.1 ms apart. The flame radius grows from 8 mm to 12 mm. The engine runs at 1500 rev/min. Find the flame expansion speed and the crank angle between the frames.

  1. Δr = 12 − 8 = 4 mm = 4 × 10⁻³ m; Δt = 0.1 ms = 1 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  2. S = Δr ÷ Δt = 4 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1 × 10⁻⁴ = 40 m/s.
  3. Crank speed: 1500 rev/min × 360° ÷ 60 s = 9000 °/s.
  4. Crank angle between frames: 9000 × 1 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.9°.

Answer. The flame front expands at 40 m/s, and the frames are 0.9° of crank angle apart.

Exam traps

  • Quoting an optical-engine flame speed as a production value: the compression ratio, walls and chamber differ.
  • Treating a line-of-sight image as a slice: direct imaging and extinction add up light along the whole path.
  • Swapping Mie and Rayleigh: Mie comes from droplets and is strong; Rayleigh from gas molecules and is weak.
  • Giving 532 nm as the natural output of Nd:YAG: it is the doubled frequency of 1064 nm.

Takeaways

  1. CO comes from too little oxygen, HC from fuel the flame never reaches (crevices first), and NO from a hot flame with spare oxygen; NO peaks near φ = 0.9.
  2. Soot forms in the hot rich core of a diesel spray and mostly burns out; PM is what survives plus SOF, sulphate and ash, and it trades against NOx.
  3. In-cylinder methods (retard, EGR, injection strategy) stop pollutants forming; after-treatment converts them, and a lean diesel needs DOC, DPF and SCR because a three-way catalyst works only at λ = 1.
  4. Each gas is measured by its own physics: infrared absorption, ozone chemiluminescence, flame ionisation and light extinction; a ppm reading becomes g/km or g/kWh only through mass.
  5. A limit means nothing without its cycle: India’s cars are tested on the 11.0 km MIDC and held to BS-VI limits equal to Euro 6.
  6. Gas fuels cut CO₂ per MJ (methane 22 % below petrol) but carry four to seven times less energy per litre of tank.
  7. A dual-fuel engine lights a lean gas charge with a diesel pilot; it knocks at high load and slips methane at light load.
  8. A stratified charge engine fires a rich zone in a lean charge to drop the throttle; an LHR engine insulates the chamber, but most of the saved heat goes to the exhaust.
  9. HCCI and RCCI keep every parcel lean and below about 2000 K to avoid both soot and NOx; RCCI uses two fuels of different reactivity as its control lever.

Understand Explain how carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbons and oxides of nitrogen form in a spark-ignition engine, and show how the equivalence ratio affects each. 13 marks

Points to cover. CO: too little oxygen on the rich side; dissociation and frozen CO oxidation in expansion; HC: crevices (largest), oil film, quench layer, liquid fuel and misfire, valve overlap; partial burn-up in expansion and exhaust; NOx: thermal NO by the Zeldovich mechanism; needs high temperature, oxygen atoms and time; Effect of φ: CO and HC high rich, NO peaks near φ = 0.9, HC rises again at lean misfire; Sketch of the three curves against air–fuel ratio.

Model answer.

  1. Define the mixture: φ = actual fuel–air ratio ÷ stoichiometric fuel–air ratio. For petrol the stoichiometric air–fuel ratio is about 14.7, so φ = 1.1 is an air–fuel ratio of 13.4 and φ = 0.9 one of 16.3.
  2. CO formation. Carbon in the fuel first forms CO, which burns on to CO2 mainly by CO + OH → CO2 + H. On the rich side (φ > 1) there is not enough oxygen to finish this step, so CO rises steeply with richness, to a few per cent at φ = 1.2. Near stoichiometric, hot burned gas holds some CO from the dissociation CO2 ⇌ CO + ½O2; as the gas cools in expansion the CO-burning reactions slow and freeze, leaving a few tenths of a per cent. In lean mixtures CO is low.
  3. HC formation. HC is fuel that escapes the flame. (i) Crevices: the top-land gap, ring grooves and plug thread fill with mixture during compression; the flame cannot enter a gap this narrow, and the gas returns during expansion. This is the largest source. (ii) Oil film: fuel vapour dissolves in the liner oil at high pressure and comes out after the flame. (iii) Wall quenching: the flame dies about 0.1 mm from cold walls. (iv) Liquid fuel on cold walls and misfire, mainly at cold start. (v) Valve overlap: some fresh charge short-circuits to the exhaust. Part of this HC burns in the hot gas during expansion and in the exhaust port, which is why spark retard lowers HC.
  4. NOx formation. Thermal NO forms in the burned gas behind the flame by the Zeldovich mechanism: O + N2 → NO + N, N + O2 → NO + O, extended by N + OH → NO + H. The first step breaks the N≡N bond, so the rate rises very steeply above about 1800 K. NO therefore needs high peak temperature, oxygen atoms and residence time. Prompt NO in the flame front and fuel nitrogen add small amounts. In a petrol engine NO is almost all of the NOx.
  5. Effect of the equivalence ratio (the sketch shows the trends):
    MixtureCOHCNOx
    Rich, φ = 1.2high, several %highlow: no spare O2
    Stoichiometric, φ = 1about 0.5 %moderatehigh
    Slightly lean, φ = 0.9lowlowest regionpeak
    Very lean, φ ≈ 0.7lowrising: slow flames, misfirelow: cooler flame
  6. Sketch: three curves against air–fuel ratio 10 to 20; CO falls steeply from the rich side and flattens past 15; HC falls and rises again beyond about 18; NO is a hump peaking near 16.3, with a narrow band at 14.7 marking the three-way catalyst window.
  7. Other variables: spark advance, compression ratio and load raise peak temperature and NO; EGR and retard lower NO; a cold engine raises HC and CO.
  8. Conclusion: the three gases come from three different failures (oxygen shortage, fuel hidden from the flame, an over-hot flame), so no single setting of φ minimises all three. This is why petrol engines are held at λ = 1 and finished with a three-way catalyst.
Understand Describe the formation of soot in a diesel spray, and explain the difference between smoke, particulate matter and the soluble organic fraction. How is PM measured? 13 marks

Points to cover. Rich core φ 2 to 4, 1600–2600 K; Pyrolysis, nucleation, surface growth, coagulation, agglomeration, oxidation; PM = soot + SOF + sulphate + ash; smoke is the visible soot; Smoke colours and causes; Gravimetric PM with dilution at 47 °C; opacimeter and filter smoke number.

Model answer.

  1. Where soot forms: in a diesel spray the liquid core evaporates into a region where the local equivalence ratio is 2 to 4 and the temperature 1600 to 2600 K. There is too little oxygen to burn the fuel, so it breaks down instead.
  2. Six steps. (1) Pyrolysis: fuel molecules crack into small pieces such as acetylene and build polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH). (2) Nucleation: PAH stick into the first particles of 1 to 2 nm. (3) Surface growth: acetylene adds to the surface; mass rises fast. (4) Coagulation: small spheres merge into primary particles of 10 to 30 nm. (5) Agglomeration: the spheres stick into chains a few hundred nm long. (6) Oxidation: when the jet mixes with air, OH and O2 burn the soot. Most of the soot formed is burnt; exhaust soot is what survives step 6.
  3. Particulate matter is defined by measurement: all material caught on a filter from diluted exhaust at 47 °C. It contains soot (dry carbon), the soluble organic fraction (unburnt fuel and lubricating oil that condense on the soot, often half the mass at light load), sulphate with bound water (from fuel sulphur) and ash (from oil additives).
  4. Smoke is the visible part. Black smoke is soot from too much fuel for the air; blue smoke is burning lubricating oil; white smoke is unburnt fuel droplets or water vapour, typical of a cold start. A clear exhaust can still carry SOF and sulphate, so smoke and PM are not the same.
  5. Gravimetric PM measurement. A sample of exhaust is diluted with filtered air (full-flow CVS or partial flow), cooled to 47 °C so the SOF condenses as it would in the air, and drawn through a conditioned filter. The filter is weighed on a microbalance before and after. Worked figure: 0.90 mg on the filter from 0.30 m³ of gas diluted 8 times gives 3.0 × 8 = 24 mg/m³ raw; with 0.05 m³/s of exhaust that is 1.2 mg/s, and at 40 kW, 4.32 g/h ÷ 40 = 0.108 g/kWh.
  6. Smoke measurement. An opacimeter passes light across the exhaust and reports opacity N or the absorption coefficient k = −ln(1 − N) ÷ L; 45 % opacity on a 0.430 m path is k = 1.39 m⁻¹. A filter-paper meter reads the blackness of paper after a fixed volume as a Bosch or filter smoke number (0 to 10). Both see mainly soot.
  7. Factors: higher injection pressure, swirl and low-sulphur fuel lower PM; higher load, retard and EGR raise it. Retard and EGR are the NOx controls, which gives the NOx–PM trade-off.
  8. Conclusion: soot is born in the rich core and mostly burnt later; PM adds the liquid and ash parts and must be weighed, while smoke meters see only the black part.
Understand Classify emission control methods into in-cylinder treatments and after-treatment, and describe four devices or methods of each class. 13 marks

Points to cover. In-cylinder: retard, EGR, injection strategy, λ control, chamber design, crankcase and evaporative control; After-treatment: TWC, DOC, DPF, SCR, LNT; Reaction or mechanism and operating window of each; Matching to petrol and diesel engines; The two classes are used together.

Model answer.

  1. Classification: in-cylinder methods reduce what the engine forms; after-treatment converts or traps it between the exhaust valve and the tailpipe. Modern engines need both.
  2. In-cylinder 1, spark or injection retard: the burn moves later, the peak temperature falls and NO falls; costs fuel, and in a diesel raises PM.
  3. In-cylinder 2, exhaust gas recirculation: exhaust in the intake replaces oxygen and adds CO2 and H2O, which absorb heat, so the flame is cooler. Rate = (CO2 intake − CO2 ambient) ÷ (CO2 exhaust − CO2 ambient); with 0.04, 1.80 and 12.0 % it is 14.7 %. Cooled EGR packs more mass; too much raises PM and HC.
  4. In-cylinder 3, injection strategy (CI): high common-rail pressure for fine sprays; pilot injection to shorten the delay and soften the premixed burst; post injection to burn soot or heat the filter.
  5. In-cylinder 4, closed-loop λ control and chamber design (SI): an oxygen sensor holds λ = 1 for the catalyst; a compact chamber with small crevices lowers HC. Crankcase ventilation returns blow-by gas to the intake, and a charcoal canister holds tank vapour.
  6. After-treatment 1, three-way catalyst: Pt, Pd, Rh on a honeycomb; CO + ½O2 → CO2, HC + O2 → CO2 + H2O, 2NO + 2CO → N2 + 2CO2. Works only within about ±0.5 % of λ = 1 and above light-off, about 250 to 300 °C.
  7. After-treatment 2, diesel oxidation catalyst: burns CO, HC and part of the SOF in lean exhaust and turns NO into NO2 for the next devices.
  8. After-treatment 3, diesel particulate filter: a wall-flow honeycomb with alternate channels plugged; gas passes through porous walls and soot stays. Passive regeneration by NO2 at 250 to 400 °C, active at about 600 °C with a post injection; ash remains.
  9. After-treatment 4, selective catalytic reduction: AdBlue (32.5 % urea) gives NH3; 4NH3 + 4NO + O2 → 4N2 + 6H2O; works from about 200 °C; a slip catalyst removes leftover ammonia. The lean NOx trap is the alternative: it stores nitrate while lean and is purged rich.
  10. Matching:
    EngineLine
    Petrol at λ = 1TWC with two oxygen sensors
    Lean petrolTWC plus LNT
    DieselDOC → DPF → urea → SCR → slip catalyst
  11. Conclusion: in-cylinder methods usually trade one pollutant against another (retard and EGR: NOx down, PM up); after-treatment removes the remainder, and low-sulphur fuel keeps it working.
Understand With neat sketches, explain the working of the NDIR analyser, the chemiluminescence NOx analyser, the flame ionisation detector and a smoke meter. 13 marks

Points to cover. NDIR: source, chopper, sample and reference cells, gas-filled detector; CO 4.7 µm, CO₂ 4.3 µm; Beer–Lambert; CLD: NO + O₃ → NO₂* + O₂; photomultiplier; converter for NO₂; η correction; FID: hydrogen flame, ion current ∝ carbon atoms, ppmC; heated; Smoke meters: opacimeter (k) and filter paper (Bosch number); What each cannot measure.

Model answer.

  1. Sample handling first: probe, heated line at about 190 °C, soot filter; the FID takes hot wet gas; NDIR and CLD take dried gas; the smoke meter takes raw exhaust.
  2. NDIR (CO, CO2). Sketch: infrared source → rotating chopper → sample cell and reference cell side by side → detector chamber filled with the gas to be measured → amplifier and meter. The chopper alternates the beam; the gas-filled detector absorbs only the wavelengths of its own gas (CO near 4.7 µm, CO2 near 4.3 µm), so the difference between the two beams measures that gas without a prism. Absorption follows Beer–Lambert, I = I0e−acL; for example, a span gas of 2.0 % CO passing 70 % of the light and a sample passing 80 % give c = 2.0 × ln 0.80 ÷ ln 0.70 = 1.25 % CO. Water absorbs in the infrared, so the sample is dried.
  3. Chemiluminescence analyser (NO, NOx). Sketch: sample → switching valve (bypass or NO2→NO converter) → reaction chamber fed with ozone from an ozone generator → optical filter → photomultiplier → meter. NO + O3 → NO2* + O2; the excited molecule emits light in proportion to NO. Bypass gives NO; through the converter gives NOx. With 380 and 420 ppm and converter efficiency 0.92, NO2 = 40 ÷ 0.92 = 43.5 ppm and NOx = 423.5 ppm. Water and CO2 quench the light, so the sample is dried.
  4. Flame ionisation detector (HC). Sketch: sample and hydrogen fuel mixed → burner jet with air → collector electrode around the flame with a polarising voltage → electrometer. Organic carbon forms ions in the flame; the current is proportional to the number of carbon atoms, so the reading is ppmC (one ppm propane ≈ 3 ppmC). The FID is heated to about 190 °C so heavy HC do not condense; it does not see CO, CO2 or water and under-reads oxygenates.
  5. Smoke meters. Opacimeter (Hartridge type): a lamp and a photocell at the ends of a tube through which exhaust flows; opacity N = share of light blocked; k = −ln(1 − N) ÷ L. Filter-paper (Bosch) meter: a pump draws a fixed volume through white paper; a reflectometer reads the blackening on a 0 to 10 scale. Both respond mainly to soot, not PM mass.

Understand What is a stratified charge engine? With a neat sketch, explain the working of a divided-chamber (pre-chamber) stratified charge engine and of a direct-injection stratified engine, and list their merits and demerits. 13 marks

Points to cover. Rich at the plug, lean overall; quality control of load, throttle open; Ignition limits of a homogeneous mixture and pumping loss; Divided chamber: third valve, rich pre-chamber, flame jets; Open chamber DI: early swirl-carried systems; wall-, air-, spray-guided GDI; Merits: efficiency, low CO, lean knock margin; demerits: HC, soot, NOx without TWC, complexity.

Model answer.

  1. Definition: a stratified charge has a mixture strength that varies through the chamber, rich (φ about 1 to 1.3) at the spark plug and lean elsewhere, so the charge is lean overall (air–fuel ratio 20 to 40 or more).
  2. Why: a homogeneous petrol mixture can be ignited only between about φ = 0.7 and 1.3. A normal engine therefore keeps φ near 1 and reduces the quantity with a throttle. The throttle causes pumping loss. Worked figure for a 1.5 L engine at 2000 rev/min: throttled at 0.50 bar intake and 1.05 bar exhaust, pumping work per cycle = 0.55 × 10⁵ × 1.5 × 10⁻³ = 82.5 J, or 82.5 × 16.7 = 1375 W; unthrottled at 0.95 bar, 15 J or 250 W. The saving, 1125 W, is 7.5 % of a 15 kW output.
  3. Divided-chamber engine (sketch: cylinder, head with a small pre-chamber holding the plug and a small auxiliary valve, nozzle to the main chamber, main intake valve, piston). In the three-valve type the auxiliary valve admits a rich mixture to the pre-chamber while the main valve admits a lean one. The plug fires the rich mixture easily; it burns and its pressure drives flame jets (a torch) through the nozzle into the lean main charge, which they ignite at many points.
  4. Worked figure: 0.05 g of air at an air–fuel ratio of 12 in the pre-chamber (0.00417 g fuel) and 0.50 g at 22 in the main chamber (0.02273 g) give 0.55 ÷ 0.0269 = 20.4 overall, λ = 1.39, while the plug sees λ = 0.82.
  5. Open-chamber direct-injection engine. Fuel is injected into the cylinder late in compression so a rich cloud reaches the plug at the spark. Early systems of the 1970s used strong swirl to carry the spray past the plug. Modern stratified GDI uses a shaped piston bowl (wall-guided), the air motion (air-guided) or a precise piezo injector next to the plug (spray-guided). At high load the engine switches to an early injection and a homogeneous λ = 1 charge.
  6. Merits: (1) little or no throttling, so low pumping loss and better part-load economy; (2) lean, cooler gas with a higher ratio of specific heats, so a higher cycle efficiency; (3) lean end gas resists knock, allowing a higher compression ratio; (4) low CO; (5) with a pre-chamber, a wide lean limit.
  7. Demerits: (1) HC from over-lean edges of the cloud and from the pre-chamber crevices; (2) soot from the rich zone near the injector; (3) lean exhaust, so a three-way catalyst cannot remove NOx: a lean NOx trap and low-sulphur fuel are needed; (4) plug fouling and complex control of the cloud over the whole speed range; (5) a third valve or a high-pressure injector adds cost.
  8. Conclusion: stratification turns a petrol engine into a quality-controlled engine like a diesel; its efficiency gain is real, but tighter NOx and particle limits have pushed most makers back to λ = 1 direct injection, while pre-chamber jet ignition has returned for very lean engines.
Understand Explain the concept of the low heat rejection (LHR) engine. Describe the methods of insulating the combustion chamber and discuss the effects on performance and emissions. 13 marks

Points to cover. Heat balance: a quarter to a third of fuel energy to coolant; Thermal barrier coatings (YSZ, 0.3–0.5 mm, bond coat), ceramic inserts, air-gap pistons; ΔT = q t ÷ k; Effects: efficiency gain small, exhaust energy up, volumetric efficiency down, NOx up, delay shorter, oil limit; Suits diesel and turbocompounding, not SI.

Model answer.

  1. Concept: in a diesel about a quarter to a third of the fuel energy passes through the chamber walls into the coolant. An LHR engine insulates the surfaces the hot gas touches so this heat stays in the gas. The aim is more work, or hotter exhaust that a turbine can use. The extreme idea, the “adiabatic” engine, would need no cooling system at all.
  2. Methods. (1) Thermal barrier coatings: 0.3 to 0.5 mm of yttria-stabilised zirconia, plasma-sprayed on a metallic bond coat, on the piston crown, head face, valve faces and liner top; conductivity about 1 to 2 W/(m·K), against about 150 for aluminium. (2) Monolithic ceramic parts or inserts (silicon nitride, zirconia) for piston caps, liners and port liners. (3) Air-gap pistons and liners: a separate crown or sleeve leaves a thin air layer as insulation. (4) Low-conductivity metal parts such as a steel crown on an aluminium piston.
  3. How much a coating holds, for a mean flux of 0.5 MW/m²:
    zirconia, 0.5 mm, k = 1.0:   ΔT = 0.5 × 10⁶ × 0.0005 ÷ 1.0 = 250 K
aluminium, 10 mm, k = 150:  ΔT = 0.5 × 10⁶ × 0.010 ÷ 150 = 33 K
  4. So the coated surface runs far hotter and the heat flux falls (the figures are a first estimate at fixed flux).
  5. Heat balance, worked figure. A diesel with 100 kW of fuel power: brake 33 kW, coolant 30 kW, exhaust 30 kW, other 7 kW. Insulation cuts coolant heat by 40 %, freeing 12 kW; if 15 % of that becomes work:
    ItemBaseline, kWLHR, kW

  6. Brake efficiency rises only from 33.0 to 34.8 %, while exhaust energy rises by a third: the gain comes mainly if a turbocharger or a turbo-compound turbine recovers the exhaust.
  7. Effects on performance. Hot walls heat the incoming air, so volumetric efficiency and power density fall. Ignition delay shortens, so diesel knock and noise fall, and low-cetane fuels (alcohols, vegetable oils) ignite better. Lubricating oil on the liner breaks down above about 250 °C, limiting the wall temperature; coatings can crack and spall under thermal cycling.
  8. Effects on emissions. NOx rises because the gas is hotter. CO and HC usually fall because the quench layers are thinner and the late burn is hotter. Smoke may fall from better burn-out or rise if the lower air density makes the mixture richer.
  9. Conclusion: the LHR engine suits the turbocharged diesel; it is unsuitable for petrol engines, where hot walls cause knock. Today the idea returns as thin temperature-swing coatings that heat and cool within each cycle.
Understand Distinguish dual-fuel, bi-fuel and multi-fuel engines. Explain why a gas-diesel dual-fuel engine needs a pilot, and discuss substitution, knock and methane slip. 13 marks

Points to cover. Definitions with examples; Gas metering: mixer, gas carburettor, port injection (fumigation); Methane: cetane near zero, long delay at 800–860 K; pilot as ignition source; Substitution and gas energy share; pilot 10–30 %, micro-pilot 1 %; Knock at high load, misfire and slip at light load; multi-fuel compromises.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. Dual fuel: two fuels burn together every cycle; a gas carries most of the energy and a diesel pilot ignites it (a CNG-diesel genset). Bi-fuel: two fuels carried, one burnt at a time (a CNG-petrol car). Multi-fuel: one engine that runs on any one of several liquid fuels (diesel, kerosene, jet fuel) without hardware change.
  2. Gas supply. Gas is mixed with the intake air (fumigation) by a venturi mixer, a gas carburettor with a zero-pressure regulator, or timed port injection. The charge entering the cylinder is a lean, premixed gas–air mixture.
  3. Why a pilot. At a compression ratio of 17 the charge reaches roughly T = 320 × 170.35 ≈ 860 K, about methane’s self-ignition temperature of 810 K. Methane’s cetane number is near zero, so its ignition delay at that temperature is far longer than the few milliseconds near top dead centre. A small diesel injection ignites within its normal delay; the burning pilot sprays act like many spark plugs and start flames through the gas–air charge.
  4. Pilot size. In converted engines the pilot is commonly 10 to 30 % of the fuel energy at high load and more at light load; large gas engines use a micro-pilot of about 1 %. A common-rail injector meters such small pilots accurately.
  5. Substitution, worked figure (40 kW; diesel alone 10 kg/h; dual fuel 2.5 kg/h diesel at 42.5 MJ/kg and 6.6 kg/h CNG at 50 MJ/kg):
    diesel substitution = (10 − 2.5) ÷ 10 = 75 %
gas energy share = 330 ÷ (106.25 + 330) = 75.6 %
efficiency: 144 ÷ 425 = 33.9 % (diesel); 144 ÷ 436.25 = 33.0 % (dual fuel)
  6. Knock appears at high load: the premixed charge is large, and with an early pilot, hot intake or high compression ratio the end gas self-ignites before the flames arrive. It caps the gas share at full load.
  7. Light-load penalty and methane slip. At light load the gas–air charge is very lean; flames from the pilot are slow and die out, so CO and unburnt methane rise and efficiency falls. Methane slip is this unburnt methane, plus some from crevices and from short-circuiting during valve overlap. Because methane warms the climate far more per kg than CO2, slip can cancel much of the CO2 benefit. Remedies: a bigger pilot and less gas at light load, partial throttling or hot EGR to enrich the charge, and an oxidation catalyst for methane.
  8. Multi-fuel engines fix a compromise compression ratio (often above 20) so low-cetane fuels ignite, and compensate the pump for fuel density and viscosity; cetane, viscosity, lubricity and volatility set the usable fuel range.
  9. Conclusion: dual fuel saves diesel and CO2 only in the middle of the load range; knock limits it at the top and slip at the bottom.

Apply An engine is to run first in HCCI mode and then in RCCI mode. Describe the operating strategy, the variable that sets ignition timing and the expected emissions in each case. Points to cover. HCCI: lean premixed, self-ignition, no flame; levers indirect; τ ratio 900 → 950 K = 2.40; HCCI emissions: NOx and soot near zero; HC, CO high at light load; ringing at high load; RCCI: port low-reactivity + direct high-reactivity; ratio and timing set ignition; PRF 80; RCCI wider load range; still needs oxidation catalyst.

Model answer.

  1. HCCI strategy. Fuel is premixed with air (port injection or early direct injection) to a lean charge, φ about 0.2 to 0.5, or diluted with EGR. Compression heats the whole charge to self-ignition; there is no spark and no flame front, so the charge burns almost at once.
  2. What sets HCCI timing. Only the chemistry: the ignition delay falls steeply with charge temperature. Levers are indirect: intake temperature, EGR rate, trapped hot residual gas by variable valve timing, compression ratio and fuel reactivity.
  3. Sensitivity:
    τ900 ÷ τ950 = exp[15 000 × (1/900 − 1/950)]
           = exp[15 000 × (1.1111 − 1.0526) × 10⁻³]
           = exp(15 000 × 5.848 × 10⁻⁵) = exp(0.877) = 2.40
  4. A 50 K warmer charge ignites 2.4 times sooner; even 10 K moves the burn by crank degrees, so HCCI needs intake temperature control to a few kelvin.
  5. HCCI emissions. NOx near zero, because no parcel exceeds about 2000 K; soot near zero, because no parcel is rich. HC and CO are high at light load, since the coolest charge near the walls and in crevices never finishes reacting. At high load the whole charge burns too fast (ringing, a very high rate of pressure rise), which sets the upper load limit.
  6. RCCI strategy. A low-reactivity fuel (petrol, ethanol or natural gas) is port-injected to form a lean premixed charge; a high-reactivity fuel (diesel) is injected directly, early enough to mix partly. Ignition starts in the parcels richest in diesel and spreads through the reactivity gradient, so the heat release is spread over more crank angle.

Analyze Analyse the trade-off between NOx and soot in a direct-injection diesel engine using the φ–T map, and explain how low-temperature combustion (PCCI, HCCI, RCCI) escapes it. 13 marks

Points to cover. φ–T map: soot island φ > 2, 1600–2600 K; NOx above about 2200 K at φ ≤ 1; Diesel spray path crosses both; Levers: EGR and retard cut NOx, raise soot; more air, pressure, advance cut soot, raise NOx; LTC: lean or well-mixed, peak below about 2000 K; Costs: ignition control, HC and CO, ringing, load range; after-treatment still needed.

Model answer.

  1. The map. Plot every small parcel of in-cylinder gas by its local equivalence ratio φ and local temperature T. Soot forms in an island of rich, hot states, roughly φ above 2 at 1600 to 2600 K. Thermal NO forms where the gas is hotter than about 2200 K with oxygen to spare, near or below φ = 1. (Sketch: T across, φ up; the soot island upper middle, the NOx band lower right.)
  2. Path of a conventional diesel spray. Fuel enters cold and very rich, heats as it mixes, and passes through the soot island in the spray core. The diffusion flame round the jet burns near stoichiometric at 2500 K or more, which is inside the NOx band. One spray therefore makes both pollutants.
  3. Why the levers trade:
    ChangeEffect on the pathNOxSoot
    More EGRlower flame temperature, less O2downup: less burn-out
    Injection retardlater, cooler burndownup: less time to burn out
    Higher injection pressureleaner core, faster mixingup slightlydown
    Injection advancemore premixed, hotterupdown
  4. Each lever moves the spray out of one region and towards the other, so a plot of soot against NOx for a set of settings is a curve running from high NOx–low soot to low NOx–high soot: the trade-off curve.
  5. How LTC escapes. The idea is to keep every parcel out of both regions at once: (1) mix the fuel before it burns, so no parcel is rich (φ below about 1, often 0.2 to 0.5); (2) dilute with EGR or excess air, so the peak stays below about 2000 K. Then the path never enters the soot island or the NOx band.
  6. PCCI injects one fuel early so it partly premixes; the core is smaller, soot and NOx both fall, but some rich parcels remain. HCCI premixes fully and self-ignites by compression; NOx and soot are near zero. RCCI premixes a low-reactivity fuel and adds a high-reactivity fuel directly; the reactivity gradient spreads the burn and gives a control lever.
  7. Worked figure on control: with τ = A exp(E/RT) and E/R = 15 000 K, raising the charge from 900 to 950 K shortens the delay 2.4 times, which shows why timing drifts and why RCCI’s fuel ratio (for example PRF 80 at part load, PRF 90 at high load) is valuable.
  8. Costs of LTC. Ignition is set by chemistry, not by a spark or injection; HC and CO rise because the cool lean charge near the walls does not finish burning; the burn can be too fast at high load (ringing); the load range is limited; and a diesel oxidation catalyst is still needed for HC and CO.
  9. Conclusion: the trade-off belongs to the diffusion flame, not to the diesel engine as such; by moving the burn into the lean, cool corner of the map, LTC lowers both pollutants together, at the price of control.
Evaluate Evaluate whether a three-way catalytic converter alone can control the emissions of a lean-burn or stratified-charge petrol engine, and discuss the alternatives. 13 marks

Points to cover. TWC needs reductants for NO, so works only within about ±0.5 % of λ = 1; Lean exhaust: CO, HC oxidised; NO not reduced; Alternatives: LNT with rich purges; urea SCR; λ = 1 operation with EGR; GPF for particles; Costs: fuel for purges, sulphur, urea tank, complexity; Judgement: the efficiency gain must pay for the extra after-treatment.

Model answer.

  1. How the TWC works. At λ = 1 it oxidises CO and HC with oxygen and reduces NO with the CO, H2 and HC in the same gas, for example 2NO + 2CO → N2 + 2CO2. Both jobs succeed only in a narrow window, about ±0.5 % of λ = 1 (air–fuel ratio about 14.6 to 14.8), held by oxygen sensors and closed-loop injection.
  2. Lean exhaust. A stratified or lean-burn engine runs at λ = 1.3 to 2 or more (for example λ = 1.39 for an overall air–fuel ratio of 20.4). The exhaust holds several per cent oxygen. The CO and HC are oxidised, but the oxygen uses up the reductants before they can reach the NO, so NO passes almost unconverted. A TWC alone therefore controls CO and HC but not NOx.
  3. Is engine-out NOx low enough anyway? Lean operation does lower NO compared with the peak at φ = 0.9, and very lean or diluted charges lower it further, but stratified engines have near-stoichiometric burning zones round the plug, so engine-out NOx stays far above a limit such as 0.06 g/km. The TWC alone fails.
  4. Alternatives:

    Lean NOx trap (LNT)stores NO2 as barium nitrate while lean; a rich pulse of a few seconds every minute or so reduces itfuel for purges (part of the lean gain), sulphur poisoning needs low-sulphur fuel and hot desulphation
    Urea SCRNH3 reduces NOx in lean gasan AdBlue tank and dosing on a car; works from about 200 °C
    Return to λ = 1 with heavy cooled EGRkeeps the TWC; EGR reduces throttling and knocksmaller efficiency gain than lean burn

  5. A usable lean petrol line is TWC (for cold start and stoichiometric modes) plus LNT, with 10 ppm sulphur fuel; NOx sensors after the LNT decide when to purge.
  6. Evaluation. The lean engine saves, for example, about 7.5 % of the output in pumping loss at part load, plus some cycle efficiency. The LNT purges, the desulphation events and the extra precious metal take back part of this, and real-driving tests show the purges are hard to keep efficient over the whole map. This is why many makers dropped stratified lean operation when Euro 6 and BS-VI limits and particle-number limits came, and moved to λ = 1 direct injection with cooled EGR, where a TWC and a GPF suffice.
  7. Conclusion: no, a TWC alone cannot control a lean engine’s NOx; lean burn is worth its gain only with an LNT or SCR, and today the cost of that after-treatment usually outweighs the benefit in cars.
Evaluate A city replaces its diesel buses with CNG buses and later tightens its standard to BS-VI. Evaluate the emission and practical consequences of each step, using formation chemistry, after-treatment needs, storage and the driving cycle. 16 marks

Points to cover. CNG: premixed SI, near-zero soot, CO₂ per MJ 22 % below petrol; NOx and methane remain; Storage: 200 bar, 8.4 MJ/L; refuelling network; BS-VI: CNG with TWC at λ = 1; diesel with DOC + DPF + SCR + 10 ppm fuel; Urban cycle: idle, cold start, low exhaust temperature hurt SCR; Judgement: fuel change and after-treatment are complementary.

Model answer.

  1. Step 1: diesel to CNG. A CNG bus engine is a spark-ignition engine burning a premixed gas. With no liquid spray there is no rich core, so soot and PM almost vanish: the visible black smoke of old diesel buses disappears, the main local health gain. Methane carries less carbon per unit energy, 0.0549 kg CO2/MJ against about 0.074 for diesel, but a spark-ignition engine is less efficient than a diesel, so the tailpipe CO2 per km saving is smaller than the fuel figures suggest.
  2. What remains: NOx, because the premixed flame is hot; and unburnt methane, which an ordinary catalyst barely oxidises and which warms the climate about 28 times as much as CO2 per kg over 100 years. Lean-burn CNG engines of the first generation, with only an oxidation catalyst, had high NOx.
  3. Practical side. CNG is stored at 200 bar with real density about 168 kg/m³ at 15 °C, so a litre of cylinder holds about 8.4 MJ, against 35.7 MJ for a litre of diesel. Buses need many roof cylinders, the range falls, and the city needs compressor stations and a gas pipeline; cylinders need periodic testing. Fuel cost per km is usually lower, and India’s large CNG fleets show the change is workable.
  4. Step 2: BS-VI. For CNG buses, the engine moves to stoichiometric operation with a three-way catalyst coated for methane and closed-loop λ control, which removes NOx, CO and most methane together; this is a modest, cheap step. For any remaining diesel buses BS-VI demands the full line: DOC, DPF, urea dosing and SCR, a slip catalyst and on-board diagnostics, plus 10 ppm sulphur fuel. Evaluate Stratified charge, low heat rejection, HCCI and RCCI engines are all attempts to raise efficiency. Compare their principles, efficiency gains, emissions and practical problems, and judge which are most promising. 16 marks

Points to cover. Stratified: rich at plug, lean overall, no throttle; ≈ 7.5 % pumping saving; NOx needs LNT; LHR: insulated chamber; small brake gain, exhaust energy up; NOx up, oil limit; HCCI: lean premixed self-ignition; near-zero NOx and soot; no timing control, ringing, HC and CO; RCCI: two fuels, reactivity gradient; control lever, wider range; two fuel tanks; Judgement with a comparison table.

Model answer.

  1. Where the losses are: a part-load petrol engine loses work to pumping and to the slow, hot, stoichiometric burn; a diesel loses a quarter to a third of its fuel energy to the coolant; both lose work when emission controls force retard or rich operation. Each of the four engines attacks one of these losses.
  2. Stratified charge. Principle: a rich zone at the plug lights a lean overall charge, so load is controlled by fuel with the throttle open. Gain: pumping loss falls (worked figure: 1375 W throttled against 250 W unthrottled at 2000 rev/min, a saving of 7.5 % of 15 kW) and the lean cycle is more efficient. Emissions: low CO; HC from lean edges; soot near the injector; NOx cannot be handled by a TWC, so an LNT is needed. Problems: control of the cloud, plug fouling, sulphur sensitivity of the LNT.
  3. LHR. Principle: thermal barrier coatings (0.3 to 0.5 mm zirconia, ΔT about 250 K at 0.5 MW/m²) keep heat in the gas. Gain: small at the shaft (worked figure: 33.0 to 34.8 % when 12 kW of coolant heat is saved), because most saved heat leaves in the exhaust; larger with turbocompounding. Emissions: NOx up from hotter gas; CO and HC down. Problems: hot walls lower volumetric efficiency, oil breaks down above about 250 °C, coatings crack; unsuitable for petrol engines because of knock.
  4. HCCI. Principle: a lean premixed charge self-ignites by compression without a flame. Gain: diesel-like efficiency without throttling and with low heat loss. Emissions: NOx and soot near zero; HC and CO high at light load. Problems: no direct timing control (the delay changes 2.4 times between 900 and 950 K), ringing at high load, a narrow load range, cold start; pure HCCI is not in production, though spark-assisted versions are.
  5. RCCI. Principle: a port-injected low-reactivity fuel and a directly injected high-reactivity fuel; the ratio (for example global PRF 80 at part load, 90 at high load) and the injection timing set the burn. Gain: research engines report diesel-like or better efficiency over a wider load range. Emissions: very low NOx and soot; HC and CO need an oxidation catalyst. Problems: two fuels and two tanks, refuelling habits, transient control; not yet in production.

Glossary

Air-gap piston
a piston whose crown is separated from its body by a thin air layer that insulates it; used in LHR engines.
Ammonia slip
ammonia that leaves the SCR catalyst unreacted; burnt in a slip catalyst.
Bi-fuel engine
an engine that carries two fuels and burns one at a time, such as a CNG-petrol car.
Bosch smoke number
a 0 to 10 reading of how black a filter paper turns after a fixed exhaust volume passes through it.
Chemiluminescence
light given off by a chemical reaction; NO + O₃ gives excited NO₂, whose light measures NO.
Crevice
a narrow gap, such as the top land above the first ring, that the flame cannot enter.
Divided-chamber engine
a stratified charge engine with a rich pre-chamber, fed by its own small valve, whose flame jets burn a lean main charge.
Diesel particulate filter (DPF)
a wall-flow honeycomb that traps soot and is cleaned by regeneration.
Dual-fuel engine
an engine burning a gas and a diesel pilot together every cycle; the pilot ignites the gas.
Equivalence ratio (φ)
actual fuel–air ratio divided by the stoichiometric one; above 1 is rich.
Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR)
returning part of the exhaust to the intake to cool the flame and lower NOx.
Flame ionisation detector (FID)
an analyser that burns the sample in a hydrogen flame and measures the ion current, giving HC in ppmC.
HCCI
homogeneous charge compression ignition: a lean premixed charge that self-ignites by compression, with no flame front.
Lean NOx trap (LNT)
a catalyst that stores NOx as nitrate in lean running and reduces it during short rich pulses.
Light-off temperature
the temperature at which a catalyst converts half of a pollutant; about 250 to 300 °C for a three-way catalyst.
Low heat rejection (LHR) engine
an engine with an insulated combustion chamber that sends less heat to the coolant.
Methane slip
unburnt methane leaving a gas or dual-fuel engine.
MIDC
Modified Indian Driving Cycle: the speed–time trace used to certify cars in India, built on the European NEDC.
NDIR
non-dispersive infrared analyser for CO and CO₂, using a detector filled with the gas measured.
Opacity
the share of light blocked by a column of exhaust, in per cent.
Particulate matter (PM)
all material collected on a filter from diluted exhaust at 47 °C: soot, SOF, sulphate and ash.
PCCI
premixed charge compression ignition: one fuel injected early so that it partly premixes before it self-ignites.
ppmC
parts per million of carbon atoms, the unit of an FID reading.
RCCI
reactivity controlled compression ignition: a port-injected low-reactivity fuel ignited by a directly injected high-reactivity fuel.
Ringing
a very fast rise of cylinder pressure when a premixed charge burns at once, which limits HCCI at high load.
Selective catalytic reduction (SCR)
reduction of NOx to nitrogen by ammonia from urea, on a catalyst in lean exhaust.
Soluble organic fraction (SOF)
unburnt fuel and oil condensed on soot particles.
Stratified charge
a charge rich at the spark plug and lean elsewhere, lean overall.
Thermal barrier coating
a thin ceramic layer, usually yttria-stabilised zirconia, that insulates a hot engine surface.
Three-way catalyst (TWC)
a catalyst that oxidises CO and HC and reduces NOx at the same time, only near λ = 1.
