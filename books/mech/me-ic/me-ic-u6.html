6.1 Formation of CO, unburnt hydrocarbons and NOx

An engine cannot burn its fuel perfectly every cycle. Carbon monoxide comes from a rich mixture, unburnt hydrocarbons from a flame that dies or hides, and nitrogen oxides from a flame that becomes too hot. These three engine-out gases are measured before any catalytic converter. The same trade-off controls petrol and diesel tuning, cold starts, and every emission standard.

You need Unit 3 (stages of combustion, flame quenching) and Unit 2 (mixture formation and ignition timing).

CO: the rich-mixture gas

Every regulated gas starts from a different failure of combustion. Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colourless, odourless gas made when carbon in fuel is partly oxidised: C + ½O2 → CO. Complete oxidation gives CO2. In a spark-ignition engine, CO rises steeply when the equivalence ratio φ exceeds 1. Petrol's stoichiometric air-fuel ratio is about 14.7:1 by mass; at φ = 1.1 the charge has more fuel than the oxygen can fully burn.

φ = (F/A)actual ÷ (F/A)stoichiometric

φ is the equivalence ratio; F/A is fuel-air ratio by mass; actual is the running mixture; stoichiometric is the chemically exact mixture.

Two reactions adjust CO after it forms. The water-gas shift CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2 removes CO when water and oxygen are available. Above about 2000 K, CO2 dissociates back as CO2 ⇌ CO + ½O2, so even a lean flame leaves a small CO floor. Cold walls freeze the shift reaction and leave more CO during warm-up.

A diesel engine runs globally lean, so engine-out CO is low. Local rich pockets, low-temperature quench and poor mixing still make some CO. CO is controlled by holding φ near 1, by air injection into the exhaust, and by the catalytic converter. The converter needs a narrow window around stoichiometric mixture to oxidise CO and hydrocarbons while reducing NOx.

Unburnt hydrocarbons: flame death and hiding places

Unburnt hydrocarbons (HC) are fuel molecules that leave the cylinder without burning. Total hydrocarbons (THC) are measured as equivalent carbon atoms, usually ppm C1 or ppm C3. Six paths matter.

  • Wall quenching: the flame cools and dies within roughly 1 mm of a cold wall. The boundary layer survives, especially with cold coolant.
  • Crevice volumes: the piston top land, ring grooves, and spark-plug gap hold mixture that the flame cannot enter. It leaves during expansion.
  • Oil-film absorption: fuel dissolves in the lubricating oil when pressure is high and returns later.
  • Short-circuiting: with valve overlap, fresh mixture can pass from intake to exhaust before the flame arrives.
  • Misfire: a cycle with no flame leaves all its fuel as HC.
  • Poor atomisation: a cold engine, low fuel pressure, or a wide spray makes liquid films that burn slowly.

Cold-start HC can be most of a petrol car's HC. In a diesel, over-lean outer spray and fuel wetting the nozzle also add HC. Spark timing that is too retarded lowers peak temperature and raises HC. HC falls as the mixture leans out, until lean misfire sets in.

NOx: the hot-flame gas and the trade-off

NOx means nitric oxide NO plus nitrogen dioxide NO2. In engine exhaust, NO is usually the larger share; NO2 is a small part that grows at light load and in diesel exhaust. Thermal NO forms from atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen in the hot burned gas.

O + N2 → NO + N; N + O2 → NO + O; N + OH → NO + H

N2 is nitrogen from air; O, N, OH and H are radicals in the flame; NO is nitric oxide. These three reactions are the Zeldovich mechanism.

The first reaction has a high activation energy, so thermal NO rises steeply above about 1800 K. It depends on peak temperature, oxygen concentration and residence time. A slightly lean mixture, φ ≈ 0.9, gives high temperature with oxygen still present; that is where NOx peaks. Richer mixtures lower temperature and oxygen, so NOx falls. Prompt NO forms in the flame front by reactions of hydrocarbon radicals with N2. It is small in most engines but matters in very rich, short flames.

NOx is reduced by lower peak temperature: retarded spark, exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), lower compression ratio, or late injection. These same changes often raise CO or HC. From rich to lean: CO and HC fall as oxygen becomes available; NOx rises to a peak near air-fuel ratio 15 and then falls as excess air cools the flame and dilutes the charge. The three-way catalyst works only in a narrow band around stoichiometric, so the engine is held there and the remaining gases are converted in the exhaust. High load raises peak temperature and NOx; cold coolant raises HC and CO.

Worked example

Question. A petrol engine passes 0.05 m³/s of dry exhaust at standard conditions, where the density of CO is 1.25 kg/m³. The NDIR analyser reads 0.4% CO by volume and brake power is 60 kW. Find the CO mass flow and the brake-specific CO in g/kWh.

  1. CO volume fraction = 0.4% = 0.4 ÷ 100 = 0.004.
  2. CO volume flow = 0.004 × 0.05 m³/s = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s.
  3. CO mass flow = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s × 1.25 kg/m³ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s.
  4. Convert to grams per second: 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s × 1000 g/kg = 0.25 g/s.
  5. Brake-specific CO = 0.25 g/s ÷ 60 kW = 4.17 × 10⁻³ g/(s·kW).
  6. Convert seconds to hours: 4.17 × 10⁻³ × 3600 = 15 g/kWh.

Answer. The CO mass flow is 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ kg/s (0.25 g/s), and the brake-specific CO is 15 g/kWh.

Exam traps

  • Assuming NOx rises with richness because the flame gets hotter. NOx peaks slightly lean of stoichiometric, near φ ≈ 0.9, where temperature is still high and oxygen is still present.
  • Saying NO comes from fuel nitrogen. In ordinary petrol and diesel, thermal NO comes from N2 in the air; fuel nitrogen matters only in some special fuels.
  • Treating HC as only unburned fuel vapour. THC includes partially burned molecules and oil-derived species, and ppm C1 counts carbon atoms.
  • Using wet exhaust volume when the analyser reads dry gas. Percent CO is on a dry basis, so water must be removed before the NDIR measurement.

6.2 Smoke and particulate matter

Diesel smoke is the visible tip of a particle born inside the fuel-rich core of the spray. That core has far more fuel than oxygen, so fuel cracks into carbon fragments, grows into spheres, sticks into chains, and partly burns later in the expansion stroke. What leaves the tailpipe is soot plus unburnt fuel, lube oil and sulphate. This chapter defines particulate matter, follows the six growth steps, and shows why injection changes and EGR that cut NOx usually raise smoke.

You need: 6.1 for the other pollutants, Unit 3 for CI combustion stages and diesel knock, and Unit 2 for spray formation and nozzles.

What counts as particulate matter

Particulate matter (PM) is everything that collects on a filter when diluted exhaust is pulled through it at a fixed temperature, usually 47 °C. The filter is weighed before and after; the gain is PM mass. PM is not one substance. It has three main parts plus a small ash fraction.

Dry soot is carbon. It is black, and it dominates the black smoke you see. Blue and white smoke come from other parts (lube oil, unburnt fuel, water), as Section 3 explains. The soluble organic fraction (SOF) is unburnt diesel and lube oil that condenses on the soot; at light load it can be most of the mass. Sulphate and bound water come from sulphur in the fuel and from combustion water. A small ash fraction remains from lube-oil additives.

Smoke is a visible subset of PM. A clear exhaust can still carry SOF and sulphate. PM is therefore reported as mass in mg/m³ or g/kWh, not by looking at the plume.

Six steps from rich core to exhaust

Inside the spray the core is fuel-rich: the equivalence ratio φ is well above 1. φ is actual fuel–air ratio divided by stoichiometric fuel–air ratio. Little oxygen is present there.

  1. Pyrolysis: hot fuel cracks into small hydrocarbons such as C2 and C3 fragments; no oxygen is needed.
  2. Nucleation: these fragments form the first tiny soot particles, a few nanometres across.
  3. Surface growth: fuel fragments attach to the particle surface; mass rises fast, size rises more slowly.
  4. Coagulation: particles collide and merge into fewer, larger spheres.
  5. Agglomeration: spheres stick into chains and clusters; SOF and sulphate condense on them as the gas cools.
  6. Oxidation: in the expansion stroke, oxygen reaches the hot particles and burns part of the soot away.

Steps 1–5 sit in the rich core and its boundary. Step 6 happens later, when the jet has mixed with air. Exhaust PM is what survives step 6, so early soot can still burn out.

PM = m_soot + m_SOF + m_sulphate + m_water + m_ash

PM is the mass collected on the filter; each m is the mass of that component in the same sample, in mg. The ash mass is usually a few per cent of PM.

Smoke colour and smoke number

Smoke colour identifies the failure. Black smoke is soot: too much fuel for the oxygen in the rich core. Blue smoke is lube oil burning, often worn rings or valve guides. White smoke is unburnt fuel or water vapour, common in a cold start when the cylinder is too cold to ignite the diesel.

  • Opacity, in %: the fraction of light blocked through a column of exhaust. It depends on path length and particle size.
  • Bosch smoke number: a 0–10 scale from the blackening of filter paper by a fixed gas sample.
  • Filter smoke number (FSN): a similar 0–10 paper-blackening scale, used with different sample conditions.
  • Mass concentration, in mg/m³: the actual PM mass on a filter. Opacimeters and paper meters see mostly black soot; they miss SOF and sulphate. A low Bosch number does not prove low PM mass.

What moves smoke, and the NOx–PM trade-off

The rich core shrinks when fuel and air mix better. These changes move PM. The first three cut it, the last three raise it:

  • Higher injection pressure: finer droplets, smaller rich core, less PM; NOx may rise.
  • Smaller nozzle holes: better atomisation, less PM.
  • More swirl: faster air–fuel mixing, less PM.
  • Higher load: more fuel for the same air, richer core, more PM.
  • Injection retard: lower peak temperature and NOx, but less soot oxidation, so more PM.
  • EGR: exhaust gas displaces oxygen and lowers temperature, cutting NOx and raising PM.

The last two are the trade-off in one line: almost every change that cuts NOx raises PM. Retard lowers peak temperature and NOx but leaves less time and heat for soot oxidation. EGR displaces oxygen and lowers temperature, so NOx falls and PM rises.

Worked example

Question. A diesel engine of 40 kW brake power is sampled for 20 s. The exhaust drawn through the filter is 0.8 m³ corrected to standard conditions, and the clean filter gains 24 mg. Find the PM concentration and the brake-specific PM.

  1. Take the 0.8 m³ as the total exhaust flow over the 20 s; a real dilution tunnel takes a proportional sample of the total exhaust.
  2. PM concentration = filter gain ÷ exhaust volume = 24 mg ÷ 0.8 m³ = 30 mg/m³.
  3. Exhaust volume flow = 0.8 m³ ÷ 20 s = 0.04 m³/s.
  4. PM mass flow = 30 mg/m³ × 0.04 m³/s = 1.2 mg/s.
  5. PM per hour = 1.2 mg/s × 3600 s/h = 4320 mg/h.
  6. Brake-specific PM = 4320 mg/h ÷ 40 kW = 108 mg/kWh = 0.108 g/kWh.

Answer. PM concentration is 30 mg/m³ and brake-specific PM is 108 mg/kWh, that is 0.108 g/kWh.

Exam traps

  • Writing 'smoke = particulate matter'. Smoke is the visible black carbon; PM also includes SOF and sulphate, which can dominate mass at light load.
  • Treating opacity %, Bosch smoke number and FSN as interchangeable. They use different optics, path lengths and sample conditions.
  • Forgetting that Bosch and FSN meters see mostly black soot, so a low reading does not prove low PM mass.
  • Mixing units: mg/m³ is concentration, g/kWh is brake-specific mass; divide by brake power and time correctly.
  • Assuming EGR always helps. It cuts NOx but usually raises PM.

6.3 Emission control: in-cylinder and after-treatment methods

Every emission control is a choice of place: stop the pollutant forming inside the cylinder, or convert and trap it in the exhaust line. In-cylinder methods change mixing, timing and temperature. After-treatment adds catalysts, filters and urea. Petrol engines near stoichiometric use a three-way catalyst; diesels run lean and need an oxidation catalyst, a particulate filter and SCR or a lean NOx trap. The skill is matching the device to the engine's air-fuel ratio and exhaust temperature, not buying the largest catalyst.

You need: 6.1 and 6.2 for where each pollutant is born, Unit 2 for injection and ignition timing, and Unit 3 for air motion and combustion chambers.

In-cylinder treatments

In-cylinder control keeps the pollutant from leaving the engine. For CI engines, retarding injection timing lowers peak flame temperature and so lowers NOx. It costs fuel and raises particulate matter (PM). Higher injection pressure and common rail make finer sprays, better mixing and less soot. Multiple injection splits one pulse into pilot, main and post pulses. A pilot softens ignition; a post pulse late in expansion adds fuel for DPF regeneration or reduces soot. Injection rate shaping changes how fast fuel enters; a slow start lowers NOx and noise. Chamber and swirl design, from Unit 3, shorten the rich core where soot grows. For SI engines, spark retard lowers NOx but costs power; closed-loop stoichiometric control uses a lambda sensor to hold λ = actual AFR ÷ stoichiometric AFR near 1. Water injection cools the charge and lowers NOx. Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) sends exhaust back to the intake. It dilutes oxygen, adds CO2 and H2O that absorb heat (thermal effect), and changes reaction chemistry; together these lower NOx. Hot EGR is simple but less dense; cooled EGR packs more mass for the same volume. High-pressure EGR takes gas before the turbine; low-pressure EGR takes it after the DPF and needs a cleaner path. EGR raises PM and fuel use if overdone. Two small lines: crankcase ventilation routes blow-by gas back to the intake; an evaporative canister traps fuel vapour from the tank and purges it at the right moment.

After-treatment: three-way catalyst and diesel line

After-treatment treats the gas after the exhaust valve. The three-way catalytic converter (TWC) is for stoichiometric petrol engines. It carries platinum, palladium and rhodium. At λ≈1 it runs three reactions: CO + ½O₂ → CO₂ (CO is carbon monoxide), HC + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (HC is unburnt hydrocarbon), and CO + NO → ½N₂ + CO₂ (NO is nitric oxide, part of NOx). The window is narrow, about λ = 0.99 to 1.00 (a few tenths of an air-fuel ratio around 14.7:1 for petrol). A lambda sensor and closed loop keep the mixture there; with spare O₂ the NO reaction stops, with excess fuel the oxidation reactions stop. The diesel line is different because diesel is always lean. The diesel oxidation catalyst (DOC) oxidises CO and HC and turns NO into NO₂, which helps the next devices. The diesel particulate filter (DPF) is a wall-flow honeycomb: alternate channel ends are blocked, so gas passes through porous walls and soot stays. Passive regeneration burns soot with NO₂; active regeneration uses a late post-injection to raise temperature. Ash remains and slowly blocks the filter. Selective catalytic reduction (SCR) injects urea solution, AdBlue, 32.5% urea in water. It decomposes to ammonia. The standard reaction is 4NH₃ + 4NO + O₂ → 4N₂ + 6H₂O. A fast SCR route also uses NO₂. Light-off is near 200 °C, and too much ammonia causes ammonia slip. The lean NOx trap (LNT) stores NOx as barium nitrate while lean, then a short rich purge reduces it. Sulphur poisons the LNT.

Matching device to engine

Choose by air-fuel ratio first. A stoichiometric petrol engine gets a TWC and a lambda sensor. A lean-burn petrol or diesel gets a DOC, then either SCR with urea or an LNT. A DPF is standard on the modern diesel; a gasoline particulate filter (GPF) is added to petrol where PM is regulated. SCR needs a supply of AdBlue and a warm exhaust; LNT avoids urea but suffers sulphur poisoning and needs rich purges. A DPF is not optional on a modern diesel if PM is regulated. The devices act in order: DOC, DPF, urea mixer, SCR. The DOC makes NO₂ for DPF and SCR. The DPF removes soot but its regeneration costs fuel. The SCR removes NOx but can slip ammonia. The LNT stores NOx but needs a rich purge.

Exam traps

  • Fitting a three-way catalyst to a lean-burn or diesel engine. The TWC needs λ within about ±0.01 of 1 (roughly ±0.2 in air-fuel ratio around 14.7:1); spare O₂ blocks NOx reduction and excess fuel blocks CO and HC oxidation.
  • Thinking EGR always saves fuel. EGR lowers NOx but raises PM and fuel use if overdone; cooled EGR also adds a cooler load.
  • Confusing the DOC and the DPF. The DOC oxidises gases; the DPF traps solid soot.
  • Forgetting DPF ash. Regeneration burns soot, not ash; ash stays and slowly blocks the filter.
  • Using the CO₂ EGR formula as an exhaust mass fraction. The formula divides by (CO₂ exhaust − CO₂ intake), so the answer is a ratio of recirculated exhaust to fresh air, not a fraction of the exhaust flow.

6.4 Emission measurement: NDIR, chemiluminescence, FID and smoke meters

An exhaust bench does not measure 'pollution' as one number. It measures each gas by a physical effect that belongs to that gas: CO and CO2 absorb infrared light, NO emits light when it meets ozone, hydrocarbons make ions in a hydrogen flame, and smoke blocks light or blackens paper. The bench exists because an engine change or a catalytic converter must be proved in numbers on a test bed and at type approval. It is used on engine dynamometers, in vehicle certification, and in workshop and roadside checks.

You need: 6.1 for where each pollutant comes from and 6.2 for what smoke contains.

Sampling decides the answer

Most wrong emission numbers are sampling errors, not analyser errors. For engine-out work the probe sits in the exhaust manifold or downpipe, before the first catalyst or filter. For tailpipe compliance it sits after the after-treatment. The sample must reach the bench unchanged: a heated line at about 190 °C keeps water and heavy hydrocarbons as vapour, and a filter stops soot. If the line is cold, water condenses and dissolves CO2 and NO2; the reading falls and the error looks like an engine fault.

Water removal matters twice. Water absorbs infrared light and quenches the chemiluminescence signal. A cooler or dryer removes it before NDIR and CLD, but a dryer can also scrub some gases, so the bench is calibrated with the same dryer in line.

Raw sampling is used on a steady test bed. For a driving cycle, constant-volume sampling (CVS) dilutes the whole exhaust with filtered air to a fixed total flow, and a bag collects a proportional sample. The bag is then analysed. Dilute sampling lowers water and prevents condensation, at the cost of measuring low concentrations. The limit that the number is compared with is set by a cycle, covered in 6.5.

NDIR for CO and CO2, and oxygen by magnetism

NDIR means non-dispersive infrared: the analyser uses a broad infrared source and an optical filter, not a prism or grating. A chopper alternates the radiation between a sample cell and a reference cell filled with a gas that does not absorb. The detector sees a pulsed signal; the depth of the pulse gives absorption. CO has a strong band near 4.6 µm and CO2 near 4.3 µm. The same instrument is good for percent-level CO2 and tenth-percent CO, because the absorption is strong and the path is short.

The weakness is selectivity. Water absorbs across the infrared, and the CO2 band overlaps the CO band. A narrow filter and a compensation cell reduce this, but NDIR is poor for low-ppm NOx and for hydrocarbons. Their absorption is weak and many species overlap. NDIR is therefore a CO and CO2 instrument, not a general gas analyser.

Oxygen is measured separately. O2 has two unpaired electrons, so it is paramagnetic; a paramagnetic analyser measures the magnetic pull of the sample against a reference. Calibration uses a zero gas, usually nitrogen, and a span gas with a known concentration of each gas. The span gas sets the gain; the zero gas sets the offset.

Chemiluminescence for NO and NOx

The chemiluminescence detector (CLD) measures NO by a reaction that makes light. Ozone, O3, is generated inside the instrument and mixed with the sample. NO + O3 → NO2* + O2. The star means excited. When NO2* returns to its ground state it emits a photon, and a photomultiplier tube counts the photons. The light output is proportional to the NO concentration.

The instrument measures NO and NOx in two passes. In the NO pass the sample goes straight to the reaction chamber. In the NOx pass it first goes through a converter that reduces NO2 to NO, usually a heated catalyst or a molybdenum converter. The difference gives NO2: NO2,app = NOx,app − NO. The converter is not perfect. If its efficiency is ηconv, the true NO2 is NO2,app / ηconv.

NO<sub>2,true</sub> = (NO<sub>x,app</sub> − NO) ÷ η<sub>conv</sub>

NO2,true is true NO2 in ppm; NOx,app is the reading with the converter in line in ppm; NO is the reading with the converter bypassed in ppm; ηconv is converter efficiency, from 0 to 1.

A spiking test checks ηconv: a known amount of NO2 (the spike) is added to a sample, and ηconv is the measured rise in the NOx reading divided by the amount of NO2 added. Water and CO2 quench the excited NO2*, so the sample is dried or the reading is corrected. A CLD is the standard bench for NOx because it responds to NO directly and reaches low ppm.

FID for hydrocarbons, smoke meters for particles

The flame ionisation detector (FID) burns the sample in a small hydrogen–helium flame. Carbon atoms form ions, and a polarised electrode collects them. The current is proportional to the number of carbon atoms reaching the flame, not to the number of molecules. The output is therefore in ppmC: parts per million of carbon atoms.

ppmC = ppmmol × nC

ppmC is the FID reading in carbon-atom ppm; ppmmol is the molecule concentration in ppm; nC is the number of carbon atoms in one molecule.

One ppm of propane, C3H8, gives about 3 ppmC. One ppm of methane responds at roughly 1 ppmC per ppm in a well-set bench, although some analysers show a slightly low methane response; the strongly under-responding species are oxygenates such as methanol and formaldehyde, and CO. On CNG engines, check the analyser's response factor for methane before comparing a ppmC reading with a ppm limit. A heated FID at about 190 °C stops heavy hydrocarbons condensing in the line. A non-methane cutter oxidises every hydrocarbon except methane; total HC minus the cutter reading gives non-methane HC.

Smoke is measured by light, not by gas cells. An opacimeter, such as the Hartridge type, shines a light across a fixed path of exhaust and measures extinction; the result is opacity in percent. A filter-paper reflectometer pulls a fixed volume through a paper, measures how black the paper is, and converts the reflectance to a Bosch smoke number, usually 0 to 10. Neither gives PM mass. PM mass comes from a gravimetric filter: dilute exhaust passes through a conditioned filter, and a microbalance weighs it before and after. The result is reported in mg/km or mg/kWh.

Worked example

Question. A chemiluminescence analyser reads 380 ppm with the NO2 converter bypassed and 420 ppm with the converter in line. A spiking test shows the converter efficiency is 0.92. Find the apparent NO2, the true NO2, the true NOx, and the amount by which the uncorrected bench under-reports NOx. Give answers to one decimal place where needed.

  1. The bypass reading is the NO concentration: NO = 380.0 ppm.
  2. The reading with the converter in line is the apparent NOx: NOx,app = 420.0 ppm.
  3. Apparent NO2 = NOx,app − NO = 420.0 − 380.0 = 40.0 ppm.
  4. True NO2 = apparent NO2 ÷ ηconv = 40.0 ÷ 0.92 = 43.478... ppm, which rounds to 43.5 ppm.
  5. True NOx = NO + true NO2 = 380.0 + 43.5 = 423.5 ppm.
  6. The uncorrected bench reports 420.0 ppm, so the under-report is 423.5 − 420.0 = 3.5 ppm, about 4 ppm.

Answer. True NO2 is 43.5 ppm and true NOx is 423.5 ppm; the uncorrected bench under-reports NOx by 3.5 ppm, about 4 ppm.

Exam traps

  • FID output is ppmC, not ppm of molecules. One ppm of propane is about 3 ppmC. Do not compare an FID reading directly with a ppm limit written for one hydrocarbon.
  • Ignoring converter efficiency. The apparent NO2 is always low; divide by ηconv before adding it to NO. A converter with ηconv = 0.92 loses about 8% of the NO2.
  • Letting the sample line run cold. Condensed water removes NO2 and CO2 and quenches CLD, so the reading falls and the error looks real.
  • Treating opacity or Bosch smoke number as PM mass. They measure light extinction or blackness; PM mass needs a filter and a microbalance.
  • Using NDIR for low-ppm NOx or hydrocarbons. Its absorption is weak and overlapping; use CLD for NOx and FID for hydrocarbons.

6.5 Emission standards and driving cycles

An emission standard is a pair: a fixed driving cycle that fixes the work done, and a limit in g/km or g/kWh that the exhaust must stay under during that cycle. The cycle is a speed-time trace with a defined cold start, gear schedule and weighting. Certification uses it so two vehicles are compared on the same test, not on two different trips. India uses this system for BS-VI cars, two-wheelers, trucks and off-road engines.

You need: 6.1 for where each gas forms, 6.4 for how the gas is measured, and Unit 5 for test cells and dynamometers.

A test, not a trip

A certification test is not a measurement of a real trip. It is a scripted speed-time trace. A driver or robot follows the trace on a chassis dynamometer. The cold start is included because the first minutes produce much CO and HC. The gear schedule is fixed. The exhaust is diluted and samples are collected in bags. For each pollutant, the mass collected over the cycle is divided by the distance driven. The result is in g/km for a light vehicle.

e = m / d, b = m / W

e is the distance-specific emission in g/km; m is the pollutant mass collected over the cycle in g; d is the cycle distance in km; b is the brake-specific emission in g/kWh; W is the work produced over the cycle in kWh.

A bench reading in ppm is a concentration. A limit in g/km or g/kWh is a mass per unit work. The two cannot be compared without the exhaust mass flow over the cycle and the pollutant density. This is why a certificate always names the cycle and the unit. See 6.4 for the bench instruments.

Cycles in use

Up to BS-III, Indian cars were tested on the Indian Driving Cycle and its modified version. The modified cycle adds a higher-speed phase. BS-VI cars use the Worldwide Harmonised Light Vehicle Test Cycle (WLTC), with a cold start and four speed phases. The United States uses FTP-75, which weights a cold start and a hot start. Europe used the older NEDC before moving to WLTC. Two-wheelers use the WMTC. Heavy and off-road engines are tested on an engine dynamometer on steady-state or transient cycles, and their limits are in g/kWh.

The cycle matters because acceleration, speed and load decide how hot the flame and catalyst become. A low-speed city cycle with a cold start gives more CO and HC per km. A high-speed cycle gives more NOx per km in many engines. A standard fixes the cycle so the limit has one meaning. Changing the cycle changes the number, even for the same vehicle.

India's path and in-service checks

India moved to BS-IV nationwide from April 2017 and to BS-VI from April 2020. Fuel sulphur was cut from 50 ppm to 10 ppm. BS-VI broadly matches Euro 6. It adds a particle number limit for diesel cars and real driving emission (RDE) checks. RDE uses portable equipment on a road trip. It does not replace the laboratory cycle. It checks that the vehicle also stays clean outside the test.

Corporate average fuel economy (CAFE) norms are in km/L of petrol equivalent. They set a fleet average fuel consumption, not a pollutant limit. The pollution under control (PUC) certificate is a quick in-service check, often at idle. It is not a type-approval test and does not cover the full cycle.

Worked example

Question. A car is certified over a cycle of 11.0 km. The bag sample contains 0.66 g of NOx. The limit for that class is 0.06 g/km. Find the result and the margin. The manufacturer wants a 20% margin below the limit. Find the target emission and the allowed NOx mass over the cycle.

Exam traps

  • Comparing a result from one cycle with a limit written for another cycle.
  • Forgetting the cold-start phase, where much of the CO and HC is produced.
  • Mixing g/km, g/kWh and ppm as if they were the same unit.
  • Thinking lower fuel sulphur alone meets BS-VI; the engine, catalyst and filter must also be changed.
  • Treating a PUC certificate as a full type-approval result.

6.6 Alternative fuels: CNG, hydrogen and others

An alternative fuel changes three things at once: the carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, the octane or cetane number, and the storage problem. India imports most of its crude oil, so energy security and city air quality push the search for substitutes. A fuel with more hydrogen and less carbon gives less CO2 per megajoule. A high octane number lets a spark-ignition engine run at higher compression. The same fuel may need 200 bar or 20 K storage. You cannot optimise one property alone. You must trade carbon, ignition quality and storage together.

You need Unit 1 (fuels and calorific value), Unit 2 (mixture preparation) and Unit 3 (knock, cetane and octane), plus 6.1 for why each pollutant forms.

CNG and LPG: gas fuels

CNG is 85–95% methane, with RON about 120 and LHV about 50 MJ/kg. It is lighter than air and stored at 200–250 bar in steel or composite cylinders. Because CNG mixes with air as a gas, it can run lean and at high compression without knock. CO2 per MJ is lower than petrol. The HC in the exhaust is mostly methane; a normal oxidation catalyst does not light it off easily, so the catalyst must be formulated to oxidise methane. Methane itself is a strong greenhouse gas, so a small leak matters.

LPG is propane–butane, with RON about 105, and liquefies at about 10 bar. It stores in a simple steel tank and gives clean combustion, but it still has carbon and its supply is tied to refinery and natural gas processing output. CO2 per MJ is modestly lower than petrol.

CO₂ per kg fuel = n × 44.0 ÷ M; CO₂ per MJ = (CO₂ per kg fuel) ÷ LHV

n is mol CO2 per mol fuel; M is fuel molar mass in g/mol; LHV is lower heating value in MJ/kg.

Alcohols, biodiesel and other liquids

Ethanol and methanol carry oxygen in the molecule. Ethanol has RON about 109 and LHV about 27 MJ/kg. Its high latent heat of vaporisation cools the incoming charge and helps against knock. The same cooling hurts cold starting. India blends ethanol with petrol as E10 and E20; the blend needs compatible seals and pipes because ethanol attacks some elastomers and can corrode aluminium. Methanol is similar but more toxic and corrosive.

Biodiesel is made by transesterification: vegetable oil or animal fat reacts with methanol, using a catalyst, to give fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) and glycerol. Its cetane number is about 50. The oxygen in FAME cuts soot, but it tends to raise NOx, and unburnt fuel dilutes the lube oil. Neat vegetable oil is not biodiesel; it is too viscous.

DME (CH3OCH3) has cetane about 55 and stores as a liquid near 5 bar; it burns with very little soot. Biogas is 50–70% methane, the rest mainly CO2, so it must be cleaned and has lower LHV than CNG. Producer gas from biomass gasification is mostly N2, CO and H2 with LHV about 4–6 MJ/Nm³ (roughly 4 MJ/kg), best for stationary engines. Ammonia (NH3) has no carbon, LHV about 18.6 MJ/kg, and stores near 10 bar or −33 °C, but it needs a pilot fuel and can release NOx.

Hydrogen and the comparison table

Hydrogen has LHV about 120 MJ/kg, the highest of the list. It has no carbon, so burning pure hydrogen gives no fuel-derived CO2, CO or soot. Its flammability range is about 4–75% by volume in air, its minimum ignition energy is near 0.02 mJ (about a tenth of petrol), and its flame speed is high. Flashback and injection timing therefore dominate the design. NOx still forms from atmospheric nitrogen at high peak temperature. Storage is compressed gas at 350 or 700 bar, liquid near 20 K, or in metal hydrides. Production is labelled grey, blue or green.

Question. Compare CO2 per megajoule for petrol taken as C8H18 and for methane. Petrol LHV = 44 MJ/kg; methane LHV = 50 MJ/kg. Use molar mass 114.2 g/mol for petrol and 16.04 g/mol for methane, and 44.0 g/mol for CO2.

6.7 Dual fuel and multi-fuel engines

A dual-fuel engine burns two fuels in one cycle: a gas that carries most of the energy, and a small diesel pilot that lights it. The reason is cetane. Methane and most gaseous fuels have a cetane number near zero, so air compressed to a diesel compression ratio will not ignite them in usable time; the pilot supplies the ignition. Multi-fuel means something different: one engine that runs on any one of several fuels, one at a time. You meet dual fuel in CNG buses, biogas gensets and LNG ships; multi-fuel in military trucks and farm engines.

You need: 6.6 for cetane, octane and calorific value, Unit 2 for injection and mixture preparation, and Unit 3 for ignition delay and knock.

Three words that are not synonyms

A dual-fuel engine burns two fuels in the same cycle, together. The gas carries most of the energy and the diesel is only the ignition source; both are inside the cylinder on every cycle.

A bi-fuel engine carries two fuels but burns one at a time. A CNG-petrol car is bi-fuel: a selector switches the fuel and the two never burn together.

A multi-fuel engine accepts any one of a range of single fuels, such as diesel, kerosene, a petrol blend or biodiesel, one at a time, without a hardware change at the roadside. Nothing burns together; the claim is fuel flexibility.

Metering the gas and lighting it

The gas is mixed with air before the intake valve. Three devices do it. A venturi mixer uses the pressure drop at a throat to draw gas in and hold the air-gas ratio roughly constant. A gas carburettor adds a zero-pressure regulator and a metering valve. Port injection puts solenoid injectors in the intake port and times the gas pulse, which gives better ratio control and less risk of a backfire in the manifold. Mixing gas into the intake air is called fumigation.

  1. Gas is metered into the air in the intake, giving a lean premixed charge.
  2. The charge is compressed. At a diesel compression ratio it is not hot enough, for long enough, for methane: a cetane number near zero means an ignition delay the cycle cannot wait for.
  3. A small diesel pilot is injected near top dead centre.
  4. The pilot burns first and its flame ignites the gas-air charge, then expansion follows as in a diesel.

Pilot size is quoted as a share of the total fuel energy, typically 5–30%. The share is small at high load, where the gas does the work, and large at light load, where a lean charge needs a bigger ignition source. Electronic common-rail injection sets the pilot mass per cycle accurately; a mechanical pump cannot.

A micro-pilot, a very small diesel injection that replaces the spark plug, ignites mixtures too lean for a spark in large-bore gas engines.

Substitution ratio, knock and the light-load penalty

SR_mass = (ṁ_d − ṁ_df) ÷ ṁ_d

ṁ_d is the diesel mass flow on diesel alone, in kg/h; ṁ_df is the diesel flow in dual-fuel mode, in kg/h; SR_mass is the fraction of diesel mass replaced.

SR_energy = (ṁ_g × LHV_g) ÷ (ṁ_g × LHV_g + ṁ_df × LHV_d)

ṁ_g is the gas mass flow, in kg/h; LHV_d and LHV_g are lower calorific values, in MJ/kg; SR_energy is the gas share of the fuel energy.

Knock in a dual-fuel engine appears at high load, where the substitution ratio is highest and the premixed charge is largest. If the pilot is advanced, the intake air hot or the compression ratio high, the end gas ignites before the flame front arrives. Methane's high octane number delays this, so knock is less of a limit than in a petrol engine, but it still caps the substitution at full load.

At light load the charge is very lean. Flame speed falls, some cycles burn incompletely, and CO and unburnt HC climb while efficiency drops (see 6.1). The unburnt methane leaving the cylinder is methane slip. It is natural gas itself, and it warms the climate far more per kg than CO2, so a few hundred ppm of slip matters more than the HC number suggests. Slip also comes from valve overlap, when fresh charge short-circuits to the exhaust.

Multi-fuel engines and producer gas

A multi-fuel engine differs from a diesel in what the pump and the chamber can be told to do. Injection timing must follow cetane: a low-cetane fuel has a long ignition delay and needs advance. Compression ratio is fixed at build, so it is a compromise: high enough for reliable diesel ignition, but capped because the petrol-like fuels in the range knock at high compression. Boost and nozzle size are matched to the fuel's calorific value and viscosity.

Four properties set the fuel range. Cetane decides whether the fuel ignites at all. Viscosity sets the spray and the pump's internal leakage. Lubricity protects pump and injector from wear. Volatility matters for petrol blends, which can vapour-lock a hot pump.

One example: some military trucks used a multifuel diesel with a density compensator on the distributor pump, which trims the injected volume as fuel density changes, so one setting works for diesel, kerosene and petrol blends.

Producer gas is made by burning wood, rice husk or charcoal in a restricted air supply; it is mostly CO and H2, with a calorific value several times lower than natural gas per unit volume. A producer-gas engine is therefore derated, and the diesel pilot share stays higher than with CNG. This is the classic rural off-grid set: gasifier, diesel engine, village load.

In shipping, dual fuel means LNG with a small pilot in a large two-stroke or four-stroke engine. At full load the pilot is a very small energy share; the penalty is methane slip, not diesel use.

Exam traps

  • Quoting substitution by mass when the question asks for the share of energy. The two calorific values differ, so 75% by mass becomes 72.1% by energy here.
  • Calling a CNG-petrol car a dual-fuel engine. It is bi-fuel: the fuels are switched, never burnt together.
  • Treating the pilot as a fixed 10%. The pilot is a share of energy set per operating point; it is large at light load and small at high load.
  • Assuming more substitution is always better. At light load it brings misfire, CO, HC and methane slip; at high load it brings knock.
  • Confusing methane slip with the HC limit. Slip is unburnt methane, counted as HC, but each kg warms the climate far more than a kg of CO2, so a small HC number still matters. It comes from incomplete or missed combustion of the lean charge, and from fresh charge short-circuiting to the exhaust during valve overlap.

6.8 Advanced combustion: HCCI, RCCI and low temperature combustion

Low-temperature combustion (LTC) is a way to burn fuel that avoids both NOx and soot at the same time. It dilutes and premixes the charge, so the peak temperature stays below about 1900 K and the local fuel-air ratio stays lean. The price is that ignition is no longer set by a spark or an injection pulse; chemical kinetics set it. HCCI and RCCI are the two main forms. HCCI has appeared in a few production petrol engines as a lean-burn mode; RCCI so far exists only in research engines.

You need: Unit 3 (ignition delay, knock and stages of combustion), 6.1 and 6.7.

The φ–T window

A conventional diesel spray burns with a diffusion flame. Inside the spray core φ is well above 1, so there is little oxygen and soot grows. Around the flame sheet the temperature is high, often above 2200 K, so NOx forms. If you add more air to cut soot, the flame gets hotter and NOx rises. If you cool the charge with EGR to cut NOx, soot rises. This is the soot-NOx trade-off.

The φ–T map plots local equivalence ratio against local temperature. φ is the equivalence ratio: actual fuel-air ratio divided by stoichiometric fuel-air ratio. Both axes are local gas properties inside the cylinder, not bulk cycle averages. The high-soot region sits on the rich side, roughly φ above 2 in the standard φ–T map, and at 1500–2500 K. The high-NOx region sits at T > about 1900 K. A window at φ below about 1 and T below about 1900 K has low values of both. LTC means any combustion strategy that keeps the burn inside this window. Two conditions are needed: the charge must be lean or well diluted, and the peak temperature must stay low. The figure shows the trade-off at a fixed rich φ: NOx climbs steeply above 1900 K while soot falls. NOx alone would be cured by cooling, and soot alone by raising temperature, so neither lever works by itself. The low-emission window needs the lean φ condition as well: only at φ below about 1 and T below about 1900 K are both low.

LTC burns cooler and leaner than a normal diesel. The heat release is spread out, and the flame, if any, is weak. The efficiency can still be diesel-like because the engine runs unthrottled and with a high compression ratio. The difficulty is ignition: a lean, cool mixture ignites slowly and unpredictably, and the burn rate is set by chemistry.

HCCI: lean auto-ignition

HCCI (homogeneous charge compression ignition) mixes fuel and air before compression, like a petrol engine, but ignites by compression heat, like a diesel. The charge is lean: φ is roughly 0.2–0.4. There is no spark plug and no flame front. As the piston rises, the whole charge reaches ignition conditions, and many sites ignite almost together. The burn is controlled by chemical kinetics, not by a flame speed.

τ = A exp(E/(R T))

τ is ignition delay in ms or s; A is a pre-exponential constant; E/R is the activation-temperature constant in K (about 15 000 K for diesel-like fuel); T is charge temperature in K.

A small rise in T shortens τ sharply. That is why HCCI needs intake air heating, hot EGR or a high compression ratio to make ignition happen, and why intake temperature control is critical.

Control knobs are intake temperature, EGR rate, variable valve timing (traps hot residual gas), compression ratio, fuel blending and stratification. None of these is a direct ignition-timing actuator like a spark or an injection pulse.

  • Gains: diesel-like efficiency from lean, unthrottled running; low pumping loss; near-zero NOx and soot.
  • Costs: narrow operating window; high HC and CO at low load because the charge is too cool to burn fully; very high pressure rise rate (ringing) at high load; hard cold start.

PCCI and RCCI

PCCI (premixed charge compression ignition) uses one fuel and an early direct injection. The fuel has time to mix before ignition, so the charge is partly premixed. Soot is lower than in a normal diesel because the rich core is smaller, but a φ gradient remains. Ignition timing is still set by kinetics, so PCCI shares HCCI's control problem.

RCCI (reactivity controlled compression ignition) uses two fuels with different reactivity. A low-reactivity fuel — petrol, ethanol or CNG — is port-injected to make a lean premixed charge. A high-reactivity diesel is injected later, directly into the cylinder. Ignition starts in the diesel-rich zones. The diesel then ignites the surrounding lean mixture. The reactivity gradient sets the burn rate: more diesel or a later diesel injection speeds up or delays the burn. This gives control over a wider load range and higher efficiency than HCCI.

Think of RCCI as stratification of chemistry rather than stratification of fuel alone. The port fuel sets the lean background; the direct fuel sets the ignition. It is not the same as a conventional dual-fuel engine, where a diesel pilot simply lights a gas-air mixture (see 6.7). In RCCI the two fuels are chosen for their ignition behaviour and the ratio is changed with load.

Worked example

Question. Two HCCI runs have the same charge pressure. Run 1 has charge temperature 900 K; run 2 has 950 K. Ignition delay is τ = A exp(E/(R T)) with E/R = 15 000 K. Find τ900/τ950. State the practical meaning.

  1. Write the ratio: τ900/τ950 = [A exp((E/R)(1/900))] ÷ [A exp((E/R)(1/950))] = exp[(E/R)(1/900 − 1/950)].
  2. Compute the reciprocal temperatures: 1/900 = 1.1111 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹; 1/950 = 1.0526 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹.
  3. Difference = 5.848 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹.
  4. Multiply by E/R = 15 000 K: exponent = 15 000 × 5.848 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.8772.
  5. exp(0.8772) = 2.404.
  6. A 50 K rise cuts the delay by a factor of about 2.4. Small temperature errors change ignition timing a lot, so HCCI needs tight intake temperature control.

Answer. τ900/τ950 ≈ 2.4, so the 950 K run has about 2.4 times shorter ignition delay.

Exam traps

  • HCCI is not a spark-ignition engine running lean. There is no spark and no flame front; the whole charge auto-ignites.
  • LTC does not make NOx exactly zero. It keeps peak temperature below the NOx-forming range, so NOx is near zero.
  • Cooling alone does not kill soot. If local φ stays above 1, soot can still form; LTC needs a lean or premixed charge as well.
  • High-load HCCI ringing is not the same as spark knock. It is a very high pressure rise rate from fast heat release.
  • RCCI is not just a diesel pilot in a gas engine. The two fuels are chosen for a reactivity difference and the burn is set by that gradient.

6.9 Combustion visualization: optical engines and laser diagnostics

Combustion is fast, hot and hidden inside metal. To see it, you build an engine with a quartz or sapphire window, light the inside with a laser, and record the scattered light with a high-speed camera. The window engine is not the production engine: its compression ratio, wall temperature and run time differ. Only a few universities teach this chapter, because the hardware is costly. It is used to check spray shape, flame speed, soot and the low-temperature strategies of 6.8.

You need Unit 3 (flame propagation, spray and air motion), 6.2 for soot incandescence and 6.8 for low-temperature strategies.

An engine you can look into

Optical access means a transparent path for light into the cylinder. A quartz or sapphire window can sit in the cylinder head, the liner or the piston crown. Sapphire is stronger and passes more infrared; quartz is cheaper and good in visible light. The Bowditch extended piston puts a 45° mirror in the piston and a window in the crown, so the camera looks along the cylinder axis. A fully transparent cylinder gives the widest view but weakens the structure. An optical spark plug or injector keeps the same location as the metal part.

An optical engine is not a metal engine. It usually runs a lower compression ratio, has colder or hotter walls, a simplified chamber and a short run limited by window fouling. Fouling is soot and oil on the glass; it dims the signal. Often it fires only a few cycles, so cycle-to-cycle variation is large. The images show mechanism, not production numbers.

Light tuned to a molecule, a droplet or a particle

High-speed direct photography records flame growth and soot incandescence, the glow of hot soot. Schlieren and shadowgraph turn refractive-index gradients into brightness changes, showing density gradients and the flame front. Mie scattering uses a laser and seeded droplets; the droplets scatter strongly and reveal spray shape and penetration. Rayleigh scattering comes from gas molecules; it is weak and gives temperature or concentration after calibration. Laser-induced fluorescence (LIF) excites a chosen molecule and records longer-wavelength emission; it maps fuel, the OH radical or NO. Particle image velocimetry (PIV) uses two short laser pulses and seeded particles; cross-correlation gives the velocity field. Laser Doppler anemometry (LDA) measures the Doppler shift from particles crossing fringes, giving swirl or tumble. Two-colour pyrometry takes soot emission at two wavelengths; the ratio gives soot temperature. Light extinction measures laser light lost across the cylinder and gives soot volume fraction. Endoscopy uses a small borescope in a production engine.

The usual lasers are Nd:YAG at 532 nm, excimer at 308 nm and dye lasers for tunable wavelengths. nm is nanometre, 10−9 m. A light sheet, not a beam, is what illuminates a plane: cylindrical optics spread the beam into a thin sheet. The flame speed from two frames is Sf = Δr ÷ Δt, where Sf is mean flame speed in m/s, Δr is the change in flame radius in m and Δt is the time between frames in s.

Worked example

Question. Two high-speed frames are 0.1 ms apart. Flame radius grows from 8 mm to 12 mm. Engine speed is 1500 rev/min. Find mean flame speed, time for one crank angle degree, and crank angle between frames.

  1. Δr = 12 − 8 = 4 mm = 4 × 10−3 m; Δt = 0.1 ms = 1 × 10−4 s.
  2. Sf = Δr ÷ Δt = 4 × 10−3 ÷ 1 × 10−4 = 40 m/s.
  3. One revolution takes 60 ÷ 1500 = 0.04 s. One crank angle degree takes 0.04 ÷ 360 = 1.11 × 10−4 s.
  4. Crank angle = 1 × 10−4 ÷ 1.11 × 10−4 ≈ 0.9°.

Answer. Mean flame speed is 40 m/s; one crank angle degree takes 1.11 × 10−4 s; the frames are 0.9° crank angle apart.

Exam traps

  • Quoting an optical-engine flame speed as a production value; lower compression, different walls, a simplified chamber and a short run all shift it.
  • Forgetting window fouling: soot and oil on quartz dim the signal and limit the run to a few fired cycles.
  • Treating a line-of-sight image as planar: extinction and direct photography integrate along the beam.
  • Swapping Mie and Rayleigh: Mie comes from seeded droplets; Rayleigh comes from gas molecules.
  • Using a beam instead of a sheet for planar imaging: a beam lights a line, a sheet lights a plane.

Takeaways

  1. CO, unburnt HC and NOx each come from a different failure of combustion — too little oxygen, a flame that died or hid, and a flame that got too hot — so a single fix cannot remove all three.
  2. Diesel particulate grows inside the rich core of the spray and then partly burns out later; smoke meters see only the black carbon part, not the whole mass.
  3. In-cylinder methods stop the pollutant forming, after-treatment converts or traps it afterwards; modern engines need both together.
  4. Each exhaust gas is measured by a physical effect unique to it: infrared absorption for CO and CO₂, ozone chemiluminescence for NO, a hydrogen flame for HC, filter blackening for smoke.
  5. An emission standard is meaningless without its driving cycle, because the same engine gives different g/km on different speed traces.
  6. An alternative fuel changes C/H ratio, octane or cetane number and storage difficulty at the same time, so all three must be traded together.
  7. A dual-fuel engine uses a diesel pilot to light a gas that would not ignite on compression alone; a multi-fuel engine runs on one of several fuels at a time.
  8. Low-temperature combustion avoids both NOx and soot by staying cool and lean, but the price is that ignition is set by chemical kinetics, not by spark or injection timing.
  9. Optical engines and laser diagnostics turn a cylinder into a measuring cell and give the spatial detail that exhaust-gas analysis cannot.

Glossary

A colourless, odourless, toxic gas formed when fuel carbon is only partly oxidised, mainly in rich or quenched regions.

A honeycomb coated with noble metal that speeds up oxidation or reduction reactions in the exhaust without being consumed.

Diesel particulate filter (DPF)
A wall-flow filter that traps soot, cleaned by burning the collected carbon in a periodic regeneration event.
Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR)
Returning part of the exhaust to the intake to dilute the charge and lower the peak combustion temperature, mainly to cut NOx.
Lean NOx trap (LNT)
A catalyst that stores NOx during lean operation and reduces it during brief rich excursions.
Low-temperature combustion (LTC)
Any strategy that keeps local peak temperature below the NOx threshold and local equivalence ratio below the soot threshold.
Octane number
A scale of resistance to knock in a spark-ignition engine; higher means the fuel can stand a higher compression ratio.
Opacity
The fraction of light blocked by an exhaust sample, expressed in percent; a fast but approximate smoke measure.
Optical engine
A research engine with a transparent piston crown, liner or spacer so the combustion chamber can be photographed or laser-lit.
Oxides of nitrogen (NOx)
Mainly NO with some NO₂, formed from atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen at high temperature inside the flame.
Ozone
O₃, used in the chemiluminescence analyser as the reagent that makes excited NO₂ emit light.
Particulate matter (PM)
All solid and liquid material collected from diluted exhaust on a filter at a set temperature, including carbon, SOF, sulphate and ash.
Pilot injection
A small early injection of high-cetane diesel used to ignite a main charge of gas or a low-reactivity fuel.
ppmC
Parts per million of carbon atoms, the unit of the FID, which counts carbon rather than molecules.
Regeneration
The burning off of soot stored in a DPF, either passively by NO₂ or actively by raising the exhaust temperature.
Ring of fire
The narrow gap region between piston crown and liner wall where flame quenches and leaves unburnt fuel.
Selective catalytic reduction (SCR)
Reduction of NOx to nitrogen and water by ammonia on a catalyst, used on lean exhaust where a TWC cannot work.
Soot
The carbon-rich solid core of diesel particulate, formed by pyrolysis and growth of fuel molecules in an oxygen-poor zone.
Stoichiometric air-fuel ratio
The air mass per unit fuel mass that exactly supplies the oxygen for complete combustion; about 14.7 for gasoline and 14.5 for diesel.
Unburnt hydrocarbons (HC)
Fuel or partly burnt fuel leaving the cylinder unchanged, from crevices, wall quenching, oil films, misfire or valve overlap.
Urea solution (DEF / AdBlue)
A 32.5% aqueous urea solution injected into the exhaust to make the ammonia that SCR needs.
