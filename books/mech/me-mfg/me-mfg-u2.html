2.1 Plastic deformation, yield criteria; hot and cold working

Bulk forming works because metal does not crack the moment it is pushed; it first stretches elastically, then flows permanently. The stress at which permanent flow starts is the yield strength. After that, cold metal keeps getting stronger as it strains, while hot metal keeps its flow stress almost constant because recrystallisation removes strain hardening. This one idea decides the force, the press, the finish and the tolerance for rolling, forging, extrusion and drawing.

You need the stress–strain curve from engineering mechanics: stress = force/area, strain = change in length/original length, and elastic modulus.

True stress, true strain and the flow curve

Engineering stress uses the original area A0 and falls after necking. Metal forming needs the stress on the area that exists now, A, because the bar is thinning as it extends. Engineering strain uses the original length; true strain compares each small extension with the length at that moment.

σ = F/A, ε = ln(L/L0)

F is the instantaneous load in N, A is the instantaneous cross-section area in m², L is the current length, L0 is the original length, σ is true stress in Pa (MPa in practice), ε is true strain, dimensionless. True strains add over passes; engineering strains do not.

Cold working raises the stress needed to keep deforming. The curve of flow stress against true strain is the flow curve. A common fit for annealed metal is the power law below.

σ = K εⁿ

σ is flow stress in MPa, ε is true strain, K is the strength coefficient in MPa (the stress at ε = 1 on the fitted curve), n is the strain-hardening exponent, dimensionless. n = 0 means perfect plasticity; annealed steels often have n between 0.2 and 0.3.

The power-law fit is valid only for ε > 0. At ε = 0 it gives σ = 0, which is wrong: real metal yields at σy, not at zero stress. Flow curves are read from the yield stress onward, and the fit is never used at zero plastic strain.

For a pass that starts from the annealed state, the average stress needed is the mean flow stress.

σ̄ = K εⁿ/(n + 1) = σ/(n + 1)

σ̄ is the mean flow stress over the pass in MPa, σ is the flow stress at the exit strain, n is the strain-hardening exponent. Use σ̄ in the load formula in load estimation; using the exit flow stress alone overestimates the load.

Yield criteria: when does flow start?

A point inside a part has three principal stresses σ1, σ2 and σ3, ordered σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3. A yield criterion combines them into one number and compares it with the tensile yield strength σy. Some syllabi call this section plasticity or yield theories.

Tresca's maximum shear stress criterion says flow starts when the largest shear stress reaches σy/2. It is simple and conservative, and it ignores the middle stress σ2.

σ1 − σ3 = σy

σ1 is the largest principal stress, σ3 the smallest, both in MPa; σy is the yield strength in simple tension in MPa.

von Mises' distortion energy criterion says flow starts when the distortion energy reaches the value at yield. It fits ductile metals better.

(σ1 − σ2)² + (σ2 − σ3)² + (σ3 − σ1)² = 2σy²

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa; σy is the tensile yield strength in MPa. In plane stress, σ3 = 0, so √(σ1² + σ2² − σ1σ2) = σy.

Under pure shear Tresca gives τ = 0.5σy and von Mises gives τ = σy/√3 ≈ 0.577σy. The two differ by about 15%; Tresca is the safer design choice.

Rolling of wide strip and strip drawing are nearly plane strain: the width cannot change, so σ2 = (σ1 + σ3)/2, and von Mises gives the plane-strain yield value below. Wire drawing is axisymmetric: the diameter changes, σ2 = σ3, and yield is at σ1 − σ3 = σy directly, with no 1.15 factor. Using σy instead of 1.15σy in plane strain underestimates the load by about 13%, that is, by a factor of 1.15.

σ1 − σ3 = (2/√3)σy ≈ 1.15σy

σ1 − σ3 is the stress difference that causes yield in plane strain, in MPa; σy is the tensile yield strength in MPa.

Hot working and cold working

Cold working is deformation below the recrystallisation temperature. Grains flatten, dislocations multiply and the flow stress rises. The product is stronger and less ductile, but it may crack, so annealing between passes is common. Cold rolling gives the smooth sheet used for car bodies and appliance panels.

Hot working is deformation above the recrystallisation temperature. New grains form during and after deformation, so strain hardening is wiped out and the flow stress stays almost flat. Large reductions are possible, but oxide scale gives a rough surface and loose tolerance. Hot rolling of slabs and forging of crankshafts are examples.

The recrystallisation temperature is roughly 0.4 of the melting point on the Kelvin scale.

T_recryst ≈ 0.4 Tm

T_recryst is the recrystallisation temperature in K, Tm is the melting point in K. Add 273.15 to a Celsius melting point first. For steel with Tm ≈ 1773 K, T_recryst ≈ 709 K ≈ 436 °C; for aluminium with Tm ≈ 933 K, T_recryst ≈ 373 K ≈ 100 °C.

Warm working sits between about 0.3 Tm and 0.4 Tm: less force than cold working and less scaling than hot working. It is a separate regime just below T_recryst, where recovery rearranges dislocations and lowers the flow stress without forming new grains. Recovery is not recrystallisation, so warm working behaves differently from cold working even though it is below the recrystallisation temperature.

Worked example

Question. A mild steel bar is cold worked from the annealed state to a true strain of 0.15. Its flow curve is σ = 550 ε^0.25 MPa, with σ in MPa. (a) Find the flow stress at the end of the pass. (b) Find the mean flow stress. (c) The melting point is 1500 °C. Find the recrystallisation temperature in K and °C, and state whether working at 900 °C is hot or cold working.

  1. Given ε = 0.15, K = 550 MPa, n = 0.25, Tm = 1500 °C.
  2. εⁿ = 0.15^0.25 = 0.6223.
  3. σ = K εⁿ = 550 × 0.6223 = 342.3 MPa.
  4. σ̄ = σ/(n + 1) = 342.3/1.25 = 273.8 MPa.
  5. Tm = 1500 + 273.15 = 1773.15 K.
  6. T_recryst = 0.4 Tm = 0.4 × 1773.15 = 709.3 K = 709.3 − 273.15 = 436.1 °C.
  7. 900 °C is above 436.1 °C, so the work is hot working.

Answer. Flow stress 342.3 MPa, mean flow stress 273.8 MPa, recrystallisation temperature 709.3 K (436.1 °C); 900 °C working is hot working.

Exam traps

  • Using engineering stress and strain after the ultimate point, where they fall, instead of true values that keep rising.
  • Treating Tresca and von Mises as the same: they differ by about 15% in shear, and Tresca is conservative.
  • Forgetting the 1.15 factor in plane strain (rolling of wide strip, strip drawing) and using σy directly, so the calculated load is about 13% too low. Wire drawing is axisymmetric and needs no factor.
  • Putting Celsius into T_recryst ≈ 0.4 Tm; the rule needs Kelvin.
  • Calling the exit flow stress the mean flow stress; the mean is σ/(n+1) for a pass from zero strain.

2.2 Rolling: principle, rolling mills and defects

Rolling passes a strip between two rotating rolls set closer than the strip is thick. Friction at the roll surface drags the strip into the gap; the strip leaves thinner and longer. This one idea makes most flat steel: car bodies, food cans, roofing sheet, and railway rails by shape rolling. It is the highest-volume bulk forming process because it is continuous, fast and cheap per tonne. The same mechanics explain why a heavy pass may refuse to enter the rolls, and why rolled sheet can have wavy edges or a buckled centre.

You need: 2.1 for flow stress and the hot–cold split.

The roll bite and the friction condition

Two rolls of radius R (mm) turn toward each other. The gap is set to the exit thickness h₁ (mm); the entry strip is h₀ (mm). The draft is Δh = h₀ − h₁, in mm. The strip touches each roll along an arc of horizontal length L (mm). For shallow bites, L ≈ √(RΔh). The bite angle α is the angle between the entry point and the roll centre line.

Friction pulls the strip in; the roll also pushes it back. The strip enters only if μ ≥ tan α, where μ is the coefficient of friction (no unit). For small α, tan α ≈ √(Δh/R). Squaring gives Δh ≤ μ²R. So Δhmax = μ²R. Use the radius R, never the diameter.

Δhmax = μ²R

Δhmax is the largest thickness reduction in one pass (mm); μ is the coefficient of friction (no unit); R is the roll radius (mm).

L = √(R Δh)

L is the contact length along the arc (mm); R is the roll radius (mm); Δh = h₀ − h₁ is the draft (mm).

Near entry the strip is slower than the roll surface: backward slip. At the neutral point strip speed equals roll surface speed. After it the strip is faster: forward slip. Forward slip = (vexit − vroll)/vroll, where vexit is exit strip speed and vroll is roll surface speed.

Mills: from slab to coil

A two-high mill has two rolls. A reversing two-high mill runs the strip back and forth. A three-high mill passes the strip between top and middle rolls one way, then middle and bottom rolls the other way. A four-high mill uses two small work rolls backed by two large rolls that stop bending. A cluster mill, such as a Sendzimir mill, supports small work rolls with many backup rolls for very thin hard strip. A tandem mill has several stands in line, one pass each.

Hot rolling starts with a reheated slab. A reversing roughing mill breaks it down; a finishing train of four-high stands rolls it to coil thickness; a run-out table sprays water; a down coiler winds the strip. Hot rolling is above the recrystallisation temperature, so the metal is soft and the surface carries oxide scale. Cold rolling is at room temperature. It gives better finish and tolerance, but work-hardens the metal, so annealing is needed between passes. Roll camber and roll bending control crown, the small centre-to-edge thickness difference.

Flat rolling makes plate, sheet and strip. Shape rolling makes rails and sections; some syllabi use these two names. Roll pass design for sections is left to a dedicated course.

Defects and rolled products

Rolled strip has named defects. Wavy edges: edges elongate more than the centre, so the extra length buckles into waves. Centre buckle: the centre elongates more, so the middle lifts. Edge cracking: edges are held back and tear. Alligatoring: a horizontal split at a slab end because surface and centre deform differently. Laps and seams: folded metal or cracks along the strip. Scale: oxide pressed into a hot-rolled surface. Roll marks: repeating marks from a damaged roll.

Rolled products: a bloom, a large square section; a billet, a smaller square section; a slab, a wide rectangle for flat products; plate, thick flat product; sheet, thin flat product; strip, sheet in coil form; rail, a shaped product. Bloom, billet and slab are intermediate shapes. Loads are estimated in 2.6; forging and extrusion are in 2.3 and 2.4.

Worked example

Question. A 250 mm wide strip is rolled from 20 mm to 15 mm in one pass on rolls of radius 300 mm. The coefficient of friction is 0.10. (a) Is the pass possible? (b) What minimum μ would make it possible? (c) Find the contact length.

  1. Data: h₀ = 20 mm, h₁ = 15 mm, R = 300 mm, μ = 0.10, width b = 250 mm. Width does not enter the bite condition.
  2. Draft: Δh = h₀ − h₁ = 20 − 15 = 5 mm.
  3. Maximum draft: Δhmax = μ²R = 0.10² × 300 = 0.01 × 300 = 3 mm.
  4. Compare: 5 mm > 3 mm, so friction cannot pull the strip in. The pass fails.
  5. Required friction: μ ≥ √(Δh/R) = √(5/300) = √0.01667 = 0.1291 ≈ 0.13.
  6. Contact length: L = √(RΔh) = √(300 × 5) = √1500 = 38.73 mm ≈ 38.7 mm.

Answer. The pass is not possible at μ = 0.10; it needs μ ≈ 0.13 or two passes, and L ≈ 38.7 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the roll diameter in Δhmax = μ²R. R is the radius; a diameter value doubles the wrong quantity.
  • Draft is Δh in mm. Reduction ratio is r = Δh/h₀, no unit. A 5 mm draft on a 20 mm strip is r = 0.25, not 5.
  • A pass with Δh > μ²R cannot be forced by more roll force alone. The strip slips or buckles; change μ, use a smaller draft, or use larger R.
  • Wavy edges and centre buckle look similar but have opposite causes. Wavy edges: edges longer. Centre buckle: centre longer.
  • The neutral point is not the middle of the contact arc. It is where strip speed equals roll surface speed; its position moves with friction and tension.

2.3 Forging: types, operations and defects

Forging squeezes a hot or cold billet between dies, again and again, until metal fills the shape. Each squeeze is local, so grain flow bends with the contour instead of being cut through. The part is therefore stronger and tougher than the same shape machined from bar. Forging makes crankshafts, connecting rods, railway wheels, spanners and many hand tools. The die cavity, the flash gap and the number of blows decide whether metal fills the corners or folds over itself. This chapter covers the types, operations, machine choice and defects to reject.

You need 2.1 for hot working and flow stress.

Open-die, closed-die and related types

Forging changes shape by repeated local compression between dies. Open-die forging uses flat or simple dies. The metal is not fully enclosed, so the operator turns and moves the billet between blows. Common open-die operations are:

  • Upsetting: height falls and cross-section grows.
  • Drawing out: length grows and cross-section falls.
  • Fullering: a rounded tool spreads metal sideways.
  • Edging: a rough outline is formed before blocking.
  • Swaging: repeated hammering while the billet rotates reduces the diameter.
  • Punching: a hole is pushed through the billet.

Closed-die or impression forging traps metal in a shaped cavity. A blocker impression makes a rough shape; a finisher impression gives the final shape. Excess metal escapes as a thin flash through the flash gap and collects in a gutter. Precision forging uses a very small flash or none. Upset forging and heading gather metal at the end of a bar. Roll forging uses grooved rolls. Rotary swaging uses dies inside a spinning ring.

A connecting rod follows the closed-die sequence in the figure: billet heating, upsetting, edging, blocking, finishing, trimming. Volume stays constant, so A₁h₁ = A₂h₂, where A is cross-section area in mm² and h is height in mm.

Machines, temperatures and grain flow

A hammer gives a rapid blow; a press gives a slower squeeze; an upsetter works horizontally on a bar end.

Typical starting temperatures: carbon steel 1100–1250°C, aluminium alloys 350–500°C, copper alloys 700–900°C. Too cold means high load and cracks; too hot means burnt metal. Presses are quieter and give deeper fill; hammers are cheaper and faster for simple shapes.

Grain flow is the direction in which the original grains and inclusions line up after deformation. In forging, flow follows the die contour, so the crankshaft, connecting rod, railway wheel and spanner have continuous fibres around corners. Machining cuts these fibres. The forged part therefore resists fatigue better. Flash is trimmed after finishing, usually in a trimming press.

Defects and their causes

Check each forging against this list.

  • Unfilled section: metal never reached a die corner; the corner is missing.
  • Cold shut or lap: a folded surface was pressed in but did not weld; a line remains.
  • Flash crack: a crack along the flash, often from cold flash or a sharp gutter.
  • Scale pit: oxide scale was pressed into the surface, leaving a pit.
  • Die shift or mismatch: upper and lower dies are misaligned, so the two halves step.
  • Internal cracks: centre cracks from low temperature, too fast a squeeze or poor shape.
  • Decarburisation: surface carbon is lost in the furnace, so the skin is soft.
  • Burnt metal: overheating oxidises grain boundaries; the forging is brittle and must be scrapped.

A cold shut is a folded lap; an unfilled section is missing metal. Do not mix them. A flash crack is not a crack in the useful part unless it runs inward. The part is accepted only after flash trimming and a check for these defects.

Worked example

Question. A cylindrical billet 80 mm diameter and 100 mm long is upset forged to a height of 60 mm. Find the final diameter, the average contact area, and the press load for a mean flow stress of 250 MPa and a friction multiplier of 1.2.

  1. Volume constancy: V = (π/4)d₁²h₁ = (π/4)(80 mm)²(100 mm) = 502,655 mm³.
  2. Final diameter: d₂ = d₁√(h₁/h₂) = 80√(100/60) = 103.3 mm.
  3. Final area: A₂ = V/h₂ = 502,655 mm³ / 60 mm = 8,378 mm².
  4. Load: F = k σ A₂ = 1.2 × 250 N/mm² × 8,378 mm² = 2.513 × 10⁶ N = 2.51 MN. Here k is the friction multiplier, σ is mean flow stress, and A₂ is contact area; load formulas are in 2.6.

Answer. Final diameter 103.3 mm, average contact area 8,378 mm², press load about 2.51 MN.

Exam traps

  • The flash gap is not wasted space. It raises die pressure so the cavity fills; a perfectly closed die would need a much larger press.
  • Cold shut (lap) is a folded and unwelded surface. Unfilled section is missing metal at a corner.
  • Flash crack is a crack at the flash; do not call every crack a flash crack.
  • Burnt metal and decarburisation both come from heating, but burnt metal cannot be recovered by machining; decarburisation is a soft surface layer.
  • Die shift is a mismatch between die halves, not a shift in grain flow.

2.4 Extrusion: direct, indirect and impact

Extrusion pushes a metal billet through a die opening with a hydraulic ram. The metal takes the die's cross-section and leaves as one long continuous piece. Aluminium window sections, heat-sink fins, ladder rails and toothpaste tubes are made this way. This chapter covers direct, indirect and impact extrusion, and the defects that spoil the product.

You need: 2.1 for hot and cold working and for the mean flow stress σ̄.

Ram, container, die: the basic setup

An extrusion press has three parts. The container is a thick steel cylinder. A die with the required opening is clamped at one end. A ram enters at the other end and pushes a billet, a solid metal cylinder, often preheated. The metal upsets, flows through the die and leaves as a continuous length: a 300 mm billet becomes many metres of bar. Rolling and forging shape metal in steps or blows (see 2.2 and 2.3); extrusion makes one constant cross-section in a single push.

The taper leading into the die opening is the die angle, quoted as the included angle 2α, where α is the semi-die angle measured from the extrusion axis.

R = A₀ / A_f = (D₀ / D_f)²

R is the extrusion ratio (no unit); A₀ is the billet cross-section area in mm²; A_f is the product cross-section area in mm²; D₀ is the billet diameter in mm and D_f the product diameter in mm, for round sections only.

R measures how hard the pass is. Cold extrusion keeps R below about 3 for steel, because flow stress is already high. Hot extrusion of aluminium runs at R from about 10 to 100, and thin hollow sections need the upper end.

p = σ̄ ln R

p is the ideal extrusion pressure in MPa (1 MPa = 1 N/mm²); σ̄ is the mean flow stress of the metal in MPa; ln R is the natural logarithm of the extrusion ratio.

This p pays only for changing the shape. Real ram pressure is higher, because friction on the container wall and internal shearing add to it (see 2.6).

Direct and indirect: where the friction sits

Direct (forward) extrusion is the common picture. The ram touches the billet and pushes it down the container; the die stays at the far end. The billet slides against the container wall over its whole length, so friction is large. Ram pressure is highest just after flow starts, falls as the billet shortens and less of it rubs, and rises slightly at the end when the short butt is squeezed out. That butt is left behind and cut off.

Indirect (backward) extrusion moves the die instead. The die sits on the front of a hollow ram and the far end of the container is closed by a solid block. The ram pushes the die into the billet; metal flows backwards through the die and up the hollow bore. The billet does not slide on the container wall, so that friction disappears. Ram force is lower for the same reduction, often by a quarter or more, and a larger R per pass becomes possible.

The price is mechanical. A hollow ram is weaker, so press capacity limits the work, and the product must pass through the ram. Die friction remains in both arrangements: the die surface still rubs on the flowing metal.

Impact and hydrostatic extrusion

Impact extrusion is a single-blow cold process. A slug of soft metal sits in a die cavity. A punch comes down fast, and the metal has nowhere to go but up through the narrow gap between punch and die wall, so a thin-walled cup with a closed bottom forms in a fraction of a second. Toothpaste tubes, aerosol cans and zinc battery cases are made this way. The metal flows instead of being squeezed flat, so the wall can be far thinner than the punch travel.

Hydrostatic extrusion surrounds the billet with a fluid, oil or a polymer, at high pressure. The fluid itself drives the billet through the die. No ram touches the billet and there is no container friction. The whole billet is under compression, so brittle metals can be extruded, and two metals can be clad in one pass. Sealing the high-pressure fluid makes the equipment costly.

Hot or cold, tubes, and what goes wrong

Hot extrusion runs above the recrystallisation temperature (see 2.1). Flow stress is low, so R can be large and the press smaller, but oxide scale spoils the surface; steel is usually extruded with a glass lubricant that melts into a protective film. Cold extrusion runs at room temperature, needs phosphate coating and soap lubricant, and gives a smooth, accurate, strain-hardened product at higher flow stress and a smaller R per pass.

With a flat-faced die, metal in the corners stops moving and forms a dead-metal zone. It acts like a natural die of smaller angle and makes flow more uniform, but lumps of it can break away into the product. Die angle is a compromise: too small and the die surface is long and rubs; too large and the metal shears internally.

For a tube, metal flows through an annular gap between the die and a mandrel, a fixed or floating plug that sets the bore. A port-hole die splits the billet into legs around a central mandrel, and the legs weld back together under pressure, giving hollow aluminium profiles such as window frames and heat sinks.

  • Pipe (tailpipe): at the back end the flow turns inward and drags surface oxide and lubricant to the axis, leaving a hollow pipe. Discard the butt.
  • Surface cracking, bambooing: transverse cracks in rings like a bamboo stem, from hot shortness, too high a temperature or too fast a ram.
  • Chevron cracking (centre burst): arrow-shaped cracks on the axis, from tensile stress in the core when R is low or the die angle large.
  • Die lines and blistering: longitudinal marks from a worn die; blisters from gas or lubricant trapped under the skin.

The machine is a horizontal hydraulic press, so the long product runs out along the floor. Aluminium window sections, heat sinks, ladder rails, door frames and copper busbars are typical products.

Worked example

Question. A 100 mm diameter aluminium billet is direct extruded into a 40 mm diameter bar. The mean flow stress of the aluminium at the working temperature is 200 MPa. Find the extrusion ratio, the ideal extrusion pressure, the ram force and the percentage area reduction.

Exam traps

  • R is an area ratio. Writing R = 100/40 = 2.5 instead of (100/40)² = 6.25 throws the whole calculation off.
  • Indirect extrusion removes billet–container friction, not die friction. The die still rubs on the flowing metal in every route.

  • Pipe and chevron cracking are different defects. Pipe is a hollow channel at the back end on the axis, fed by surface oxide; chevron cracks are internal arrowheads in the core, driven by tensile stress.
  • 1 MPa = 1 N/mm². Multiply pressure in MPa by area in mm² to get newtons; mixing millimetres with metres gives an error of 10⁶.

2.5 Wire and tube drawing

Wire drawing pulls a rod or wire through a converging die. The metal is not pushed: a capstan grips the front end and pulls, so the wire leaves thinner and longer. The pulling stress must stay below the metal's own flow stress; if it does not, the wire breaks instead of drawing. Tube drawing uses the same pull on a hollow tube, with or without an internal plug. You see the process in electrical cable, cycle spokes, guitar strings and heat-exchanger tubes.

You need: 2.1 for flow stress and cold working, and 2.4 for the die-pressure idea and the difference between pushing and pulling.

Wire drawing: pull through a converging die

Wire drawing changes a round rod into a smaller round wire. The die is a hard insert with a hole that narrows. The wire enters on one side, passes through the hole, and a rotating capstan on the other side pulls it. Friction at the die wall and the work of changing shape both need force. The capstan supplies that force as tension in the drawn wire, not as compression behind the rod.

σ<sub>d</sub> = φ σ̄ ln(A<sub>0</sub>/A<sub>f</sub>)

σd is the draw stress in MPa; φ is the friction-and-shape factor, about 1.2 to 1.5; σ̄ is the mean flow stress of the metal in MPa; A0 is the entry cross-section area and Af is the exit area, both in mm².

  1. Bell: the flared entry that guides wire and lubricant into the die.
  2. Approach: the converging cone where the diameter is reduced; its included angle 2α is the approach angle. A small angle lowers die pressure but makes a longer contact.
  3. Bearing or land: a short parallel section that sets the final diameter. Too long adds friction; too short gives poor size control.
  4. Back relief: the opening behind the bearing, so the drawn wire does not rub after sizing.

The area reduction in one pass is r = 1 − Af/A0 = 1 − (df/d0)². The ideal drawing limit comes from σd = σ̄: ln(A0/Af) = 1, so Af/A0 = e⁻¹ = 0.368 and r = 63.2%. Real passes are much smaller because friction, die wear and the risk of centre cracks grow. Many wire mills take about 20% area reduction per pass. The 36% in the worked example passes the stress check (σd/σ̄ = 0.58) but is a calculation exercise; in the shop the same reduction is usually split, as in the figure (about 19% then about 21%), because centre-burst risk and die wear, not just the stress check, set the limit.

Machines, dies and lubrication

A single-hole machine has one die and one rotating capstan. The capstan surface speed is the drawing speed. Multi-hole machines place several dies in line, with a capstan between each pair. Each capstan runs faster than the previous one because the wire is thinner and longer. The tension between dies must be controlled; too much back tension adds to the draw stress.

Lubrication separates wire from die. Dry drawing uses soap or grease powders, usually for coarse wire. Wet drawing immerses die and wire in oil-water emulsion, usually for fine wire and high speed. The lubricant also carries away heat. Cemented carbide dies suit steel wire; diamond dies suit very fine wire where wear matters.

Wire for ropes is often patented: it is heated and cooled to make a fine pearlitic structure, then drawn. Galvanised wire is zinc coated before or after drawing; drawing after coating needs care so the coating does not score the die. The figure shows a 5 mm rod drawn to 4.5 mm, annealed, then drawn to 4 mm.

Wire defects include centre burst and chevron cracking, which are internal cracks on the centre line, die scratches, die wear, and breakage when draw stress exceeds the metal's strength.

Tube drawing: sinking and supported drawing

A tube is a hollow cylinder. Tube drawing pulls it through a die just as wire is pulled. Without internal support the process is tube sinking. The outside diameter falls, the wall usually thickens a little, and the bore can become slightly non-circular or eccentric. Sinking is cheap but cannot hold wall thickness closely.

A fixed plug sits inside the die; the tube wall is squeezed between plug and die. It controls inside diameter and wall thickness. A floating plug is free and finds its own position by force balance; it is easy to set and good for long tubes. A moving mandrel travels with the tube and is removed afterwards by reeling or leaching. It gives the best inside surface and uniform wall, at higher cost.

Tube defects include eccentric bore and wall-thickness variation. Check the outside diameter and wall at both ends; a drifting plug shows as a taper.

Worked example

Question. A 5.0 mm diameter wire is drawn to 4.0 mm in one pass. The mean flow stress is 350 MPa and the friction-and-shape factor is 1.3. Find the true strain, draw stress, the ratio σd/σ̄ and the area reduction. State whether the pass is safe.

  1. Entry diameter d0 = 5.0 mm and exit diameter df = 4.0 mm.

  7. The ratio 0.58 is below 1, so the draw stress is below the metal's flow stress; the pass meets the stress check. In the shop this 36% is usually split into two passes, about 19% then about 21%, as in the figure, because centre-burst risk and die wear set the practical limit.

  • Forgetting the safety check σd < σy, where σy is the flow (yield) strength of the wire leaving the die. If the draw stress exceeds it, the wire breaks instead of drawing.
  • Mixing diameter ratio with area ratio. Area ratio is (d0/df)², not d0/df.
  • Forgetting that multi-pass wire drawing needs an anneal between heavy passes. Cold work raises flow stress, so the next pass may break the wire.

2.6 Load estimation in forming processes

Every bulk forming load is a mean flow stress times a contact area, raised by a friction and shape factor. The mean flow stress σ̄ is the average stress that keeps the metal yielding through the pass. The contact area comes from the process geometry. Load estimation tells you whether a mill, press or die can take the force and whether the motor can supply the power. Only a few universities teach this chapter in detail.

You need: 2.1 for flow stress, and 2.2 to 2.5 for the contact geometry of each process.

Stress times contact area

Bulk forming load follows one pattern: load = mean flow stress × contact area × correction. The mean flow stress σ̄ (MPa; use Pa in the formulas) is the average stress needed to keep the metal yielding over the strain of that pass; use it, not the initial yield stress, because strain and stress vary through the zone. Contact area A (m²) is the area where tool touches work, not the whole billet. The correction K (dimensionless) covers friction and shape. For extrusion the result is written as a pressure: p = σ̄(a + b ln R). R = A₀/A_f is the extrusion ratio; A₀ is billet area and A_f is product area; a and b are constants set by die angle and friction. Ideal frictionless cold extrusion has a = 0 and b = 1, so p = σ̄ ln R. A friction estimate can use b ≈ 1.5, which lifts pressure at every R. This is the only load idea in this chapter.

Rolling, forging and drawing loads

Rolling uses the projected contact area under one roll, A = wL. Use σ̄ in Pa and lengths in m. F = σ̄ w L. w is strip width (m); L = √(RΔh) is the projected contact length (m); R is roll radius (m); Δh = h₀ − h₁ is the draft (m), with h₀ inlet and h₁ exit thickness. Torque per roll T = F L/2 (N·m). Total power P = 2Tω for two rolls, with ω = 2πN/60 rad/s and N mill speed in rev/min.

Forging: F = σ̄ A K_f. A is plan area. For a disc of radius r and height h, K_f ≈ 1 + 2μr/(3h); μ is friction coefficient. Extrusion force is p times ram area, with p = σ̄(a + b ln R). Drawing stress σ_d = σ̄ ln(A₀/A_f) × φ; φ > 1 is a friction factor, and σ_d must stay below yield or the wire breaks. Ideal work per unit volume w_ideal = σ̄ ln(A₀/A_f) for drawing or extrusion. Efficiency η = w_ideal/w_actual. Choose press tonnage above peak load with a margin. Energy per part = average force × stroke.

Worked example

Question. A 300 mm wide strip is rolled from 10 mm to 7 mm on rolls of radius 250 mm. The mean flow stress is 350 MPa and the mill runs at 50 rev/min. Estimate the roll force and the total power.

  1. Draft Δh = h₀ − h₁ = 10 − 7 = 3 mm = 0.003 m.
  2. Projected contact length L = √(0.250 × 0.003) = 0.027386 m = 27.386 mm.
  3. Contact area per roll = wL = 0.300 × 0.027386 = 0.008216 m².
  4. Roll force F = σ̄ w L = 350×10⁶ × 0.008216 = 2.876×10⁶ N = 2.876 MN.
  5. Torque per roll T = F L/2 = 2.876×10⁶ × 0.027386/2 = 39,375 N·m = 39.375 kN·m.
  6. Angular speed ω = 2π×50/60 = 5.236 rad/s.
  7. Total power P = 2Tω = 2 × 39,375 × 5.236 = 412,334 W = 412.3 kW.

Answer. Roll force ≈ 2.876 MN (about 2.88 MN); total power ≈ 412.3 kW (about 412 kW).

Exam traps

  • Using MPa as a force: 1 MPa = 10⁶ N/m², so multiply by an area in m².
  • Using billet area instead of contact area wL under the rolls.
  • Mixing mm and m inside √(RΔh).
  • Forgetting the factor 2 in power; T is per roll.
  • In forging, using whole surface area instead of plan area.

2.7 Powder metallurgy: powders, compaction and sintering

Powder metallurgy makes a metal part by pressing fine metal powder into a die shape and heating it below the melting point. The particles weld by diffusion, so the part needs little or no machining. The route suits small, complex parts in large numbers: gears, bushes, self-lubricating bearings, tungsten carbide inserts and magnets. It also handles metals that are hard to cast or machine, and mixtures that do not mix even in a melt, such as copper–graphite for brushes, copper–tungsten for contacts, or tungsten carbide with a cobalt binder. The price is porosity, which lowers strength and ductility.

You need: 2.1 for how particles deform plastically and lock together under pressure, and the casting unit for the contrast with solidification.

Powder making and tests

Powder making fixes particle size and shape. Water atomisation: high-pressure water jets break a molten metal stream into droplets that freeze irregular and spongy; cheap, used for iron and copper. Gas atomisation: inert gas makes round droplets; spherical powder flows well but costs more, used for superalloys and aluminium. Oxide reduction: a metal oxide is heated with carbon or hydrogen, leaving sponge metal; iron and tungsten powders come this way. Electrolysis: direct current deposits pure dendritic powder from a salt solution. Pulverising: crushing and ball milling break brittle metal or sponge into powder. Carbonyl process: a metal carbonyl vapour decomposes into very fine, pure, round powder.

Powder tests predict die filling. Particle size comes from sieving or microscopy; shape from a micrograph. Apparent density is loose powder mass divided by its volume. Tap density is measured after tapping the container, so it is higher. Flow rate is the time for 50 g to pass a standard funnel. Free-flowing round powder fills a die evenly.

Blending mixes powders and additives. A lubricant such as zinc stearate, about 0.5–1 % by mass, lowers die-wall friction. Graphite can do the same and add carbon. A poor mix gives density variation and weak spots.

Compaction and the green part

Compaction presses the blended powder into a die with upper and lower punches. Pressure is roughly 100–800 MPa. Particles first slide and rearrange, then deform plastically and cold-weld at contacts. The compact is called green because it is unsintered. Green density ρg = m/V is its mass divided by its volume. Green strength is the bending or crushing strength before sintering; it must survive ejection and handling.

ρ<sub>g</sub> = m / V,   P = 1 − ρ<sub>g</sub>/ρ<sub>s</sub>,   T<sub>s</sub> = (0.7 to 0.9) T<sub>m</sub>

ρg is green density (g/cm³); m is compact mass (g); V is compact volume (cm³); P is porosity as a fraction; ρs is solid metal density (g/cm³); Ts is sintering temperature (K), usually 0.7–0.9 Tm and often about 0.7–0.8 Tm for iron and copper base parts; Tm is melting temperature (K).

Porosity P = 1 − ρg/ρs, where ρs is the solid metal density. For iron ρs = 7.87 g/cm³. A green density of 6.8 g/cm³ leaves P = 0.136, or 13.6 % pores. More pressure raises green density, but friction and trapped air limit it. Stepped parts need separate punches; undercuts cannot be pressed.

Cold isostatic pressing (CIP) fills a rubber bag with powder and presses it in a fluid. Pressure acts from all sides, so density is uniform and large parts are possible. Hot isostatic pressing (HIP) uses hot gas to close pores; it belongs with the high-pressure powder processes in Unit 7. Roll compaction feeds powder between rolls and makes a green strip. Metal injection moulding mixes fine powder with polymer binder, moulds a small complex shape, removes the binder, and sinters it.

Sintering, finishing and properties

Sintering heats the green part below its melting point, usually 0.7–0.9 Tm (often about 0.7–0.8 Tm for iron and copper base parts), where Tm is the absolute melting temperature. Particles stay solid. Atoms diffuse, necks grow between touching particles, and pores shrink. Three stages: initial necking at contacts; intermediate stage with rounded pores and fast density rise; final stage with closed or isolated pores. The part densifies and gains strength.

Atmosphere protects the metal. Hydrogen reduces surface oxides. Nitrogen is a cheaper inert carrier. Endogas, made by burning a hydrocarbon with limited air, supplies CO, H2 and N2. A continuous belt furnace has a preheat zone, a hot sinter zone and a cooling zone. Batch vacuum furnaces suit reactive metals. Spark plasma sintering passes pulsed current through a graphite die and powder under pressure, so heating is fast and grain growth is limited; it is a specialised route.

Finishing corrects size and properties. Sizing presses the part in a die to fix dimensions. Coining presses it for sharper detail and higher density. Repressing and resintering repeat compaction and heating. Infiltration melts copper into an iron skeleton to fill pores. Impregnation fills pores with oil or resin; oil-filled bronze bushes are self-lubricating.

Density controls properties. Pores lower strength, ductility and impact resistance; hardness follows the metal matrix. Advantages: near-net shape, little machining, controlled porosity, mixed compositions. Limits: powder and die cost, shape limits, residual porosity. Uses include gears, bushes, oil-impregnated bearings, tungsten carbide inserts with cobalt binder, and powder magnets.

Worked example

Question. An iron gear blank of volume 12.5 cm³ is die compacted to a green density of 6.8 g/cm³. Solid iron has density 7.87 g/cm³ and melts at 1538 °C. Find the powder mass, the green porosity, and a sintering temperature range using 0.7–0.8 Tm. Give a common furnace value.

  1. Volume V = 12.5 cm³ and green density ρg = 6.8 g/cm³.
  2. Powder mass m = ρgV = 6.8 × 12.5 = 85 g.
  3. Solid density ρs = 7.87 g/cm³. Porosity P = 1 − 6.8/7.87 = 1 − 0.864 = 0.136.
  4. As a percentage, P = 13.6 %.
  5. Melting point 1538 °C = 1538 + 273 = 1811 K.
  6. 0.7 Tm = 0.7 × 1811 = 1267.7 K; 0.8 Tm = 1448.8 K.
  7. In Celsius: 1267.7 − 273 = 994.7 °C and 1448.8 − 273 = 1175.8 °C.
  8. A common furnace setting is 1120 °C in a hydrogen–nitrogen atmosphere.

Exam traps

  • Sintering does not melt the powder. The particles stay solid and bond by diffusion; liquid-phase sintering is a special case, not the standard P/M route.
  • Use green density to find the mass of a green compact, not solid density. Solid density would give the mass of a pore-free part.
  • Apparent density is loose powder density; tap density is after tapping; green density is the compacted part. Do not swap these terms.
  • Higher porosity lowers strength and ductility. It does not raise hardness; hardness mainly follows the metal matrix.
  • Zinc stearate is a die-wall lubricant, not a sintering aid and not the structural binder used in metal injection moulding.

Takeaways

  1. Plastic flow begins at yield; cold working raises flow stress with strain, while hot working keeps it low by recrystallisation.
  2. Rolling reduces thickness by pulling strip through rolls; defects come from uneven flow, friction and roll bending.
  3. Forging shapes metal by repeated squeezing; flash and gutter control die filling, and grain flow follows the contour.
  4. Extrusion pushes a billet through a die; direct extrusion has container friction, indirect extrusion reduces it, and impact extrusion makes cups.
  5. Wire drawing pulls rather than pushes; the drawing stress must stay below the metal's yield strength or the wire breaks.
  6. Every bulk forming load is approximately mean flow stress times contact area, corrected for friction and shape.
  7. Powder metallurgy compacts and sinters powder; powder flow, apparent density, green density and sintering control final quality.
  8. Defect choice and process choice depend on temperature, friction, die geometry and stress state.

Glossary

Alligatoring
Rolling defect where the strip end splits horizontally into two halves.
Atomisation
Making metal powder by breaking a melt into fine droplets.
Billet
Semi-finished cast or rolled bar used as starting stock in forging, extrusion or rolling.
Bloom
Large square or rectangular semi-finished rolled product later rolled into sections.
Die angle
Included angle of a converging die in drawing or extrusion, measured in degrees.
Draft
Thickness reduction in rolling, equal to initial thickness minus final thickness.
Drawing stress
Tensile stress in wire at the die exit, which must stay below yield strength.
Fish tail
Ragged end formed in extrusion or rolling because of uneven metal flow.
Flash
Thin fin of metal forced out between forging dies.
Green compact
Pressed powder part before sintering.
Green strength
Strength of a pressed powder compact before sintering.
Gutter
Recess around a forging die that collects excess flash.
Hydroforming
Shaping a tube or sheet with fluid pressure inside a die.
Hydrostatic extrusion
Extrusion with fluid pressure around the billet, reducing friction and raising ductility.
Isostatic pressing
Compacting powder by fluid pressure applied from all directions.
Mandrel
Internal tool over which a tube is drawn to control the bore.
Mean flow stress
Average flow stress over the strain path used in load formulas.
Neutral point
Location in the roll gap where strip speed equals roll surface speed.
Reduction of area
Fractional decrease in cross-section, equal to (initial area - final area) divided by initial area.
Redundant work
Deformation work that does not contribute to shape change, caused by internal shear.
Roll gap
Separation between the rolls at a pass, set to the required final thickness.
Sintering
Heating a compact below melting point so powder particles bond by diffusion.
Slab
Wide rectangular semi-finished product rolled into plate or sheet.
Thread rolling
Cold forming threads by pressing a blank between grooved dies.
