5.1 Orthogonal and oblique cutting; chip formation

Metal cutting turns a layer of material into a chip by intense shear. The cutting edge pushes into the work, the material ahead compresses, then slides along a narrow shear plane. Orthogonal cutting is the simple two-dimensional case: the edge is perpendicular to the cutting velocity. Oblique cutting is the three-dimensional case: the edge is tilted by an inclination angle, so the chip flows sideways. Shops use these models to choose rake angle, feed and speed, predict chip thickness and explain surface finish on lathes, milling cutters and drills.

You need plastic deformation and shear from Unit 2, Bulk Metal Forming Processes.

Orthogonal cutting: the two-dimensional case

Orthogonal cutting is the simplest model of machining. The cutting edge is a straight line perpendicular to the cutting velocity. The tool removes a rectangular layer of work material. The model has two dimensions: the cutting direction and the chip thickness direction. It ignores edge tilt and sideways chip flow, so it is a teaching and test model, not a full shop model.

  • Cutting speed v (m/min) is the relative speed between tool and workpiece.
  • Feed f (mm/rev) is the tool advance per revolution; in turning it sets the uncut chip thickness in many orthogonal tests.
  • Depth of cut d (mm) is the thickness of the layer removed in one pass. In orthogonal tests d is the uncut chip thickness t₁; the width of cut b (mm), measured along the cutting edge, is the other dimension (b equals the feed in orthogonal tube turning).
  • Uncut chip thickness t₁ (mm) is the layer thickness ahead of the cutting edge.
  • Chip thickness t₂ (mm) is the thickness of the chip after it has sheared and left the edge.
  • Rake angle α (degrees) is the angle between the rake face and a plane perpendicular to the cutting velocity.
  • Shear angle φ (degrees) is the angle between the shear plane and the cutting velocity.

Chip formation and the shear plane

The tool does not split the metal like a knife. It compresses a small zone ahead of the edge. The metal first deforms elastically. It then yields and shears plastically along a narrow band called the shear plane. Most of the work of cutting becomes heat in this band.

  1. Tool engages: the edge contacts the uncut layer of thickness t₁.
  2. Compression: the layer ahead of the edge is squeezed; elastic strain becomes plastic strain.
  3. Shear plane: intense shear starts on a narrow plane at angle φ to the cutting velocity.
  4. Chip slides: the sheared metal moves up the rake face; it is thicker, t₂ > t₁.
  5. Chip leaves: the chip curls away as continuous, discontinuous, built-up edge or segmented form.

r = t₁ / t₂

r is the chip thickness ratio (no unit); t₁ is uncut chip thickness (mm); t₂ is chip thickness (mm). Since t₂ > t₁, r is less than 1.

tan φ = (r cos α) / (1 − r sin α)

φ is the shear angle (degrees); r is the chip thickness ratio; α is the rake angle (degrees). Use degrees consistently in the trigonometric functions.

This relation is the standard orthogonal model. A larger rake angle α raises φ for the same r, so the shear plane becomes steeper. With t₁ fixed, a larger α raises r and the chip leaves thinner. Forces on the shear plane are resolved in Merchant's circle.

Chip types, built-up edge and oblique cutting

Chip type is set by work material, cutting speed, feed, rake angle, friction and coolant. The four standard types are:

  • Continuous chip: ductile work, high speed, large rake and low friction. The chip is a long ribbon, finish is good, but it can tangle; a chip breaker is used.
  • Discontinuous chip: brittle work or low speed with high friction. The chip breaks into short pieces; finish is fair and the tool sees impact loads.
  • Continuous chip with built-up edge (BUE): work material welds to the edge, grows, then breaks away. It gives a rough surface and fragments; it can protect the edge but raises friction.
  • Segmented chip: shear localises in bands, making a saw-tooth chip. It appears in hard alloys such as titanium and nickel alloys at some speeds.

Built-up edge changes the effective rake angle and the depth of cut. It is unstable, so the surface finish varies along the work. A clean continuous chip with a chip breaker usually gives the best finish.

Oblique cutting is the three-dimensional case. The cutting edge is inclined at angle i to the plane normal to the cutting velocity. The chip does not flow perpendicular to the edge; it flows at a chip flow angle. The effective rake measured in the chip flow direction differs from the normal rake angle. Only a few universities teach the full oblique geometry; most exams ask the definition, the inclination angle and the effect on chip flow.

Worked example

Question. Orthogonal turning of a mild steel tube gives uncut chip thickness t₁ = 0.25 mm and chip thickness t₂ = 0.60 mm. The rake angle is α = 10°. Find the chip thickness ratio r and the shear angle φ.

Exam traps

  • Using r = t₂/t₁. The definition is r = t₁/t₂, so r is less than 1 for a normal chip.
  • Putting degrees into sin and cos on a calculator set to radians, or getting tan φ in radians and reporting it as degrees.
  • In oblique cutting, state the rake you mean: the normal rake αn is measured in the normal plane, while the effective rake is measured in the chip flow direction. Do not mix the inclination angle i with the chip flow angle.
  • Saying a continuous chip is always good. A long continuous chip can tangle; a chip breaker is needed, and a BUE chip is continuous but gives poor finish.
  • Confusing feed f (mm/rev), depth of cut d (mm) and uncut chip thickness t₁ (mm). They have different units and different roles.

5.2 Mechanics of cutting and Merchant's circle

Metal cutting creates a chip by shear. Two forces are easy to measure on a dynamometer: cutting force Fc along the cutting speed and thrust force Ft normal to it. Merchant's circle resolves their resultant R into friction F and normal N on the rake face, and into shear Fs and normal Fn on the shear plane. This tells you how much energy goes into friction and how much into shear, so you can choose rake angle, cutting fluid and feed. It is used in turning, drilling and milling force estimates.

You need: chip thickness ratio and shear angle from 5.1.

Force resolution on the Merchant circle

In orthogonal cutting the cutting edge is straight and the chip flows at right angles to it. A dynamometer measures two forces. The cutting force Fc (N) acts along the cutting speed. The thrust force Ft (N) acts normal to it, pushing the tool away from the work. Their resultant R = √(Fc² + Ft²) is the diameter of the Merchant circle.

On the rake face, R resolves into friction F (N) along the face and normal N (N) across it. The rake angle α is the angle between the rake face and the normal to the cut surface. The friction angle β is the angle between R and N, so μ = tan β = F/N. On the shear plane, R resolves into shear force Fs (N) along the plane and normal force Fn (N) across it. The shear plane makes shear angle φ with the cutting speed. The same R closes both resolutions, which is why one circle gives friction and shear together.

F = Fc sin α + Ft cos α

F is friction force on the rake face (N); Fc cutting force (N); Ft thrust force (N); α rake angle.

N = Fc cos α − Ft sin α

N is normal force on the rake face (N); other symbols as above.

Shear plane, stress and power

Shear area As = b t1/sin φ, where b is width of cut (mm) and t1 is uncut chip thickness (mm). Shear stress on the plane is τ = Fs/As (N/mm² = MPa). Cutting power is P = Fc v, where v is cutting speed in m/s and P is in W. Specific cutting energy is u = Fc/(b t1) = P/MRR, where MRR = b t1 v is material removal rate. u is in N/mm², numerically equal to J/cm³.

F<sub>s</sub> = F<sub>c</sub> cos φ − F<sub>t</sub> sin φ,   F<sub>n</sub> = F<sub>c</sub> sin φ + F<sub>t</sub> cos φ

Fs shear force along shear plane (N); Fn normal force across shear plane (N); φ shear angle.

A<sub>s</sub> = b t<sub>1</sub> / sin φ,   τ = F<sub>s</sub>/A<sub>s</sub>,   P = F<sub>c</sub> v

As shear area (mm²); b width of cut (mm); t1 uncut chip thickness (mm); τ shear stress (MPa); P power (W); v cutting speed (m/s).

Merchant's relation is 2φ + β − α = 90°. It comes from assuming the shear angle adjusts so that cutting energy is minimum. Use it as an estimate. It fails when friction is abnormal, when a built-up edge forms, or when ploughing is large.

Using the force model

Procedure: set b, t1 and v, and measure Fc and Ft with a dynamometer. Compute F and N, then μ and β. Use Merchant's relation to estimate φ. Compute Fs, As, τ, P and u. Compare τ with the shear strength of the work material. A high μ means more friction on the rake face; change rake angle or cutting fluid. A high τ means the shear plane is carrying more stress; reduce feed or change speed.

Keep units consistent: Fc and Ft in N, b and t1 in mm, v in m/s for W. Merchant's relation is an approximation, not a law. Tool geometry is covered in 5.3, fluids in 5.4, and wear in 5.5.

Worked example

Question. An orthogonal cutting test gives cutting force Fc = 1200 N, thrust force Ft = 600 N, rake angle α = 10°, uncut chip thickness t1 = 0.25 mm, width of cut b = 2.5 mm and cutting speed v = 120 m/min. Find friction force F, normal force N, friction coefficient μ, friction angle β, shear angle φ by Merchant's relation, shear stress τ on the shear plane, cutting power P and specific cutting energy u.

  1. F = 1200 sin 10° + 600 cos 10° = 208.4 + 590.9 = 799.3 N.
  2. N = 1200 cos 10° − 600 sin 10° = 1181.8 − 104.2 = 1077.6 N.
  3. μ = F/N = 799.3/1077.6 = 0.742; β = tan⁻¹(0.742) = 36.6°.
  4. Merchant: 2φ + 36.6° − 10° = 90°, so 2φ = 63.4° and φ = 31.7°.
  5. Fs = 1200 cos 31.7° − 600 sin 31.7° = 1020.8 − 315.4 = 705.4 N.
  6. As = b t1/sin φ = 2.5 × 0.25/sin 31.7° = 0.625/0.5257 = 1.189 mm².
  7. τ = Fs/As = 705.4/1.189 = 593 N/mm² = 593 MPa.
  8. v = 120/60 = 2 m/s; P = Fc v = 1200 × 2 = 2400 W = 2.4 kW. u = Fc/(b t1) = 1200/(2.5 × 0.25) = 1920 N/mm², i.e. 1920 J/cm³.

Answer. F ≈ 799 N, N ≈ 1078 N, μ ≈ 0.742, β ≈ 36.6°, φ ≈ 31.7°, τ ≈ 593 MPa, P = 2.4 kW and u ≈ 1920 N/mm².

Exam traps

  • Merchant's relation is a minimum-energy approximation; do not use it when friction is abnormal or a built-up edge is present.
  • Fs = Fc cos φ − Ft sin φ, not Fc sin φ + Ft cos φ; the sine and cosine swap between Fs and Fn.
  • μ = F/N, not Fc/Ft; β = tan⁻¹ μ, and α, β, φ must use the same degree or radian convention.
  • For power in watts, convert v from m/min to m/s; keep Fc and Ft in the same force unit before resolving.
  • As = b t1/sin φ; sin φ is in the denominator, and b and t1 must both be in mm.

5.3 Cutting tool geometry and tool signature

A single-point cutting tool has a shank, a face, flanks, a nose and cutting edges. Its cutting behaviour is fixed by a small set of angles and a nose radius. Written in a fixed order, these numbers form the tool signature, for example 0-10-6-6-8-15-0.5. The signature lets a workshop grind, inspect and replace a tool without ambiguity. Lathe, shaping and planing work use such tools, so the signature is read every time a tool is issued.

You need: 5.1 for chip formation and orthogonal and oblique cutting, and 5.2 for force directions.

Tool parts and the angles that describe them

A single-point tool has six parts. The shank is the rectangular body held in the tool post. The face is the top surface on which the chip slides. The flank is the surface below a cutting edge; the end flank faces the end of the work and the side flank faces the machined surface. The nose is the rounded corner between the side and end cutting edges. The side cutting edge is the main edge; the end cutting edge is the short edge at the end.

  • Back rake angle: angle between the face and a plane parallel to the base, measured in the back plane. It controls chip flow along the face.
  • Side rake angle: angle between the face and a plane parallel to the base, measured in the side plane. It controls chip flow to the side.
  • End relief angle: clearance angle between the end flank and a plane perpendicular to the base. It stops the end flank rubbing.
  • Side relief angle: clearance angle between the side flank and a plane perpendicular to the base. It stops the side flank rubbing the machined surface.
  • End cutting edge angle (ECEA): angle between the end cutting edge and a plane perpendicular to the shank side.
  • Side cutting edge angle (SCEA): angle between the side cutting edge and a plane parallel to the shank side. It is also called the approach angle.
  • Nose radius: radius of the rounded nose, in millimetres.
  • Cutting edge inclination: angle between the cutting edge and a plane normal to the cutting velocity. It tilts the edge and sends the chip sideways; it is not one of the seven ASA signature slots. See 5.1.

Rake angles carry a sign. Positive rake thins the wedge and sharpens the edge. Negative rake thickens the wedge and strengthens the edge. Relief angles, ECEA and SCEA are positive.

The seven-element ASA signature

The ASA system writes a single-point tool as seven numbers in fixed order: back rake – side rake – end relief – side relief – ECEA – SCEA – nose radius. Some syllabi call this the tool designation. The first six numbers are angles in degrees. The last is nose radius in millimetres on modern drawings; older shop drawings may use 1/64 inch.

The signature 0-10-6-6-8-15-0.5 means back rake 0°, side rake 10°, end relief 6°, side relief 6°, ECEA 8°, SCEA 15° and nose radius 0.5 mm. A minus sign before a rake value is part of the number. For example, −5-8-6-6-8-15-0.5 has back rake −5°.

The slots are not interchangeable. If a regrind note says 0-10-6-6-8-15-0.5, the grinder sets each value at its own slot. Reading 10 as back rake instead of side rake puts the wrong value in the wrong rake slot, so the wedge and the chip flow direction change. The relief values in slots 3 and 4 are unaffected. The fixed order lets a tool crib compare a worn tool with its drawing and identify a loose tool without measuring every angle.

The signature does not include cutting edge inclination. The ASA signature describes the tool in its normal setting. Inclination belongs to the cutting geometry of the operation and is treated with orthogonal and oblique cutting in 5.1.

How each angle changes the cut

Back rake and side rake decide how the chip leaves the face and how much force the edge carries. A positive rake gives free chip flow and lower cutting force. A negative rake gives a blunt, strong edge for hard or interrupted cuts. Too much positive rake makes the edge weak and hot.

Relief angles stop rubbing. A side relief of 6° keeps the side flank clear of the machined surface. Too little relief causes rubbing, heat and poor finish. Too much relief removes support behind the edge and invites breakage.

ECEA and SCEA set the shape of the cut. A larger SCEA spreads the cut over a longer side edge, so the chip is thinner and notch wear at the nose is reduced. Very large SCEA increases chatter and thrust force. ECEA controls the end edge; a large ECEA reduces end rubbing but weakens the nose.

Nose radius controls finish and strength. A larger radius smooths the machined surface and strengthens the nose. Too large a radius raises thrust force and causes chatter on slender work. A small radius leaves visible feed marks.

The practical rule is a compromise. Soft ductile work takes positive rake and a moderate nose radius. Hard work takes small or negative rake, small relief and a strong nose. The exact values come from the tool material and work material, covered in 5.4.

Exam traps

  • The seven ASA values are always in fixed order. Reading 10 as back rake instead of side rake changes which rake is which, and so changes the wedge.
  • A rake angle can be negative. Dropping the minus sign makes a strong tool look sharp and changes the force.
  • The nose radius is a length in mm, not an angle in degrees. It is the last slot and has no degree symbol.
  • Back rake and side rake are measured in different planes; SCEA and ECEA belong to different cutting edges. Do not swap their names.
  • Relief is not 'more is better'. Extra relief weakens the edge; too little relief rubs the flank.

5.4 Cutting tool materials and cutting fluids

A cutting tool must stay harder than the work metal while the edge is hot. Carbon steel loses hardness first; high-speed steel holds it further; carbide, ceramic, CBN and diamond hold it further still. The tool material sets the safe cutting speed. A cutting fluid carries heat away, lowers friction, flushes chips and guards against corrosion. Together they decide whether a lathe or milling job runs smoothly or fails by rapid wear.

You need: 5.3 tool geometry and tool signature, because rake angle and nose radius change how a tool material performs.

Tool materials: hardness when hot

The cutting edge sees stress, abrasion and high temperature together. The key property is hot hardness: the hardness a material keeps at the temperature of the cutting zone. A material with low hot hardness softens, rubs and wears quickly. Toughness resists chipping under interrupted cuts. Wear resistance resists flank and crater wear. Cost decides what a shop can use every day.

  • Carbon tool steel: 0.6–1.3% carbon, cheap and sharp, but it loses its temper and softens above roughly 200–250 °C, so it is limited to hand tools and low cutting speeds.
  • High-speed steel (HSS): 18-4-1 means 18% tungsten, 4% chromium, 1% vanadium and about 0.7% carbon by mass. It is tough and holds hardness to about 600 °C. Used for drills, taps, hobs and form tools.
  • Cemented carbide: tungsten carbide (WC) grains held in cobalt (Co) binder. Very hard and wear resistant, but brittle. It is used as clamped inserts; grades change with cobalt content.
  • Coated carbide: a thin layer of TiN, TiC or Al2O3 on a carbide insert. The coating lowers friction and slows crater wear, so the insert runs at higher speed. Coated and uncoated grades are not interchangeable.
  • Cermet: ceramic particles in a metal binder. It sits between carbide and ceramic, gives good finish on steel, and is less tough than carbide.
  • Ceramic, CBN and diamond: ceramic (alumina or silicon nitride) is chemically stable and very hard but brittle. Cubic boron nitride (CBN) cuts hardened steel and cast iron. Diamond is hardest and is used on aluminium, copper, glass and composites, never on steel because carbon diffuses into iron.

Cutting fluids: four jobs, four families

A cutting fluid does four jobs: it cools the tool and work, lubricates the sliding chip on the rake face, flushes chips away, and protects the machine from corrosion. Cooling matters most at high speed, where most heat enters the chip and tool. Lubrication matters most at low speed, where friction on the rake face is large.

Fluids fall into four families.

  • Straight oil: neat mineral or fatty oil. Good lubrication, poor cooling, and it can smoke. Used for low-speed severe work and some non-ferrous metals.
  • Soluble oil: oil dispersed in water. Good cooling with some lubrication. The common general-purpose fluid for turning, milling and drilling.
  • Semi-synthetic: a water-mixed fluid with less oil and more chemical additives. It balances cooling, lubrication and cleanliness.
  • Synthetic: no mineral oil; water with chemical additives. Best cooling and corrosion control, often used in grinding and high-speed machining.

Selection follows the job. Water-based fluid where heat is the problem; neat oil where friction is the problem. Magnesium is unsafe with water because it can react and burn; aluminium needs an inhibitor to stop staining. Cast iron can be cut dry because graphite in the chip helps lubrication.

Application methods are flooding, mist and minimum quantity lubrication (MQL). Flooding sends a continuous stream at the cutting zone and is the shop default. Mist carries fine fluid droplets in an air stream; the droplets evaporate at the cutting zone, so it cools reasonably well but lubricates poorly, and the airborne droplets are a health risk that needs extraction and PPE. MQL sends a small, metered oil flow carried in an air jet straight to the cutting zone. It replaces the flood stream, cuts fluid waste and disposal cost, and suits high-speed cuts in a rigid machine where the chips carry most of the heat. See 5.5 for how heat turns into wear.

Matching tool and fluid to speed

The tool material sets a speed limit. Carbon steel runs slowly. HSS runs faster and survives shock. Uncoated carbide runs faster still on steady cuts. Coated carbide adds a thermal barrier and lower friction, so it runs faster again. Ceramic, CBN and diamond run at the top end, but they are brittle and need rigid machines.

The fluid must match that choice. At low speed with HSS, a neat oil or rich soluble oil gives lubrication. At high speed with coated carbide, a water-based flood carries heat away. In a rigid CNC cell, MQL may replace flood for a controlled cut.

Never treat the fluid as a coolant only. It also controls friction, chip clearing and rust. A wrong fluid can stain aluminium, crack a carbide insert by thermal shock, or create a fire risk with magnesium. The correct pair of tool and fluid lowers temperature, slows wear and holds surface finish.

Worked example

Question. An 18-4-1 high-speed steel contains 18% tungsten, 4% chromium, 1% vanadium and 0.7% carbon by mass. A tool blank has mass 250 g. Find the mass of tungsten, chromium, vanadium and carbon in the blank.

  1. Mass of blank = 250 g.
  2. Tungsten = 18% of 250 g = (18 ÷ 100) × 250 = 45 g.
  3. Chromium = 4% of 250 g = (4 ÷ 100) × 250 = 10 g.
  4. Vanadium = 1% of 250 g = (1 ÷ 100) × 250 = 2.5 g.
  5. Carbon = 0.7% of 250 g = (0.7 ÷ 100) × 250 = 1.75 g.

Answer. The 250 g blank contains 45 g tungsten, 10 g chromium, 2.5 g vanadium and 1.75 g carbon.

  • 18-4-1 gives the composition by mass: 18% tungsten, 4% chromium and 1% vanadium. The remaining mass is mostly iron (about 76%) plus about 0.7% carbon. Do not read the three numbers as grams, kilograms or ratio units; they are mass percentages, so a 250 g blank holds 45 g tungsten, not 18 g.

  • Higher cost does not always mean a better tool. A brittle ceramic insert can fail on an interrupted cut where tough HSS survives.

5.5 Tool wear, tool life and machinability

A tool does not cut forever. Heat, friction and rubbing wear the cutting edge, and wear grows faster as speed rises. Tool life is the cutting time before wear reaches a set limit. Taylor's equation links speed and life with a power law. Machinability ranks materials by how easily they cut. These ideas set cutting speed, feed and depth of cut on a lathe or milling machine, so a shop gets the most parts per edge without scrapping the job.

You need: cutting forces from 5.2 and tool materials from 5.4.

Wear on the cutting edge

Two faces of a single-point tool wear. The flank face rubs the new surface; flank wear VB is the average width of the wear land, in mm. The rake face carries the chip; crater wear is a hollow on the rake face, measured as depth KT in mm. Notch wear appears at the depth-of-cut line. Chipping is small fracture of the edge. A built-up edge (BUE) is chip material welded to the edge; when it breaks away it pulls tool particles and leaves a rough finish.

  • Abrasion: hard inclusions in the work scratch the tool; strong at low speed.
  • Adhesion: hot metal welds to the tool and tears off, taking tool material.
  • Diffusion: atoms move from tool to chip at high temperature.
  • Oxidation: the hot edge reacts with air.

Tool life T is the cutting time in minutes before the tool stops meeting the criterion. A common ISO-style limit is average flank wear VB = 0.3 mm, with maximum 0.6 mm. Surface finish, chatter or spindle power may end life earlier. A worn tool raises cutting force and temperature, so the last minutes of life are costly.

Taylor's tool life equation

Taylor's equation links cutting speed and tool life by a power law. It is used to choose a speed for a wanted life, or to predict life after a speed change.

V Tⁿ = C

V is cutting speed in m/min; T is tool life in min; n is the tool-life exponent; C is a constant for the tool-work pair.

Taking logs gives log V + n log T = log C, a straight line on log-log axes. Typical n is about 0.1 for high-speed steel (HSS), 0.2 to 0.3 for carbide, and 0.4 to 0.6 for ceramic. C is the speed that gives T = 1 min, so C carries units that depend on n. With n = 0.25, doubling V divides T by 2⁴ = 16. A carbide tool that lasts 40 min at 120 m/min lasts only about 2.5 min at 240 m/min. High-n ceramics tolerate speed better; that is why they run fast on cast iron and nickel alloys.

Machinability and cutting parameters

Machinability is how easily a material cuts. A material with good machinability gives low cutting force, long tool life, good surface finish and short broken chips. The machinability index compares a material with free-cutting steel, taken as 100%. A higher index means easier cutting. Ductile metals build a BUE; hard inclusions abrade the tool; low thermal conductivity keeps heat at the edge and speeds diffusion wear.

MRR = 1000 v f d

MRR is material removal rate in mm³/min; v is cutting speed in m/min; f is feed in mm/rev; d is depth of cut in mm. If v is in mm/min, MRR = v f d.

Cutting speed has the strongest effect on tool life. Feed and depth of cut raise chip area, force and MRR, but too much feed spoils finish and too much depth can cause chatter. A shop first picks v from Taylor's equation for the life it wants, then picks f and d within finish and power limits.

Worked example

Question. A carbide tool follows Taylor's equation V T^0.25 = 300, with V in m/min and T in min. (a) Find tool life at V = 120 m/min. (b) Find tool life at V = 240 m/min. (c) Find the cutting speed for T = 60 min.

  1. Write the equation: V T^0.25 = 300.
  2. At V = 120 m/min: 120 T^0.25 = 300, so T^0.25 = 300/120 = 2.5. Then T = 2.5⁴ = 39.0625 min.
  3. At V = 240 m/min: 240 T^0.25 = 300, so T^0.25 = 1.25. Then T = 1.25⁴ = 2.4414 min.
  4. For T = 60 min: V = 300 / 60^0.25. Now 60^0.25 = 2.7832, so V = 300 / 2.7832 = 107.79 m/min.

Answer. Tool life is about 39.1 min at 120 m/min, about 2.44 min at 240 m/min, and the speed for 60 min life is about 107.8 m/min.

Exam traps

  • Taylor exponent n is positive. A negative n turns the falling tool-life curve into a rising one.
  • C is not a fixed speed for all tools; its value and unit depend on n. Do not compare C values when n differs.
  • Tool life falls as a power law, not linearly with cutting speed. A small speed rise can cut life many times.
  • Average flank wear 0.3 mm and maximum 0.6 mm are criteria, not universal death of the tool; surface finish may fail earlier.
  • Crater wear is on the rake face; flank wear is on the flank face. Do not swap the two in a diagram.

5.6 Lathe: types and operations

A lathe is the oldest and most common machine tool. It grips the workpiece in a chuck and spins it, while a single-point tool is pushed into the rotating surface. Because the work turns and the tool only translates, every cut sweeps a circle, so the result is a surface of revolution: a cylinder, a shoulder, a taper or a screw thread. Shafts, bushes, pulleys, bolts and axle pins are all born on a lathe. Turned parts are the starting point for most other machining, which is why the lathe is the first machine tool in any workshop.

You need: 5.1 for cutting action, 5.3 for tool geometry, and 5.5 for tool life, which sets the cutting speed you may use.

Parts that hold the work and move the tool

The bed is a rigid cast-iron base with machined guideways on top. Every sliding member rides these ways, so the bed decides the accuracy of the whole machine.

The headstock is fixed at the left end. It carries the spindle, driven by belts or gears from the motor, and the chuck or faceplate that grips the work. The tailstock slides on the ways at the right end; its quill holds a centre to support the free end of a long bar, or a drill for making holes.

The carriage slides along the bed between the two ends. On it sit the cross-slide, which moves the tool at right angles to the axis, the compound rest, which can be swivelled to any angle for short tapers, and the tool post, which clamps the tool.

  • Feed rod: drives the carriage and cross-slide for ordinary turning and facing.
  • Lead screw: a precision screw that moves the carriage in step with the spindle, giving the fixed ratio needed for thread cutting.
  • Two motions: the cutting motion is the rotation of the work and sets the cutting speed; the feed motion is the travel of the tool and sets the chip thickness.

Never use the lead screw for plain turning. The carriage travel for turning is fast and continuous, and it wears a screw that must stay accurate for threads.

Types of lathe

Speed lathe: the simplest form. Headstock, tailstock and a hand-operated tool rest, with no carriage and no power feed. Spindle speeds are high, and it is used for wood turning, polishing and metal spinning.

Bench lathe: a small lathe mounted on a bench for fine work on small parts.

Engine lathe: the general-purpose machine, also called a centre lathe. It has a sliding carriage, a powered feed rod, a lead screw, a compound rest and back gears that give a wide range of spindle speeds. This is the default lathe of a workshop.

Toolroom lathe: an engine lathe built to closer tolerances, with a wider speed range and more accessories. It makes jigs, fixtures and gauges, where the size tolerance is tight.

Capstan and turret lathe: the tailstock is replaced by a hexagonal turret that indexes to bring six preset tools into position in turn. The carriage carries a four-way tool post, and adjustable stops set the travel of each slide. The operator does not measure every part; the machine repeats a fixed sequence, so unskilled labour can hold the size and the cycle time is short. It suits batch production, typically hundreds to a few thousand identical parts per batch, before the setup cost forces a move to an automatic lathe.

Automatic lathe: cams, or a control system, sequence every motion with no operator. Single-spindle and multi-spindle versions make small parts in very large numbers. CNC lathes replace cams and handwheels with servo axes driven by a part program; see Unit 6, CNC and Non-Traditional Machining.

Operations and the numbers behind them

Turning reduces the diameter over a length: the tool feeds along the axis. Facing produces a flat end: the tool feeds radially inward. Chamfering cuts a sloped edge on a corner. Grooving cuts a narrow recess, for example a circlip groove. Parting, or cutting off, feeds a thin tool radially to the centre to separate the finished part.

Knurling presses a patterned roller into the surface for grip; it is a forming operation, not a cutting one. Drilling is done from the tailstock, and boring enlarges and trues an existing hole with a single-point tool. Threading uses the lead screw so that one spindle revolution advances the tool by exactly the pitch. Taper turning is done by swivelling the compound rest, by setting over the tailstock, or with a taper turning attachment.

N = 1000 v / (π D)

N is spindle speed in rev/min; v is cutting speed in m/min; D is the workpiece diameter in mm; 1000 converts metres to millimetres.

fₘ = f N and T = L / (f N)

f is feed in mm/rev; fₘ is feed rate in mm/min; N is spindle speed in rev/min; T is machining time in min; L is tool travel along the feed direction in mm, including approach and overtravel.

MRR = 1000 v f d

MRR is the material removal rate in mm³/min; v is cutting speed in m/min; f is feed in mm/rev; d is depth of cut in mm, measured radially.

Depth of cut is measured from the surface to the tool nose, that is, radially. The tool removes a layer of thickness d from the radius (one side only), so the radius falls by d and the diameter, being twice the radius, falls by 2d in one pass.

Worked example

Question. A mild steel bar of 50 mm diameter is to be turned down to 44 mm over a length of 150 mm. Cutting speed is 30 m/min, feed is 0.2 mm/rev and the depth of cut is 2 mm per pass. Find the spindle speed, the feed rate, the machining time per pass, the material removal rate and the number of passes needed. State the total turning time.

  1. Data: D₁ = 50 mm, D₂ = 44 mm, L = 150 mm, v = 30 m/min, f = 0.2 mm/rev, d = 2 mm per pass.
  2. Spindle speed: N = 1000 v / (π D₁) = (1000 × 30) / (π × 50) = 30 000 / 157.08 = 190.99 rev/min, so about 191 rpm.
  3. Feed rate: fₘ = f N = 0.2 × 190.99 = 38.2 mm/min.
  4. Time per pass: T = L / (f N) = 150 / 38.2 = 3.93 min.
  5. Material removal rate: MRR = 1000 v f d = 1000 × 30 × 0.2 × 2 = 12 000 mm³/min.
  6. Stock to remove on radius: (D₁ − D₂)/2 = (50 − 44)/2 = 3 mm. Passes = 3 / 2 = 1.5, so 2 passes; the first takes 2 mm of radius (diameter 50 → 46 mm) and the second takes the remaining 1 mm (diameter 46 → 44 mm).
  7. Total time = 2 × 3.93 = 7.85 min.

Answer. N ≈ 191 rpm, feed rate ≈ 38.2 mm/min, time ≈ 3.93 min per pass, MRR = 12 000 mm³/min, 2 passes, total turning time ≈ 7.85 min.

Exam traps

  • Depth of cut is radial, so the diameter drops by 2d per pass. Students who count the diameter reduction per pass as d instead of 2d get twice the number of passes (3 instead of 1.5). The correct pass count is (D₁ − D₂)/(2d).
  • Feed is in mm/rev and feed rate is in mm/min. Dividing the length by f instead of by f N gives an answer larger by a factor of N (750 min instead of 3.93 min), because feed per revolution is not a speed.
  • The lead screw is for threading only; ordinary turning and facing use the feed rod. An examiner may ask why the two are separate.
  • For facing, the tool travel L is the radial distance from the outer edge to the centre, not the length of the job.
  • A turret lathe has no tailstock. Its hex turret does the work of the tailstock and also carries several cutting tools.

5.7 Drilling and milling; up and down milling

Drilling and milling both spin a multi-point cutter, but the work moves differently. A drill feeds along its own axis to make a hole. A milling cutter feeds sideways across the work on a table, so it makes flat faces, slots and profiles. The direction of table feed against cutter rotation separates up milling from down milling. These processes appear in every workshop: bolt holes in a pump body, keyways in a shaft, flat faces on a gearbox cover. The same speed, feed and time formulas serve manual and CNC machines.

You need: 5.1 for chip formation and 5.6 for rotation and feed.

Drilling: one rotating tool, many hole operations

Drilling makes a hole with a rotating tool fed along its axis. The common twist drill has a shank, a body with two helical flutes, and a point. The flutes curl chips out and carry cutting fluid in. The point angle is 118° for general steel; the chisel edge at the centre does little cutting and needs a small approach allowance. Helix angle sets the rake face and chip flow. A drill usually makes a slightly rough, slightly oversize hole.

Other hole operations use the same spindle. Reaming removes a small stock with a multi-edge reamer to improve size and finish. Boring enlarges a hole with a single-point tool and can correct its position. Counterboring makes a flat-bottom cylindrical enlargement for a socket-head screw. Countersinking makes a conical enlargement for a flat-head screw. Tapping cuts an internal thread with a tap. Drilling starts the hole; reaming and boring follow it when accuracy matters.

N = 1000v/(πD); feed rate = fN; time = (L + A)/(fN); MRR = (π/4)D² fN

N is spindle speed in rev/min; v is cutting speed in m/min; D is drill diameter in mm; f is feed in mm/rev; L is plate thickness in mm; A is approach in mm; feed rate is in mm/min; time is in min; MRR is material removal rate in mm³/min.

Milling and the two feed directions

Milling uses a rotating multi-tooth cutter while the work is clamped to a table that feeds. In horizontal milling the spindle is horizontal and a slab cutter on an arbor makes a flat surface. In vertical milling the spindle is vertical; a face mill makes large flat faces and an end mill makes slots, pockets and profiles. A slot cutter cuts narrow slots and a form cutter carries the contour it must leave. The table feed per tooth is fz; for z teeth at N rev/min the table feed is fzzN mm/min.

Up milling (conventional milling) has the table feed opposite to cutter rotation at the cut. The tooth starts at zero chip thickness, rubs, then the chip grows thin to thick. The cutter lifts the work, so a lightly held job can chatter. Down milling (climb milling) has the feed with the cutter. The tooth starts at high chip thickness and the chip falls thick to thin. Cutting force presses the work onto the table, giving a better finish and longer tool life. The cutter also pulls the table, so backlash in the feed screw must be removed.

Indexing and choosing the operation

A dividing head indexes the work between cuts for flats, splines and gear teeth. For a simple dividing head with a 40:1 worm, one turn of the crank turns the work 1/40 rev. To divide a circle into N equal parts, the crank turns are 40/N. Example: N = 8 gives 40/8 = 5 crank turns per part. Use the index plate for fractional turns.

Choose drilling for a round hole along an axis. Choose reaming or boring only when the hole needs tight size or position. Choose milling for flat faces, slots and shaped contours. Face milling is common for a top surface; slab milling for a long flat; end milling for a pocket. A drill press feeds the drill by hand or power along a fixed axis; a milling machine feeds the table under a cutter. The feed screw and the cutter must be stiff because a long overhang bends. CNC milling is covered in Unit 6; the same cutter and feed logic applies there.

Worked example

Question. A 20 mm diameter twist drill is to make a through hole in a 40 mm thick plate. Cutting speed is 25 m/min and feed is 0.15 mm/rev. The approach allowance is 5 mm. Find the spindle speed, feed rate, drilling time and material removal rate.

  1. Spindle speed: N = 1000v/(πD) = 1000 × 25 / (π × 20) = 25 000 / 62.832 = 397.9 rev/min.
  2. Feed rate = fN = 0.15 × 397.9 = 59.7 mm/min.
  3. Total travel = L + A = 40 + 5 = 45 mm. Drilling time = 45 / 59.7 = 0.754 min (about 45.2 s).
  4. MRR = (π/4)D² fN = 0.7854 × 400 × 59.7 = 18 750 mm³/min.

Answer. Spindle speed 397.9 rev/min (about 398 rev/min), feed rate 59.7 mm/min, drilling time 0.754 min, and MRR 18 750 mm³/min.

  • Spindle speed uses drill diameter D, not plate thickness L. Thickness enters only the time.
  • Counterboring is cylindrical for a socket-head screw; countersinking is conical for a flat-head screw.
  • In up milling the chip starts thin and ends thick; in down milling it starts thick and ends thin.
  • Simple indexing crank turns = 40/N, not N/40.

5.8 Gear cutting: forming and generation

Gear teeth are made by copying the tooth space or by generating the involute. Copying uses a form cutter shaped to the gap; generating uses a cutter and workpiece driven in a fixed ratio so the involute appears as a trace. The first is simple but needs many cutters; the second is fast and accurate and is standard for spur and helical gears. Gearboxes in scooters, pumps, lathe headstocks and industrial drives use these methods. This chapter covers gear terms, forming, hobbing, shaping, rack planning and a dividing-head check.

You need: 5.7 for milling cutters and simple indexing; the involute idea from geometry.

Gear terms and forming

Spur gear size is fixed by module m (mm) and tooth count z. Module is the pitch diameter per tooth: m = d/z, so pitch diameter d = mz. Circular pitch p = πm is the arc from one tooth to the next on the pitch circle. Addendum is m above the pitch circle; dedendum is 1.25m below it. Outside diameter is d + 2m. These values set cutter depth and blank diameter.

Forming copies the gap between two teeth. A form milling cutter has the inverse tooth profile; it is fed along the blank and the blank is indexed one pitch between cuts. A broach does the same with a long stepped tool that cuts all teeth in one stroke, mainly for internal gears or large batches. The cutter number depends on tooth count. The involute changes with z, so one cutter cannot cut every gear correctly; a set covers ranges, and the nearest number is chosen. Some syllabi call this copy milling.

Forming suits repair shops, small batches and gears where a suitable cutter exists. It leaves indexing marks and cutter profile error. A low-speed gear may pass, but a continuously running gearbox needs generated teeth.

Generation by synchronized motion

Generation does not copy the gap. A cutter and workpiece rotate in a fixed ratio, and the cutter edges sweep the involute. Hobbing uses a hob: a worm with gashes that form cutting edges. The hob axis is tilted by its lead angle; hob and blank rotate as a worm and gear pair. Axial feed moves the hob along the blank to cut the full face width; radial feed plunges to full depth. In conventional hobbing the cutter rotation opposes the feed; in climb hobbing it runs with the feed, giving thinner chips and better finish on rigid machines. Both are hobbing types; axial and radial are feed directions, not the same classification.

Gear shaping uses a pinion-shaped cutter. It reciprocates along the tooth and rotates with the work in the correct ratio; the involute is generated by the rolling motion. It reaches close shoulders and internal gears. Rack planning uses a rack-shaped cutter that reciprocates while the work rotates; the straight rack flank generates the involute. Only a few universities teach rack planning in detail. Generation is faster and more accurate than forming for production, but the machine setting must be exact.

Choosing between forming and generation

Use forming for one-off repair, large module, or when no hob is available. Use generation for batch production of spur and helical gears. Forming leaves indexing marks and cutter profile error; generation gives smooth spacing because the same rolling motion repeats each tooth. A form-milled gear can pass a low-speed duty, but a gearbox that runs continuously needs generated teeth. Powder metallurgy can mould small gears without cutting; it is an alternative covered in Unit 2. Hardened gears are finished by grinding or honing, covered in 5.9.

The choice also depends on gear type. External spur and helical gears are usually hobbed; internal gears and close shoulders favour shaping. Rack planning is mainly a teaching method for the involute, not a common production route. In the shop, the first check is module and tooth count, then the machine setup, cutter number for forming, and the index plate for milling. A wrong module is a scrap gear, not a small error.

Worked example

Question. A 30-tooth spur gear of module 3 mm is to be cut on a milling machine with a simple dividing head of ratio 40:1. Find pitch diameter, circular pitch, addendum, dedendum, outside diameter, and the indexing crank turns. Choose a 24-hole circle.

  1. Given tooth count z = 30, module m = 3 mm, dividing-head ratio R = 40 crank turns per work turn, hole circle H = 24 holes.
  2. Pitch diameter d = mz = 3 × 30 = 90 mm.
  3. Circular pitch p = πm = π × 3 = 9.425 mm.
  4. Addendum = m = 3 mm.
  5. Dedendum = 1.25m = 1.25 × 3 = 3.75 mm.
  6. Outside diameter = d + 2m = 90 + 6 = 96 mm.
  7. Crank turns per tooth = R/z = 40/30 = 1.333 turn. The fraction is 1/3 turn. On a 24-hole circle, holes = (1/3) × 24 = 8 holes. So use 1 full turn plus 8 holes.

Answer. The gear has pitch diameter 90 mm, circular pitch 9.425 mm, addendum 3 mm, dedendum 3.75 mm, outside diameter 96 mm, and indexing crank turns 1.333 turn, that is 1 turn plus 8 holes on a 24-hole circle.

Exam traps

  • Module is the pitch diameter per tooth in mm; diametral pitch is the inch-based reciprocal. Do not mix them in one calculation.
  • The form cutter number depends on tooth count. One arbitrary cutter gives the wrong involute for another z.
  • Hobbing is generation, not forming. Conventional and climb are hobbing types; they are not forming methods.
  • For indexing, multiply the fractional crank turn by the chosen hole-circle count, not by 40.
  • Axial feed and radial feed are hobbing feed directions; they are not the same as conventional and climb hobbing.

5.9 Grinding and finishing: lapping, honing, superfinishing

Abrasive processes cut with tiny hard grains instead of a single cutting edge. Each grain removes a very small chip, so the work becomes more accurate and smoother than after turning or milling. Grinding, honing, lapping and superfinishing all work this way. They appear on bearing races, engine cylinder bores, gauge blocks and hydraulic valve parts, where a few micrometres decide fit and life.

You need: the machine motions in 5.6 Lathe and 5.7 Milling, and cutting fluids from 5.4.

Grinding: the abrasive wheel

Grinding uses a wheel made of many hard abrasive grains held in a bond. The grains act as tiny cutting tools. As the wheel turns, each grain cuts a small chip and then dulls. The wheel is described by five items: abrasive type, grit, grade, structure and bond. Grit number gives grain size; a higher number means a finer grain. Grade is the strength of the bond holding the grain; letters run from soft A to hard Z, so K is medium. Structure is the spacing of grains; 5 is medium dense. Bond V is vitrified, a glass-like bond. The suffix is the maker's record.

v = π D N / 60

v is wheel surface speed in m/s; D is wheel diameter in m; N is wheel speed in rev/min (rpm); π ≈ 3.1416.

A typical wheel marked A 46 K 5 V 8 has aluminium oxide abrasive (A), grit 46, grade K, structure 5, vitrified bond V, and record 8. Aluminium oxide suits steel; silicon carbide suits cast iron and non-ferrous work. Cubic boron nitride and diamond wheels cost more and hold their edges longer.

Surface, cylindrical and centerless grinding

Surface grinding holds a flat work on a table under the wheel. The table reciprocates and the wheel feeds down. Cylindrical grinding rotates the work between centres while the wheel feeds radially or axially. Centerless grinding supports the work on a rest blade between a grinding wheel and a slower regulating wheel; the work is not held between centres.

Wheel speed is high, often 25–35 m/s. Feed and depth of cut are small. Coolant from 5.4 carries heat away and washes chips. A wheel glazes when dull grains remain; it burns the work and raises force.

Dressing removes dull grains and loaded metal, so it sharpens the wheel. Truing restores the wheel's shape and concentricity. A diamond tool does both. Dressing is not truing: one changes the cutting edge, the other changes geometry.

Honing and lapping

Honing corrects the size and shape of an internal cylinder. Bonded abrasive stones rotate with the tool and also reciprocate along the bore. The stones expand outward under light pressure. The two motions cross the scratches, producing a crosshatch pattern that holds oil. Honing removes little material and creates much less heat than grinding. Engine cylinders and hydraulic bores are honed.

Lapping uses loose abrasive slurry between a lap and the work. The lap is a soft plate, often cast iron, with grooves to carry the slurry. The work and lap move relative to each other under light pressure. The grains roll and scratch, removing very small amounts. Lapping makes flat faces and close fits: gauge blocks, valve seats and optical flats.

Honing is not lapping. Honing uses bonded stones in a bore; lapping uses loose grains on a lap for flat or matching faces. Only a few syllabi ask for the exact stone pressure; keep the idea of light pressure.

Superfinishing, polishing and comparison

Superfinishing, also called microfinishing, uses a fine stone pressed lightly on a rotating work. The stone oscillates rapidly along the surface. The pressure is so low that it removes only the fragmented surface layer and no measurable geometry. It is used on bearing races and camshafts. Polishing and buffing use soft wheels or cloth with fine abrasive for appearance; they do not guarantee size.

Question. A surface grinding wheel of diameter 250 mm runs at a surface speed of 30 m/s. Find its rotational speed in rpm. The wheel is marked A 46 K 5 V 8; state the meaning of each part.

  1. Diameter D = 250 mm = 0.250 m.

  3. Rearrange: N = 60 v / (π D).
  4. Substitute: N = 60 × 30 / (π × 0.250) = 1800 / 0.7854 = 2291.8 rpm.
  5. Rounded: N ≈ 2292 rpm.
  6. Marking: A = aluminium oxide; 46 = grit number; K = grade; 5 = structure; V = vitrified bond; 8 = manufacturer's record.

Answer. The wheel turns at about 2292 rpm; the marking reads aluminium oxide, grit 46, grade K, structure 5, vitrified bond, record 8.

  • Wheel speed is in m/s, not rpm. In v = πDN/60, put D in metres or the answer is wrong by 1000.
  • Dressing sharpens; truing restores shape. Do not write that truing sharpens a wheel.
  • Honing is not lapping. Honing uses bonded stones inside a bore; lapping uses loose slurry on a lap.
  • Higher grit number means finer grain. A harder grade letter, towards Z, is not a finer grit.
  • Centerless grinding does not use centres; the regulating wheel controls rotation and feed.

Takeaways

  1. Metal cutting is shear concentrated in a thin zone: the uncut layer becomes a chip only because it shears along the shear plane.
  2. Orthogonal cutting is the clean two-dimensional model used for equations; real turning, milling and drilling are oblique.
  3. The Merchant circle turns two measurable forces into the friction on the rake face and the shear force on the shear plane.
  4. A single-point tool needs a fixed order of seven numbers so that any grinder can reproduce it from a drawing.
  5. Tool material is chosen for hot hardness; the cutting temperature, not room-temperature hardness, sets the limit.
  6. Tool life drops steeply as cutting speed rises, and Taylor's equation captures that fall in one line.
  7. Lathe, drilling and milling are separated by their relative tool–work motion, not by the shape of the machine.
  8. Gear teeth are made either by copying the space with a form cutter or by generating the involute through synchronized motion.
  9. Abrasive finishing removes very little material with many hard grains, which is why it improves accuracy and finish but not size.

Glossary

Built-up edge
Welded lump of work material on the tool nose that forms at low speed and breaks off periodically.
Chip
The layer of work material that has been sheared off and slides over the rake face.
Depth of cut
Thickness of the layer removed in one pass, measured perpendicular to the machined surface.
Feed
Distance moved by the tool per revolution of the work or per tooth of the cutter.
Flank
Surface of the tool facing the newly machined work surface, below the cutting edge.
Friction angle
Angle β whose tangent equals the coefficient of friction at the chip–tool interface.
Hobbing
Gear generating process in which a worm-shaped hob and the blank rotate in a fixed ratio.
Honing
Low-speed abrasive finishing of a bore with rotating and reciprocating bonded sticks.
Indexing
Equal rotation of the work between cuts, done on a dividing head, to space teeth or flutes.
Lapping
Finishing with loose abrasive in a fluid between the work and a soft lap, giving very fine finish and flatness.
Machinability
How easily a material can be cut, judged by tool life, forces, power, surface finish and chip form.
Machining
Removing material as chips with a harder cutting tool to bring a part to shape, size and finish.
Material removal rate
Volume of material cut per unit time, usually in mm³/min, equal to speed × feed × depth of cut in turning.
Merchant circle
A force-circle construction that resolves measured cutting and thrust forces into shear and friction components.
Oblique cutting
Cutting where the cutting edge is inclined to the cutting velocity, so the chip flows at an angle.
Orthogonal cutting
Two-dimensional cutting where the cutting edge is perpendicular to the cutting velocity.
Rake angle
Angle between the tool face and the reference plane; positive rake cuts freely, negative rake strengthens the edge.
Rake face
Top surface of the tool over which the chip slides.
Relief (clearance) angle
Angle between the flank of the tool and the machined surface, which stops the flank rubbing.
Shear angle
Angle φ between the shear plane and the cutting velocity direction.
Shear plane
Narrow zone, treated as a plane at angle φ, where the uncut layer shears into a chip.
Superfinishing
Finishing with a fine stone oscillated at small amplitude and light pressure to remove fragmented surface layers.
Tool life
Time, in minutes, for which a tool cuts before wear reaches the chosen criterion, usually VB = 0.3 mm.
Up milling
Conventional milling where cutter rotation opposes the table feed, so the chip starts at zero thickness.
