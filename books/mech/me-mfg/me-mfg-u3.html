3.1 Sheet metal formability

Sheet metal formability is the amount of strain a sheet can take before it necks or tears. A press forms a blank between a punch and a die; the useful shape is limited by how the metal hardens and how it resists thinning. Engineers judge it with the power law, the Lankford coefficient and the forming limit diagram. It decides whether a car panel, a steel cupboard door or a phone chassis can be pressed without splitting.

You need: true stress, true strain and plastic deformation from Unit 2: plastic deformation and yield criteria.

Strain hardening and safe stretch

Every press operation starts with a blank: a flat sheet cut to the size that will be formed. The punch is the male tool that pushes the sheet; the die is the female tool with the opening or cavity. Clearance is the gap between punch and die at the cutting edge. In forming, the punch and die profile, the blank-holder force and friction set the strain path. Clearance matters mainly in cutting. A sheet that stretches too far thins, then necks first over a diffuse region and then in a narrow local band, and finally tears. That local neck is the failure to design against. Cutting clearance is covered in 3.2.

σ = K εn

σ is true stress in MPa; K is the strength coefficient in MPa; ε is true strain (dimensionless); n is the strain-hardening exponent (dimensionless).

On a log-log plot the power law is a straight line of slope n. A higher n means the metal hardens more as it strains, so load spreads from a thin spot to its neighbours and necking is delayed. In a simple tensile test, diffuse necking starts near ε = n, so n is a rough stretchability index. It is not the whole story: a sheet can fail in a press at strains above or below n, because the strain path changes the limit. That is why the forming limit diagram is needed.

Normal anisotropy resists thinning

Normal anisotropy r compares the size of true width strain to the size of true thickness strain in a tensile test. In a tensile test the width and thickness both shrink, so their true strains are negative. r uses the magnitudes, so r is positive. A high r means the sheet strains more in width than in thickness, so it resists thinning. Deep-drawing steels often have r between 1.5 and 2.0; many aluminium sheets sit nearer 0.6 to 0.8. Thickness strain is hard to measure directly, so it is often found from volume constancy. Example: r = 1.8 means the width strain is 1.8 times the thickness strain in size.

r = |ε<sub>width</sub>| / |ε<sub>thickness</sub>| = ln(w<sub>0</sub>/w) / ln(t<sub>0</sub>/t)

εwidth is true strain across the strip width; εthickness is true strain through the sheet thickness; w0 and w are initial and current width; t0 and t are initial and current thickness; ln is the natural logarithm. r is positive and dimensionless. r is also called the Lankford coefficient.

r is not n. n measures how stress grows with strain; r measures the direction of strain. A high-n sheet resists necking. A high-r sheet resists thinning, which helps a cup draw without splitting. Some syllabi average r over 0°, 45° and 90° to the rolling direction and call it normal anisotropy; the variation with direction is planar anisotropy.

Reading the forming limit diagram

The forming limit diagram (FLD) plots major true strain ε1 on the vertical axis against minor true strain ε2 on the horizontal axis. ε1 is the larger principal strain at a point; ε2 is the smaller principal strain, with sign. The forming limit curve (FLC) separates safe strains below it from local necking above it. On the left, ε2 is negative: tension-compression, as in drawing a cup. On the right, ε2 is positive: tension-tension, as in stretching over a dome. The lowest point is near plane strain, ε2 = 0, where sheets are least tolerant. Deep drawing in 3.4 sits on the left; stretch forming in 3.5 sits on the right.

  1. Find the critical points of the part, such as corners, domes and draw walls.
  2. Measure or simulate the true major strain ε1 and minor strain ε2 at each point.
  3. Plot each point as (ε2, ε1) on the FLD.
  4. Keep the point below the FLC with a safety margin; above the curve means local necking is predicted.

The tensile test gives K, n and r from a strip, but not the whole FLD. The Erichsen cupping test presses a steel ball into a clamped sheet until a crack appears and reports cup depth in mm; a deeper cup means better stretch formability. A full FLD is built by testing specimens of different widths, each giving a different strain path.

Worked example

Question. A steel sheet follows σ = 500ε0.20, with σ in MPa. Find the true stress at ε = 0.20 and state what happens at that strain if necking begins when ε = n.

  1. List the data: K = 500 MPa, n = 0.20, ε = 0.20.
  2. Apply the power law: σ = K εn = 500 × 0.200.20.
  3. Compute the strain term: 0.200.20 = 0.7248 (dimensionless).
  4. Multiply: σ = 500 × 0.7248 = 362.4 MPa.
  5. Necking begins when ε = n. Here n = 0.20, so ε = 0.20 is the necking strain.

Answer. The true stress is σ ≈ 362.4 MPa, and at ε = 0.20 the tensile test reaches necking because ε = n = 0.20.

Exam traps

  • Using engineering strain or tensile elongation as the only formability measure. The FLD needs true major and minor strains.
  • Swapping the FLD axes: ε1 (major) is vertical, ε2 (minor) is horizontal.
  • Treating n and r as the same. n is the strain-hardening exponent; r is the width-to-thickness strain ratio.
  • Forgetting that the safe side of the FLD is below the curve, and that plane strain near ε2 = 0 is the least tolerant path.
  • Reading a high n as a guarantee against tearing. The strain path and the local geometry also decide failure.

3.2 Shearing operations: punching and blanking

Shearing cuts sheet metal along a closed line by driving a punch past a die. The same tool can make a hole, called punching, or cut a part, called blanking; the difference is which piece you keep. Clearance, the small gap per side between punch and die, decides whether the cut edge is clean or burred. Press shops use these operations for washers, brackets, enclosures and electrical laminations. The force calculation uses shear strength and cut perimeter, not area.

You need: 3.1 for sheet strain limits and Unit 2 for shear strength and plastic deformation.

The shearing action

Shearing is a sheet-metal cutting process. A punch, the male tool, moves down past a die, the female opening. The sheet between them is loaded in shear. When the shear stress reaches the ultimate shear strength τ of the sheet, the metal fractures along the tool edges. The cut-out piece is a blank. Clearance c is the gap between punch and die per side. It is kept small, often 5% to 10% of sheet thickness t per side. Soft aluminium may use about 5%; hard steel may need about 10%. Zero clearance is not used: it raises force and causes secondary shear. Shearing exists because it makes holes and flat parts fast, in one press stroke, with little material waste. A washer, a bracket, a fan cover and a motor lamination all begin as sheared blanks.

  1. Place the strip on the die, with the punch above it.
  2. Lower the punch; the sheet is compressed and then sheared.
  3. At shear stress τ, a crack starts at the punch and die edges and meets.
  4. The slug or blank separates and falls; the punch returns.

Punching, blanking and related cuts

Punching and blanking use the same tool and the same cut. The difference is what you keep. In punching, also called piercing, the punch removes a slug. The sheet with the hole is the product; the slug is scrap. In blanking, the cut-out piece is the product; the surrounding strip is scrap. A 50 mm disc cut from a strip is a blank if you keep the disc. The same 50 mm punch makes a punching if you keep the strip and throw away the disc. Both are high-volume, low-cost operations.

  • Notching removes a small piece from the edge of a sheet.
  • Trimming cuts away excess edge left by an earlier operation.
  • Slitting cuts a sheet along a line, often with circular blades, to make narrower coils.
  • Shaving removes a thin layer from a cut edge to improve finish and size.

After shearing, the part may go to bending or deep drawing.

Clearance, force and the cut edge

The punch force depends on the cut perimeter, not the hole area.

F = τ × L × t

F is peak punching force in N; τ is ultimate shear strength of the sheet in N/mm²; L is the total cut perimeter in mm; t is sheet thickness in mm.

A shear angle on the punch face starts the cut at one side. The peak force falls, but the punch travels farther, so the work done is nearly the same. This protects the press.

  • Rollover: a rounded top edge where the punch presses in.
  • Burnish: a smooth vertical band cut by plastic shear.
  • Fracture: a rough, angled zone where the metal tears.
  • Burr: a sharp lip at the bottom.

Correct clearance gives a clean burnish band, roughly one-third of thickness. Too little clearance causes secondary shear and a tall burr. Too much clearance causes heavy rollover, a rough fracture and a large burr.

Worked example

Question. A 2.0 mm thick steel sheet is punched with a 50 mm diameter hole. The ultimate shear strength of the steel is 300 N/mm². Find the punching force in N and kN.

  1. Cut perimeter L = π × d = π × 50 mm = 157.0796 mm, which rounds to 157.1 mm.
  2. Use the shearing force formula F = τ × L × t.
  3. Substitute: F = 300 N/mm² × 157.0796 mm × 2.0 mm.
  4. F = 94,247.8 N, which rounds to 94,248 N.
  5. Convert: 94,248 N ÷ 1000 = 94.2 kN.

Answer. The punching force is about 94.2 kN, or 94,248 N.

Exam traps

  • Using tensile strength in place of ultimate shear strength: the formula needs τ, the shear value.
  • Using hole area πd²/4 instead of cut perimeter times thickness, L × t.
  • Swapping punching and blanking: in punching the slug is scrap, in blanking the blank is product.
  • Treating clearance as a total gap: tables give clearance per side, as a percentage of thickness.
  • Forgetting that a shear angle lowers peak force, not the total shearing work; also keep N and kN consistent.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Fine blanking uses a counter-punch and a ring to clamp the sheet, giving a nearly vertical cut edge for gears and brake parts. Magnetic pulse shearing uses a pulsed electromagnetic coil to drive a flyer into the sheet at high speed, reducing burr in thin aluminium and copper. Servo presses can program punch speed through the stroke to cut noise and tool wear. Search terms: fine blanking, magnetic pulse shearing, servo press blanking, punch wear monitoring.

3.3 Bending of sheet metal

Bending forms a sheet along a straight line without deliberately changing its thickness. The flat blank is not simply the sum of the outside dimensions. The bend consumes an arc of neutral-axis length called the bend allowance; for a 90° bend the flat length is the sum of outside dimensions minus the bend deduction. Springback, the elastic recovery after the punch lifts, makes the final angle open up, so the tool must overbend or bottom the sheet. Bending makes brackets, enclosures, car panels and appliance bodies. A shop cutting a blank for a UPI payment terminal enclosure uses this calculation before the first cut.

You need 3.1 for ductility and 3.2 for blank edges.

Bend line, radius and neutral axis

Bending is a forming operation. A straight bend line divides the sheet. The punch pushes the sheet into a die. Material inside the bend compresses; material outside stretches. The neutral axis is the surface that neither stretches nor compresses. Its position is K × t from the inside surface. Here t is sheet thickness in mm, and K is the K-factor, a dimensionless number usually between 0.33 and 0.50. A common estimate for mild steel is K = 0.40. The inside bend radius R is measured in mm on the inner surface. The bend angle θ is the angle through which the sheet turns, in degrees. A 90° V-bend has θ = 90°.

The bend allowance BA is the arc length of the neutral axis. It is the length of sheet consumed by one bend.

BA = (π/180) θ (R + Kt)

BA is the bend allowance in mm; θ is the bend angle in degrees; R is the inside bend radius in mm; K is the K-factor; t is the sheet thickness in mm. The factor π/180 converts degrees to radians. The quantity R + Kt is the radius of the neutral axis.

For θ = 90°, R = 4.0 mm, t = 2.0 mm and K = 0.40, BA = 7.54 mm. This number goes into the flat blank.

Bend allowance and bend deduction

The flat blank length is the sum of the straight lengths plus one bend allowance for each bend. Do not use the outside radius R + t in place of R + Kt. The neutral axis lies inside the outer surface, so the outside radius overestimates the arc length.

For a 90° bend, the outside setback is OSSB = R + t. The bend deduction is BD = 2(R + t) − BA. Here BD is in mm, R is inside radius, t is thickness, and BA is bend allowance. The flat length can also be found as the sum of outside dimensions minus BD. For the same 90° bend, R = 4.0 mm, t = 2.0 mm and BA = 7.54 mm, so BD = 2(4.0 + 2.0) − 7.54 = 4.46 mm.

If the bend line is too near the sheet edge, the outer fibre has little material to hold it. The edge cracks or tears. Keep the bend line a few thicknesses away from the edge. The exact clearance depends on ductility and is checked by trial.

Springback and minimum bend radius

Springback is elastic recovery after the punch is removed. The bent part does not keep the full punch angle; it opens slightly. Springback increases with high yield strength, small thickness and large bend radius. A high-strength steel bracket springs back more than a soft mild steel bracket of the same shape.

Compensation uses overbending or bottoming. With θ as the turn angle, overbending sets the punch to a larger turn angle than the final turn angle. If a 90° turn springs back by 2°, set the punch to 92° turn (92° − 2° = 90°). If a shop quotes the included angle between flanges instead, that is the angle 180° − θ; for the same case set 88° included to finish at 90° included. Bottoming presses the sheet against the die bottom at the end of the stroke; the through-thickness plastic strain reduces springback. Stretch bending also reduces it by keeping the sheet in tension.

The minimum bend radius is the smallest inside radius that avoids cracking on the outer surface. A smaller R means a sharper bend and a larger outer-fibre strain. Ductile sheet can bend to about its own thickness; less ductile sheet needs a larger radius. Edge cracking is different from centre cracking: it starts at the free edge when the bend line is too close.

Bending methods

V-bending uses a V-shaped die. The sheet rests on two shoulders, and the punch descends into the V. The punch depth sets the bend angle. V-bending is common on a press brake and can make different angles with the same die.

Edge bending clamps the sheet and bends a flange over the die edge. It makes long straight flanges, such as the side of an enclosure. The clamp holds the sheet flat while the punch bends only the projecting edge.

Roll bending passes the sheet through three rolls. The middle roll is offset, so the sheet takes a large-radius curve. It makes cylindrical shells, tanks and pipe sections. Roll bending gives a continuous curvature, not a single sharp bend.

Figure 1 shows the V-bending mechanism. The punch presses the 2.0 mm sheet into the die; the punch is set past 90° turn so the bent part finishes near 90° turn after springback.

Worked example

Question. A 2.0 mm thick mild steel sheet is bent to 90° with an inside bend radius of 4.0 mm. Take K = 0.40. (a) Find the neutral-axis offset Kt in mm. (b) Find the bend allowance BA in mm. (c) For this 90° bend, find the bend deduction BD = 2(R + t) − BA in mm.

  1. Write the data: t = 2.0 mm, R = 4.0 mm, θ = 90°, K = 0.40.
  2. Neutral-axis offset: Kt = 0.40 × 2.0 = 0.80 mm.
  3. Neutral-axis radius: R + Kt = 4.0 + 0.80 = 4.80 mm.
  4. Angle in radians: (π/180) × 90 = π/2 = 1.5708.
  5. Bend allowance: BA = 1.5708 × 4.80 = 7.5398 mm ≈ 7.54 mm.
  6. Bend deduction: BD = 2(4.0 + 2.0) − 7.5398 = 12.0 − 7.5398 = 4.4602 mm ≈ 4.46 mm.

Answer. Kt = 0.80 mm, BA = 7.54 mm, and BD = 4.46 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using the outside radius R + t in the bend allowance instead of the neutral-axis radius R + Kt.
  • Forgetting K or setting K = 0.50 for every material; K depends on material and R/t and usually lies between 0.33 and 0.50.
  • Mixing bend allowance with bend deduction: BA is added to straight lengths, while BD is subtracted from the sum of outside dimensions.
  • Treating springback as a bend allowance error. Springback changes the final angle after unloading; compensate by overbending or bottoming.
  • Putting the bend line too close to the sheet edge, which causes edge cracking before the bend reaches full angle.

3.4 Deep drawing and cup drawing

Deep drawing pushes a flat sheet blank into a die with a punch, so the sheet flows into a cup instead of stretching like a balloon. Cup drawing is the simple cylindrical case. The process exists because one piece, with no seam, makes stronger and leak-tight containers: steel tumblers, pressure-cooker shells, beverage cans and car panels. The main decisions are blank size, draw ratio, blank-holder force, and whether to redraw or iron the wall. Wrinkling, tearing, earing and wall thinning are the signs that those decisions went wrong.

You need 3.1 for formability and strain limits, 3.2 for how the blank is cut, and 3.3 for the corner radius at the cup bottom.

The cup-drawing mechanism

Deep drawing is a sheet-metal process in which a punch pushes a flat blank into a die opening. The sheet is held at the edges, so the metal flows inward and takes the die shape instead of stretching uniformly. Cup drawing is the simple case: a circular blank becomes a cylindrical cup with one closed end. The blank is first cut by blanking (see 3.2).

The sequence is: place the blank on the die; clamp its flange with a blank holder; push the punch down; the flange slides inward and the wall rises around the punch; remove the cup. The cup bottom stays near the original thickness. The wall may thin or thicken depending on clearance and ironing.

D_b = √(d² + 4dh)

D_b is the blank diameter in mm; d is the cup diameter in mm; h is the cup height in mm. The sheet is thin and the corner radius is ignored.

This formula equates the surface area of the blank to the area of the cup, ignoring the corner radius and any thinning. It gives a starting blank size. A larger blank than needed wastes material and raises the load; a smaller blank leaves the cup short. The same idea appears in steel tumblers and pressure-cooker shells.

Draw ratio, blank holder and redrawing

The draw ratio measures how severe a draw is. A large DR means the flange must shrink a lot as it becomes the wall. If DR is too large for the sheet, the wall tears near the punch corner. The safe limit is found from the forming limit diagram (see 3.1) or shop trials, not from one universal number.

DR = D_b / D_p

DR is the draw ratio (no unit); D_b is the blank diameter in mm; D_p is the punch diameter in mm.

r = 1 − D_p / D_b = 1 − 1 / DR

r is the reduction as a fraction; D_b is the blank diameter in mm; D_p is the punch diameter in mm; DR is the draw ratio.

The blank holder presses on the flange with blank holder force, BHF. It stops the unsupported flange from buckling into wrinkles. Too little BHF gives a wavy flange; too much BHF locks the flange, raises the punch load and tears the cup. The setting is a balance: enough friction and support to suppress wrinkles, not so much that the wall cannot pull inward.

Redrawing uses a second draw with a smaller punch and die. The first draw makes a shallow cup; later draws reduce its diameter and increase its height. This keeps each draw ratio inside the safe range. Annealing may be needed between draws if the sheet strain-hardens too much. Redrawing is not the same as ironing; redrawing changes the cup diameter without deliberately thinning the wall.

Ironing, defects and variants

Ironing forces the cup through a die that is slightly smaller than the cup wall. The wall thins and becomes smoother and more uniform. Ironing raises the punch load and can cause tearing if the wall is already thin. It is used for beverage cans and thin-walled containers where wall thickness and finish matter.

  • Wrinkling: the flange buckles when BHF is too low or clearance is too large.
  • Tearing: the wall pulls apart when DR is too high, BHF is too high, or the punch corner radius is too small.
  • Earing: uneven flange height from planar anisotropy in rolled sheet; ears form where the sheet flows differently.
  • Orange peel: a rough surface from coarse grains; severe stretching makes grain boundaries show.

A variant, hydroforming, uses fluid pressure instead of a rigid punch to push the blank into the die. Some syllabi list it with cup drawing; details belong to advanced study. Corner radius at the cup bottom should follow bending limits (see 3.3); a sharp corner concentrates strain and tears.

Worked example

Question. A cylindrical cup of diameter 50 mm and height 60 mm is to be drawn from a 2.0 mm thick steel sheet. Estimate the blank diameter using D_b = √(d² + 4dh). Take the punch diameter for the first draw as the cup diameter. Find the draw ratio. State whether one draw is likely to be enough.

  1. Data: cup diameter d = 50 mm, height h = 60 mm, sheet thickness t = 2.0 mm. Take punch diameter D_p = d = 50 mm for the first draw.
  2. Blank diameter: D_b = √(d² + 4dh) = √(50² + 4 × 50 × 60) = √(2500 + 12000) = √14500 = 120.4 mm.
  3. Draw ratio: DR = D_b / D_p = 120.4 / 50 = 2.41.
  4. A draw ratio of 2.41 is high for many sheet metals. The first draw is likely to need a redraw with a smaller punch, unless the sheet is very formable and trials show otherwise.

Answer. Blank diameter is about 120.4 mm and the first-draw draw ratio is about 2.41, so one draw is likely not enough and redrawing is likely.

Exam traps

  • Do not use punch diameter as blank diameter in the draw ratio; DR = D_b / D_p, not D_p / D_b.
  • Blank holder force is not 'tighter is better'. Too little force gives wrinkles; too much force raises the punch load and tears the cup.
  • Ironing is not the same as redrawing. Ironing deliberately thins the wall; redrawing mainly changes the cup diameter.
  • The blank formula D_b = √(d² + 4dh) ignores the corner radius and wall thinning. Do not treat it as exact for a thick sheet or a large-radius cup.
  • Earing is not random wrinkling. It comes from direction-dependent sheet properties, so a larger blank holder force does not cure it.

Deep drawing is a sheet-metal process in which a punch pushes a blank into a die so the sheet flows into a hollow shape. Cup drawing is the cylindrical case that makes a cup with one closed end.

Understand Why can increasing blank holder force both help and hurt a draw? 3 marks

It helps by pressing the flange flat and stopping wrinkle formation. It hurts when too high: the flange cannot slide inward, punch load rises, and the cup wall tears.

Apply A cylindrical cup has d = 40 mm and h = 50 mm. Estimate the blank diameter. Take D_p = 40 mm and find the draw ratio. 4 marks

Analyze A plant sees a wavy flange on the first draw. A trainee suggests increasing the blank holder force a lot. Analyse the likely cause and the risk of that change. 5 marks

The wavy flange points to wrinkling from low blank holder force or excessive clearance. A large increase may flatten the flange but can lock it, raise punch load and tear the wall. Adjust BHF with lubrication, clearance and draw ratio instead of only increasing force.

Analyze Explain why earing appears in a drawn cup and why a larger blank holder force does not cure it. 5 marks

Earing is uneven flange height caused by planar anisotropy: the rolled sheet flows differently in different directions. The blank holder controls friction and buckling, not the direction-dependent flow, so it cannot remove ears.

Understand State two differences between deep drawing and wire drawing. 3 marks

Deep drawing forms a sheet blank into a cup; wire drawing pulls a rod or wire through a die to reduce its diameter. Deep drawing changes a flat sheet into a hollow part; wire drawing is a bulk reduction of a solid section.

Servo presses with programmable blank-holder force are used to reduce wrinkling and tearing in auto panels and cans. 3.5 Stretch forming, embossing and coining

Three sheet processes shape metal without cutting it. Stretch forming grips the sheet at its edges and pulls it over a form block; the sheet is stretched by tension, so it takes the block's shape and springs back very little. Embossing and coining press the sheet between closed dies so the die surface is copied onto the metal. Embossing makes shallow raised patterns. Coining squeezes the metal hard enough to change its thickness. You meet these in aircraft skins, car panels, nameplates and the coins in your pocket.

You need: strain, necking and forming limits from 3.1, and springback from 3.3.

Stretch forming: tension over a form block

A stretch forming press holds the blank by its two long edges in serrated jaws called grips. A rigid form block, shaped like the inside of the finished part, sits between the grips. The block rises into the sheet, or the grips move apart, and the sheet is pulled over the block. The sheet stays under tensile stress while it touches the block, so it wraps the block tightly instead of merely bending over it.

e = (L_f − L_0) / L_0 ε = ln(L_f / L_0)

e is the engineering strain (no unit); ε is the true strain (no unit); L_f is the final gripped length in mm; L_0 is the original gripped length in mm; ln is the natural logarithm. Both strains are positive in tension.

Springback is the elastic recovery after the load is removed. In stretch forming the sheet carries membrane tension, and the bending stress through the thickness is small beside it. Only the elastic part comes back, roughly the yield strain, near 0.2% for aluminium, so the part stays close to the block. Plain bending springs back far more (see 3.3).

  1. Clamp the blank edges in the serrated grips, leaving a scrap margin.
  2. Bring the form block up until it touches the middle of the sheet.
  3. Pull the grips apart until the sheet wraps the whole block surface.
  4. Hold for a moment, release the load, and trim off the gripped margin.

Parts made this way: aircraft fuselage and wing skins, wing leading edges, large car body panels, sink bowls and curtain-wall panels. Aluminium alloys, titanium and austenitic stainless steel are chosen because they stretch far before necking (see 3.1). Only shallow, gently contoured shapes pay off; single curvature is easiest, compound curvature is possible, and deep or sharply cornered shapes tear. The margin under the teeth is deformed and becomes scrap.

Embossing and coining: pressing detail into the sheet

Embossing uses a matched pair of dies: one carries the pattern raised, the other carries the same pattern sunk. The sheet is pressed between them, and the pattern stands out on one face and sinks in on the other. The metal flows mainly in its own plane, so the thickness change is small and the pattern depth is usually less than the sheet thickness. Foil, nameplates, instrument dials, appliance panels and the stiffening ribs on car panels are embossed.

Coining also uses closed dies, but the cavity is sealed by a rim or collar, so the metal has nowhere to escape. The press squeezes at very high pressure, the metal flows in all directions, and the thickness changes on purpose. A coin is the standard example: the blank is thicker than the finished coin at its flat field, and the die faces and the collar close on it, so one or two blows force the surplus metal into the rim and the relief on both faces at once. Coining also makes medals, jewellery, dental caps and small precision parts. The flow is severe, so blanks are often annealed between blows and the dies are hard tool steel or carbide.

Separating the three processes

All three shape the sheet by pressure, not by cutting, and none of them needs the punch-and-die clearance of blanking. What separates them is the state of stress in the metal.

  • Stretch forming: tension across the whole sheet, one form block, large gentle parts, low springback.
  • Embossing: compression between matched dies, shallow pattern, thickness nearly constant.
  • Coining: compression with the cavity closed, deep relief, thickness changes deliberately.

Faults to watch. In stretch forming, tearing starts at the grips or at a sharp corner of the block, and coarse grains give an orange-peel surface. In embossing, too much depth tears the thin wall of the pattern. In coining, an oversized blank folds into laps because the surplus metal has nowhere to go.

Worked example

Question. A 1.2 mm thick aluminium sheet is stretch formed. The length between the grips is 800 mm before the pull and 860 mm after it. (a) Find the engineering strain. (b) Find the true strain. (c) Estimate the final thickness, assuming the width does not change. (d) Say why the two strains differ.

  1. Known values: original gripped length L_0 = 800 mm, final gripped length L_f = 860 mm, original thickness t_0 = 1.2 mm.
  2. Extension = L_f − L_0 = 860 − 800 = 60 mm.
  3. Engineering strain e = 60 / 800 = 0.075, that is 7.5%.
  4. True strain ε = ln(L_f / L_0) = ln(860 / 800) = ln 1.075 = 0.0723.
  5. Constant volume with no width change gives t_f = t_0 / (L_f / L_0) = 1.2 / 1.075 = 1.116 mm, so the sheet thins by 1.2 − 1.116 = 0.084 mm.
  6. Difference between the strains = 0.075 − 0.0723 = 0.0027. It is small because the stretch is small; at 50% stretch the gap becomes large, and true strain is the correct measure there because it adds up the strain of each small increment.

Exam traps

  • Saying embossing and coining both keep the thickness constant. Coining changes thickness on purpose; embossing changes it very little.
  • Using engineering strain where true strain belongs. At 7.5% stretch the gap is small (0.075 against 0.0723), but at large stretch true strain is the one that adds up.
  • Drawing a matching die cavity for stretch forming. There is only a form block; the sheet is pulled over one surface, not squeezed between two.
  • Writing ε = ln(L_0 / L_f). The ratio is final over original, so ε comes out positive in tension.
  • Believing stretch forming removes springback completely. It only reduces it; a small elastic recovery always remains.

Check yourself

Remember Define engineering strain and true strain for a stretched sheet. 2 marks

Engineering strain e = (L_f − L_0)/L_0, based on the original length. True strain ε = ln(L_f/L_0), which adds the strain of each small increment. L_f is the final length and L_0 the original length, both in mm.

Understand Why is springback smaller in stretch forming than in simple bending? 3 marks

In bending only a thin outer layer yields while the core stays elastic, so the recovery is a large share of the bend. In stretch forming the whole section is above yield in membrane tension, with little bending stress through the thickness, so the recovered elastic strain is only about the yield strain.

Apply A sheet is stretched from 500 mm to 540 mm between grips. Find the engineering strain and the true strain. 4 marks

L_0 = 500 mm, L_f = 540 mm. e = (540 − 500)/500 = 0.08 = 8%. ε = ln(540/500) = ln 1.08 = 0.0770.

Apply A stretch-formed part must reach a true strain of 0.05. By what percentage does its length increase? 3 marks

ε = ln(L_f/L_0) = 0.05, so L_f/L_0 = e^0.05 = 1.0513. The length increases by 5.13%.

Understand Give two differences between embossing and coining. 3 marks

Embossing uses matched dies and makes shallow detail with almost no thickness change; coining uses a closed die with a rim, very high pressure, and changes thickness. In embossing the pattern is raised on one face and sunk on the other; coining fills fine relief through the full thickness.

Analyze A coining die is built without a rim or collar. What goes wrong? 5 marks

The metal escapes sideways instead of being forced into the detail, so the pressure stays low, the relief is not filled, and the blank spreads and thickens unevenly. The rim is what seals the cavity and keeps the stress high.

Analyze Why are the grips serrated, and why is the gripped margin scrapped? 4 marks

Serrations stop the sheet slipping so the full pull reaches the sheet instead of the jaws. The teeth leave notches and the metal under the grips is deformed, so a margin is left outside the part and trimmed off.

Remember Name two products made by coining and two made by embossing. 2 marks

Coining: coins and medals. Embossing: nameplates and the ribbed panels on appliances.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In industry, stretch forming is often helped or replaced by hydroforming, where oil at high pressure pushes the sheet onto a one-sided tool; tube hydroforming makes bicycle frame parts and car exhaust sections. High-energy-rate forming uses an explosive charge or a magnetic pulse instead of a slow press. Springback compensation by die correction and by active blank-holder control is an active research area. 3.6 Shaping of plastics: injection moulding and extrusion

Thermoplastics soften when heated and harden when cooled, so they can be shaped again and again. Injection moulding melts plastic pellets and forces the melt into a closed mould, cycle after cycle, to make discrete parts such as phone cases, bottle caps and car trims. Extrusion pushes the same melt through a die without a closed mould, giving a continuous pipe, film or profile. The key idea is the same: heat the polymer until it flows, shape it, then cool it below its softening point. Cooling, not filling, usually sets the production rate.

You need: the basic idea of force and pressure; the sheet-metal chapters on bending and deep drawing are useful analogies for tooling, but polymer melt flow is new.

Polymer melt and the injection moulding machine

A polymer is a long-chain molecule made by joining many small units called monomers. A thermoplastic is a polymer that softens on heating and can be remelted; examples are polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene and ABS. A thermoset is a polymer that cures into a cross-linked network and cannot be remelted; examples are epoxy and phenolic resin. The melt is the hot, flowable polymer. Viscosity is its resistance to flow: high viscosity means a thick, slow-flowing melt.

  • Hopper: holds plastic pellets.
  • Barrel and screw: heaters melt the pellets; the rotating screw mixes and conveys the melt.
  • Nozzle: connects the barrel to the mould and injects the melt.
  • Mould: contains the cavity that gives the part its shape; the clamp holds it closed.
  • Injection unit: stops screw rotation and pushes the screw forward as a ram.
  1. Fill: the screw moves forward and melt fills the mould cavity.
  2. Pack: pressure is held while the cooling part shrinks, so more melt enters.
  3. Cool: water channels in the mould remove heat until the part is stiff enough.
  4. Eject: the mould opens, ejector pins push out the part, and the clamp closes for the next cycle.
t<sub>c</sub> = t<sub>fill</sub> + t<sub>pack</sub> + t<sub>cool</sub> + t<sub>eject</sub>

tc is cycle time in seconds; tfill is fill time; tpack is pack or hold time; tcool is cooling time; teject is ejection and mould-reset time.

Cycle time, shrinkage and common defects

Cooling usually takes the largest share of the cycle because plastics conduct heat poorly. A thick wall cools much more slowly than a thin wall, so wall thickness controls output more than fill speed does. Pack pressure decides how much shrinkage is compensated while the part is still molten. High pack pressure reduces sink marks but can raise frozen-in stress and warpage.

  • Short shot: incomplete fill; causes include low injection pressure, low melt temperature, cold mould or blocked gate.
  • Flash: excess plastic at the parting line; causes include too high pack pressure or poor mould closing.
  • Sink marks: local surface depressions; thick ribs or bosses shrink after the skin freezes.
  • Weld lines: weak visible lines where two melt fronts meet and do not knit fully.
  • Warpage: uneven cooling or uneven packing leaves different shrinkage across the part.

Process control sets melt temperature, mould temperature, injection speed, pack pressure and cooling time. The operator should not fix a defect by changing one setting blindly: short shot and flash can both appear when pressure is wrong, but in opposite directions. The examiner often asks for the cycle order and for the reason cooling dominates.

Extrusion and compression moulding

Extrusion uses a screw extruder: pellets fall into a hopper, the rotating screw melts and conveys them along a heated barrel, and the melt is forced through a die. A melt pump, often a gear pump, steadies flow and pressure. The extrudate then passes through a cooling bath or air cooling and is pulled by a haul-off at controlled speed. The product is continuous: pipe, film, sheet, rod, cable insulation or a profile such as a window frame.

Injection moulding and extrusion both use a screw and a melt, but their logic differs. Injection moulding is cyclic and makes discrete parts in a closed mould. Extrusion is continuous and makes a constant cross-section through an open die. A long pipe is therefore an extrusion product, not an injection moulding.

Some syllabi add compression moulding for thermosets. A measured charge of compound is placed in a heated mould cavity. The mould closes and applies pressure, so the charge fills the cavity and cures by chemical cross-linking. After cure, the mould opens and the part is ejected. Compression moulding is cyclic, but it is not extrusion and does not use a rotating screw. Mould design details belong to a later course.

Worked example

Question. An injection moulding cycle has fill 2 s, pack 3 s, cool 15 s and eject 5 s for one cavity. Find the cycle time and the output per hour.

  1. Cycle time tc = fill + pack + cool + eject = 2 s + 3 s + 15 s + 5 s = 25 s.
  2. Output per hour N = 3600 s/h ÷ 25 s/part = 144 parts/h.

Answer. The cycle time is 25 s, and the output is 144 parts per hour.

Exam traps

  • Calling extrusion a cyclic process that makes discrete parts; extrusion is continuous through a die.
  • Using only fill time as cycle time and forgetting pack, cool and eject.
  • Saying a thermoset can be remelted like a thermoplastic; thermosets cure and cannot be remelted.
  • Confusing short shot with flash: short shot is incomplete fill, flash is excess plastic at the parting line.
  • Treating viscosity as density; viscosity is resistance to flow, and it falls when melt temperature rises.

Check yourself

Remember Define thermoplastic and thermoset. 2 marks

A thermoplastic softens on heating and can be remelted. A thermoset cures into a cross-linked network and cannot be remelted.

Remember Name the four stages of the injection moulding cycle in order. 2 marks

Fill, pack, cool, eject.

Understand Why does cooling usually dominate injection moulding cycle time? 4 marks

Plastics have low thermal conductivity, so heat leaves the part slowly. The part must be stiff enough to eject without damage, so cooling time is often longer than fill and pack.

Apply A cycle has fill 1.5 s, pack 2.5 s, cool 12 s and eject 4 s. Find cycle time and output per hour. 4 marks

Cycle = 1.5 + 2.5 + 12 + 4 = 20 s; output = 3600/20 = 180 parts/hour.

Analyze A part shows a short shot near the far end of the cavity. Give two likely causes and one suitable correction. 5 marks

Low injection pressure or low melt temperature can make the melt freeze before filling. Cold mould or a blocked gate can also cause it. Raise melt or mould temperature, increase injection pressure or speed, or clear the gate.

Understand What is melt viscosity, and why does melt temperature matter? 3 marks

Viscosity is resistance to flow. Higher melt temperature usually lowers viscosity, so the melt fills easier, but too high a temperature can degrade the polymer.

Analyze A part has a weld line. Explain the likely cause and give one fix. 5 marks

Two melt fronts meet after flowing around a core or from two gates, and they do not knit fully. Raise melt or mould temperature, increase injection speed, or move the gate.

Apply A plant must make a 6 m constant-cross-section PVC pipe. Should it use injection moulding or extrusion? Give the reason. 4 marks

Use extrusion. The pipe has a constant cross-section and is long, so a continuous die and haul-off are natural. Injection moulding is cyclic and would need an impractical long mould.

Not for the exam. Industry uses multi-cavity hot-runner moulds, co-extrusion, twin-screw compounding and simulation to balance fill, pack and cooling. Recycling and bio-based thermoplastics make melt viscosity and thermal history important again. Search next for polymer rheology, injection moulding simulation and extrusion die design.

Takeaways

  1. Formability is limited by necking or tearing, and n, r̄ and the FLD help judge safe strain.
  2. Punching and blanking use the same shear tool, but the product is a hole in punching and the slug in blanking.
  3. A bent part needs bend allowance in the blank length, and springback is corrected by overbending.
  4. Deep drawing needs controlled draw ratio and blank holder force; ironing thins the cup wall.
  5. Stretch forming uses tension over a form block, while embossing and coining use closed dies; coining changes thickness.
  6. Thermoplastics are shaped by injection moulding in cycles and by extrusion continuously.
  7. Defects link to process variables: wrinkles and tears in drawing, burr in shearing, short shots in moulding.
  8. Process choice depends on batch size, shape, material and required surface detail.
