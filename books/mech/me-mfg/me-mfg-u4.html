4.1 Classification of welding processes

There is no single process called welding. There are three families, and every one ends the same way: two metal surfaces become one continuous mass at the atomic level. Either the parent metal melts, or it stays solid and is pressed and heated into contact, or only a lower-melting filler melts. Once you can place any process in its family, the rest of welding — flames, arcs, electrodes, defects — files away neatly. This classification is used every day to choose a process for a car body, a radiator, a PCB or a pipeline.

You need only that every metal has a fixed melting point. The freezing of a melt is covered in Unit 1, Metal Casting Processes.

Three families, one goal

Two metal surfaces touch only at a few high spots. Atoms elsewhere are too far apart to bond. Joining closes that gap until the surfaces become one continuous mass at the atomic scale. This closing is called coalescence. Every joining process is one route to coalescence.

Fusion welding melts the parent metal at the joint. The melted pool is the fusion zone; it mixes with any filler and freezes into one piece with the parent on both sides. SMAW, gas welding, laser welding and resistance spot welding all work this way.

Solid-state welding melts nothing. The surfaces are cleaned, pressed together and held below melting. Pressure breaks the oxide and flattens the high spots, and atoms diffuse across the interface with time. Friction, friction stir, ultrasonic, explosive and diffusion bonding are solid-state. No melt means no cast structure in the joint and little distortion.

The third family melts only the filler. A filler with a lower melting point melts, wets the solid parent surfaces and freezes. The parent keeps its grain structure, its heat treatment and its flatness. Brazing uses a filler melting above 450 °C; soldering uses one melting below 450 °C.

Filler words. An autogenous joint uses no filler: the parent alone closes the gap, as in spot and friction stir welding. A homogeneous joint uses filler of the same composition as the parent; a heterogeneous joint uses a different filler, such as a silver alloy joining a carbide tip to a steel shank.

T_filler < T_parent; brazing if T_filler > 450 °C, soldering if T_filler < 450 °C

T_filler is the melting temperature of the filler metal and T_parent that of the parts being joined, both in °C. The 450 °C line is a convention about the filler only, not a process temperature.

Joints, welds and choosing a process

A joint is the shape of the assembly. Butt: two plates in line, edge to edge. Lap: one plate lies on the other. T-joint: one plate stands on the face of another. Corner: two plates meet at a corner. Edge: two plate edges stand side by side.

A weld is the metal formed. A groove weld fills a prepared bevel between plates and is the usual weld in a butt joint. A fillet weld is triangular and sits in the corner of a lap or T-joint. A plug weld fills a hole in the top plate to reach the plate below. A spot weld is one small nugget; a seam weld is a line of overlapping spots made by rolling electrodes.

  • Thickness: thin sheet favours soldering, laser, TIG or spot; thick plate favours multi-pass arc welding into a groove.
  • Metal: aluminium needs AC TIG or MIG; copper and dissimilar pairs are easiest brazed.
  • Position: overhead and vertical work is hard for a large molten pool; low-heat processes help.
  • Distortion: shrinkage on freezing pulls the plate; solid-state and laser put in little heat.

Whatever the process, it must control heat, cleanliness and air. When one of the three is wrong, a defect appears — see 4.9.

Worked example

Question. Classify each of these five joints and state, for each, whether the parent metal melts and whether a filler is used: (a) shielded metal arc welding of a mild steel butt joint; (b) friction stir welding of 6 mm aluminium plates; (c) brazing a carbide tip to a steel shank with a silver alloy that melts at 700 °C; (d) resistance spot welding of two 1 mm car-body sheets; (e) soldering a PCB joint with Sn-63Pb, which melts at 183 °C. Second part: a copper-to-brass radiator joint must run at 300 °C. Is solder acceptable, and what would you use instead?

  1. (a) The arc melts the plate edges, so this is fusion. The consumable electrode is matching steel filler, so the joint is homogeneous. Parent melts: yes. Filler: yes.
  2. (b) 6 mm is plate thickness, not a temperature. The rotating tool stirs the metal below melting, so this is solid state. Parent melts: no. Filler: none, so autogenous.
  3. (c) 700 °C is above 450 °C, so this is brazing. Carbide and steel stay solid; only the silver alloy melts and wets both. Parent melts: no. Filler: yes, and heterogeneous.
  4. (d) Current through the sheet contact melts a small nugget, so this is fusion. Parent melts: yes, only inside the nugget. Filler: none, so autogenous. The 1 mm is sheet thickness.
  5. (e) 183 °C is below 450 °C, so this is soldering. Copper pad and component lead stay solid. Parent melts: no. Filler: yes, heterogeneous.
  6. Second part: at 300 °C service the 183 °C filler is 117 °C below its melting point. It softens and remelts, so solder is not acceptable.
  7. Copper melts at 1085 °C. The replacement filler must melt above 450 °C to be a brazing alloy — at least 150 °C above the 300 °C service temperature — and below copper's 1085 °C. A silver-based alloy melting near 700 °C is typical.

Answer. Solder melting at 183 °C sits 117 °C below the 300 °C service temperature and would soften and remelt. Braze instead with a filler melting above 450 °C and below copper's 1085 °C; a silver-based alloy near 700 °C is typical.

Exam traps

  • Calling brazing and soldering 'welding'. The parent never melts, so the joint is filler-held and not a fusion joint; writing 'brazing is a type of welding' loses the mark.
  • Calling resistance spot welding solid-state because a press closes on the sheets. The press only supplies force; the current melts a nugget, so it is fusion.
  • Mixing up the heat-affected zone with the fusion zone. The HAZ never melted; it only got hot enough for its structure to change.
  • Reading 450 °C as a process temperature. It is a line drawn on the filler's melting point alone.
  • Thinking autogenous means without heat. It means without filler.

4.2 Gas welding, welding flames and gas cutting

Oxy-acetylene welding burns a controlled gas flame to melt the joint edges and a filler rod. The flame is hot, about 3200 °C at its inner cone, cheap to set up and easy to control on thin sheet, repair work and site jobs where electricity is not reliable. The same station also cuts steel with an oxygen jet. The key idea is small: the oxygen-to-acetylene ratio at the torch sets the flame type, and the flame type must match the metal.

You need Chapter 4.1 for where gas welding sits among fusion processes.

The gas welding station

An oxygen cylinder stands at about 150 bar; a common water capacity (internal volume) is 40 L. One bar is roughly atmospheric pressure. An acetylene cylinder stands at about 15 bar, but it is packed with a porous mass soaked in acetone. Acetylene dissolves in the acetone, so more gas can be stored safely. Each regulator reduces storage pressure to a steady working pressure. Acetylene working pressure stays near 1 bar, because free acetylene above roughly 1.5 bar can decompose without air. Hoses carry the gases to the torch. Flashback arrestors stop a flame from travelling back into a cylinder. Inside the torch, a mixing chamber sets the oxygen-to-acetylene ratio. Interchangeable tips match flame size and gas flow to plate thickness.

  1. Open the cylinder valves. Set the acetylene regulator to about 1 bar and the oxygen regulator to the tip maker's pressure.
  2. Purge each hose to remove air.
  3. Open the torch acetylene valve a little, light the gas, then open the torch oxygen valve slowly to set the flame.
  4. Adjust the flame to neutral, oxidising or carburising before welding.
  • Never use oil or grease on oxygen fittings; oxygen makes grease burn violently.
  • Keep cylinders upright and secured.

Flame chemistry and the three flames

The flame burns in two stages. In the inner cone, one volume of acetylene reacts with one volume of oxygen to give carbon monoxide and hydrogen. In the outer envelope, those gases burn with more oxygen, mostly from the surrounding air, giving carbon dioxide and water. Complete combustion needs 2.5 volumes of oxygen per volume of acetylene. The torch is not set to 2.5:1 because the outer envelope draws oxygen from air. A neutral torch setting is about 1:1 by volume. The hottest point is at the tip of the inner cone, about 3200 °C.

C₂H₂ + O₂ → 2CO + H₂

C₂H₂ is acetylene, O₂ is oxygen, CO is carbon monoxide and H₂ is hydrogen; this is the primary reaction in the inner cone.

Too much oxygen gives an oxidising flame: short pointed cone, hissing sound. Too much acetylene gives a carburising flame with a feather of unburned acetylene between cones. The setting is made at the torch.

Leftward, rightward and gas cutting

Leftward welding moves the torch from right to left, with the filler rod ahead of the flame. It suits thin plates, usually up to about 5 mm. Rightward welding moves from left to right, with the filler rod behind the flame. It gives better penetration on thicker plates. The names describe the direction of travel, not the hand that holds the torch.

Gas cutting cuts mild steel by burning it in a jet of pure oxygen; the flame only preheats the steel.

  1. Preheat the steel to its ignition temperature with a neutral flame.
  2. Open the cutting oxygen jet. The iron burns in the oxygen, not simply melts.
  3. Blow the iron oxide slag out of the kerf with the same jet.
  4. Move the torch steadily along the marked line.

The cut width is the kerf. Drag is the lag of the bottom of the cut behind the top; too much drag means the torch is moving too fast or the oxygen pressure is wrong. Gas cutting works well on mild steel. Oxy-hydrogen gives a lower flame temperature and is used for thin work and some brazing. Air-acetylene is cheaper but cooler, common for soldering and small heating jobs.

Worked example

Question. An oxygen cylinder has a water capacity of 40 L and is charged to 150 bar. Treat oxygen as ideal at constant temperature. (a) Find the free oxygen volume at 1 bar in litres and cubic metres. (b) A neutral flame uses oxygen and acetylene at about 1:1 by volume at the torch. An acetylene cylinder has 40 L water capacity and delivers about 6 m³ of free acetylene. How many acetylene cylinders can one oxygen cylinder serve? (c) If the torch consumes about 300 L/h of oxygen at this 1:1 setting, how long does the oxygen cylinder last?

  1. At constant temperature, p₁V₁ = p₂V₂. Here p₁ = 150 bar, V₁ = 40 L and p₂ = 1 bar.
  2. V₂ = 150 × 40 ÷ 1 = 6000 L. Since 1000 L = 1 m³, this is 6 m³.
  3. For acetylene, use the manufacturer's gas content: about 6 m³ (6000 L) of free acetylene per 40 L cylinder. It is stored dissolved in acetone, not as free gas at 15 bar.
  4. Number of acetylene cylinders = 6 m³ ÷ 6 m³ = 1.
  5. At a 1:1 torch setting the oxygen flow is also about 300 L/h. Time = 6000 L ÷ 300 L/h = 20 h.

Answer. Free oxygen is 6000 L (6 m³); one oxygen cylinder serves about one acetylene cylinder; the pair lasts about 20 h at 300 L/h.

Exam traps

  • Neutral flame is about 1:1 by volume at the torch, not by mass. Complete combustion still needs 2.5 volumes of oxygen per volume of acetylene, with the rest drawn from air.
  • Acetylene cylinder pressure is about 15 bar, but the working pressure at the torch is only about 1 bar. Free acetylene above roughly 1.5 bar can decompose.
  • The hottest point is at the tip of the inner cone, not in the outer envelope.
  • Gas cutting is burning of iron in an oxygen jet, not melting by the preheat flame. The preheat only starts the reaction.
  • Leftward and rightward name the direction of travel; kerf is cut width and drag is the bottom lagging behind the top.

4.3 Physics of the welding arc and power sources

An arc is a gas made electrically conducting by ionisation. It exists because a hot cathode emits electrons, the gas column supplies ions and electrons, and the anode collects them. The arc voltage is the sum of three drops, and its shape on a voltage-current plot decides which power source can hold it steady. This physics sets the source choice for manual, TIG, MIG and submerged arc welding, and it fixes the duty cycle a machine can safely deliver.

You need: the heat-source idea from Chapter 4.2 and basic circuit ideas (voltage, current, resistance) from the physics course.

The arc is a conducting gas

An arc is a gas that has been made electrically conducting. The gas between the electrode and the work is normally an insulator. The power source keeps a voltage across the gap. The cathode is heated by the arc and by the current; hot enough, it emits electrons by thermionic emission. Those electrons accelerate towards the anode. On the way they collide with gas atoms and knock out more electrons. The gas becomes a plasma: a mixture of positive ions, free electrons and neutral atoms. This ionisation is what lets a few tens of volts drive tens or hundreds of amperes.

The arc has three regions. At the cathode, a thin cathode drop accelerates electrons out of the metal. In the middle, the plasma column carries the current and most of the visible light. At the anode, an anode drop collects electrons into the work. The temperature is a few thousand kelvin near the metal surfaces and much higher in the column. The cathode spot is small and does not sit still; it wanders over the surface. On DC, magnetic forces from the current path can push the arc sideways; this is arc blow.

V_arc = V_c + V_a + E L

V_arc is arc voltage in V; V_c is cathode drop in V; V_a is anode drop in V; E is the column voltage gradient in V/mm; L is arc length in mm. V_c and V_a are nearly fixed; E L grows with arc length.

A plasma arc squeezes the column through a small nozzle. The same three drops remain, but the column gradient is higher, so the arc is hotter and stiffer. That is why a plasma arc can cut or weld with a narrow column while a free arc spreads.

Static characteristic and source matching

Plot arc voltage against current at a fixed arc length; this is the static characteristic. At low current the voltage falls as current rises, because more current means more ionisation and a better conducting column. In the middle range the curve is nearly flat. At high current the voltage rises again, because the column is constricted and the cathode and anode drops increase. The source has its own voltage-current curve. The arc runs where the two curves cross: at that current the source voltage equals the arc voltage.

A drooping source falls steeply as current rises. It behaves as a constant-current source and holds the arc steady when the arc length changes. Manual SMAW and TIG use drooping sources. A flat source holds nearly constant voltage. It is used for MIG and for SAW with a self-adjusting arc. If the arc shortens, the flat source sends more current, the wire burns off faster, and the arc length comes back. A rising source, whose voltage increases with current, is used in some high-current submerged-arc work.

Open-circuit voltage is the voltage before the arc strikes; it is typically 50-90 V. Arc voltage while welding is typically 20-40 V. The heat does not divide equally. More heat lands at the anode; two-thirds of the arc heat is often quoted at the anode. In DCEN the electrode is negative and the work is positive, so the work receives the larger share. In DCEP the electrode is positive, so the electrode receives it.

Power sources and duty cycle

Power sources convert the supply to a low-voltage, high-current output with a suitable V-I curve. An AC transformer steps down the mains and uses leakage reactance to droop. A DC rectifier adds a diode bridge to a transformer. A motor-generator uses a motor to drive a DC generator; it is heavy and mostly historical. An inverter rectifies the mains, switches it at high frequency, and transforms it with a small core; it is light, efficient and can shape the curve electronically.

I₁² D₁ = I₂² D₂

I₁ and I₂ are currents in A; D₁ and D₂ are duty cycles as fractions. The relation holds for one machine, because the thermal limit depends on I² times the fraction of time.

Duty cycle is the fraction of a standard 10-minute period for which the machine can run at a stated current without overheating. A 300 A, 60% machine can weld 6 minutes in 10 at 300 A. At a lower current it can run longer; at a higher current it must run for a smaller fraction. The rated current is always quoted at the rated duty cycle, not at 100%.

Worked example

Question. A welding power source is rated 300 A at 60% duty cycle. (a) Find the current it can supply continuously at 100% duty cycle. (b) If the same machine is used at 250 A, find the allowed duty cycle and the welding time in each 10-minute period.

  1. For one machine, the thermal limit gives I₁² D₁ = I₂² D₂, with currents in A and duty cycles as fractions.
  2. (a) Put I₁ = 300 A, D₁ = 0.60 and D₂ = 1.0. Then I₂ = √(300² × 0.60 ÷ 1.0) = 300 × √0.60 = 232.38 A.
  3. (b) Put I₁ = 300 A, D₁ = 0.60 and I₂ = 250 A. Then D₂ = (300² × 0.60) ÷ 250² = 54 000 ÷ 62 500 = 0.864.
  4. The welding time in a 10-minute period is 0.864 × 10 = 8.64 minutes.

Answer. At 100% duty cycle the source supplies 232.38 A; at 250 A the allowed duty cycle is 86.4%, that is 8.64 minutes of welding in every 10 minutes.

Exam traps

  • Duty cycle is based on a standard 10-minute period, not a day, not a shift, and not the arc-on time alone.
  • The rated current is quoted at the rated duty cycle. A 300 A, 60% machine is not a 300 A continuous machine.
  • Open-circuit voltage (50-90 V) is not arc voltage (20-40 V). Open-circuit voltage is present before the arc strikes.
  • Drooping means constant current; flat means constant voltage. Do not swap the source curves when matching a process.
  • More heat lands at the anode. DCEN makes the work the anode, so the work gets the larger share; DCEP makes the electrode the anode, so the electrode gets it.

4.4 SMAW, TIG and MIG welding; electrodes and fluxes

An electric arc hotter than about 6000 °C melts metal whose melting point is 1500–2500 °C. Air would oxidise that molten metal, so each process shields it differently: a melting flux coating, a stream of argon, a blanket of granular flux, or a constricted nozzle. That single choice fixes the electrode, the gas, the plate thickness and the production rate. A fabricator uses stick welding on a windy site, TIG on thin stainless, and MIG on a long car-body seam.

You need: the arc and its power source from Chapter 4.3, and the three joining families from Chapter 4.1.

SMAW and electrode codes

Shielded metal arc welding (SMAW), or stick welding, uses a consumable metal rod covered with flux. The arc jumps from the rod tip to the work. The core wire melts into filler metal; the coating melts a little behind it and does five jobs: it burns to shielding gas, forms a slag blanket over the cooling bead, stabilises the arc with sodium or potassium silicates, deoxidises the weld pool, and can carry iron powder to raise deposition rate.

Current is set from the electrode diameter. A 3.2 mm general-purpose rod runs near 100–130 A; thinner rods use less. Too low gives poor fusion, too high burns the coating.

An electrode code such as E6013 is read digit by digit. E means electrode for SMAW. The first two digits, 60, give the minimum tensile strength in ksi — 60 ksi is about 410 MPa. The third digit, 1, gives the position: 1 means all positions. The fourth digit, 3, gives the coating and current type: a rutile (high-titania potassium) coating that runs on AC or DC either polarity (DCEP or DCEN).

E7018 is 70 ksi, all position, with a basic low-hydrogen iron-powder coating that runs on DCEP or AC. Low-hydrogen electrodes must be kept dry: store them in a heated quiver, and bake them before use if they have absorbed moisture. Moisture puts hydrogen into the weld and causes cracks.

Typical uses: structural steel, pipelines, repairs, and outdoor work where wind would blow away a gas shield.

TIG: tungsten, gas and polarity

Tungsten inert gas (TIG) welding, also called GTAW, uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode. The arc burns between the tungsten tip and the work, and a separate filler rod is dipped into the pool by hand or fed by a wire feeder. Argon, sometimes helium, flows around the electrode as the shield. There is no flux and no slag.

The tungsten is alloyed for electron emission: thoriated (2% ThO₂) or ceriated tungsten starts easily and carries current without melting. Thoria is mildly radioactive, so ceriated or lanthanated electrodes are common in workshops. For DC steel work the tip is ground to a point; for AC aluminium it is left rounded or balled.

Polarity matters. Steel, stainless and titanium run on DCEN — electrode negative. Electrons leave the tungsten, hit the work, and give a deep, narrow weld while the tungsten stays cool. Aluminium runs on AC. The positive half-cycle throws heavy argon ions at the work and breaks the tough Al₂O₃ oxide skin; the negative half-cycle cools the tungsten and melts the metal.

TIG is slow and has low deposition, but it is clean and precise. Use it for thin sheet, root passes in pipe, and jobs where slag must not be trapped. It needs a skilled hand because the filler is added separately.

MIG, SAW, plasma and fluxes

Metal inert gas (MIG) welding, or GMAW, feeds a continuous consumable wire through the torch. That wire is both electrode and filler, so welding does not stop to change a rod. The shield is a gas: CO₂ for cheap steel work, or an argon-CO₂ mixture (often 15–20% CO₂) for smoother spray transfer. MIG uses DCEP — wire positive, work negative — which gives a stable arc and a high melt-off rate.

Metal transfer has three common modes. Short-circuit transfer uses low current: the wire touches the pool, pinches off a droplet, and re-strikes many times a second; it suits thin sheet. Globular transfer gives large, irregular drops and a rough arc. Spray transfer needs high current and an argon-rich gas; the wire tip turns into a fine jet of droplets and gives a smooth, deep weld. MIG is fast and easy to automate, which is why car bodies and structural fabrication use it.

Submerged arc welding (SAW) buries the arc under a blanket of granular flux. The flux melts into a slag that shields the arc, and currents of several hundred amperes become possible. Deposition is very high. SAW suits thick plate and long straight seams — pressure vessels and ship hulls — but the work must be flat or rotated under the head.

Plasma arc welding pushes the arc through a small water-cooled nozzle. The constriction raises the power density. In transferred mode the work is part of the circuit, and the arc can keyhole through a plate: it pierces a hole that moves along the joint and closes behind. In non-transferred mode the arc strikes inside the torch, which suits cutting and spraying.

Fluxes are classified by how they are supplied — electrode coating, granular bed, paste — and by chemistry. Acid (rutile) fluxes are easy to run; basic (limestone and fluorspar) fluxes give cleaner, tougher welds on high-strength steel.

Worked example

Question. A MIG weld runs at 200 A and 25 V. The travel speed is 5 mm/s and the arc efficiency is 0.8. (a) Find the heat input in J/mm and kJ/mm. (b) The joint is 6 m long. How much net heat enters the joint?

  1. Heat input per length of weld is H = η × V × I / v, where η is arc efficiency (0.8, dimensionless), V is arc voltage (25 V), I is current (200 A), and v is travel speed (5 mm/s).
  2. H = 0.8 × 25 × 200 / 5 = 0.8 × 5000 / 5 = 4000 / 5 = 800 J/mm.
  3. In kilojoules per millimetre: 800 / 1000 = 0.8 kJ/mm.
  4. Length in millimetres: 6 m × 1000 mm/m = 6000 mm.
  5. Net heat into the joint = H × length = 0.8 kJ/mm × 6000 mm = 4800 kJ (the arc itself delivers 1 kJ/mm × 6000 mm = 6000 kJ; 0.8 of that enters the work).

4.5 Resistance and spot welding

Resistance welding passes a very large current through two overlapping metal sheets held by water-cooled copper electrodes. The worst contact in the circuit is the faying surface, where the sheets touch. That contact resistance makes heat exactly at the joint. No filler, flux or arc is used. A press applies force, current flows for a few cycles, and a small molten nugget forms. Spot welding is the common form; car bodies, appliance panels, steel furniture and sheet-metal bins use it.

You need: the fusion-process family from Chapter 4.1 and duty cycle and electrical ratings from Chapter 4.3.

Heat made at the faying surface

An arc welding heat source sits outside the metal. Resistance welding makes its own heat inside the joint. A transformer secondary sends current through a top electrode, the top sheet, the faying surface where the sheets touch, the bottom sheet and the bottom electrode. The circuit has four resistances: the electrode bulk, the electrode-to-sheet contact, the sheet bulk and the sheet-to-sheet contact. At the start the faying surface is usually the largest, because two rough surfaces touch only at small asperities. Melting therefore begins exactly there.

H = I²Rt

H is heat in joules; I is current in amperes; R is total resistance in ohms; t is current flow time in seconds.

Typical currents are 5 kA to 20 kA at 1 V to 10 V. Weld time is a few cycles to a fraction of a second; at 50 Hz one cycle is 20 ms. A pneumatic or servo cylinder applies electrode force. Copper-chromium electrodes are water-cooled: copper carries current well and water removes heat, so the electrode stays hard. Force controls contact resistance and holds the molten nugget in place. Too little force gives expulsion of molten metal.

Squeeze, weld, hold and off

Every spot weld follows four steps. Squeeze: the electrodes close and force rises before any current. Weld: current flows and the nugget grows. Hold: current is off but force stays on while the nugget solidifies. Off: force releases and the electrodes open. The figure uses 150 ms squeeze, 100 ms weld, 150 ms hold and 100 ms off. Current flows only in the 100 ms weld step. Applying current before full force is a common cause of spatter.

Nugget growth is fast. The contact resistance falls as the metal heats and melts, so a constant-voltage supply sends more current. The nugget grows outward until the current stops. Expulsion happens when the molten nugget pushes through the sheet surface; it leaves a crater and a weak weld. Electrode wear opens the contact face, lowers current density and slowly shrinks the nugget. Current shunting through earlier spots also reduces the current at the new spot.

Types, uses and limits

Spot welding uses fixed electrodes and makes one nugget per cycle. Seam welding uses roller electrodes and a row of overlapping spots, so it makes a continuous leak-tight seam. It is not a different heat source. Projection welding puts small raised bumps on one sheet; current concentrates at each bump and several welds form in one stroke. Flash butt welding, upset welding and percussion welding are resistance variants for joining rod ends or small parts. Used widely for car bodies, appliance panels, steel furniture, bins and battery tabs.

  • No filler metal, no flux and no shielding gas are needed.
  • Cycle time is short and the gun is easy to automate; car bodies use thousands of spots.
  • Only lap joints fit the process, and the sheets must be thin and similar.
  • Shunt current, electrode wear and access to both sides limit design.
  • Pressure without melting is solid-state welding, covered in Chapter 4.6; defects go to Chapter 4.9.

Worked example

Question. Two 1 mm steel sheets are spot welded at 10 000 A for 0.1 s, and the total resistance between the electrodes is 100 µΩ. Find the heat generated. Then find the energy needed to melt a nugget of 4 mm diameter and 1.5 mm thickness. Take density 7850 kg/m³, specific heat 460 J/kg·K, melting temperature rise 1500 K and latent heat 270 kJ/kg. What fraction of the generated heat forms the nugget?

  1. H = I²Rt. Here I = 10 000 A, R = 100 µΩ = 100 × 10⁻⁶ Ω and t = 0.1 s. H = (10 000)² × (100 × 10⁻⁶) × 0.1 = 1000 J.
  2. Nugget volume V = (π/4)d²h, with d = 4 mm and h = 1.5 mm. V = (π/4)(4)²(1.5) = 18.85 mm³.
  3. Convert volume: 1 mm³ = 10⁻⁹ m³, so V = 18.85 × 10⁻⁹ m³. Mass m = ρV = 7850 × 18.85 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.48 × 10⁻⁴ kg.
  4. Melting energy = m(cΔT + L) = 1.48 × 10⁻⁴ × (460 × 1500 + 270 000) = 142.05 J.
  5. Fraction = 142.05 ÷ 1000 = 0.142 = 14.2%. The rest, 857.95 J, is conducted into the electrodes and the surrounding sheet.

Exam traps

  • Do not use H = I²Rt as the nugget melting energy. Most heat is conducted into the water-cooled electrodes and the sheet; the example forms only about 14% of the heat into the nugget.
  • Do not call seam welding a separate heat source. It is a row of overlapping spot welds made by roller electrodes.
  • Do not forget unit conversion: 100 µΩ is 100 × 10⁻⁶ Ω, and one 50 Hz cycle is 20 ms, not 1 s.
  • Electrode force does not make the heat. It sets contact resistance and prevents expulsion; low force can cause spatter and weak welds.
  • Do not use resistance welding for thick sections or dissimilar metals without checking; the process favours thin sheets of similar resistivity.

4.6 Solid-state welding: friction, friction stir, ultrasonic, explosive

Solid-state welding joins two metals without melting either. Clean faces are pressed together and heated below the melting point; atoms cross the interface and the pieces become one. No molten pool means no cast nugget, no gas porosity and no hot cracking. The process suits aluminium alloys that arc welding handles badly, and pairs of different metals that form brittle compounds when molten. Friction stir welded panels ride in trains and ships; friction welded shafts sit in cars and drilling rigs.

You need the three-way classification of chapter 4.1, and plastic deformation and recrystallisation from Unit 2, Bulk Metal Forming Processes.

The solid-state idea

Every solid-state process needs three things. Surfaces must be clean, because oxide and grease hold atoms apart. They must be forced into intimate contact, with pressure high enough to flatten surface asperities. Heat and time must then let atoms diffuse across the original interface.

Temperature stays below the melting point, usually 0.5 to 0.9 of the melting point on the absolute scale, in kelvin.

T<sub>join</sub> ≈ 0.5 T<sub>m</sub> to 0.9 T<sub>m</sub>

Tjoin is interface temperature in kelvin; Tm is melting point of the parent metal in kelvin.

Fusion welding melts metal and freezes it as a cast nugget in a heat-affected zone. That nugget is coarser and weaker than wrought parent metal, and melting brings porosity, hot cracking and loss of alloying elements. Solid-state joining skips the melt: wrought structure survives and the heat-affected zone stays narrow. It also joins metals that form brittle intermetallic compounds when molten, such as aluminium to steel.

The cost is hardware. Parts must be clean and closely fitted, and the machine needs heavy clamps plus a press, spindle or vibration source. These processes finish the classification of chapter 4.1: the parent metal stays solid from start to end.

Friction welding: rotary and linear

Rotary friction welding holds one part in a spinning chuck and the other in a stationary clamp, then pushes them together with axial force.

  1. Contact: faces rub at v = πDN/60 and friction makes heat.
  2. Friction heating: in seconds the interface reaches 0.8 to 0.9 Tm. Metal softens but no pool forms.
  3. Burn-off: axial force squeezes softened, oxide-covered metal out as flash. A fixed burn-off length passes.
  4. Braking: drive stops and rotation ends.
  5. Upset: a larger axial force forges the hot faces together. Flash is machined off later.

Controlling variables are rotational speed, friction pressure, burn-off length, upset pressure and upset time. A cycle takes seconds and runs without an operator.

Linear friction welding replaces spin with back-and-forth oscillation of a few millimetres at about 50 Hz under pressure. It joins flat, non-round parts, such as compressor blades to a disk to make a blisk.

Uses: axle and drive shafts, drill pipes, valve bodies, tool shanks, aluminium-to-steel transition joints.

v = πDN/60

v is rubbing speed at the interface in m/s; D is diameter of the rotating part in m; N is rotational speed in rev/min.

Friction stir welding

Friction stir welding (FSW) uses a non-consumable rotating tool with a shoulder, a flat disc that rubs the top surface, and a pin (probe), a short stub that projects into the joint line.

  1. Plunge: the spinning tool is pushed into the joint until the shoulder touches the plate.
  2. Dwell: a few seconds of rubbing raise the metal to about 0.8 Tm.
  3. Traverse: the tool moves along the joint. Softened metal flows around the pin and the shoulder forges it behind the tool.
  4. Withdraw: the tool lifts out and leaves an exit hole, later cut off or filled.

No melting happens. The pin leaves a stir zone of fine, recrystallised grains, flanked by a thermomechanically affected zone and an ordinary heat-affected zone.

Aluminium alloys of the 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx families hot crack, porosify or lose strength in arc welding; FSW is their home ground. Copper, magnesium, steel and titanium are also welded this way.

Defects: a tunnel or wormhole when the tool travels too fast for metal to fill behind it; a kissing bond, an unbonded line where oxide was never broken; excess flash; tool wear. Chapter 4.9 covers inspection.

Uses: ship deck panels, train floor panels, rocket fuel tanks, battery trays and heat sinks.

Ultrasonic, explosive and pressure bonding

Ultrasonic welding clamps parts between a vibrating tip (sonotrode) and an anvil and applies lateral shear vibration at 20 to 40 kHz. The vibration scrubs the surfaces, cracks the oxide film and lets clean metal bond under clamp force. Bulk temperature stays far below melting, so foils, dissimilar pairs such as aluminium to copper, IC wire bonds and plastics are welded this way.

Explosive welding stands a flyer plate at a small stand-off above a base plate and lays explosive on top. Detonation drives the flyer down at an angle. The collision point sweeps along and throws a jet of metal out of the closing gap; the jet strips oxide from both surfaces and very high pressure bonds them. The interface comes out wavy, the sign of a sound bond. Large clad plates — stainless steel on carbon steel, aluminium or titanium on steel — for chemical vessels and ship hulls are made this way.

Diffusion bonding holds cleaned parts in vacuum at 0.5 to 0.8 Tm under a few MPa for minutes to hours. Atoms diffuse across the interface and the bond forms with almost no deformation; titanium, ceramics and honeycomb panels are joined so. Cold pressure welding and roll bonding use no external heat: heavy deformation breaks the oxide and extrudes fresh metal, and a rolling mill presses two strips into one bimetal sheet.

Worked example

Question. Two 25 mm diameter steel rods are joined by rotary friction welding. The chuck turns at 1500 rev/min, the axial friction pressure is 40 MPa and the coefficient of friction at the interface is 0.5. Find the rubbing speed at the interface and the friction power dissipated.

  1. Rubbing speed: v = πDN/60 = π × 0.025 × 1500 / 60 = 1.963 m/s ≈ 1.96 m/s.
  2. Contact area: A = πD²/4 = π × 0.025² / 4 = 4.909 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  3. Axial force: F = p × A = 40 × 10⁶ × 4.909 × 10⁻⁴ = 19 635 N ≈ 19.6 kN.
  4. Friction force: F_f = μF = 0.5 × 19 635 = 9817 N.
  5. Friction power: P = F_f × v = 9817 × 1.963 = 19 277 W ≈ 19.3 kW.

Answer. The rubbing speed is 1.96 m/s and the friction power is about 19.3 kW (19 277 W).

Exam traps

  • Friction stir welding does not melt the metal. It softens it to about 0.8 of the melting point; no molten pool forms.
  • Friction welding is not limited to round bars. Linear friction welding oscillates one flat part against another and joins blisk blades and other non-round shapes.
  • Friction welding and friction stir welding are different. In friction welding one part spins; in FSW a separate tool spins and the parts stay still.
  • Explosive welding does not melt the plates. The jet cleans both surfaces and the bond is solid-state; any local melt is thrown out with the jet.
  • Ultrasonic welding is a joining process. Ultrasonic inspection is a different use of ultrasound and belongs with the defect methods of chapter 4.9.

4.7 Laser welding and cold metal transfer welding

Two processes sit at opposite ends of the heat-input scale. Laser welding focuses a beam of light to a spot a few tenths of a millimetre wide, reaching power densities a thousand times those of an arc: fast welds, narrow heat-affected zones, little distortion. Cold metal transfer (CMT) is a MIG arc that cuts its own current at every short circuit, giving an almost spatter-free weld at low heat input. Both are chosen for thin sheet, for dissimilar metals and for parts that must not distort — car bodies, battery tabs, instrument panels.

You need: chapter 4.3 for power density and heat input, and chapter 4.4 for the MIG process that CMT modifies.

Laser welding: a beam squeezed into a small spot

A laser emits light of a single wavelength as a narrow parallel beam. A lens focuses that beam to a spot a few tenths of a millimetre wide. All the beam power lands inside that small spot, so the power density is very high.

q = P / A

q is the power density at the work in W/cm²; P is the laser power reaching the work in W; A is the area of the focused spot in cm².

An arc gives about 10³ to 10⁴ W/cm². A focused laser gives 10⁵ to 10⁷ W/cm², roughly a thousand times more. That one fact explains the rest: the metal melts in microseconds, welding is fast, and so little heat enters that distortion stays small.

The 1 µm beams travel to the work head along a flexible optical fibre. A CO₂ beam cannot, so mirrors steer it.

Two operating modes. Below about 10⁶ W/cm² the surface melts and heat flows inwards by conduction: conduction mode, a shallow rounded weld. Above that, the metal boils. The vapour pushes a narrow hole — the keyhole — down through the melt. The beam reflects inside the hole and is absorbed almost completely, so one pass can penetrate several millimetres. The hole travels with the beam and the melt closes behind it, leaving a deep narrow weld with parallel sides and a small heat-affected zone.

The vapour above the keyhole ionises into a plasma plume. The plume absorbs and defocuses the beam, so a shielding gas — helium, argon or a mixture — blows it aside. The same gas protects the molten metal from air, as in arc welding (chapter 4.4).

What laser welding does well, and what it will not forgive

  • Heat input per millimetre is small, so distortion is small (chapter 4.3).
  • Travel speed of several metres per minute on 1 mm sheet.
  • No vacuum, no electrode and no flux; the beam never wears out.
  • Access from one side only; the beam can even pass through a glass window.

Electron beam welding is the other high-energy-beam process: a focused electron beam instead of light. It penetrates even deeper, but the work must sit inside a vacuum chamber, which limits part size and slows the cycle. A laser needs no vacuum.

Fit-up is the hard limit. There is usually no filler wire, so a gap cannot be bridged; the beam goes through the gap and cuts instead of joining. Edges must be cut clean and clamped tight, with any gap a small fraction of the sheet thickness.

Highly reflective metals resist the beam. Cold aluminium and copper reflect most of the 1 µm light and almost all of the 10.6 µm light. Absorption jumps once the keyhole opens, but starting the weld needs extra power, a tilted head or a darker surface. This is why copper busbars and thin aluminium are harder to weld than steel.

Cost and safety complete the picture: high capital cost, and a Class 4 laser needs an interlocked enclosure, eye protection and fume extraction. A collapsing keyhole can also trap gas and leave porosity.

Cold metal transfer: a MIG arc that switches itself off

Cold metal transfer (CMT) is a modified MIG process. In ordinary MIG the wire is fed forward at a steady speed and the arc runs at high current. When the wire touches the pool, a short circuit forms and the current bursts through the thin bridge of liquid metal until it explodes. That explosion throws spatter and dumps heat into the plate.

CMT removes the explosion by controlling two things at once: the current and the position of the wire.

  1. The wire is fed forward and the arc burns at low current, melting the tip into a drop.
  2. The drop touches the pool. The short circuit is sensed within microseconds and the current is cut to near zero.
  3. The wire drive reverses and pulls the wire back. The drop is left in the pool, pulled off by surface tension, not blown off by current.
  4. The wire is fed forward again and the arc re-ignites at low current; the cycle repeats, often more than a hundred times a second.

Because the drop detaches with almost no current flowing, spatter is nearly zero. Because the current is low for most of the cycle, heat input per millimetre is small. The arc stays stable at currents far below normal MIG, so sheets thinner than 1 mm can be joined without burning through.

Uses: thin automotive sheet, aluminium-to-steel joints (the aluminium filler melts and wets the steel without melting it, giving a brazed joint), cladding and repair of worn surfaces, and root passes on pipe. The metal still melts — the name means low heat, not a cold weld.

Worked example

Question. A 2 kW fibre laser welds 1 mm steel sheet. The focused spot diameter is 0.4 mm and the travel speed is 3 m/min. (a) Find the power density at the work in W/cm². (b) Find the heat input in J/mm. (c) Compare this with the 800 J/mm of the MIG weld in chapter 4.4 and say what the difference means for distortion.

  1. Spot radius r = 0.4 / 2 = 0.2 mm = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  2. Spot area A = π r² = π × (2 × 10⁻⁴)² = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  3. Power density q = P / A = 2000 W / 1.2566 × 10⁻⁷ m² = 1.59 × 10¹⁰ W/m².
  4. Convert to cm²: 1 m² = 10⁴ cm², so q = 1.59 × 10¹⁰ / 10⁴ = 1.59 × 10⁶ W/cm². This is in the keyhole range (10⁵ to 10⁷ W/cm²).
  5. Travel speed v = 3 m/min = 3 / 60 = 0.05 m/s.
  6. Heat input H = P / v = 2000 / 0.05 = 40 000 J/m = 40 J/mm.
  7. Ratio = 800 / 40 = 20. The laser puts about twenty times less energy into each millimetre of weld, so the weld cools faster, the heat-affected zone is narrower and distortion is much smaller.

Answer. Power density 1.59 × 10⁶ W/cm² (keyhole mode) and heat input 40 J/mm, about 20 times less than the 800 J/mm MIG weld, hence far less distortion.

Exam traps

  • Cold metal transfer is not a cold weld. The wire and the pool still melt; the name only means the heat input is low.
  • Power density is not total power. A 2 kW laser carries less total power than a 20 kW arc, yet its power density is a thousand times higher.
  • A laser weld does not tolerate a loose fit-up. With no filler wire, a gap becomes a cut instead of a joint.
  • Laser welding needs no vacuum; electron beam welding does. Questions often swap these two.
  • Unit slip: 1 W/cm² = 10⁴ W/m². Divide W/m² by 10⁴ to reach W/cm², not by 10².

4.8 Brazing, soldering and adhesive bonding

Brazing, soldering and adhesive bonding join parts without melting the parent metal. A filler with a lower melting point wets the surfaces and freezes in the gap; in adhesive bonding the filler is a polymer. Soldering and adhesive bonding run cool enough that the parent metal keeps its strength and heat treatment. Brazing runs hotter, so it avoids melting and distortion of the parent, but its temperature can still temper or anneal some alloys; check the parent's tempering temperature before choosing brazing. These processes allow dissimilar materials to join, and give clean joints. You meet brazed pipe fittings and carbide tool tips, soldered circuit boards, and glued aircraft panels and car trim.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the third joining family and Chapter 4.2 for torch heating.

Brazing: filler melts above 450 °C

Brazing joins parts with a filler metal that melts above 450 °C but below the melting point of the parent metal. The parent stays solid. The filler is drawn into a narrow gap by capillary action, the same effect that pulls water up a thin tube. A typical gap is 0.03 mm to 0.2 mm. The joint is designed as a lap, so load is carried in shear over an overlap area, not across a butt line.

h = 2γ cosθ / (ρ g d)

h is the capillary rise (m); γ is the filler surface tension (N/m); θ is the contact angle; ρ is the filler density (kg/m³); g = 9.81 m/s²; d is the gap (m).

Before heating, a flux such as borax or a fluoride mixture is applied. The flux dissolves oxide on the metal and protects the clean surface from air, so the filler can wet it. Heating can be by torch, furnace, induction, dip or resistance. The heat source only raises the joint to the filler flow temperature; it does not melt the parent.

Common fillers are silver-based alloys for strong joints at moderate temperature, and copper-zinc (brass) fillers for steel and cast iron. A brazed joint is usually stronger than a soldered joint and can serve at higher temperature, but it is not as strong as a welded joint of the same parent metal. Brazing is used for pipe fittings, heat exchangers, carbide tool tips, bicycle frames and repair of castings. It suits dissimilar metals because the parent never enters the melt.

Soldering: filler melts below 450 °C

Soldering uses a filler that melts below 450 °C. The classic tin-lead eutectic (63% Sn, 37% Pb) melts at 183 °C; lead-free alloys commonly melt around 217 °C to 230 °C. The parent metal stays solid, so the joint is a thin layer of solder between the parts. Rosin flux removes oxide and helps wetting.

Hand soldering uses an iron; mass production uses wave soldering for through-hole boards and reflow soldering for surface-mount boards, where solder paste is printed and the board is heated. Because the filler melts below 450 °C, the parent keeps its heat treatment and does not warp as in welding. The joint must be mechanically supported because solder is soft and creeps under load. It cannot be used near high temperature: a soldered joint may fail if it gets hot enough to soften. Soldering is standard in electronics because it makes electrical contact and a modest mechanical joint at low temperature, without damaging components.

Adhesive bonding: a polymer layer joins the parts

Adhesive bonding joins parts with a polymer layer. No metal filler melts. The joint holds by mechanisms quoted as mechanical interlocking (adhesive keying into a rough surface), adsorption (molecular attraction at the interface) and diffusion (polymer chains crossing a compatible interface). Surface preparation is decisive: clean, degrease, abrade or etch, and use a primer where specified.

Design the joint for lap shear, so the adhesive layer is loaded along the overlap, not peeled apart. Adhesives join thin sheets without rivet holes, dissimilar metals without galvanic contact, and composites without drilling fibres. Limits are low service temperature, creep under steady load, and ageing from moisture, ultraviolet light and salt. Adhesive bonds need a controlled gap and often a jig until the polymer cures. They are used for car trim, aircraft panels, electronic potting and repair of thin sheets where welding would distort.

The 450 °C line separates brazing from soldering. In all three lower-temperature processes the parent metal is never melted; only the filler or adhesive changes state.

Exam traps

  • Saying brazing or soldering melts the parent metal. Only the filler melts; the parent stays solid.
  • Forgetting the 450 °C line. A filler melting above 450 °C is brazing; below 450 °C is soldering.
  • Using a soldered joint where a brazed joint is needed for load or temperature. Solder is soft and low melting.
  • Setting the brazing gap to zero for maximum contact. Zero gap leaves no room for filler; too large a gap weakens capillary action. Stay near 0.03–0.2 mm.
  • Treating flux as optional on a clean-looking surface. Oxide is thin and invisible; flux is still needed to let the filler wet the metal.

4.9 Welding defects and inspection

A weld defect is any flaw that makes a joint weaker, leaky or unsafe. Most defects come from three things a weld must control: enough heat, a clean surface, and protection of the molten pool from air. Inspection exists to find these defects before the part goes into service. The same logic is used on pipelines, pressure vessels, bicycle frames, bridge girders and the welded seams of a stainless steel kitchen sink.

You need: Chapter 4.4 and Chapter 4.5 for how welding processes make heat and protect the molten pool.

Where defects come from

Three conditions decide whether a weld is sound: enough heat to melt the correct volume, a clean surface, and a shield that keeps air away from the molten pool. Every common defect is a failure of one of these.

Porosity is gas trapped as bubbles. It comes from a damp electrode, oil or rust on the plate, or a shield gas flow that is too low or disturbed by wind. Dry the electrode and clean the joint. Slag inclusion is flux or oxide left between passes. It comes from poor inter-run cleaning or a weaving technique that folds slag into the pool. Chip and brush each pass.

Incomplete fusion means the filler or bead did not melt into the side wall. Incomplete penetration means the weld root did not reach the full joint depth. Both come from current too low, travel too fast, or a joint gap or groove that is wrong. Raise the current and match the gap. Undercut is a groove melted into the parent metal at the toe and not filled. It comes from current too high, an arc too long, or a wrong torch angle.

Overlap is metal that sits on the surface without fusing. Spatter is droplets outside the bead; it does not weaken the joint but costs cleaning time. Burn-through is a hole melted through thin sheet. An arc strike is a small local melt outside the weld; it can start a crack. Distortion and residual stress come from uneven heating and cooling. Use a balanced bead sequence and clamps.

Cracks and residual stress

Cracks are the most dangerous defect because they concentrate stress. Hot cracks form while the metal is solidifying, when low-melting films at grain boundaries cannot carry shrinkage strain. They appear in the centreline of the bead. Remedy: correct filler, lower heat input, and a bead shape that is not too deep and narrow.

Cold cracks, also called hydrogen-induced cracks, appear hours or days after welding. Hydrogen from moisture or grease enters the arc and collects in the steel. With a hard microstructure and residual stress, the hydrogen opens a crack. Remedy: dry electrodes, preheat, low-hydrogen consumables, and post-weld heat treatment to soften the structure and let hydrogen escape.

A crater crack forms at the end of a bead where the arc stops and the pool shrinks. Fill the crater before breaking the arc. Lamellar tearing happens in thick plate: non-metallic inclusions in the through-thickness direction separate when weld shrinkage pulls the plate. Remedy: better plate quality, joint design that avoids through-thickness strain, and preheat. Residual stress stays in the part after welding. Post-weld heat treatment, peening, and a good bead sequence reduce it. A crack found by inspection is never accepted; it is ground out and rewelded.

Inspection methods

Inspection asks two questions: what defect is there, and is it larger than the code allows? Visual inspection comes first. It finds undercut, overlap, spatter, arc strikes, and bad bead shape.

Dye penetrant testing finds surface-breaking flaws. Clean the surface, apply a penetrant, remove the excess, and apply a developer. Penetrant trapped in a crack bleeds out as a coloured line. It works on any non-porous material. Magnetic particle testing finds surface and near-surface flaws in steel. Magnetise the part and dust it with iron powder; flux leakage at a defect holds the powder.

Ultrasonic testing sends a high-frequency sound pulse into the weld. A defect reflects the pulse. The echo time gives the sound path, and the beam angle gives the depth. Radiography passes X-rays or gamma rays through the weld onto a film or detector. Porosity and slag show as darker or lighter patches because they change the thickness of metal. A tight crack parallel to the beam can be missed.

Destructive tests cut a sample from a production weld. The bend test bends a strip over a former; a sound weld stretches without opening. The nick-break test breaks a notched sample and shows the fracture face. Tensile, hardness, and macro-section tests give strength, hardness, and a picture of the weld zones. Acceptance criteria in a code set limits: no cracks, porosity below a size and count, undercut below a depth, and penetration above a minimum.

d = c × t / 2, depth = d × cos θ

d is the sound path length (m); c is the shear wave speed in the material (m/s); t is the echo time (s); θ is the refracted beam angle in the metal (for example 45°, 60° or 70°); depth is the vertical distance below the surface (m); the factor 2 accounts for the pulse travelling down and back. For a 0° straight beam, cos θ = 1 and depth = d.

Worked example

Question. An ultrasonic test on a 20 mm thick butt weld uses a 0° straight-beam shear wave probe. A single echo returns at t = 7.5 µs after the pulse. The shear wave speed in steel is c = 3200 m/s. Find the depth of the reflector. State whether the result lies in the root region.

  1. Write the pulse-echo relation: d = c × t / 2, where d is the sound path, c is the shear wave speed, and t is the echo time. The wave travels down and back, so divide by 2. For a 0° straight beam, depth = d.
  2. Substitute the values: d = 3200 m/s × 7.5 × 10⁻⁶ s / 2.
  3. Multiply: 3200 × 7.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.024 m. This is the total path length.
  4. Divide by 2: d = 0.024 / 2 = 0.012 m.
  5. Convert to millimetres: 0.012 m × 1000 = 12.0 mm.
  6. The weld is 20 mm thick, so 12.0 mm lies just below mid-thickness. A sidewall lack of fusion or a mid-wall slag line is more likely; a root defect would appear near 20 mm depth. Confirm with a second probe angle before naming the defect.

Answer. The reflector is 12.0 mm below the surface, just below mid-thickness, so it is reported as a possible sidewall lack of fusion or mid-wall slag line until another probe angle confirms it. A root defect would appear near 20 mm depth.

Exam traps

  • Incomplete fusion means the bead did not melt into the side wall; incomplete penetration means the root did not reach full depth. They look similar on a macro-section but have different causes and remedies.
  • Radiography is good for porosity and slag because they change the amount of metal in the beam path. It can miss a tight crack that lies parallel to the beam, because the ray path sees almost no thickness change.
  • Hot cracks form during solidification and appear immediately; cold hydrogen cracks can appear hours or days later. A weld that passed inspection on day one is not automatically safe on day three.
  • A µs is 10⁻⁶ s. Forgetting the factor 2 (the down-and-back path) doubles the depth: 3200 × 7.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.024 m = 24 mm instead of 12 mm, which would place the defect outside a 20 mm plate and hide the real defect location.
  • A crack is never accepted by a code, even if it is small. It is ground out to sound metal and rewelded, then inspected again.

The formation of one continuous body from two contacting surfaces by atomic or molecular attraction.
Cold metal transfer
A controlled MIG process with short-circuit transfer and wire retraction, giving low heat input.
Duty cycle
The percentage of a 10-minute period for which a power source can carry its rated current without overheating.
Fillet weld
A triangular weld placed in the corner between two surfaces at an angle.
Friction stir welding
A solid-state process in which a rotating tool stirs plasticised metal along a seam without melting it.
Fusion welding
Welding in which the parent metal melts at the joint and solidifies to form the weld.
Fusion zone
The region of parent metal that melts during welding and solidifies as part of the weld.
Heat-affected zone
The parent metal near the weld that does not melt but whose properties change because of the heat.
Lack of fusion
A weld defect in which the weld metal fails to fuse to the parent metal or to a previous pass.
Lap joint
A joint in which one member overlaps another and is welded at the edge.
Neutral flame
An oxy-acetylene flame with oxygen and acetylene near a 1:1 ratio.
Oxidizing flame
An oxy-acetylene flame with excess oxygen, giving an oxidising atmosphere.
Penetrant testing
A surface inspection method in which a liquid dye enters surface-breaking defects and is then revealed.
Porosity
A weld defect consisting of gas pockets trapped in the solidified weld metal.
Radiography
Internal inspection using X-rays or gamma rays, where density differences show defects on a detector.
Resistance spot welding
A resistance welding process that makes a local molten nugget between overlapping sheets held under electrode force.
Soldering
Joining with a filler metal that melts below 450 °C and wets the parent surfaces without melting them.
Solid-state welding
Welding in which the parent metal remains solid and coalescence occurs by pressure, heat and diffusion.
T-joint
A joint in which one member meets another at approximately 90° to form a T shape.
Welding
A joining process that produces coalescence of materials by heating, pressure or both, with or without filler.
