7.1 Development, classification and benefits of additive manufacturing

Additive manufacturing (AM) builds a solid part from a 3D model by adding material layer by layer. It exists because it removes part-specific moulds, dies and fixtures, so one machine can make different shapes by changing the CAD file. It is used for prototypes, jigs, custom implants and small batches of end-use parts. Some syllabi call the whole group 3D printing; that is an umbrella term, not one process. The standard classification below is by how each layer is formed.

You need: Unit 3 basics of plastics and a general manufacturing overview; the process chain is in Chapter 7.2.

From models to functional parts

Additive manufacturing (AM) makes a part by adding material in thin layers. A 3D model is sliced into cross-sections. The machine builds each cross-section and bonds it to the previous one. Machining removes material; casting and forming need a mould or die. AM needs no part-specific tooling, so a new shape needs only a new CAD file.

In the 1980s, layer-by-layer machines made polymer models for shape checking. That use was called rapid prototyping (RP). Better lasers, powders and controls later allowed functional polymer, metal and ceramic parts. The broader term AM covers models, tools and end-use parts. Some syllabi call the whole group 3D printing. The CAD-to-part chain is in Chapter 7.2.

AM is classified by how each layer is formed, not only by material. This matters because the same material can be printed by more than one family. The next section lists the seven standard families.

Seven standard process families

The seven families differ in the state of the material and the energy source. Vat photopolymerisation cures a liquid resin with light. Material extrusion pushes a melted thermoplastic filament through a nozzle. Powder bed fusion fuses selected powder with a laser or electron beam. Material jetting deposits liquid droplets from printheads. Binder jetting deposits a binder into a powder bed. Sheet lamination bonds and cuts thin sheets. Directed energy deposition feeds powder or wire into a laser melt pool. A material such as nylon can appear in more than one family.

Common examples: stereolithography (SLA), fused deposition modelling (FDM), selective laser sintering (SLS), selective laser melting (SLM), multi-jet modelling (MJM), laminated object manufacturing (LOM) and laser engineered net shaping (LENS).

Benefits, limits and batch choice

AM pays off when tooling would dominate the cost. A mould, die or fixture is needed for every design change in conventional routes. AM uses the same machine for many shapes. This gives four benefits.

  • No part-specific tooling: one-offs and small batches avoid mould cost.
  • Complex internal channels: curved cooling channels and lattice shapes can be built inside a part.
  • Low-volume production: spare parts can be made on demand instead of stored.
  • Mass customisation: each part can have its own CAD file, as in dental aligners.

The limits are equally real. Build speed is low, and the machine envelope limits part size. Surface finish is often rough, and support or powder removal adds labour. Material cost is high and the choice is narrower than conventional grades. Anisotropy means properties differ along the build direction because layers bond; a test bar in one direction may not represent another. For a small batch, compare total cost, not only machine price. Rapid tooling and end-use AM are in Chapter 7.9.

Worked example

Question. A bracket needs a small batch. CNC setup costs ₹15,000 and each part costs ₹2,000; AM setup costs ₹2,000 and each part costs ₹3,500. Find the break-even batch size and which route is cheaper for 5 parts.

  1. Let n be the batch size in parts. CNC cost is CC = 15,000 + 2,000n rupees. AM cost is CA = 2,000 + 3,500n rupees.
  2. At break-even, CC = CA: 15,000 + 2,000n = 2,000 + 3,500n.
  3. Move terms: 15,000 − 2,000 = 3,500n − 2,000n, so 13,000 = 1,500n.
  4. n = 13,000 ÷ 1,500 = 8.666..., which rounds to 8.67 parts. Since parts are whole, AM is cheaper up to 8 parts and CNC is cheaper from 9 parts.
  5. For n = 5: CA = 2,000 + 3,500 × 5 = 2,000 + 17,500 = ₹19,500.
  6. For n = 5: CC = 15,000 + 2,000 × 5 = 15,000 + 10,000 = ₹25,000.
  7. Difference = ₹25,000 − ₹19,500 = ₹5,500; AM is cheaper.

Answer. The break-even batch size is about 8.67 parts; for 5 parts AM costs ₹19,500 against CNC ₹25,000, so AM is cheaper by ₹5,500.

Exam traps

  • Do not classify AM only by material. The standard families are by layer-forming mechanism; nylon, for example, can be used in powder bed fusion and material extrusion.
  • 3D printing is an umbrella term, not one process. Do not use it as a synonym for FDM alone.
  • Rapid prototyping is not the same as all AM. RP is the early model-making use; AM also covers tools and end-use parts.
  • AM is not automatically cheaper. Setup is low but per-part cost is high; check the break-even batch size, and include support removal and post-processing.
  • Do not ignore anisotropy. Properties differ along the build direction, so one test direction does not certify the part.

7.2 Rapid prototyping and the AM process chain

Rapid prototyping makes a physical model from a CAD design quickly, so a designer can hold a part, check its shape, fit and function, and correct errors before costly tooling. The model is not machined or cast from a drawing; it is built layer by layer from a digital file. The fixed route goes from CAD solid to STL mesh, build orientation, supports, sliced layers, layer build and post-processing. Industry uses it for new product checks, custom medical guides, pump housings and consumer product prototypes.

You need Chapter 7.1 and basic CAD drawing.

Why rapid prototyping exists

Market pressure pushes a product through many design changes before a production line is ordered. A drawing or screen view hides clashes, weak corners and awkward assembly. A rapid prototype is a physical model made directly from the CAD data, usually in hours or a few days, so the team can check three things: form (does it look and measure right), fit (does it mate with the next part), and function (does it work in a simple test).

Early AM was used mainly for such models, hence the name rapid prototyping. The same chain now makes tooling inserts and end-use parts, covered in Chapter 7.9. The value is shorter design iteration: one physical check can expose an error that several drawings miss.

A Chennai appliance maker may print a fan housing to test airflow and screw bosses before cutting a mould. If a boss is 2 mm too short, the change is made in CAD and a new model is printed; no mould is scrapped. The prototype need not use final material. It must answer the question asked of it: shape, fit, or a short functional trial.

The AM process chain

The generic chain converts a CAD solid into machine instructions and then a part. Some syllabi call this the AM process chain.

  1. CAD model: a closed solid or a well-defined surface model, saved in the design unit, usually millimetres.
  2. STL file: the surface is approximated by triangles; each triangle has three vertices and a normal that gives its facing direction.
  3. Orient and support: choose the build direction, place supports under overhangs (faces that lean over empty space) and decide the build plate position.
  4. Slice: the software cuts the supported model into thin horizontal layers and creates tool paths.
  5. Build: the machine adds material layer by layer; the path depends on the process.
  6. Post-process: remove the part, take off supports, clean, cure, machine or paint as needed.

The order is fixed because each step depends on the previous one. Slicing cannot start until orientation and supports are fixed; the machine cannot build until slices exist. The build direction also decides which faces need support and which faces touch the build plate. The layer-building physics belongs to Chapter 7.3 onwards.

STL, orientation and layer thickness

STL is a surface triangle mesh, not a solid model of tiny volume cells. A flat face may need two triangles; a curved face may need hundreds. More triangles give a smoother curve but a larger file. Common STL errors are holes, flipped normals, zero-area triangles and several disconnected shells. The file has no units; the designer and the machine operator must agree whether 1 means 1 mm or 1 inch. A 40 mm part exported in inches can arrive as about 1016 mm.

Build orientation changes build time, support volume and surface finish. A tall thin part may need many supports; laid flat it may need fewer supports but a poorer face on the build plate side. Round holes may become slightly oval if the layer plane cuts them at an angle.

N = H ÷ t

N is the number of layers; H is the part height in mm along the build direction; t is the layer thickness in mm. Both H and t must use the same unit.

Thin layers, say 0.1 mm, give a smoother curved surface and finer detail but double the layer count compared with 0.2 mm. More layers mean longer build time and more chances for a small error. Thick layers are faster but show visible stair steps on sloping faces.

Worked example

Question. A cylindrical test model is 40 mm tall. It is sliced at a layer thickness of 0.2 mm. (a) Find the number of layers. (b) The designer changes the layer thickness to 0.1 mm. Find the new number of layers.

  1. Height H = 40 mm. First layer thickness t₁ = 0.2 mm.
  2. Use N = H ÷ t. N₁ = 40 mm ÷ 0.2 mm = 200. The unit mm cancels.
  3. New layer thickness t₂ = 0.1 mm.
  4. N₂ = 40 mm ÷ 0.1 mm = 400.
  5. Check: halving the layer thickness doubles the layer count: 200 × 2 = 400.

Answer. At 0.2 mm the model has 200 layers; at 0.1 mm it has 400 layers.

Exam traps

  • STL is a surface triangle mesh, not a solid model of tiny volume cells. Do not say STL stores material inside the part.
  • Layer count is height ÷ layer thickness, not volume ÷ layer volume. Mixing the two gives a meaningless number.
  • Halving layer thickness doubles layer count for the same height; it does not halve build time.
  • Build orientation is chosen before slicing. A support-free orientation may still be rejected because a critical face becomes rough.
  • Removing supports and post-curing are part of the AM chain; the part is not finished when the machine stops.

7.3 Stereolithography

Stereolithography is an additive process that builds a part from a liquid photopolymer. A UV laser or projector draws one cross-section on the resin surface. Where the light falls, the resin cures into a thin solid layer. The platform then moves down by one layer thickness, a blade spreads fresh resin, and the next cross-section is drawn. Supports hold overhangs. After building, the part is cleaned and post-cured. It is used for detailed prototypes, dental models, jewellery patterns and transparent parts. Some syllabi call the machine stereolithography apparatus, SLA.

You need: the AM process chain from Chapter 7.2 and the basic idea that a photopolymer liquid hardens when UV light strikes it.

How the vat process works

Stereolithography is a vat photopolymerisation process. A vat is a tank of liquid photopolymer: a resin made of monomers, oligomers and a photoinitiator. The photoinitiator absorbs UV light and starts a chain reaction that turns the liquid into a solid polymer network. The build platform starts just below the resin surface, leaving a thin gap. A UV laser is focused to a small spot and steered by a pair of small motor-driven mirrors, called galvanometer mirrors. The mirrors move the beam along the cross-section of one layer. Only the scanned area cures; the rest stays liquid. The first layer sticks to the platform. The platform then moves down by one layer thickness and a recoating blade, also called a wiper, spreads a fresh film of resin over the cured layer. The next cross-section is scanned and bonds to the previous layer. This repeats until the part is complete.

A projector-based machine, often called digital light processing, flashes a whole layer at once through a mask. The layer-by-layer logic is the same. The cured part is lifted off, drained, washed in solvent, supports are removed, and it is post-cured under strong UV to finish the polymer network.

  1. Lower the platform to leave a thin resin gap above the previous layer.
  2. Scan the layer cross-section with the UV beam, or project the full layer image.
  3. Let the exposed resin cure and bond to the layer below.
  4. Move the platform down by one layer thickness.
  5. Spread fresh resin with the recoating blade.
  6. Repeat, then drain, clean, remove supports and post-cure.

Machine settings, resins and uses

The main machine parts are the UV laser or projector, the galvanometer scan head, the focusing lens, the resin vat, the build platform, the recoating blade and the control software. The scan head moves the laser spot quickly, so the beam can trace fine borders and hatch lines. Hatch spacing is the distance between neighbouring scan lines; it must be small enough that the cured lines join. The spot size and layer thickness set the resolution, the smallest detail the machine can hold. Thin layers give smoother surfaces and finer detail but take longer because there are more layers.

N = H ÷ t

N is the number of layers, H is the part height in mm, and t is the layer thickness in mm.

  • Standard resin: rigid and clear; for visual prototypes.
  • Tough or ABS-like resin: better impact strength; for snap fits and casings.
  • Flexible resin: rubber-like; for gaskets and grips.
  • Castable resin: burns out cleanly; for jewellery and investment casting patterns.
  • Dental and medical resins: tooth models, surgical guides and hearing-aid shells.

SLA is not selective laser sintering. SLS uses a powder bed and a laser that sinters thermoplastic powder; SLA uses a liquid resin that cures by chemical reaction. Do not write one for the other in an exam; see Chapter 7.5 for SLS. SLA parts are accurate and smooth, but they are weaker than moulded or metal parts and can creep under load. Hollow parts need drain holes.

Accuracy, supports and after-treatment

Supports are thin pillars built from the platform or from the part up to an overhang. They carry the overhang while it is still soft and stop it bending. Supports are removed after building, so they leave small marks. The part is drained, washed in solvent such as isopropyl alcohol to remove uncured resin, and post-cured under UV. Post-cure raises strength and stability but can also cause slight shrinkage. Designers allow for this by testing a small coupon first.

Accuracy is affected by spot size, layer thickness, resin shrinkage, and the depth of cure. If the laser gives too much energy, the cured line becomes wider than the scan path, a condition called overcure; it can fill fine gaps. Too little energy leaves tacky, weak layers. Build orientation matters: a flat face parallel to the platform is smooth, but a steep overhang needs more supports.

SLA is used for form-and-fit models, master patterns for investment casting, dental aligners and surgical guides, jewellery, transparent fluid-flow models, and small production parts when the resin suits the duty.

Worked example

Question. A stereolithography part is 30 mm tall and is built with layers 0.1 mm thick. The hatch length, the total scan path in one layer, is 4,000 mm. The laser scan speed is 500 mm/s. Find the number of layers, the scan time for one layer, the total scan time, and the scan time in minutes. Ignore recoating and post-cure in the scan time.

  1. Number of layers: N = H / t = 30 mm / 0.1 mm = 300 layers.
  2. Scan time for one layer: tlayer = L / v = 4,000 mm / 500 mm/s = 8 s.
  3. Total scan time: Tscan = N × tlayer = 300 × 8 s = 2,400 s.
  4. Convert to minutes: Tscan = 2,400 s / 60 s/min = 40 min.

Answer. The part has 300 layers, each layer takes 8 s, and the total scan time is 2,400 s, which is 40 min; the real build also needs recoating and post-cure time.

Exam traps

  • SLA uses a liquid photopolymer, not a thermoplastic filament; the laser cures only the scanned area, and overhangs need supports.
  • SLA is vat photopolymerisation; SLS is powder bed fusion. SLS sinters powder, SLA cures resin. Mixing the names loses marks.
  • The layer time is hatch length divided by scan speed, not layer thickness divided by scan speed. Keep millimetres with millimetres and seconds with seconds.
  • Post-cure is a separate UV step after cleaning; it is not part of the layer scan time. Recoating, lifting and draining also add to the total build time.
  • Projector or DLP stereolithography cures a full layer image at once, but it is still a layer-by-layer vat process, not material jetting.

7.4 Fused deposition modelling

Fused deposition modelling (FDM) is a material extrusion additive process. A thermoplastic filament is pushed by drive rollers into a heated nozzle, melted, and laid as a thin road along a computer-controlled path. The bed lowers by one layer height and the next raster is deposited, so the part grows as a stack of layers. It exists because the machine is simple, clean and low-cost. It is used for prototypes, jigs, fixtures, teaching models and some end-use plastic parts. The standard family name is material extrusion; FDM is a trademark name.

You need: Chapter 7.2 for the STL-to-part chain, and Unit 3, Shaping of plastics: injection moulding and extrusion for polymer flow.

The material extrusion idea

Material extrusion makes a part by forcing a melted polymer through a small nozzle. The feedstock is a solid filament, usually 1.75 mm or 2.85 mm diameter, wound on a spool. Two drive rollers grip the filament and push it. The nozzle is heated, so the polymer becomes a viscous melt. The nozzle moves in x and y over a build platform, called the bed. The bed moves down in z after each layer. The extruded road is called a raster. A layer is a set of parallel rasters, often at an angle to the previous layer.

  • Common filaments are PLA (polylactic acid), ABS (acrylonitrile butadiene styrene), PETG (polyethylene terephthalate glycol), nylon and PEEK (polyether ether ketone).
  • PLA is easy to print and low-warp. ABS and PETG are tougher. Nylon is wear-resistant. PEEK is high-temperature and needs a hot nozzle and a heated chamber.

From filament to layered part

  1. Load the filament, set nozzle and bed temperatures for the polymer, and home the axes.
  2. Drive rollers feed filament into the heater; the melt leaves the nozzle as a road.
  3. The slicer sets layer height h (mm), raster angle (degrees), infill (%) and support.
  4. The nozzle traces the raster for one layer; the bed lowers by h; the next layer is deposited.
  5. Remove support and post-process: trim, sand, anneal or vapour-smooth if needed.
  • Layer height h: vertical thickness of one layer, in mm.
  • Raster angle: direction of roads in a layer, in degrees from the x axis. Alternating 45° and 135° is common.
  • Infill: internal fill fraction, from 0% hollow to 100% solid.
  • Support: extra material under overhangs, removed after printing.

Bonds between roads and between layers form by cooling and polymer diffusion, not by melting the whole part. Strength is lower along z, so the part is anisotropic. Raster angle, layer height and nozzle temperature change strength. Nozzle diameter sets road width; layer height sets vertical step. They are not the same number.

Build time and process settings

Build time is estimated from the path length, not the solid volume. For a part with N layers, let L be the extruder path length in one layer in mm, and v the print speed in mm/s. Then T = N L / v. T is build time in s. This ignores travel between rasters, acceleration, layer changes and support. If support is used, add its path length to L. Layer height h sets vertical growth: total height = N h. A smaller h gives smoother walls but needs more layers, so build time rises. Infill changes L: 20% infill has a shorter path than 100% infill, but the outer walls and top layers still use solid roads. Print speed is limited by melt flow: too fast and the filament slips or the road is thin.

T = N L / v

T is build time in s; N is number of layers; L is extruder path length in one layer in mm; v is print speed in mm/s. Add support path to L. Total height = N h, with h the layer height in mm.

Worked example

Question. A FDM part has 100 layers, each of height 0.2 mm. The extruder path in each layer is 250 mm long and the print speed is 50 mm/s. Find the build time, ignoring travel, acceleration and layer-change time. Give the answer in seconds and minutes.

  1. Number of layers N = 100. Layer height h = 0.2 mm. Total height = N h = 100 × 0.2 = 20 mm.
  2. Extruder path in one layer L = 250 mm.
  3. Print speed v = 50 mm/s.
  4. Time for one layer t1 = L / v = 250 / 50 = 5 s.
  5. Total build time T = N t1 = 100 × 5 = 500 s.
  6. Convert to minutes: 500 / 60 = 8.3333 min.

Answer. The build time is 500 s, that is 8.3333 min.

Exam traps

  • FDM is material extrusion, not sintering or curing. There is no laser, powder bed or resin vat.
  • Layer height is not nozzle diameter. Nozzle diameter sets road width; layer height sets the vertical step.
  • Part strength depends on raster angle and layer bonding. Do not use bulk polymer strength for a printed part.
  • The build-time formula T = N L / v ignores travel, acceleration, support and layer changes. Exams often add these.
  • Support is not infill. Support holds overhangs and is removed; infill stays inside the part.

7.5 Powder bed fusion and selective laser sintering

Powder bed fusion (PBF) is an additive manufacturing family that spreads a thin layer of powder and fuses only the chosen cross-section with a laser or electron beam. Unfused powder stays in place and supports the next layer, so overhangs need no separate support structure. SLS fuses polymer powder by partial melting; SLM and DMLS fully melt metal powder. PBF makes complex brackets, dental crowns, implants and small production batches where tooling cost would dominate. This chapter follows the layer cycle and the main process variants.

You need: Chapter 7.2 for the AM process chain, and Unit 2, Powder metallurgy sintering, for particle bonding.

The layer cycle

A powder bed fusion machine has a powder supply, a recoater blade or roller, a build platform and a focused energy beam. The platform starts below the powder bed top. The recoater spreads a fresh blanket of powder. The beam scans the CAD slice. Scanned powder fuses; loose powder stays around the part.

  1. Lower the build platform by one layer thickness, for example 0.05 mm.
  2. Raise powder from the supply or push it from a hopper.
  3. Spread powder with the recoater at constant speed.
  4. Scan the cross-section with the laser or electron beam.
  5. Fuse particles by sintering or melting.
  6. Lower the platform and repeat until all slices are built.
  7. Remove the part and recycle or discard loose powder.

SLS, SLM, DMLS and EBM

SLS uses a laser to fuse polymer powder by partial melting. Particle surfaces soften and bond; the centre may stay solid. SLM and DMLS use a laser to fully melt metal powder into dense parts. EBM uses an electron beam in vacuum and a hot powder bed. All are powder bed fusion. Some syllabi call the laser metal version laser powder bed fusion. Laser machines use argon or nitrogen; EBM uses vacuum. The same CAD slice drives every variant; only the energy source and powder change.

  • SLS: nylon and other polymers; good for brackets, ducts and visual models.
  • SLM/DMLS: steel, aluminium, titanium and cobalt alloys; good for dense functional metal parts.
  • EBM: titanium and other reactive metals; hot build reduces residual stress but needs vacuum.
  • Unfused powder supports overhangs in SLS; metal parts still need anchors for heat flow and warping.

Powder, stress and finishing

Powder quality controls part quality. Particles are tens of micrometres across. Moisture, oxygen and contamination change flow and fusion. Used powder is sieved and blended with fresh powder after checks. Reactive powders such as titanium and aluminium need inert gas and careful housekeeping because fine powder can burn or explode. Laser PBF builds in an argon or nitrogen atmosphere; EBM builds in vacuum. After the build, an unpacking station removes loose powder safely. Metal parts may need CNC machining on mating faces because as-built surfaces are rough. The build direction sets surface finish and anisotropy. Oxygen pick-up lowers ductility. A sieve check and a flow test are routine. Fine powder also needs grounded equipment.

  • Thermal stress: steep heating and cooling cause warping and cracks; use supports, preheat and scan strategy.
  • Post-processing: depowder, stress relief, support removal, machining, polishing or heat treatment.
  • Cost: powder handling and post-processing often exceed scan time in a real production plan.

Worked example

Question. A PBF part has layer area 5,000 mm², hatch spacing 0.1 mm, scan speed 800 mm/s, layer thickness 0.05 mm and part height 20 mm. Estimate the laser scan time, ignoring recoating and beam jumps.

  1. Scan length per layer: L = A / h = 5,000 / 0.1 = 50,000 mm.
  2. Time per layer: t = L / v = 50,000 / 800 = 62.5 s.
  3. Number of layers: N = H / z = 20 / 0.05 = 400.
  4. Total scan time: T = N × t = 400 × 62.5 = 25,000 s.
  5. In hours: T = 25,000 / 3,600 = 6.94 h, about 6.94 h.

Answer. The estimated laser scan time is 25,000 s, about 6.94 h; real build time is higher because recoating adds time.

Exam traps

  • Unfused powder supports overhangs in SLS. In SLM, metal overhangs still need anchors because heat must flow away and warping must be controlled.
  • SLS is not SLM. SLS sinters polymer powder by partial melting; SLM fully melts metal powder.
  • Use hatch spacing in the scan-length formula, not layer thickness. Area divided by hatch spacing gives scan length; layer thickness gives the number of layers.
  • Powder bed fusion is not binder jetting. Binder jetting glues powder with a liquid binder and does not use a laser or electron beam to fuse each slice.
  • EBM uses an electron beam in vacuum, not a laser. Do not label it as a laser process.

7.6 Laser engineered net shaping (LENS)

Laser engineered net shaping (LENS) builds metal by melting powder that is flying through the air, not powder lying in a bed. A laser makes a small melt pool on a solid metal substrate; a nozzle blows alloy powder into that same pool. The pool freezes behind the moving beam as a track, and track over track makes a layer. LENS is the best-known member of directed energy deposition (DED). Shops use it to repair worn shafts and turbine parts, to clad a cheap base with a hard surface, and to build near-net metal shapes that are later machined.

You need: the powder bed picture in Chapter 7.5 and the laser melt pool of laser welding in Unit 4, Laser welding.

A melt pool fed by blown powder

A focused laser beam strikes a solid metal surface and forms a small molten pool, a few millimetres across. Metal powder is blown down a nozzle into that same pool. The powder grains melt as they arrive, the pool grows, and the motion system drags the pool along the surface. The liquid metal left behind freezes into a track.

Nothing sits in a bed. A powder grain exists only for the fraction of a second between the nozzle and the pool. Grains that miss the pool fly past and are collected for reuse, so the powder you feed is never the powder you deposit.

LENS belongs to a family called directed energy deposition (DED): energy and material are delivered to the same point at the same time. Some syllabi use the names laser cladding, laser metal deposition or direct metal deposition for the same family. LENS is a trade name that entered textbooks. All of them share one physics: a moving melt pool fed with blown powder or wire.

V̇ = η × ṁ ÷ ρ

V̇ is deposited volume per hour (cm³/h); ṁ is the powder mass feed rate (g/h); ρ is the alloy density (g/cm³); η is the catchment efficiency, the fraction of blown powder that actually enters the pool (a number below 1, set by nozzle alignment and beam focus).

An inert gas such as argon does two jobs: it carries the powder out of the nozzle and it shields the hot metal from oxygen. Titanium and nickel alloys react badly with air at these temperatures, so the shield gas is not optional.

Running a LENS build

  1. Clamp the substrate, or the worn part being repaired, flat under the head and set the nozzle height.
  2. Set laser power, spot diameter, scan speed, powder feed rate and shield gas flow.
  3. Deposit the first track slowly; the pool must wet the substrate and bond to it.
  4. Step sideways by the hatch spacing, about half the track width, and run the next track in the reverse direction.
  5. On finishing a layer, raise the head by one layer height and repeat the scan pattern.
  6. Cool, unclamp, stress relieve, then machine the rough surface to final size.

Typical numbers: laser power from a few hundred watts to a few kilowatts, track width about 0.5 mm to 3 mm, layer height about 0.2 mm to 1 mm, powder grains a few tens of micrometres across. Small spot and low powder rate give a finer track but a slower build.

The deposit is rough and slightly oversized, so every functional surface is machined afterwards. Residual stresses build up because each new track shrinks as it cools against metal that is already solid.

Repair, cladding and near-net shapes

  • Repair: a worn shaft, a damaged turbine blade root or a cracked die is built back up only where metal is missing, then machined to size. The part itself is the substrate.
  • Cladding: a hard or corrosion-resistant alloy such as a cobalt or nickel alloy is laid on a cheaper base, for valve seats, pump sleeves and shear blades.
  • Near-net preforms: a bulky metal shape is deposited close to final size and finished by machining, saving the material lost in cutting from solid.
  • Materials: carbon and stainless steels, tool steels, titanium alloys such as Ti-6Al-4V, and nickel superalloys such as Inconel 625 and 718.

A hybrid machine mounts the LENS head on a CNC milling spindle, so one setup deposits metal and then mills it flat. This keeps a dimension under control at every layer and removes the rough skin before the next layer goes down.

LENS loses to powder bed fusion where fine detail matters. It cannot build small internal cooling channels, thin lattices or sharp fine features, because the pool is about a millimetre wide and the powder spray spreads wider than that. Its strengths are the opposite ones: large parts, high deposition rate, a substrate that can be an existing component, and no powder bed to fill or clean.

Worked example

Question. A worn shaft of diameter 50 mm and length 100 mm must be built up by 0.5 mm on the radius over its full length using LENS. The machine deposits metal at a volume rate of 20 cm³/h. Find the volume of metal needed and the deposition time in hours and minutes.

  1. Initial radius r₁ = d/2 = 50/2 = 25 mm. Final radius r₂ = 25 + 0.5 = 25.5 mm.
  2. Volume of the built-up sleeve: V = π(r₂² − r₁²) × L.
  3. r₂² = 25.5² = 650.25 mm²; r₁² = 25² = 625 mm²; difference = 25.25 mm².
  4. V = π × 25.25 × 100 = 7,932.5 mm³ ≈ 7,933 mm³.
  5. Convert to cm³: V = 7,933 / 1,000 = 7.93 cm³.
  6. Time t = V ÷ V̇ = 7.93 cm³ ÷ 20 cm³/h = 0.397 h.
  7. In minutes: t = 0.397 × 60 = 23.8 min.

Answer. Volume to deposit is 7.93 cm³ and the deposition time is 0.397 h, that is about 23.8 minutes.

Exam traps

  • Calling LENS a powder bed process. There is no bed, no recoater and no surrounding powder; the powder is blown into the pool and the rest flies past.
  • Forgetting the substrate. The first track must wet and bond to solid metal, so LENS always needs something to build on, unlike a bed process that starts on a plate under loose powder.
  • Mixing radial and diametral build-up. A 0.5 mm radial build adds 1 mm to the diameter, so the radius, not the diameter, goes into r₂.
  • Using feed rate as deposition rate. Powder fed × catchment efficiency is what lands, so the deposited volume rate is always smaller than ṁ ÷ ρ.
  • Expecting fine internal channels. The pool is about a millimetre wide; small channels and lattices belong to powder bed fusion.

7.7 Laminated object manufacturing

Laminated object manufacturing (LOM) glues thin sheets one on another and cuts each sheet to the cross-section shape. A heated roller bonds the sheet; a laser or blade cuts the outline; the platform drops by one sheet thickness. Sheet is cheap and the leftover block holds the part, so large models need no extra support. It is used for big concept models, casting patterns and paper or plastic prototypes.

You need: Chapter 7.2 for the CAD-to-part chain and slicing, and Unit 3, Sheet Metal and Plastics Processing, for handling thin sheets.

The sheet and the machine

LOM is a sheet lamination process. The raw material is a thin sheet, not powder, filament or liquid. The sheet is bonded to the layer below, cut to the outline, and a fresh sheet is laid on top. The part is a stack of glued sheets. Sheet thickness t is fixed by the roll, usually 0.05 mm to 0.2 mm; 0.1 mm paper is common.

The machine has a supply roll for fresh sheet, a take-up roll for spent sheet, a heated roller that presses the sheet and activates adhesive, and a cutting head (laser or blade) that traces the cross-section. The platform drops one sheet thickness after each layer so the stack top stays at the cutter focus.

Most LOM sheet is paper coated on one side with heat-activated adhesive, so no glue is spread at the machine. Plastic sheet, metal foil and ceramic green tape are also used. Sheet is cheap per square metre, making LOM a low-cost route for large parts. No separate support is needed: unused sheet stays in place and holds every overhang.

One layer at a time

  1. The CAD model is sliced into layers of thickness t, equal to the sheet thickness.
  2. The platform sits at the top of its stroke. The supply roll feeds one sheet over the previous layer.
  3. The heated roller presses down. Heat softens the adhesive and the new sheet bonds to the layer below.
  4. The cutting head traces the outline of this layer's cross-section. On paper machines the laser also cuts a grid outside the part.
  5. The platform moves down by t, bringing the stack top back to the cutter's focal plane.
  6. The take-up roll winds the spent sheet and the next sheet is fed. Steps 2 to 5 repeat for all n layers.
  7. The block is taken out, the grid-cut waste is broken away, and the part is sanded, sealed or painted.

T = n × (tb + tc)

T is the build time in s; n is the number of layers; tb is the time to feed and bond one sheet in s; tc is the time to cut one layer in s.

Bonding is a whole-sheet roller pass, not point by point. So a tall part with a large footprint builds fast; LOM suits big block-like models more than small detailed ones.

Waste removal, accuracy and limits

Outside the part outline the laser cuts a square grid, the cross-hatch, breaking the surrounding block into small cubes. After the build the operator lifts out the block and snaps the cubes off by hand or knife: de-cubing. The part leaves with stepped walls and slightly frayed edges, so sanding, sealing or painting follows. The excess is the same sheet cut loose, not a separate support.

In-plane accuracy (X–Y outline) depends on beam or blade position and is about ±0.1 mm to ±0.2 mm. Z depends on the sheet: each layer is one sheet plus one glue line, so vertical faces arrive in steps of t. A 0.1 mm sheet gives 0.1 mm steps. Thinner sheet gives smoother walls but more layers and build time.

  • Advantages: cheap sheet material, large parts, no separate supports, low shrinkage because the sheet is not melted, and no chemical post-curing; post-processing is simple in tools but labour-heavy because de-cubing is done by hand.
  • Limits: one sheet material per part, visible Z steps, delamination if paper is damp or the roller is cold, fragile thin walls, and waste trapped inside closed hollow shapes.

7.8 Material jetting, multi-jet modelling and binder jetting

Two additive manufacturing families use a printhead. Material jetting fires droplets of liquid photopolymer or molten wax and cures them with ultraviolet light, like an inkjet printer. Binder jetting fires a liquid binder into a powder bed, gluing one cross-section at a time. Neither uses a laser or filament nozzle. They make smooth visual models, colour prototypes, wax casting patterns, and, in binder jetting, metal or foundry-sand parts finished in a furnace. The two mechanisms differ from SLS and SLA.

You need: AM classification in Chapter 7.1, photopolymer curing from Chapter 7.3, and the powder bed idea from Chapter 7.5.

Material jetting: droplets cured by UV light

A material jetting printer has a printhead with fine nozzles, like a home inkjet head. The head sweeps over the build platform and fires photopolymer droplets only where the slice needs material. A UV lamp beside the head travels the same track and cures the droplets at once, so one sweep leaves a solid line of plastic.

Layers are thin, usually 15 to 30 micrometres, because one droplet is a few tens of micrometres wide. Thin layers give smooth walls and sharp detail: a gear tooth or thin rib prints clean, with less stair-stepping than thick FDM layers.

Overhangs need support. The same head jets a soft gel where the part needs a scaffold. After the build the operator removes the part and washes the gel away with a water jet. A heated head can jet molten wax; that wax pattern goes into investment casting.

  1. Slice the CAD model into layers 15 to 30 micrometres thick.
  2. The platform starts empty; the head does the work.
  3. Sweep the head along length L, firing droplets on slice pixels and gel on support pixels.
  4. Pass the UV lamp over the same track to cure the layer.
  5. Lower the platform one layer thickness and repeat.
  6. Remove the part, wash away support gel, post-cure if needed.

Multi-jet modelling (MJM) and build time

Multi-jet modelling (MJM, also called PolyJet) is material jetting with many jets in one head. A block of nozzles covers a wide strip, so a full layer needs few sweeps. Two or more photopolymer resins load at once, and the slicing software chooses which jet fires at each point.

Mixing two resins in different ratios on one layer gives digital materials: a rigid resin blended with a rubber-like one gives intermediate stiffness in one part. Some machines add cyan, magenta, yellow and black resins, so a coloured model comes from one build.

MJM is not FDM. FDM melts a thermoplastic filament and drags it from a nozzle; MJM throws liquid droplets and cures them with light. MJM parts are photopolymer, so they are brittle, soften in strong sunlight and are not load-bearing.

t_total = (H / h) × (W / w) × (L / v)

t_total is print time in seconds; H is part height in mm; h is layer thickness in mm; W is part width across head travel in mm; w is printhead width in mm; L is sweep length in mm; v is print speed in mm/s. Powder spreading and head return are ignored; the same arithmetic applies to binder jetting.

Binder jetting: glue inside a powder bed

Binder jetting reverses the idea. The build box is filled with powder: polymer, ceramic, metal or foundry sand. A roller spreads a flat layer 0.08 to 0.2 mm deep. A printhead jets liquid binder, a glue, only on the cross-section pixels. The powder wets, the binder sets, and the layer bonds in the shape of the slice. The piston drops the bed one layer thickness, fresh powder spreads, and the cycle repeats.

At the end the box holds a cake of loose powder with the part buried inside. The operator digs it out and blows loose powder away. What remains is a green part: weak and chalky, like an unbaked sandcastle, held only by dried binder. Strength comes later. Options are oven curing, sintering in a furnace where the binder burns off and particles fuse, or infiltration with bronze or epoxy to fill pores. Sintering shrinks the part, so the CAD model is scaled up before printing.

  • No laser. Melting energy comes from a furnace outside the machine, so the printer is fast and cheap.
  • SLS melts powder with a laser, needs no binder, and gives a denser part in one machine.
  • SLA cures resin in a vat and uses no support powder.
  • Foundry sand is common: printed sand moulds and cores replace hand-rammed moulds and allow shapes patterns cannot make.

Worked example

Question. A binder-jetted part is 25 mm tall, built with 0.1 mm layers. The layer to be printed is 100 mm wide across the head travel, the printhead is 10 mm wide, and the print speed is 200 mm/s. The sweep length equals the part width. Find the print time, ignoring powder spreading and head return.

  1. Number of layers = H / h = 25 mm / 0.1 mm = 250 layers.
  2. Passes per layer = W / w = 100 mm / 10 mm = 10 passes.
  3. Time for one sweep = L / v = 100 mm / 200 mm/s = 0.5 s.
  4. Time per layer = passes × sweep time = 10 × 0.5 s = 5 s.
  5. Total print time = layers × time per layer = 250 × 5 s = 1250 s.
  6. In minutes: 1250 / 60 = 20.83 min, about 20.8 min.

Answer. The printhead needs 1250 s, that is 20.83 min (about 20.8 min), ignoring powder spreading.

Exam traps

  • Binder jetting uses no laser. The binder glues powder; strength comes later from curing, sintering or infiltration. A laser sintering powder describes SLS.
  • The green part is not finished. It is weak, porous and shrinks during sintering, so the CAD model is printed oversize.
  • MJM is material jetting, not FDM. No filament or melting nozzle; resin is liquid and cured by UV.
  • Multi-jet modelling is not MultiJet Fusion, a powder-bed process with a similar name.
  • Print time formulas ignore powder spreading, depowdering, support removal and furnace time. Total lead time is longer.

It travels beside the printhead and cures fresh photopolymer droplets, so each sweep leaves a solid layer.

Dried liquid binder between powder particles. This weak green part is later cured, sintered or infiltrated.

MJM jets droplets and builds 15-30 micrometre layers; liquid wets the surface before curing. FDM extrudes filament, giving thicker layers, a visible raster and a nozzle-width track.

Apply A binder-jetted part is 40 mm tall with 0.08 mm layers. The layer is 60 mm wide, the printhead is 12 mm wide and the print speed is 250 mm/s. Sweep length equals part width. Find the print time in seconds and minutes, ignoring powder spreading. 4 marks

Analyze A metal bracket can be made by SLS-type powder bed fusion or by binder jetting plus sintering. Compare the two on bonding energy, part density and equipment. 5 marks

Powder bed fusion supplies laser energy inside the machine to melt each slice, so no binder is present and the part is dense in one machine. Binder jetting supplies only glue; melting happens in a separate furnace after binder burn-out, so the part is porous until sintered or infiltrated, shrinks, and needs two machines. Binder jetting prints faster and cheaper per part and suits large parts and sand moulds.

Analyze A foundry wants a complex sand core with internal channels for a cast pump body. Explain why binder jetting suits this job better than machining the core from a sand block. 4 marks

Binder jetting builds the core by gluing sand layer by layer from CAD, so internal channels and undercuts form without a pattern or split core box. Machining a sand block reaches only features open to the tool, and thin curved channels crumble. The printed core is cured and used directly.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Metal binder jetting is used for production batches of small stainless-steel and copper parts, sintered in a batch furnace. Printed sand moulds and cores are common in Indian foundries making pump bodies, impellers and automotive castings, because no pattern is needed. Research pushes nanoparticle inks and jetted functional fluids, so conductive tracks and sensors can be printed inside a plastic part.

7.9 Rapid tooling and rapid manufacturing

Rapid tooling uses additive manufacturing to make the tool — a mould insert, a die, a casting pattern or a jig — that then produces many parts by a conventional process. Rapid manufacturing uses the same AM machine to make the part that is sold, with no tool at all. Both exist because a hardened steel injection mould takes weeks and costs lakhs of rupees, while AM can deliver a working insert or pattern in days. They are used for low-volume plastic parts, casting patterns, bridge production before a hard tool arrives, and shop-floor jigs and fixtures.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for AM basics, Chapter 7.2 for the STL-to-part chain, Unit 1 for casting patterns and investment casting, and Unit 3 for injection moulding.

Tools, not parts: the two ideas

Rapid tooling means using an AM machine to make, or to help make, the tooling: a mould insert, a die, a casting pattern, a jig or a fixture. The tool then goes into a conventional machine — an injection moulding press, a casting line, a drill — and parts come out the usual way. AM is one link in the chain, not the chain.

Rapid manufacturing means the AM machine builds the part that is sold. No mould. The part is post-processed and shipped. The process chain is the same as in Chapter 7.2; only the destination of the output changes.

The split exists because tooling is the long pole. A hardened steel mould for a phone cover takes weeks of machining, EDM (Unit 6) and fitting, and costs lakhs of rupees. A printed insert or a printed pattern reaches the press in days for a fraction of that. On a small batch the tool cost cannot be recovered, so a cheap fast tool, or no tool at all, wins.

  • Rapid prototyping: the AM part is a model to look at and test.
  • Rapid tooling: the AM part makes other parts, in hundreds or thousands.
  • Rapid manufacturing: the AM part is the product.

Direct tooling: AM inserts and conformal cooling

In direct tooling the AM machine builds the tool itself. Powder bed fusion (Chapter 7.5) builds an injection mould cavity insert in tool steel or maraging steel powder. The insert is then heat treated, machined on its mating faces, and the cavity is polished.

The main gain is conformal cooling. Drilled cooling lines are straight, so thick corners stay hot while thin walls freeze fast. Uneven cooling warps the part and stretches the cycle. AM builds a channel that follows the cavity surface at a roughly constant offset — commonly 8 to 15 mm from the surface, 6 to 12 mm diameter — with round bends instead of drilled plugs.

Payoff: shorter cycle, flatter parts, less scrap. The worked example below turns a 30 s cycle into 20 s. Cost: the as-built channel surface is rough, so trapped powder must be blown out, and polishing the cavity costs extra.

  1. Design the insert and the channel path that follows the cavity surface.
  2. Build the insert by powder bed fusion.
  3. Stress relieve and heat treat to the required hardness.
  4. Clear unused powder from the cooling channels.
  5. Machine the mating faces and polish the cavity.
  6. Fit the insert into the bolster and run the mould.

Indirect tooling: patterns, soft moulds, bridge tools

Indirect tooling prints a pattern or a master, and a conventional route copies it into the working tool. The AM part never touches the final material.

Investment casting pattern (Unit 1): print the pattern in a wax-filled polymer, attach it to the tree, dip in ceramic slurry to build the shell, burn the pattern out, and pour. Complex geometry that would need a split wax die comes out in one piece.

Soft mould: cast silicone or epoxy around an AM master. The soft mould makes 20 to 100 polyurethane or wax parts — enough for design approval or a pilot batch.

Bridge tooling: an aluminium or soft-steel mould, often with AM inserts or made from an AM pattern, that supplies the first few thousand parts while the hardened production tool is being cut.

  • Soft mould for shape and fit checks in a rubber-like plastic.
  • Bridge tool when the part must reach the market before the steel tool.
  • Printed pattern when the part is metal and the batch is small.

Choosing: economics, batch size, material, surface

The choice is a cost-per-part sum.

cost per part = C_tool ÷ N + c_part

C_tool is the total tool cost in ₹, N is the batch size in parts, and c_part is the cost of one part on the machine (material, machine hour rate × cycle time, labour) in ₹.

As N grows the tool cost is spread thinner, so the tool with the longer life wins. Material narrows the choice: silicone moulds take only polyurethane and wax, aluminium bridge tools wear fast with glass-filled plastics, and AM steel inserts run common engineering plastics. Surface finish matters too — an AM insert needs polishing before the cavity is good enough for a visible part.

Jigs and fixtures are rapid tooling too: a printed drill jig or checking fixture for a curved sheet metal part is ready in a day, where machining one takes a week.

Exam traps

  • Rapid tooling is not rapid prototyping. The machine is the same, but a tool makes many parts and a prototype makes none.
  • Indirect tooling still needs a conventional step: shell building, silicone casting or copying. If the AM machine made the mould cavity directly, it is direct tooling.
  • An AM steel insert is not a hardened H13 production insert. Its life is shorter, so never quote production-mould life for it.

  • Conformal cooling changes the cooling channel path, not the gating system or the part design.

Takeaways

  1. Additive manufacturing adds material layer by layer from a 3D model, so it needs no part-specific tooling and can make internal channels that machining cannot reach.
  2. AM processes are classified by how the layer is formed: liquid resin cured, filament melted, powder fused, sheet bonded, or droplets jetted.
  3. The AM chain is fixed: CAD model, STL export, orientation, slicing, layer-by-layer build, then post-processing.
  4. Stereolithography gives the finest surface finish but needs supports and a photopolymer that is weak and sensitive to sunlight.
  5. Fused deposition modelling is the cheapest and simplest AM route, and layer height, raster angle and infill set the trade-off between time, strength and finish.
  6. Powder bed fusion supports the part in loose powder, so overhangs need no extra support and one build can nest many parts.
  7. LENS builds or repairs metal parts by blowing powder into a laser melt pool, and is used on shafts, dies and turbine blades rather than on a powder bed.
  8. Laminated object manufacturing bonds and cuts sheets, giving low cost and large size but poor internal features after de-cubing.
  9. Jetting processes split into material jetting, which deposits and cures liquid droplets, and binder jetting, which glues powder with a printed binder.
  10. Rapid tooling and rapid manufacturing extend AM from prototypes to moulds and to end-use parts, which pays off for low volume and mass customisation.

Glossary

Additive manufacturing
A family of processes that make a part by adding material in layers from a 3D model.
Binder
A liquid adhesive jetted into a powder bed to glue particles into the chosen shape.
Blown powder
Metal powder carried by a gas stream through a nozzle directly into the laser melt pool.
Cross-hatch
A criss-cross cut pattern in the waste material that makes removal easy and gives even strength.
Curing
Chemical hardening of a resin or binder, triggered by UV light, heat or time.
De-cubing
Breaking away the cross-hatched waste block that surrounds a laminated object manufacturing part.
Filament
A thin spooled strand of thermoplastic, usually 1.75 mm or 2.85 mm diameter, fed to an FDM nozzle.
Galvanometer scanner
A pair of small moving mirrors that steer a laser beam quickly across the resin or powder surface.
Hatch spacing
Distance between two neighbouring scan lines of a laser inside one cross-section.
Infill density
The percentage of the interior volume that is filled with material; the rest is left as air gaps.
Mass customisation
Producing many individually different items at close to mass-production cost, with no extra tooling per design.
Melt pool
The small pool of liquid metal formed where the laser or beam strikes the material.
Photopolymer
A resin that hardens when exposed to ultraviolet light.
Post-processing
All operations after the build, such as support removal, cleaning, curing, machining and painting.
Powder bed
A flat tray of loose powder, levelled thin before each layer is fused.
Printhead
A nozzle assembly that fires small droplets of liquid material or binder on demand.
Rapid manufacturing
Direct AM production of the final part sold or used by the customer.
Rapid tooling
Making a mould, die, insert or pattern by AM so that production tooling arrives in days.
Raster
One straight deposited road of material; a set of parallel rasters fills a layer.
Sintering
Heating powder particles until their surfaces bond together, without melting the whole particle.
Slicing
Cutting the 3D model into thin parallel cross-sections that the machine will build one by one.
STL
A surface-only file format that represents a part as a mesh of flat triangles.
Subtractive manufacturing
Making a part by removing material from a larger block, as in turning, milling and drilling.
Support structure
Temporary material built under overhangs to hold up the next layer, removed after the build.
Toolpath
The path the nozzle, laser or printhead follows inside each layer, generated by slicing software.
