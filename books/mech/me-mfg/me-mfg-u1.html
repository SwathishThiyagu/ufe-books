1.1 Sand casting and moulding sand properties

Sand casting pours liquid metal into a mould made of packed sand. The sand is cheap, holds a shape, and survives the heat long enough for the metal to freeze. A two-part mould is split into cope and drag; passages let metal in and gas out. The mould is not a permanent tool: it is broken after each casting. Foundries use sand casting for pump bodies, manhole covers, machine bases and brake drums. The sand's strength, permeability and refractoriness decide whether the mould fills cleanly or fails.

The two-part mould and its passages

A sand mould is a refractory sand shape: it does not fuse or soften at the temperature of the molten metal. It is packed inside a flask, a box open at the top and bottom. The upper half is the cope; the lower half is the drag. The empty space left by the pattern is the mould cavity. The pattern is a replica of the casting and is withdrawn before pouring; allowances are covered in 1.2. Metal enters through the sprue, a vertical channel. It flows along the runner, a horizontal channel in the drag, and enters the cavity through the gate. A riser is a reservoir of liquid metal that feeds shrinkage as the casting freezes. A core is a sand shape that forms an internal hole or passage. The gating and riser design is in 1.4.

Making a two-part mould follows a fixed order. The drag flask is placed on a board. The pattern is placed in it. Sand is rammed around the pattern. The pattern is drawn out. The runner and gate are cut. For the cope, the drag is turned over. The cope flask and the other half of the pattern are placed with a sprue pin. Sand is rammed. The sprue pin is withdrawn, leaving the sprue. The cope is lifted and set on the drag with locating pins. The figure shows this order; the mould is destroyed after pouring.

Green sand, dry sand and loam

Green sand is moist moulding sand. It is a mix of silica sand grains, a clay binder such as bentonite, water and small additives. Water makes the clay sticky and lets grains slide during ramming. Green means wet, not green in colour. Green sand is one kind of foundry sand; the general name foundry sand covers green sand, dry sand and loam. Dry sand is a mould rammed from similar sand and then dried or baked before pouring. Drying raises strength and lowers the gas left in the mould. Loam is a mixture of sand, clay and water with a high clay content. It is plastic, and large moulds are built by plastering loam over a brick or iron frame. Green sand suits small and medium castings made at high rates. Dry sand and loam suit large, heavy castings where strength and rigidity matter more than speed.

P = VH ÷ (pAt)

P is the permeability number (dimensionless); V is air volume in cm³; H is specimen height in cm; p is air pressure in g/cm²; A is specimen cross-section area in cm²; t is time in minutes.

A standard specimen is 50 mm diameter and 50 mm high. Air of volume V passes through it in time t under pressure p. A higher P means gas escapes more easily. Green strength is measured by compressing the moist standard specimen in a universal sand testing machine until it fails. Dry strength is measured on the same specimen after drying. Permeability is measured on the moist specimen with the standard air-flow test. These two tests, strength and permeability, tell most of the story: harder ramming raises strength but lowers permeability.

Worked example

Question. A standard sand specimen is 50 mm diameter and 50 mm high. 2000 cm³ of air passes through it in 2 min under a pressure of 10 g/cm². Find the permeability number.

  1. Diameter d = 50 mm = 5 cm, so radius r = d ÷ 2 = 2.5 cm.
  2. Cross-section area A = πr² = π × 2.5² = 19.63 cm².
  3. Height H = 50 mm = 5 cm. Air volume V = 2000 cm³. Pressure p = 10 g/cm². Time t = 2 min.
  4. Numerator VH = 2000 × 5 = 10000 cm⁴. Denominator pAt = 10 × 19.63 × 2 = 392.6.
  5. P = 10000 ÷ 392.6 = 25.47, which rounds to 25.5.

Answer. The permeability number is about 25.5 (dimensionless).

Exam traps

  • Permeability is not porosity. A sand can be porous but still resist gas flow if the pores are not connected.
  • In A = πr² use r = 2.5 cm, not the 5 cm diameter. Using the diameter gives four times the area and P near 6.4.
  • Keep p in g/cm² and t in minutes. Mixing Pa or seconds changes the number.
  • Green strength is the strength of moist sand; dry strength is measured after baking. Do not use one for the other.
  • Ramming harder raises green strength but cuts permeability; exam questions often ask for both effects.

1.2 Patterns: types, materials and allowances

A pattern is the mould-maker's copy of the casting. It is packed in sand, withdrawn, and the space left behind is filled with liquid metal. The metal shrinks as it cools, and the pattern must be pulled out of the sand, so the pattern is never exactly the size of the finished part. Allowances fix that difference. Patterns decide the mould cavity, the parting line and much of the tooling cost, so a 1% error in a pattern makes every casting in the batch 1% wrong.

You need: 1.1 Sand casting and moulding sand properties, because a pattern is only meaningful as the shape that is packed in sand.

The pattern is a copy, not the part

A pattern is the positive replica of the casting. Ram sand around it, withdraw it, and the empty cavity left behind is the negative that the metal fills. A core box does the same job for a core.

Three objects are easy to confuse. The part is the finished machined component on the drawing. The casting is the rough metal that leaves the mould, with machining stock still on it. The pattern is the tool that makes the mould for that casting.

The pattern is not the same size as the casting for two reasons. Liquid metal shrinks when it freezes and cools (see 1.5), so the cavity must be bigger than the cold casting. And the pattern must come out of the rammed sand without tearing the cavity walls, so vertical faces are tapered and the pattern may be rapped before withdrawal. Each effect becomes an allowance.

A pattern is reused many times, so its material is chosen by how many moulds it must survive and how fine a surface the casting needs.

Types of pattern

The shape of the casting and the batch size decide the pattern type.

  • Solid pattern: one piece with no joint. Cheapest, used when a flat face can act as the parting line.
  • Split pattern: two halves meeting at the parting line, located by dowel pins. Used when the shape cannot be withdrawn from one side.
  • Cope-and-drag pattern: also two halves, but each half is moulded separately in the cope and the drag and is located by pins in the flask. Used for large castings.
  • Match plate pattern: the cope half and the drag half mounted on opposite faces of one plate, with runners and gates attached. One ramming makes both halves, so it suits machine moulding and long runs.
  • Sweep pattern: a profile board rotated about a vertical or horizontal axis over a bed of sand. Used for large circular shapes such as a bell or a big flange, where a full pattern would be huge.
  • Skeleton pattern: a frame carrying only the outline; the sand inside is strickled to shape by hand. Used for very large, simple shapes such as a big rectangular box.
  • Loose piece pattern: the main body plus separate pieces at projections that would block withdrawal; the loose pieces are drawn out after the main pattern.

The five allowances

An allowance is a deliberate difference between the pattern and the casting. Four allowances make the pattern bigger or differently shaped; one makes it smaller. Apply them in a fixed order and state the order, because the examiner marks it.

L_pattern = L_casting × (1 + s) + m

L_pattern is the pattern length in mm along one direction; L_casting is the casting length in mm in the same direction; s is the shrinkage as a decimal fraction (10 mm/m = 0.01); m is the machining allowance in mm on that dimension, zero if the surface is not machined.

  • Shrinkage allowance: positive. The cavity must be larger than the cold casting. Values depend on the metal; handbooks quote about 10 mm/m (1%) for grey cast iron, about 20 mm/m (2%) for steel and about 13 to 16 mm/m for aluminium alloys. Treat these as examples, not constants — use the figure your foundry has proved for its metal and mould.
  • Machining allowance: positive stock on surfaces that will be machined, so the tool can reach clean metal. It grows with casting size and with the roughness of the process.
  • Draft allowance: taper on vertical faces, the faces parallel to the direction of withdrawal. Without it the sand tears. It is quoted in degrees, commonly 0.5° to 2°, and is larger on internal faces.
  • Distortion allowance: long, thin, U- or V-shaped castings warp as they cool; the pattern is given the opposite camber so the casting straightens out.
  • Shake (rapping) allowance: negative. Rapping the pattern before withdrawal enlarges the cavity, so the pattern is made a little smaller. It is a fraction of a millimetre and is often neglected in sums that say so.

Pattern materials

No single material wins everywhere. Wood is easy to shape and cheap but moves with moisture. Metal holds size and gives a better surface but is heavy and costly. Plastic sits between the two.

Worked example

Question. A grey cast iron block is to be 800 mm long after casting and cleaning. The foundry uses a shrinkage allowance of 10 mm/m for grey cast iron. The 800 mm dimension is not machined, draft is applied on the sides only, shake is neglected and distortion is nil. Find the shrinkage allowance and the pattern length.

  1. Convert the shrinkage to a fraction: s = 10 mm/m = 10 ÷ 1000 = 0.01 mm per mm, which is 1%.
  2. Shrinkage allowance = L_casting × s = 800 mm × 0.01 = 8 mm.
  3. Machining allowance on this dimension m = 0 mm, because the surface is not machined.
  4. Pattern length = L_casting × (1 + s) + m = 800 × 1.01 + 0 = 808 mm.
  5. Check in reverse: when the 808 mm cavity fills and the metal cools, it contracts by about 1%, giving 808 × 0.99 = 799.9 mm, which is the required 800 mm to within a tenth of a millimetre.

Answer. The shrinkage allowance is 8 mm and the pattern length is 808 mm.

Exam traps

  • 10 mm/m is 1%, not 10%: an 800 mm casting grows by 8 mm, not 80 mm.
  • Draft goes on vertical faces — the faces parallel to withdrawal — never on the parting plane.
  • Shake allowance is negative; the pattern is made smaller, not larger.
  • A match plate is a mounting method, not a material. A split pattern and a cope-and-drag pattern are both two-piece, but the cope-and-drag halves are located by pins in the flask, not by dowels in the pattern.
  • Shrinkage is a linear allowance applied once to each dimension; it is not a volume percentage, so do not cube it.

1.3 Moulding methods and moulding machines

A moulding method is the choice of where the mould is made, what holds the sand, and how the sand is packed. A small brass bush is moulded on a bench; a two-tonne pump casing is moulded in a pit in the foundry floor. The sand may be moist green sand, baked dry sand, gas-hardened or resin-bonded. The ramming that presses sand around the pattern is done by hand in job foundries and by jolt and squeeze machines in production foundries. These choices set the mould strength, the surface finish and the number of moulds made per shift.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for moulding sand properties, and Chapter 1.2 for the pattern and its allowances.

Bench, floor and pit moulding

A moulding method is named first by the place where the mould is made. In bench moulding the mould is made on a work bench, with flasks small enough for one moulder to lift and turn. It suits small castings made in quantity, such as pipe fittings and small impellers, because the moulder sits at one place and repeats the same job.

In floor moulding the drag is placed on the foundry floor and the flask is lifted by crane or by two moulders. Medium and large castings use this. In pit moulding the drag is a pit dug in the floor and lined with sand and brickwork; the pattern sits in the pit and the cope is a normal flask on top. The pit gives one casting, the mould is broken after pouring, and the pit is re-lined for the next job. Very large castings such as a mill liner or an anvil block are made this way, because no flask could carry that mass of sand.

  1. Level the drag on the floor and seat the pattern on it.
  2. Dust parting sand on the pattern so the sand does not stick to it.
  3. Fill sand in layers of about 50 mm and ram each layer evenly, keeping the rammer away from the pattern face.
  4. Ram harder near the flask walls and around the sprue pin, where the sand must hold its shape.
  5. Roll over the drag, fit the cope, locate the pins, ram the cope, and cut the sprue and riser.
  6. Lift the cope, draw the pattern, repair the mould surface and close the mould.

Green sand is moist clay-bonded sand used without baking. It is the cheapest mould material, the fastest to prepare, and covers the largest share of castings. Its weakness is lower strength and more moisture-related defects.

Dry sand moulds are baked through, so the clay bond sets and the mould becomes stronger and holds size better; the cost is oven time, so this is kept for large and steel castings. Skin-dried moulds are green sand moulds whose surface alone is dried with a torch or a short oven pass: strength where the metal touches, cheapness everywhere else.

Loam moulding builds the mould from clay, sand and water plastered over brickwork; there is no flask, so bells and very large gear moulds are made this way. CO2 moulding uses sodium silicate as the binder and passes carbon dioxide gas through the sand, which sets in minutes without any baking. No-bake sand uses a resin and a hardener that cure at room temperature, giving a strong mould, a good surface finish and a core that collapses after pouring.

Moulding machines: jolt, squeeze and slinger

Hand ramming gives a hardness that changes from moulder to moulder and from mould to mould. A moulding machine repeats the same ramming action every cycle, so every mould is equally hard and the ramming time falls.

A jolt machine lifts the table carrying the pattern and flask, then lets it drop on a stop. The sand is moving down when the box stops, so the grains keep going, press on the sand below, and pack hardest against the pattern. Jolting therefore hardens exactly the sand that will meet the liquid metal. Too many jolts make the mould so hard that it cannot be drawn or vented.

A squeeze machine presses the sand down with a plate or a rubber diaphragm at the top of the box. Here the sand is hardest under the plate and softest at the pattern, the opposite of jolt, so squeeze suits shallow boxes.

A jolt-squeeze machine does both: it jolts to pack the sand around the pattern and then squeezes to pack the top, so the whole mould is hard. A sand slinger throws sand into the box from a fast rotating impeller head, and the sand packs by its own impact; large boxes are filled in a minute or two. Flaskless moulding drops the flask altogether: sand is squeezed against two vertical pattern plates, the sand block is stripped and pushed into a line behind the previous ones. It is the standard high-production route for automotive castings.

N = 3600 ÷ T

N is the number of moulds made per hour and T is the complete cycle time for one mould in seconds, counting filling, jolting, squeezing, pattern draw and closing.

Worked example

Question. A jolt-squeeze moulding machine makes one complete mould in 45 s, counting sand filling, jolting, squeezing, pattern draw and closing. Find the production rate in moulds per hour and the number of moulds made in one 8-hour shift.

  1. Cycle time T = 45 s per mould. This is the time for the whole cycle, not only the jolt and squeeze part.
  2. One hour has 3600 s, so N = 3600 s/h ÷ 45 s/mould = 80 moulds per hour.
  3. Shift output = N × shift length = 80 moulds/h × 8 h = 640 moulds.
  4. Check the trap: if 45 s were the ramming time alone, the rate would be wrong, because every mould also needs the pattern draw and closing inside the same cycle.

Answer. The machine makes 80 moulds per hour, that is 640 moulds in one 8-hour shift.

Exam traps

  • Using the ramming time only instead of the full cycle time. A 45 s cycle gives 80 moulds/h; 45 s of ramming inside a longer cycle does not.
  • Swapping jolt and squeeze. Jolt hardens the sand at the pattern face; squeeze hardens the sand under the press plate at the top.
  • Green sand is not green in colour. It means moist, unbaked moulding sand bonded with clay and water.
  • Dry sand and skin-dried are different. A dry sand mould is baked right through; a skin-dried mould has only its working surface dried.
  • CO2 moulding sets the sodium silicate binder by passing gas through the sand. It is not a baking process and needs no oven.

1.4 Gating system and riser design; Chvorinov's rule

Liquid metal must reach the mould cavity fast enough, but without spraying air bubbles or carrying sand and slag. A gating system does that. After filling, the metal shrinks as it freezes, so a riser keeps feeding liquid metal into the last hot spot. Chvorinov's rule compares how long a shape takes to freeze using its volume and cooling surface area. These ideas decide whether a casting is sound or full of shrinkage cavities.

You need: 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3.

The gating system: path and purpose

A gating system is the set of channels that carries liquid metal from the ladle to the mould cavity. Its jobs are to fill the cavity in the right time, avoid turbulence that traps air, stop slag and sand from entering, and leave a clean path for metal to feed shrinkage.

Metal first lands in a pouring basin, a small reservoir on top of the sprue. It absorbs the ladle stream and lets slag float. A skim bob is a trap beside the basin or runner where lighter slag collects. The sprue is the vertical channel down the mould. Its cross-section tapers smaller downward: falling metal speeds up, and a constant area would let it separate from the walls and suck air. The sprue well is a deeper pocket at the sprue bottom; it kills the falling jet and reduces sand erosion. The runner is the horizontal channel in the parting plane, and the ingate is the final opening into the cavity. The smallest section in the path is the choke; it controls flow rate.

Gating ratio is sprue : runner : ingate area ratio. A pressurized system has the smallest section at or near the ingate, so the runner stays full; the jet enters fast. A non-pressurized system has the choke at the sprue and larger runner and ingate areas, so metal enters slowly and quietly. Slow filling suits light alloys and sand moulds that erode easily. Fast filling suits thin sections that would freeze before filling.

Risers: feeding the last liquid

A riser is a reservoir of liquid metal that feeds the casting while it freezes and shrinks. It must stay liquid longer than the casting, so it is placed at the hot spot: the region with the largest volume-to-cooling-area ratio, which freezes last. A top riser sits above the cavity and feeds downward under gravity. A side riser sits beside the cavity, connected through a neck; it is easier to mould. A blind riser is closed at the top and hidden inside the mould; it saves metal but needs a small vent to avoid gas pressure.

Riser size is compared using the modulus M = V/A, where V is the volume of metal in cm³ or m³ and A is the cooling surface area in cm² or m². The riser must have a larger modulus than the casting section it feeds. A common shop rule is Mriser ≥ 1.2 Mcasting, and for a strong feed the riser is made to freeze about 1.5 times later. The neck must stay open until freezing ends; a narrow neck freezes early and cuts off feed.

Chvorinov's rule: freezing time from shape

Chvorinov's rule estimates solidification time from the shape of the casting:

t = k (V/A)n

t is solidification time in minutes or seconds; V is the volume of freezing metal; A is the surface area through which heat leaves to the mould; k is the mould constant in time per unit (V/A)n; n is about 2 for sand casting.

A chunky shape has large V/A, stores more heat per unit cooling area, and freezes slowly. A thin plate has small V/A and freezes fast. Use consistent units. If V is in cm³ and A in cm², V/A is in cm. Then k carries min/cm² when n = 2. The constant k depends on mould material, metal, pouring temperature and sand moisture; find it by a test casting. For k = 2 min/cm², the curve is t = 2(V/A)²; at V/A = 2 cm, t = 8 min.

Chvorinov's rule compares a riser with the casting, places chills, and checks whether a thin section freezes before the mould fills. It does not describe freezing zones inside the metal; that is in 1.5.

Worked example

Question. A steel casting has volume 1200 cm³ and cooling surface area 600 cm². Chvorinov's constant is k = 3 min/cm² and n = 2. (a) Find the solidification time. (b) A riser must solidify 1.5 times later than the casting. Find the minimum V/A of the riser.

  1. Volume-to-area ratio of the casting: V/A = 1200 cm³ ÷ 600 cm² = 2 cm.
  2. Solidification time: t = k(V/A)² = 3 min/cm² × (2 cm)² = 3 × 4 = 12 min.
  3. Required riser time: triser = 1.5 × 12 min = 18 min.
  4. Riser ratio: 18 = 3 × (V/A)riser², so (V/A)riser² = 6.
  5. Take the square root: (V/A)riser = √6 = 2.449 cm.

Answer. The casting solidifies in t = 12 min, and the riser needs V/A ≥ 2.45 cm (using √6 = 2.449 cm).

Exam traps

  • A is the heat-loss surface area, not the projected area or the area covered by the pattern. Use the area through which heat leaves the metal.
  • The riser modulus must be larger than the casting modulus, not equal or smaller. A riser that freezes first cannot feed.
  • n is about 2, not 1: doubling V/A roughly quadruples freezing time.
  • Gating ratio is an area ratio, not a length ratio. Choke location decides pressurized or non-pressurized.
  • Mixing cm and mm in V/A makes k wrong; convert all lengths first.

1.5 Solidification of castings and solidification time

A casting does not freeze all at once. Heat leaves through the mould wall, so the metal near the wall freezes first and the freezing front moves inward. Pure metals freeze at one temperature; alloys freeze over a range and form a mushy zone. The zone pattern, directional solidification and three shrinkages decide whether the casting is sound. Chvorinov's rule turns this picture into a number: solidification time depends on volume divided by cooling surface area. Foundries use it to compare the freezing time of a casting with that of its riser.

You need: 1.1 for the mould and 1.4 for gating, risers and Chvorinov's rule.

Freezing starts at the wall

Solidification begins with nucleation. The liquid metal must form stable solid nuclei before crystals can grow. Homogeneous nucleation means nuclei form inside the liquid itself; it needs high undercooling. Heterogeneous nucleation on the mould wall, on sand grains or on inclusions is easier and common in castings. Once nuclei exist, they grow by adding atoms from the liquid. Grains that grow fastest along the direction opposite to heat flow become long and columnar.

Three zones usually appear from the wall inward. The chill zone is a thin layer of fine equiaxed grains at the mould wall. The wall removes heat fast, so many nuclei survive and no grain grows large. Next is the columnar zone. These grains are long and roughly parallel to the heat flow, because the liquid ahead is cooler and growth along the steep temperature gradient wins. At the centre is the equiaxed zone: random, roughly equal-sized grains that form in the last liquid, often helped by added grain refiners.

Directional solidification means keeping this freezing front moving steadily toward the riser. Then each part of the casting freezes before the liquid feeding it is cut off. Chills, exothermic sleeves and a suitable temperature gradient help. Good directional solidification gives a sound casting; poor control leaves a mushy centre that cannot be fed. The figure shows the order from the mould wall to the centre.

Pure metals, alloys and three shrinkages

Pure metals and eutectic alloys freeze at a single temperature. The solid–liquid front is sharp. Alloys with a freezing range start freezing at the liquidus temperature and finish at the solidus temperature. Between these two temperatures, solid dendrites and liquid coexist in a mushy zone. A wide freezing range gives a long mushy zone. Liquid cannot flow easily through it, so feeding becomes difficult and small dispersed shrinkage pores can remain. A narrow range keeps the front tighter and easier to feed. Some syllabi call this solidification of alloys.

The three shrinkages are best kept separate. Liquid shrinkage is the volume decrease when liquid metal cools to its freezing point. Solidification shrinkage is the volume decrease when liquid becomes solid; most metals shrink on freezing, with grey cast iron a famous exception because graphite growth offsets it. Solid shrinkage is the volume decrease when the frozen solid cools to room temperature. A riser feeds liquid and solidification shrinkage; the pattern's shrinkage allowance compensates solid shrinkage. See 1.4 for riser sizing and 1.8 for shrinkage defects.

A freezing range also changes the zone structure. The mushy zone can block the columnar front, and equiaxed grains may grow inside it. That is why alloy castings often need grain refinement, chills and careful riser placement.

Solidification time and Chvorinov's rule

Chvorinov's rule is the working formula for solidification time. It compares castings made in the same mould from the same metal.

t = B (V/A)n

t is solidification time (s or min); V is casting volume; A is the heat-losing surface area; B is the mould constant for the metal and mould; n is the exponent, usually 2.

The rule is used as a ratio. If two castings have the same B and n, t₂/t₁ = [(V/A)₂/(V/A)₁]n. For a cube of side a, V = a³ and A = 6a², so V/A = a/6. Under the same conditions, a cube's solidification time scales with a², not a³. Doubling the side doubles the modulus and makes the time four times as large. The volume becomes eight times as large, but time does not. Use the rule to check that a riser freezes after the casting it feeds: the riser needs a larger modulus than the casting so it stays liquid while the casting freezes. The worked example applies the ratio to two cubes.

Worked example

Question. A 200 mm cube casting solidifies in 20 min in a sand mould. Using Chvorinov's rule with n = 2, find the solidification time of a 100 mm cube of the same metal in the same mould.

  1. For a cube of side a, V = a³ and A = 6a², so V/A = a/6.
  2. For the 200 mm cube: V₁ = 200³ = 8,000,000 mm³; A₁ = 6 × 200² = 240,000 mm²; (V/A)₁ = 8,000,000 / 240,000 = 33.333 mm.
  3. For the 100 mm cube: V₂ = 100³ = 1,000,000 mm³; A₂ = 6 × 100² = 60,000 mm²; (V/A)₂ = 1,000,000 / 60,000 = 16.667 mm.
  4. Chvorinov's rule: t = B (V/A)². With the same B, t₂/t₁ = [(V/A)₂/(V/A)₁]².
  5. t₂/t₁ = (16.667 / 33.333)² = (0.5)² = 0.25.
  6. t₂ = 20 × 0.25 = 5.0 min.

Answer. The 100 mm cube solidifies in 5.0 min.

Exam traps

  • Same shape, same mould: solidification time scales with the square of linear size, not with volume. A 100 mm cube takes one quarter of the 200 mm cube's time, not one eighth.
  • Do not use n = 3 for time just because volume goes as a³. Chvorinov's exponent is usually 2; V/A is a length, and the ratio uses (length ratio)^n.
  • Pure metals freeze at one temperature; alloys with a freezing range freeze between liquidus and solidus. A mushy zone is not the same as the columnar zone.
  • The chill zone is fine equiaxed grains at the wall. Columnar grains are long and point inward along heat flow; equiaxed grains at the centre are random.
  • A riser feeds liquid and solidification shrinkage. Solid shrinkage after freezing is handled by pattern shrinkage allowance, not by the riser.

1.6 Melting furnaces and pouring practice

Molten metal must reach the mould at the right composition, temperature and cleanliness. Melting furnaces supply that metal and add superheat; pouring practice carries it without turbulence or slag. A cupola burns coke in an air blast; an induction furnace heats by eddy currents; an arc furnace uses electrodes; a crucible furnace heats a pot from outside. The foundry then treats the metal in a ladle and pours through a basin at a controlled rate. This matters for every iron, steel and non-ferrous casting, from a pump body to a brake drum.

You need: 1.1 for mould and sand, and 1.5 for freezing and shrinkage.

Cupola: coke, blast and slag

Cupola is a vertical shaft furnace for cast iron. The charge is the solid load: metal scrap or pig iron, coke and limestone. Coke burns in an air blast that enters through tuyeres, small nozzles near the bottom. The coke bed is the hot column that supports the charge and supplies heat. In the melting zone, metal melts and drips down. Limestone flux combines with ash and sand to form slag, which floats and is removed at the slag notch. Molten iron collects in the well, the bottom reservoir, and is tapped at the tap hole. The action is continuous: charge at top, blast at bottom, slag and metal out.

Metal charge = melt rate × time

Melt rate is in tonnes per hour, time in hours, and metal charge in tonnes. Coke mass = coke ratio × metal charge; coke ratio is a decimal fraction.

The coke ratio is the coke mass divided by the metal charge. A higher ratio gives hotter metal but burns more coke. The figure shows the main flows.

Induction, arc and crucible furnaces

  • Induction furnace: a water-cooled copper coil surrounds a refractory crucible. Alternating current in the coil creates a changing magnetic field. That field induces eddy currents in the metal charge. Eddy currents are circulating currents inside the metal; the metal's resistance turns them into heat. The same field stirs the melt, which helps composition. The frequency sets the heating depth and the amount of stirring. Induction melting gives clean iron and steel and fine control, without coke or electrodes.
  • Electric arc furnace: graphite electrodes strike an arc on scrap. The arc heat melts the charge. It suits large heats and alloy steel; the arc is adjusted by the electrical power.
  • Crucible furnace: a crucible is a heat-resistant pot that holds the metal. The pot is heated from outside by coke, oil, gas or resistance elements. It is simple and clean for small non-ferrous melts, but slow.

Ladle treatment and pouring practice

Ladle treatment happens between furnace and mould. The ladle is a refractory-lined bucket that carries molten metal. Slag is skimmed, and alloy or inoculant is added. An inoculant is an additive that controls graphite shape in cast iron. Degassing removes dissolved gas such as hydrogen. The aim is clean metal of the right composition.

A pouring basin is a small reservoir at the top of the sprue, the vertical channel into the mould. It keeps the sprue full, traps slag and reduces turbulence. The gating system then controls flow; see 1.4.

T_pour = T_liq + ΔT_s

T_pour is pouring temperature in °C; T_liq is liquidus temperature, where freezing starts, in °C; ΔT_s is superheat in °C.

Enough superheat fills thin sections. Too little gives misrun (incomplete filling) and cold shut (two streams meeting without fusing). Too much gives sand penetration (metal entering sand gaps), gas defects (trapped gas holes) and coarse grains. Pouring speed is mass or volume per second. Slow pouring cools early; fast pouring erodes sand and entrains air. A choke, a narrow section, sets the rate. Defects are in 1.8.

Worked example

Question. A cupola melts 5 t of iron per hour. Find the metal charge for a 3 h shift. If coke is 12% of the metal charge, find the coke needed.

  1. Melt rate R = 5 t/h. Shift time t = 3 h.
  2. Metal charge M = R × t = 5 × 3 = 15 t.
  3. Coke fraction c = 12% = 0.12.
  4. Coke mass = c × M = 0.12 × 15 = 1.8 t.

Answer. The metal charge is 15 t and the coke needed is 1.8 t.

Exam traps

  • Cupola is a coke-fired shaft furnace. It does not use electric arcs or induction; those belong to electric furnaces.
  • Coke ratio is a fraction of the metal charge, not of the total charge. 12% of 15 t is 1.8 t; 12% of (15 + 1.8) t would be wrong.
  • Low pouring temperature gives misrun and cold shut; very high temperature gives sand penetration, gas defects and coarse grains.
  • An induction furnace heats by eddy currents in the charge, not by direct resistance of the coil alone. The coil is water-cooled.
  • A pouring basin is not a riser. The basin controls fill and traps slag; the riser feeds shrinkage.

1.7 Special casting: shell, investment, die and centrifugal

Special casting processes use a precision mould, either expendable or permanent, to make a casting closer to final size than sand casting. The mould may be a thin resin-bonded shell, a ceramic shell around a wax pattern, a steel die, or a rotating mould. The aim is better surface finish, tighter tolerance, thinner walls, or higher production. These processes cost more in pattern or die, so they suit parts where machining is expensive or the volume is high. You see them in pump impellers, turbine blades, automotive housings and pipes.

You need: sand casting and moulding sand, patterns and allowances and moulding methods.

Shell moulding: resin sand on a hot pattern

Shell moulding uses a metal pattern heated to about 200-300 °C. The pattern is clamped over a dump box of resin-coated sand. The sand is fine silica grain coated with a thermosetting phenolic resin. When the sand touches the hot pattern, the resin cures and a thin shell forms. The box is inverted, loose sand falls away, and the shell is cured further and stripped. Two shell halves are clamped or glued with backing for pouring. The hot pattern is the key: the mould is not heated; only the thin layer next to the pattern sets.

  1. Clean and heat the metal pattern to 200-300 °C.
  2. Clamp the dump box over the pattern and tip in resin-coated sand.
  3. Wait a few seconds for a 5-10 mm shell to cure.
  4. Invert the box so loose sand falls back.
  5. Cure the shell in an oven, then strip it from the pattern.
  6. Clamp or glue two halves and back them with sand or metal shot.

Shell moulds give a smooth surface, close tolerance, thin sections and good repeatability. Pattern and resin costs are high, and part size is limited by pattern and shell handling. Typical parts are small gears, camshafts, valve bodies and housings where machining must be low.

Investment casting: a wax pattern becomes a ceramic shell

Investment casting, or lost-wax casting, makes the pattern from wax or a wax-like polymer. The pattern is injected into a metal die and assembled on a central sprue as a tree. The tree is dipped repeatedly into ceramic slurry and stucco; each coat dries, and the built-up ceramic becomes the shell. The shell is heated so the wax melts and runs out, called dewaxing, then fired in a burnout furnace. Molten metal is poured into the hot ceramic shell. After freezing, the shell is broken away.

  1. Inject wax into a metal pattern die and remove the wax pattern.
  2. Attach patterns to a central wax sprue to form a tree.
  3. Dip the tree in ceramic slurry and sprinkle stucco; dry and repeat.
  4. Heat the shell so wax melts out; collect or discard the wax.
  5. Fire the empty shell to burn off residue and strengthen it.
  6. Pour molten metal into the hot shell and let it freeze.
  7. Break the ceramic shell and cut the castings from the tree.

Investment casting gives fine detail, thin walls and little or no machining. It casts complex shapes in steel, stainless steel, nickel and cobalt superalloys, aluminium and copper alloys. Uses include turbine blades, pump impellers, jewellery, dental parts and valve components. Limits are many steps, long cycle, expensive wax and ceramic, and usually small to medium size. The pattern is expendable.

Pressure die casting: metal forced into a metal die

Pressure die casting forces liquid metal into a hardened steel die at high pressure. The die has two halves: one carries the cavity, the other the cover. A shot sleeve and plunger push metal through runners into the cavity. Pressure is held until the casting freezes; then the die opens and ejector pins push out the part. The die is water-cooled, so cycles are short and production is high.

In a hot-chamber machine the gooseneck injection cylinder sits inside the molten metal pot. The plunger pushes metal directly into the die. It suits low-melting metals such as zinc, lead, tin and some magnesium. The cycle is fast. It is not used for aluminium because the hot metal attacks the gooseneck and picks up iron.

In a cold-chamber machine metal is ladled into a separate shot sleeve, and the plunger injects it into the die. It suits aluminium, magnesium, copper and other high-melting alloys. The melting pot is separate from the injection system, so high temperature does not damage a gooseneck.

F = p × Aproj

F is the clamping force in N; p is the metal pressure in MPa, with 1 MPa = 1 N/mm²; Aproj is the projected area in mm² perpendicular to the die opening.

Centrifugal casting: metal thrown to the mould wall

Centrifugal casting uses a rotating mould. Molten metal is poured and centrifugal force presses it against the mould wall. The three modes differ in what rotates and what shape is cast.

  • True centrifugal: the mould rotates about the axis of a hollow casting. No core is needed. Pipes and cylinder liners are made this way. The outer wall is dense; the inner surface holds lighter impurities and is machined off.
  • Semi-centrifugal: the mould rotates about a vertical central axis. A central sprue feeds cavities arranged around it. Wheels, pulleys and gear blanks are typical. The casting itself need not be hollow.
  • Centrifuging: small moulds sit around a central sprue in a rotating assembly. Centrifugal pressure fills each mould. Small precise parts are made this way.

1.8 Casting defects and remedies

A casting defect is a visible or measurable failure in a cast part. Its shape, size and location carry evidence: gas leaves round holes, shrinkage leaves rough cavities, and hot metal that stops early leaves a short edge. Foundries use this evidence to trace the cause to sand, pattern, gating, pouring or freezing, then change one variable and check the next batch. Rejection rate is money: every rejected casting has already consumed metal, melt energy and labour. The useful grouping is by cause: sand, pouring, freezing and pattern.

You need: 1.1 sand casting and moulding sand properties, 1.2 patterns, 1.4 gating and riser design, and 1.5 solidification; the mould, pattern, gating and freezing ideas explain most defects.

Read the defect before naming it

Some syllabi call this casting defects and remedies. A defect is not just a hole. The useful question is: what were the mould, metal, pattern and freezing doing when the hole formed? Read four clues. Position: top cope, bottom drag, gate, riser or core. Surface: smooth, rough, dendritic, oxidised or sandy. Shape: round, elongated, step or seam. Time: during pouring, during freezing, or after removal. A smooth round cavity high in the cope points to gas trapped by sand. A rough cavity at a thick section points to liquid shrinkage. A short, rounded edge points to metal that froze before filling. A crack with oxidised faces formed while the casting was hot. Visual and dimensional checks come first; dye penetrant finds surface-breaking cracks, while radiography and ultrasonic testing find internal defects. All of these belong to later non-destructive testing study. Record the casting number, mould number, pouring temperature and time. One defect is an anecdote; a pattern of ten is evidence.

  3. Good castings = 500 - 35 = 465.
  4. Rejection rate = (35 ÷ 500) × 100 = 7%.
  5. Yield of good castings = (465 ÷ 500) × 100 = 93%, which is also 100% - 7% = 93%.

Answer. The rejection rate is 7% and the yield of good castings is 93%.

Exam traps

  • A shrinkage cavity is rough and dendritic inside; a blowhole is smooth and round. Do not call every hole porosity.
  • A hot tear forms while the casting is hot and contracting, not after it is cold; a cold crack has a different history.
  • Misrun is incomplete filling; cold shut is two streams meeting without fusion. Both can look like a short edge or seam, but the cause and remedy differ.
  • Sand inclusion is loose sand; slag inclusion is slag. Slag often lies near the gate and is dark and glassy; sand is gritty.
  • Yield of good castings is not the same as casting yield (casting weight divided by poured metal weight). Read what the question asks.

Check yourself

Remember Name two gas defects and one shrinkage defect. 2 marks

Blowhole and porosity are gas defects; shrinkage cavity is a shrinkage defect.

Understand Why does a hot tear form while the casting is still hot? 3 marks

The casting is weak at high temperature. Contraction is blocked by a strong mould or core, so tensile strain exceeds the hot ductility and a tear opens.

Apply A foundry makes 800 castings and rejects 48. Find the rejection rate and the yield of good castings. 3 marks

Good = 800 - 48 = 752. Rejection rate = 48/800 × 100 = 6%. Good yield = 752/800 × 100 = 94%.

Analyze A casting shows a smooth round hole at the top of the cope, and another casting shows a rough cavity in a thick hub. Identify each defect and give one remedy. 4 marks

Smooth round hole high in the cope: blowhole from gas in sand; dry and vent the sand. Rough cavity in a thick hub: shrinkage cavity; add or enlarge a riser and place a chill.

Understand Distinguish misrun from cold shut. 3 marks

Misrun: metal freezes before filling a section. Cold shut: two metal streams meet but do not fuse, leaving a rounded seam.

Apply A casting shows sand inclusion and penetration. Which sand properties should be improved? 4 marks

Sand inclusion means loose sand; penetration means metal entered sand gaps. Use stronger, finer sand, better ramming, lower moisture and a mould wash.

Remember Define mismatch and core shift. 2 marks

Mismatch is a cope and drag offset at the parting line. Core shift is movement of the core, making wall thickness uneven.

Takeaways

  1. Moulding sand must be strong enough to hold its shape, permeable enough to let gas out, and refractory enough to survive the pour; the three pull against each other and the mix is a compromise.
  2. A pattern is the casting plus allowances - shrinkage, draft, machining, shake and distortion - so a wrong allowance gives a wrong part even with a perfect mould.
  3. Pattern shape and moulding method are chosen together: complex parting lines need split or loose piece patterns, and large or heavy jobs move from bench to floor to pit.
  4. In a gating system the choke sets the flow rate; a pressurized system keeps the runner full but jets, while a non-pressurized system fills smoothly and traps slag in a skim bob.
  5. A riser works only if it stays liquid longer than the casting it feeds, which happens when the riser modulus is larger than the casting modulus.
  6. Chvorinov's rule, t = k(V/A)², lets you compare freezing times of any two shapes without knowing the metal in detail; the shape with the larger volume-to-area ratio freezes last.
  7. Freezing starts at the cold mould wall and moves inward; a narrow freezing range gives a skin and a columnar zone, a wide range gives a mushy zone that is hard to feed.
  8. Melting must give the right composition and enough superheat; pouring must be smooth, central and fast enough to beat freezing but slow enough to avoid turbulence.
  9. Shell, investment, die and centrifugal casting each trade mould cost against tolerance, surface finish and production rate; sand casting stays cheapest for large, one-off parts.
  10. A defect's shape and position name its cause: rounded gas holes mean permeability or venting, ragged incomplete corners mean cold metal, cracks at a corner mean restrained shrinkage.

Apply

Apply A plate casting has volume 1200 cm³ and total cooling surface area 900 cm². A riser is a cylinder of diameter equal to its height. Using Chvorinov's rule with n = 2, find the riser size so that it freezes 25% later than the casting. 13 marks

Compute casting modulus M = V/A = 1200/900 = 1.33 cm; Freezing time ratio (t_r/t_c) = (M_r/M_c)² = 1.25; So M_r = M_c √1.25 ≈ 1.49 cm; For cylinder d = h: V = πd³/4, A = πd²/2 + πd²/4 = ... gives M = V/A = d/6; Hence d ≈ 8.9 cm, check the arithmetic; State that a real riser is made a little larger for safety.

Apply Compare pressurized and non-pressurized gating systems and state which you would choose for (a) a tall steel casting and (b) an aluminium casting with a delicate sand core. 10 marks

Choke position: at ingates for pressurized, at sprue base for non-pressurized; Pressurized: runner full, small ingates, high velocity, more turbulence; Non-pressurized: larger downstream areas, slower entry, slag floats; Choice (a): non-pressurized or partly pressurized for smooth filling of steel; Choice (b): non-pressurized, low velocity protects the core, with a skim bob; Always design so the sprue does not draw air: taper the sprue.

Apply For each case, suggest a casting process and give reasons: 100,000 small zinc alloy toy parts, a jet engine turbine blade in a nickel superalloy, a 2 m long cast iron water pipe, a 5-tonne machine tool bed. 12 marks

Zinc toys: hot chamber pressure die casting; high rate, good finish, low melting point; Turbine blade: investment casting; exact shape, superalloy, little machining; Water pipe: centrifugal casting; dense wall, no core, uniform thickness; Machine tool bed: sand casting in a pit; huge size, small batch, cheap; Each choice justified by tolerance, batch size, melting point and size.

Apply A steel casting shows a large open cavity at the top of the heaviest section. Explain why it formed and how you would redesign the mould, using the idea of directional solidification. 12 marks

The heaviest section has the largest modulus, so it freezes last; No feed reaches it, so the last liquid leaves a shrinkage cavity; Place a riser on or beside that section, with riser modulus about 1.2 times the casting modulus; Feed the riser to the hot spot; use chills or padding so thinner parts freeze first; Keep a temperature gradient rising toward the riser; Feed the section through a short neck so the neck freezes after the casting.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Classify the common casting defects and, for any four, show how the shape and location of the defect points to its cause and remedy. 15 marks

Evaluate Compare sand casting, die casting and investment casting on tolerance, surface finish, tooling cost, production rate and suitable metals, and recommend one for an automotive rocker cover in aluminium. 13 marks

Evaluate Explain solidification of a pure metal and of an alloy, and discuss how the freezing range affects the feeding of a riser. 12 marks

Pure metal: freezes at one temperature, a smooth wall-to-centre front; Alloy: freezes between liquidus and solidus, a mushy zone of dendrites; Structure zones: chill zone, columnar zone toward the heat flow, equiaxed centre; Narrow range: skin forms, short feeding channel, riser works well; Wide range: dendrites block the channel, feeding is difficult, dispersed microshrinkage; Consequences for riser size and for choosing a process.

Analyze A foundry gets cracks at the corners of a box-shaped casting and blowholes on the top face. Analyse both problems and propose a combined remedy that does not simply raise the pouring temperature. 14 marks

Glossary

Blowhole
A smooth, round, gas-filled hole inside or on the surface of a casting.
Centrifugal casting
Casting in a spinning mould, where centrifugal force holds the metal against the wall.
Chill
A metal or graphite insert in the mould wall that draws heat away fast and speeds local freezing.
Chvorinov's rule
Total freezing time t = k(V/A)², where k is a mould-and-metal constant, V the volume and A the heat-losing area.
Cope
The top half of a moulding flask.
Core
A separate sand shape set in the mould to form a hole or internal passage in the casting.
Cupola
A vertical shaft furnace, charged with alternate layers of coke, metal and limestone, used for cast iron.
Directional solidification
Design of mould and risers so that freezing progresses steadily toward the riser.
Draft
Taper given to the vertical faces of a pattern so it can be lifted out of the sand.
Drag
The bottom half of a moulding flask, on which the pattern is first placed.
Fluidity
The distance a metal flows in a standard channel before it stops; it falls as superheat falls.
Grain fineness number
An AFS index of sand coarseness; a high number means fine sand, low permeability and a smoother surface.
Green strength
Compressive strength of moist moulding sand, measured on a standard rammed specimen.
Hot tear
A ragged crack formed during cooling when contraction is blocked by a rigid mould or core.
Ingate
The last, smallest opening through which metal enters the mould cavity from the runner.
Modulus
The ratio M = V/A of a casting or riser; larger M means slower freezing.
Mould
The refractory cavity, usually rammed sand, that gives liquid metal its shape and removes its heat.
Parting line
The surface that separates cope from drag and along which the pattern is withdrawn.
Pouring basin
A funnel-shaped cup at the top of the sprue that receives the ladle stream and helps slag to float.
Riser
A reservoir of liquid metal connected to the cavity, supplying metal as the casting contracts on freezing.
Sand inclusion
Loose sand or scale trapped in the casting, giving a ragged foreign spot.
Shell moulding
Casting in a thin, resin-bonded sand shell made over a heated metal pattern.
Shrinkage cavity
A rough void left where the last metal to freeze had no liquid feed.
Superheat
The temperature of the melt above its liquidus, in °C, at the moment of pouring.
