2.1 Eulerian and Lagrangian descriptions; system and control volume

Fluid motion can be described in two ways. Follow one marked particle and record its path: the Lagrangian view. Stand at fixed points and record the velocity passing each point: the Eulerian view. Mass can also be tracked in two ways. A system is a fixed collection of mass whose boundary moves with the fluid. A control volume is a chosen region of space through which mass flows. Pipes, pumps, turbines and pollution studies all use one or both pairs.

You need Unit 1: the fluid as a continuum, and the idea of a velocity field.

Two descriptions of one motion

A fluid has no fixed identity. At every point (x, y, z) and time t, the velocity is a vector V. The list of these vectors over space is the velocity field.

V(x, y, z, t) = u(x, y, z, t) i + v(x, y, z, t) j + w(x, y, z, t) k

V is the velocity vector at a fixed point (x, y, z) at time t, in m/s; u, v, w are its x, y, z components in m/s; i, j, k are unit vectors along x, y, z.

The Eulerian description uses this field. You stand at fixed points and record what passes. A flowmeter clamped to a pipe, a camera watching a point in a channel, and a weather station are Eulerian instruments.

The Lagrangian description follows a marked particle. Label it by its starting position r0 = (x0, y0, z0). Its path is R(r0, t), and its velocity is ∂R/∂t with r0 held fixed. A drifting buoy, a dye blob and a marked fish follow this view.

Both describe the same motion. The Eulerian field is shorter to write and is the normal engineering view. The Lagrangian path tells where one piece of fluid goes; pathlines are drawn from it.

System and control volume

A system is a fixed collection of mass. Its boundary is a material surface: the same particles stay on it, so no mass crosses. A system can change shape, volume and temperature; its mass stays constant.

A control volume is a chosen region of space. Mass can cross its boundary, the control surface. The control volume may be fixed (a pipe section), moving (a pump impeller), or deforming (a balloon).

Identification: water inside a sealed bottle is a system; water between two flanges of a municipal supply pipe is a control volume. A dye blob released in a river is a system; the river reach between two bridges is a control volume.

Some syllabi call the Eulerian–Lagrangian pair methods of analysis and the mass pair the system–control volume approach. The two devices answer different questions: how does this mass move, or what passes through this region?

Which view to use

Use a control volume for machines and pipes. A pump, turbine, nozzle or pipe bend has a clear inlet and outlet; mass enters and leaves. A pipe junction is a common control volume. The control volume lets you apply mass and momentum balances to the device without tracking every particle. The continuity equation is the mass balance on this region.

Use a system to follow a fixed mass. A pollutant blob, a fuel spray or a cooling parcel is easier as a system. The pollutant mass stays the same; its shape does not. Conservation of mass for a system is simple; Unit 3's Reynolds transport theorem converts system laws into control volume laws.

Steady flow does not mean zero acceleration. In steady flow the field at a fixed point does not change with time (∂V/∂t = 0), but a particle can still accelerate by moving to a place with different velocity. A steady flow through a contracting pipe accelerates the water; the two-part acceleration is in velocity and acceleration of fluid particles.

For a quick check, ask whether the boundary is made of the same particles or is a fixed geometric surface.

Worked example

Question. A particle's position is x = 1 + 2t and y = 3t², with x and y in metres and t in seconds. (a) Find the particle velocity and acceleration in the Lagrangian view. (b) Write the Eulerian velocity field by eliminating t. (c) Evaluate the vertical velocity at the point (3, 3).

  1. Differentiate x with respect to t at fixed particle label: u = dx/dt = 2 m/s.
  2. Differentiate y: v = dy/dt = 6t m/s.
  3. Differentiate again: ax = du/dt = 0 m/s²; ay = dv/dt = 6 m/s².
  4. Eliminate t: t = (x − 1)/2 s. Then v = 6t = 3(x − 1) m/s. The horizontal component is u = 2 m/s everywhere, so V = 2 i + 3(x − 1) j.
  5. At (3, 3), x = 3 m, so v = 3(3 − 1) = 6 m/s. Check: x = 3 gives t = 1 s and y = 3(1)² = 3 m, so the point lies on the path. This Eulerian field has no t, so it is steady, yet ay = 6 m/s² is not zero.

Answer. The velocity is u = 2 m/s and v = 6t m/s, the acceleration is ay = 6 m/s², and the vertical velocity at (3, 3) is 6 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Steady flow does not mean zero acceleration. At a fixed point ∂V/∂t = 0, but a particle can accelerate by moving into a region of different velocity; a steady contraction still accelerates water.
  • The mass inside a control volume is not fixed. Mass can enter and leave; only a system has fixed mass.
  • A system boundary moves with the fluid; a control surface is a chosen boundary and may be fixed, moving or deforming. Do not use the words as synonyms.
  • Eulerian is not the same as steady. Eulerian means watch fixed points; the field may change with time.
  • A control volume need not be fixed in space; a pump impeller or a balloon is a moving or deforming control volume.

2.2 Classification of flows: steady, uniform, laminar, turbulent

A flow can be named in four independent ways. Does the velocity at a fixed point change with time? Does it change from place to place? Is the motion orderly or randomly mixed? How many space coordinates does the velocity depend on? Each question has a plain test, and the answers combine freely. Engineers use these names to pick the right formula: a steady-flow energy equation for a nozzle, a uniform-flow assumption for a long pipe, a laminar formula for oil in a narrow tube.

You need the Eulerian velocity field from Chapter 2.1, so that V is known as a function of position and time.

Steady and unsteady flow

The first naming question is about time only. A flow is steady at a point when the velocity there does not change with time, and unsteady when it does.

∂V/∂t = 0 at every fixed point

V is the velocity vector at a fixed point, in m/s; t is time, in s; ∂/∂t is the partial derivative with position (x, y, z) held constant.

  1. Mark one fixed point in the flow field.
  2. Measure or compute V at that point at several instants.
  3. If V is the same every time, the flow is steady at that point.
  4. Repeat elsewhere. The flow is steady only if the test passes everywhere.

A tap opened and left alone gives steady flow: the velocity at the tap mouth stays the same all morning. A pump trip during a Chennai power cut makes the velocity at each fixed point fall with time, so that flow is unsteady. A monsoon river is unsteady; a pipeline carrying a fixed discharge is steady.

Steady does not mean zero acceleration. A particle can speed up by moving to a place where the velocity is higher, even when nothing changes with time. That is the convective acceleration of Chapter 2.4.

Uniform and non-uniform flow

The second question is about place. A flow is uniform when the velocity does not change from point to point along the flow direction at a given instant.

∂V/∂s = 0 along the flow

s is distance measured along the flow direction, in m; V is the velocity at that instant, in m/s.

In a long straight pipe of constant diameter the average velocity is the same at every section, so the flow is uniform. In a nozzle the area shrinks and the velocity rises from section to section, so the flow is non-uniform. A widening channel is non-uniform because its depth sets the area.

Laminar and turbulent flow

The third question is about order. In laminar flow the fluid moves in thin layers that slide over each other and never mix. A dye thread injected into the stream stays a single clean thread far downstream. Honey poured from a spoon behaves this way.

In turbulent flow the motion is randomly mixed. Eddies form and break, particles cross between layers, and the velocity at a fixed point fluctuates about a mean. Fast smoke from a lamp, or water from a fully open tap, shows this.

  • Laminar: ordered layers, no cross-mixing, quiet, dye thread intact.
  • Turbulent: random eddies, strong mixing, hissing, dye thread destroyed.
  • Which appears depends on speed, pipe size and viscosity together, through the Reynolds number. That number and the changeover belong to Unit 4, Viscous Flow Through Pipes.

Laminar and steady are different ideas. A laminar flow can be unsteady, and a turbulent flow can be steady in the mean: the time-averaged velocity at a point stays constant even though it fluctuates.

Dimensions, compressibility, ideal fluid

The fourth question counts coordinates. A flow is one-dimensional when the velocity depends on one space coordinate only, two-dimensional for two, three-dimensional for three.

  • 1-D: average velocity along a pipe varies with distance along the axis only.
  • 2-D: flow between long parallel plates, far from the side walls.
  • 3-D: flow past a short bridge pier, or water entering a bell-mouth intake.

Compressible flow has density that changes with pressure; incompressible flow has constant density. Liquids such as water are treated as incompressible, and gases are incompressible at low speed. High-speed gas flow is covered in Unit 3, Fluid Dynamics and Flow Measurement.

A real fluid has viscosity and resists shear; an ideal fluid is a model with zero viscosity, used when friction is negligible. Unit 1, Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics, defines both. A flow is rotational if fluid elements spin about their own axes, and irrotational if they do not; Chapter 2.6 treats that properly.

Open channels use the same tests, adding gradually varied and rapidly varied as sub-classes of non-uniform flow. A surge or bore is rapidly varied and unsteady, as when a gate closes quickly on a canal.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at a steady 0.02 m³/s through a horizontal nozzle whose diameter falls from 100 mm at the inlet to 50 mm at the outlet. Find the area and mean velocity at each section. Then classify the flow with respect to time, place and number of coordinates.

  1. Area of a circle: A = πd²/4, with d in metres.
  2. Inlet: d₁ = 100 mm = 0.100 m, so A₁ = π(0.100)²/4 = 0.007854 m².
  3. Outlet: d₂ = 50 mm = 0.050 m, so A₂ = π(0.050)²/4 = 0.0019635 m².
  4. The same discharge passes both sections (continuity, Chapter 2.5): Q = A₁V₁ = A₂V₂.
  5. V₁ = Q/A₁ = 0.02/0.007854 = 2.5465 m/s.
  6. V₂ = Q/A₂ = 0.02/0.0019635 = 10.1859 m/s.
  7. Check: 0.007854 × 2.5465 = 0.02 m³/s and 0.0019635 × 10.1859 = 0.02 m³/s. Discharge is unchanged.
  8. Time test: discharge is fixed, so at any fixed point the velocity does not change with time. The flow is steady.
  9. Space test: velocity rises from 2.5465 m/s to 10.1859 m/s along the nozzle, so the flow is non-uniform.
  10. Coordinate test: mean velocity depends on distance along the axis only, so the flow is one-dimensional.

Answer. A₁ = 0.007854 m², V₁ = 2.5465 m/s, A₂ = 0.0019635 m², V₂ = 10.1859 m/s; the flow is steady, non-uniform and one-dimensional.

Exam traps

  • Concluding that a non-uniform flow must be unsteady. The tests are independent: a nozzle with fixed discharge is steady and non-uniform.
  • Saying a steady flow has zero acceleration. Only ∂V/∂t is zero; the convective part remains, and in a nozzle it is large.
  • Treating uniform flow as meaning the velocity is identical everywhere in the field. It means no change along the flow direction at one instant, usually the cross-section average.
  • Mixing up laminar and steady. Laminar describes order of motion; steady describes constancy in time. A turbulent flow can be steady in the mean.
  • Forgetting that a slowly varying discharge, such as a pipe filling during pump start, is unsteady even when the velocity across the section is nearly uniform: unsteady uniform flow.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Instruments answer the same four questions. A hot-wire or laser Doppler probe records velocity at a fixed point against time: a flat record means steady, fluctuations about a constant mean mean steady in the mean (as in turbulent flow), and a drift means unsteady. The randomness of the fluctuations, not their presence, marks turbulence. Particle image velocimetry maps whole planes at one instant and shows non-uniformity. A CFD solver is set to steady or unsteady before the mesh is built, and choosing wrongly wastes hours of computing.

2.3 Streamlines, pathlines and streaklines

A velocity field gives a speed and a direction at every point of a flow. Three kinds of curve draw that field on paper: the streamline, tangent to the velocity at one instant; the pathline, the track of one particle over time; and the streakline, the line of dye released from one point. Engineers use them to sketch flow through pipes and around bodies, and to read flow-visualisation photographs. The three are the same curve only when the flow is steady.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the Eulerian velocity components u, v, w, and Chapter 2.2 for what steady flow means.

Three drawings of one velocity field

In the Eulerian view a flow is a field: at every fixed point and every instant there is a velocity vector with components u, v and w, in m/s, along the axes x, y and z, in metres. The same field can be drawn in three ways. A general name for all three drawings is flow lines.

  • Streamline: a curve that, at one chosen instant, is tangent to the velocity at every point on it.
  • Pathline: the actual track traced by one marked fluid particle over a period of time.
  • Streakline: at one instant, the line joining all the particles that have already passed through one chosen fixed point.

What each drawing holds fixed makes the difference. A streamline freezes time and shows the whole field at that instant. A pathline freezes one particle and shows its history. A streakline freezes the release point and shows where all its visitors have reached. Dye from a nozzle in a water channel gives a streakline. A long-exposure photograph of a single tracer gives a pathline.

The streamline never crosses (except at a stagnation point)

Take a point where the velocity is V = ui + vj + wk. A small element of the streamline through that point is dr = dxi + dyj + dzk. For the curve to be tangent to the velocity, dr must be parallel to V, so dr × V = 0. Written out, this gives the streamline equation.

dx/u = dy/v = dz/w

u, v, w are the velocity components in m/s along x, y, z in metres; dx, dy, dz are the increments of the curve in metres. In two dimensions it becomes dy/dx = v/u, the slope of the streamline in the xy-plane.

Solving this first-order differential equation gives a family of curves with one constant. The point through which the streamline must pass fixes that constant, exactly as in the worked example below.

Two streamlines cannot cross. If they did, the crossing point would have two tangents, so the fluid there would need two velocities at once. The one exception is a stagnation point, where u = v = w = 0, the direction is undefined, and streamlines may meet and split.

A bundle of neighbouring streamlines forms a stream tube. Fluid cannot cross the tube wall, because the wall runs along the velocity everywhere. The tube behaves like a pipe with no leakage, and the discharge through every section of it is the same (see Chapter 2.5).

Pathline, streakline, and when all three merge

A pathline follows one particle. If the particle is at (x, y, z) at time t, its path obeys the three equations below, integrated with time.

dx/dt = u, dy/dt = v, dz/dt = w

u, v, w are the velocity components in m/s at the particle's position and at that same time t; x, y, z are the particle's coordinates in metres and t is in seconds.

A streakline is the photograph of a dye filament. Fix the release point P. Every particle that has passed through P leaves a visible dot; the curve through all those dots at one instant is the streakline. Smoke rising from an incense stick in still room air is a streakline: it marks the trail of particles that left the tip, not the future path of any one of them.

In steady flow the velocity at a fixed point does not change with time. A particle released at P then follows a fixed curve, every later particle released at P follows the same curve, and the instant photograph of arrivals is that curve too. So streamline, pathline and streakline all coincide. In unsteady flow they generally differ.

Exam habit: name the flow first. Steady flow, and you may use any of the three names for the one curve. Unsteady flow, and the question is asking which of the three it wants.

Worked example

Question. A steady two-dimensional velocity field has u = 2x m/s and v = 3y m/s, where x and y are in metres. Find the equation of the streamline passing through the point (1 m, 2 m). Hence find the value of y on this streamline at x = 2 m and at x = 4 m.

  1. Check steadiness: u and v contain no t, so the field is steady and the streamline is a fixed curve in space.
  2. Slope of the streamline: dy/dx = v/u = 3y/(2x).
  3. Separate the variables: dy/y = (1.5)(dx/x).
  4. Integrate both sides: ln y = 1.5 ln x + ln C, so y = C x^1.5, with C in m^(−0.5).
  5. Apply the point (1, 2): 2 = C(1)^1.5, so C = 2 m^(−0.5).
  6. Streamline through (1, 2): y = 2x^1.5, with x and y in metres.
  7. At x = 2 m: y = 2(2)^1.5 = 5.657 m.
  8. At x = 4 m: y = 2(4)^1.5 = 2 × 8 = 16 m.

Answer. The streamline is y = 2x^1.5 with x and y in metres; it passes through y = 5.657 m at x = 2 m and y = 16 m at x = 4 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A dye filament is photographed in a flow where the velocity at a fixed point changes with time. The photograph shows a straight line, but streamlines drawn for the same region at that instant are curved. Is this possible, and why?

  1. The photograph of a dye filament records a streakline: it joins all particles that earlier left the injection point.
  2. Each particle left the point at a different time and travelled through a region where the velocity was changing.
  3. So the streakline is a record of past velocities, not of the velocity field at the present instant.
  4. The streamline is drawn from the velocity field at the present instant only.
  5. In unsteady flow the two are different curves, so a straight streakline with curved streamlines is possible.
  6. Only in steady flow would both have to be the same curve.

Answer. Yes. In unsteady flow the streakline records past velocities while the streamline records the present field, so the two curves need not match.

Exam traps

  • Drawing two streamlines that cross. They can meet only at a stagnation point, where the velocity is zero and the direction is undefined.
  • Assuming the pathline and the streamline are the same curve in unsteady flow. They coincide only in steady flow; in unsteady flow, state which curve you mean.
  • Quoting the family y = C x^1.5 and stopping there. The given point must be substituted to fix C, or the answer is incomplete.
  • Turning the slope upside down. In the xy-plane the streamline slope is dy/dx = v/u, not u/v.
  • Calling a steady dye filament a pathline. At any instant a dye filament is a streakline; it equals a pathline only because the flow is steady.

2.4 Velocity and acceleration of fluid particles

A fluid particle can speed up for two reasons: the velocity at its fixed point changes with time, or the particle drifts to a place where the velocity is different. The first is the local part. The second is the convective part. Their sum is the material derivative, the true acceleration of the particle. Engineers use it to size pumps and pipes, to read pitot tubes in moving air, and to check whether a bend in a pipeline will load its supports. The same derivative appears in weather models and in CFD software.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the Eulerian field view, Chapter 2.2 for steady and uniform flow, and Chapter 2.3 for streamlines.

Two changes in one derivative

Take a velocity field V = u i + v j + w k. u, v, w are the velocity components in the x, y, z directions, in m/s. x, y, z are fixed coordinates in metres. t is time in seconds. A fluid particle is not fixed. In dt seconds it moves dx = u dt, dy = v dt, dz = w dt. Its velocity changes for two reasons: time passes at the point, and the particle moves to a new point.

a = DV/Dt = ∂V/∂t + u ∂V/∂x + v ∂V/∂y + w ∂V/∂z

a is acceleration in m/s²; V = u i + v j + w k is velocity in m/s; x, y, z are metres; t is seconds. ∂V/∂t is the local part. The three u, v, w terms form the convective part.

The local part ∂V/∂t is the change at a fixed point. The convective part is the change caused by the particle moving to a place where the field is different. Some syllabi call DV/Dt the substantial derivative or Stokes derivative. In Cartesian components:

ax = ∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x + v ∂u/∂y + w ∂u/∂z; ay = ∂v/∂t + u ∂v/∂x + v ∂v/∂y + w ∂v/∂z; az = ∂w/∂t + u ∂w/∂x + v ∂w/∂y + w ∂w/∂z

ax, ay, az are acceleration components in m/s²; u, v, w are velocity components in m/s; x, y, z are metres; t is seconds.

Steady, uniform and curved flow

In steady flow ∂V/∂t = 0 at every point, so the local part vanishes. The convective part can still be large. Water speeding up as a pipe narrows is a steady flow with particle acceleration. In uniform flow the velocity is the same at every point at a given instant, so all ∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z terms vanish and the convective part is zero. The local part can remain if the whole field changes with time.

Along a streamline, split acceleration into tangent and normal directions. For steady flow, the tangent part is V dV/ds. The normal part is V²/R, directed toward the centre of curvature. This normal term is centripetal acceleration. A fluid element on a curved path has it even at constant speed. R is the radius of curvature in metres.

a_s = V dV/ds, a_n = V²/R

a_s is acceleration along the streamline in m/s²; a_n is normal acceleration toward the centre of curvature in m/s²; V is speed in m/s; s is distance along the streamline in m; R is radius of curvature in m.

How to compute acceleration at a point

Work only with the field. Do not follow a guessed particle path. The coordinates x, y, z and time t are independent variables. The chain rule supplies the convective terms because the particle's x, y, z change with t. A common mistake is to differentiate u with respect to t while holding x, y, z fixed and stop there. That gives only the local part.

  1. Write u, v, w from the field. Set any missing component to zero.
  2. Put the given x, y, z, t into u, v, w to get the velocity there in m/s.
  3. Find ∂u/∂t, ∂u/∂x, ∂u/∂y, ∂u/∂z and the same derivatives of v and w at that point.
  4. Substitute into the three Cartesian component formulas.
  5. Write the acceleration vector with units m/s².

Worked example

Question. A flow field has u = 3x + 2t and v = 4y, with u and v in m/s, x and y in metres, and t in seconds. The z component w is zero. Find ax and ay at x = 1 m, y = 2 m, t = 1 s.

  1. At x = 1 m, y = 2 m, t = 1 s: u = 3(1) + 2(1) = 5 m/s; v = 4(2) = 8 m/s; w = 0.
  2. Derivatives: ∂u/∂t = 2 m/s², ∂u/∂x = 3 s⁻¹, ∂u/∂y = 0, ∂u/∂z = 0; ∂v/∂t = 0, ∂v/∂x = 0, ∂v/∂y = 4 s⁻¹, ∂v/∂z = 0.
  3. ax = ∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x + v ∂u/∂y + w ∂u/∂z = 2 + (5)(3) + (8)(0) + 0 = 17 m/s².
  4. ay = ∂v/∂t + u ∂v/∂x + v ∂v/∂y + w ∂v/∂z = 0 + (5)(0) + (8)(4) + 0 = 32 m/s².
  5. az = 0 for this field.

Answer. At the given point, a = (17 i + 32 j) m/s², so ax = 17 m/s² and ay = 32 m/s².

Exam traps

  • Steady flow does not mean zero acceleration. ∂V/∂t is zero, but u ∂u/∂x and similar convective terms can be large.
  • Do not differentiate u = 3x + 2t along a guessed path. x, y, z, t are independent field coordinates; the chain rule adds u ∂u/∂x and the other convective terms.
  • Keep units: ∂u/∂t is in m/s², ∂u/∂x is in s⁻¹, and u ∂u/∂x is in m/s². Do not treat ∂u/∂x as an acceleration.
  • Uniform flow kills only the convective part. Unsteady uniform flow still has local acceleration. Steady flow kills only the local part.
  • Curved streamlines give normal acceleration V²/R even when speed is constant. Straight streamlines have infinite R and zero normal term.

2.5 Continuity equation

Mass cannot appear or vanish. The continuity equation is that one sentence written in the language of flow: whatever enters a region either leaves it or piles up inside. Engineers use it to find an unknown velocity at a pipe branch, to size a nozzle, to check a flow meter, and to work out how long a tank takes to fill. It is the first of the conservation laws of fluid mechanics, and the only one that needs nothing more than mass and a boundary.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the control volume idea, and Chapter 2.2 for what steady and incompressible mean.

The balance on a control volume

A control volume is a region you draw in space with a boundary you choose. Fluid crosses the boundary; you do not follow individual particles. That fixed window is the natural bookkeeping device for flow.

The mass crossing a surface in one second is the mass flow rate ṁ, in kilograms per second. For a flat area A with fluid of density ρ moving at velocity V straight through it:

ṁ = ρ A V

ṁ is the mass flow rate, kg/s; ρ the density, kg/m³; A the area, m²; V the average velocity normal to the area, m/s.

The volume flow rate is Q = A V, in m³/s, and ṁ = ρQ. A municipal water connection is quoted in Q; a gas pipeline is quoted in ṁ or in normal cubic metres, because its density changes.

The general statement is short: the rate at which mass inside the control volume changes, plus the net rate at which mass flows out through the boundary, equals zero.

(d/dt) ∫ ρ dV + ∮ ρ (V · n) dA = 0

dV is a volume element inside, m³; dA an area element on the boundary, m²; n the outward unit normal; V the velocity vector, m/s. The surface term is positive where fluid leaves.

Two simplifications carry most of the exam marks. In steady flow nothing inside changes with time, so the first term is zero and mass out equals mass in. In incompressible flow ρ is constant and cancels, so volume out equals volume in.

Σ ṁ_in = Σ ṁ_out and Σ Q_in = Σ Q_out

the first line holds for steady flow of any fluid; the second holds when the fluid is also incompressible. The sums run over all openings of the control volume.

The differential form

Shrink the control volume to a small fixed box with sides dx, dy, dz. Let u, v, w be the velocity components in the x, y, z directions, all in m/s.

  1. Mass enters the left face at rate ρ u dy dz.
  2. Mass leaves the right face at rate ρ u dy dz + [∂(ρu)/∂x] dx dy dz.
  3. The net outflow in the x direction is therefore [∂(ρu)/∂x] dx dy dz. The y and z faces behave the same way.
  4. The mass stored inside is ρ dx dy dz, changing at the rate (∂ρ/∂t) dx dy dz.
  5. Add the three outflows to the storage term, then divide the whole balance by the volume dx dy dz.

∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρu)/∂x + ∂(ρv)/∂y + ∂(ρw)/∂z = 0

ρ is density, kg/m³; u, v, w the velocity components, m/s; t time, s; x, y, z the coordinates, m. The three space terms together are div(ρV).

For constant density the ρ comes out of every derivative and the whole equation collapses to one line:

∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y + ∂w/∂z = 0

this is div V = 0, the incompressible continuity equation. In two dimensions it is ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0.

In polar coordinates (r, θ), with radial velocity v_r and tangential velocity v_θ, the same statement is (1/r)∂(r v_r)/∂r + (1/r)∂v_θ/∂θ = 0.

This equation is why the stream function is useful: any ψ whose derivatives give u and v satisfies div V = 0 automatically, so every such ψ describes a possible incompressible flow.

Stream tubes, branches and nozzles

A stream tube is a bundle of streamlines forming a closed tube. No fluid crosses its walls, so the same mass passes every section of it.

ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂

subscripts 1 and 2 are two sections of the same stream tube or pipe; A is the cross-section area, m²; V the average velocity normal to it, m/s; ρ the density, kg/m³.

For a liquid, ρ cancels and A₁V₁ = A₂V₂. A nozzle narrows A, so V rises; a diffuser widens A, so V falls. The same relation sizes the throat of a Venturimeter, which is Unit 3.

At a branching junction in steady flow, the volume flows into the junction add up to the volume flows out. The worked example below is exactly this calculation.

Gases keep the density. Air at 5 bar is roughly five times as dense as air at 1 bar, so a gas main that loses pressure along its length speeds up even when the pipe area does not change; the mass flow stays the same. Water does not behave this way: one kilogram of water fills about one litre at any ordinary pressure, so ρ is treated as constant.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at 1.5 m/s through a 200 mm diameter pipe. The pipe branches into a 100 mm diameter pipe carrying water at 1.2 m/s and a second branch of 150 mm diameter. The flow is steady and water is incompressible. Find the volume flow rate in each branch and the velocity in the second branch.

  1. Convert diameters to metres and find the three areas, using A = πd²/4, not πd². A₁ = π × 0.200²/4 = 0.031416 m². A₂ = π × 0.100²/4 = 0.007854 m². A₃ = π × 0.150²/4 = 0.017671 m².
  2. Main pipe discharge: Q₁ = A₁V₁ = 0.031416 × 1.5 = 0.047124 m³/s ≈ 0.0471 m³/s.
  3. First branch discharge: Q₂ = A₂V₂ = 0.007854 × 1.2 = 0.009425 m³/s ≈ 0.0094 m³/s.
  4. Steady incompressible balance at the junction: Q₁ = Q₂ + Q₃, so Q₃ = 0.047124 − 0.009425 = 0.037699 m³/s ≈ 0.0377 m³/s.
  5. Velocity in the second branch: V₃ = Q₃/A₃ = 0.037699 / 0.017671 = 2.133 m/s ≈ 2.13 m/s.
  6. Check: 0.009425 + 0.037699 = 0.047124 m³/s, which matches the main pipe.

Answer. The second branch carries 0.0377 m³/s at a velocity of about 2.13 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Diameter is not radius. A 200 mm pipe has A = π × 0.200²/4 = 0.0314 m², not π × 0.200². Squaring the diameter without dividing by 4 is the most common lost mark.
  • Do not drop ρ when the fluid is a gas. A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ is for constant density only; for air or natural gas write ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂.
  • At a junction, add volume flows only when the same fluid flows in all the pipes. Mixing two different liquids needs a mass balance, not a Q balance.
  • div V = 0 does not mean the velocity is constant. It means the velocity field has no source or sink; the velocity can still rise in a nozzle.
  • For a tank that is filling or emptying, the storage term is not zero, so A₁V₁ = A₂V₂ fails. Use ṁ_in − ṁ_out = d(mass inside)/dt.

2.6 Rotational and irrotational flow; vorticity

A fluid element can move in four ways: it can shift, spin, stretch and shear. Vorticity measures the spin alone. The idea exists because two flows can look identical on a streamline sketch and behave very differently: one carries spin, the other does not. Pumps, turbines, aerofoils and weather models all rest on knowing which is which. Water circling a wash basin drain and water in a stirred tumbler look similar in a photograph, yet only one has vorticity. This chapter defines the spin, links it to the velocity field through the curl, and connects it to circulation.

You need: Chapter 2.4 for the velocity field and its derivatives, and Chapter 2.3 for streamlines.

The four motions of a fluid element

Mark a small square of fluid with sides dx and dy, both in metres, along the x and y axes. After a short time dt, in seconds, the square has done four separate things.

  • It has moved to a new place: translation.
  • It has turned: rotation.
  • Its sides have grown or shrunk: linear deformation, also called linear strain.
  • The angle between its sides has changed from 90°: shear deformation, or shear strain.

Vorticity is built from the second item only. The other three are set aside.

Take the side lying along x. Its right end moves faster in the y direction than its left end by (∂v/∂x) dx, so this side turns at the rate ∂v/∂x. Take the side lying along y. Its top end moves faster in the x direction than its bottom end by (∂u/∂y) dy, so this side turns at the rate −∂u/∂y; the minus sign appears because a positive ∂u/∂y tilts it clockwise. The rotation of the element is the average of the two.

ω_z = ½ (∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y)

ω_z is the angular velocity of the element about the z axis, in rad/s, positive counterclockwise when viewed along +z; u and v are the velocity components in m/s along x and y in m.

Vorticity is defined as twice this angular velocity. The doubling is a convention that keeps the curl formula clean, and forgetting it is the commonest error in this chapter.

ζ_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y

ζ_z is the z component of vorticity, in s⁻¹; a positive value means counterclockwise spin.

Vorticity as the curl, and circulation

In three dimensions vorticity is a vector, the curl of the velocity field.

ζ = ∇ × V

ζ is the vorticity vector in s⁻¹; ∇ × is the curl operator; V is the velocity vector in m/s. Its components are ζ_x = ∂w/∂y − ∂v/∂z, ζ_y = ∂u/∂z − ∂w/∂x and ζ_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y, with w the velocity in m/s along z.

Circulation Γ is the line integral of velocity around a closed curve C. It is the macroscopic version of vorticity: vorticity is the spin at a point, circulation is the total spin effect over a patch.

Γ = ∮ V · ds

Γ is the circulation in m²/s; ds is a small step along the closed curve C in m; the integral is taken anticlockwise by the right-hand rule.

Stokes theorem joins the two ideas. The circulation around a loop equals the flux of vorticity through any surface stretched across that loop.

Γ = ∬ ζ · n dA

n is the unit normal to the surface, chosen by the right-hand rule from the direction of travel round C; dA is a small area in m².

Irrotational flow, and two flows that look alike

A flow is irrotational when ζ = 0 at every point. Elements then translate, stretch and shear, but they never spin. This does not mean the streamlines are straight. Curvature of a streamline says nothing about spin.

A free vortex is the standard irrotational swirling flow. Its velocity is v_θ = K/r, where v_θ is the tangential speed in m/s, r is the radius in m and K is a constant in m²/s. A free vortex has zero vorticity everywhere except on the axis r = 0, where the field is not defined and the vorticity is unbounded. Water draining from a wash basin comes close to this.

ζ_z = (1/r) ∂(r v_θ)/∂r

v_θ is the tangential velocity in m/s and r the radius in m. Put v_θ = K/r and you get zero; put v_θ = ωr and you get 2ω.

A forced vortex has v_θ = ωr, like a spinning solid disc. Its vorticity is a uniform 2ω, so it is rotational everywhere. Both vortices are compared in Chapter 2.9.

Real flow in a pipe is rotational. The speed falls from a maximum on the axis to zero at the wall, so ∂u/∂y ≠ 0 and every element off the axis is spinning. See Unit 4, Viscous Flow Through Pipes. The payoff of irrotationality is that a velocity potential exists, which is Chapter 2.7.

Worked example

Question. A velocity field has u = 2y and v = −2x, in m/s, with x and y in metres. (a) Find the z component of vorticity. (b) Find the rate and sense of rotation of a fluid element. (c) State whether the flow is rotational. (d) Find the circulation around the square with corners (0,0), (1,0), (1,1), (0,1) in metres.

  1. Write the field: u = 2y m/s, v = −2x m/s.
  2. Differentiate v with respect to x: ∂v/∂x = −2 s⁻¹.
  3. Differentiate u with respect to y: ∂u/∂y = 2 s⁻¹.
  4. (a) ζ_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y = −2 − (2) = −4 s⁻¹.
  5. (b) ω_z = ζ_z / 2 = −4/2 = −2 rad/s. Negative means clockwise, so the element spins at 2 rad/s clockwise.
  6. (c) ζ_z = −4 s⁻¹ is not zero, so the flow is rotational.
  7. (d) Stokes theorem: Γ = ζ_z × A, with A = 1 m × 1 m = 1 m². So Γ = −4 × 1 = −4 m²/s.
  8. Check by direct line integral of (u dx + v dy) round the square. Bottom edge y = 0, dy = 0: ∫ 2(0) dx = 0. Right edge x = 1, dx = 0: ∫ −2(1) dy from 0 to 1 = −2 m²/s. Top edge y = 1, dy = 0: ∫ 2(1) dx from 1 to 0 = −2 m²/s. Left edge x = 0, dx = 0: ∫ −2(0) dy = 0. Total = 0 − 2 − 2 + 0 = −4 m²/s, which agrees.

Output

-4.0
-2.0
-4.0

Answer. The z vorticity is −4 s⁻¹, the element spins at 2 rad/s clockwise, the circulation around the 1 m square is −4 m²/s, and the flow is rotational.

Exam traps

  • Reading curved streamlines as rotational flow. A free vortex curves strongly and is still irrotational, so judge by ζ = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y, never by the picture.
  • Dropping the factor of two. Vorticity is ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y; angular velocity is half of that. An answer of 4 rad/s where 2 rad/s is correct loses full marks.
  • Reversing the sign convention. ζ_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y, not ∂u/∂y − ∂v/∂x, and positive means counterclockwise looking along +z.
  • Writing that a free vortex has zero vorticity everywhere. It is zero everywhere except the axis r = 0, where it is singular and unbounded.
  • Confusing irrotational with rigid or undeformed. An irrotational element stretches and shears freely; only its spin is zero.

2.7 Stream function and velocity potential

A velocity field has two components u and v at every point. Two scalar functions can store that pair. The stream function ψ works for any two-dimensional incompressible flow and gives continuity for free. The velocity potential φ works only when the flow is also irrotational, and gives the velocity as the gradient of a scalar. Engineers use these functions to draw flow nets, compute discharge between streamlines, and solve potential-flow problems around bodies.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the velocity field, Chapter 2.5 for continuity, and Chapter 2.6 for vorticity.

Stream function ψ

For a two-dimensional flow in the x-y plane, let u be the velocity component in the x direction and v the component in the y direction, both in m/s. Define the stream function ψ(x,y) by u = ∂ψ/∂y and v = −∂ψ/∂x. ψ has units of square metres per second (m²/s), because a velocity in m/s is differentiated with respect to a length in m. The signs are part of the definition; swapping them reverses the flow.

u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x

u and v are velocity components in m/s; ψ is the stream function in m²/s; x and y are coordinates in m.

Put these into the continuity equation for steady two-dimensional incompressible flow, ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0. You get ∂²ψ/∂x∂y − ∂²ψ/∂y∂x = 0. The mixed derivatives are equal, so continuity is satisfied automatically. That is why ψ exists for every such flow: pick any smooth ψ and you get a valid incompressible velocity field.

Along a streamline, dψ = (∂ψ/∂x)dx + (∂ψ/∂y)dy = −v dx + u dy. For a streamline, dy/dx = v/u, so dψ = 0. Hence ψ is constant on a streamline. The difference ψ_B − ψ_A between two streamlines is the volume flow rate per unit depth between them, in m²/s. Take the magnitude for discharge; the sign tells the direction. A single stream function needs two-dimensional incompressible flow. A general three-dimensional or compressible flow does not have one scalar ψ that gives both components this way.

Velocity potential φ

When the flow is irrotational, the vorticity is zero everywhere. In two dimensions this means ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y = 0. Then the velocity can be written as the gradient of a scalar φ, called the velocity potential. Use this sign convention: u = −∂φ/∂x and v = −∂φ/∂y. φ has units of m²/s. The minus sign makes the velocity point from high φ to low φ. Keep this convention in every problem; do not switch signs halfway.

u = −∂φ/∂x, v = −∂φ/∂y

φ is the velocity potential in m²/s; u and v are velocity components in m/s; the minus sign is the convention used here.

Lines of constant φ are equipotential lines. Along such a line dφ = −u dx − v dy = 0, so the velocity is perpendicular to the line. Thus equipotential lines cut streamlines at right angles. Because φ exists only for irrotational flow, using φ for a rotational flow is wrong. The stream function can still exist there.

Laplace equation and the pair

For a two-dimensional incompressible irrotational flow, substitute the definitions into continuity and zero vorticity. For ψ, zero vorticity gives ∂²ψ/∂x² + ∂²ψ/∂y² = 0. For φ, continuity gives ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² = 0. Both are Laplace's equation, written ∇²ψ = 0 and ∇²φ = 0. Solutions are called harmonic functions.

  • ψ: exists for 2-D incompressible flow, even if rotational; constant on streamlines; difference gives discharge per unit depth.
  • φ: exists only for irrotational flow; constant on equipotential lines; lines cross streamlines at 90°.
  • Use ψ when you need streamlines and discharge; use φ when you need a scalar potential and the flow is irrotational.

Worked example

Question. A two-dimensional flow has stream function ψ = x² − y², where ψ is in m²/s and x, y are in metres. (a) Find u and v at the point x = 2 m, y = 3 m. (b) Check continuity at that point. (c) Compute the vorticity and decide whether a velocity potential exists. (d) If it exists, find φ and its value at the same point, taking φ = 0 at the origin.

  1. Differentiate the stream function: u = ∂ψ/∂y = −2y and v = −∂ψ/∂x = −2x. Both are in m/s.
  2. At x = 2 m and y = 3 m: u = −2(3) = −6 m/s and v = −2(2) = −4 m/s.
  3. Continuity: ∂u/∂x = 0 and ∂v/∂y = 0, so ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0 s⁻¹. Continuity holds.
  4. Vorticity: ω_z = ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y = −2 − (−2) = 0 s⁻¹. The flow is irrotational, so φ exists.
  5. For φ: u = −∂φ/∂x = −2y gives ∂φ/∂x = 2y. Integrate in x: φ = 2xy + f(y).
  6. Also v = −∂φ/∂y = −2x gives ∂φ/∂y = 2x. Differentiate φ = 2xy + f(y) with respect to y: 2x + f′(y) = 2x, so f′(y) = 0 and f(y) = C.
  7. Thus φ = 2xy + C. With φ = 0 at the origin, C = 0, so φ = 2xy. At x = 2 m, y = 3 m: φ = 2(2)(3) = 12 m²/s.
  8. Check Laplace: ψ_xx = 2 and ψ_yy = −2, so ψ_xx + ψ_yy = 0. Also φ_xx = 0 and φ_yy = 0, so φ_xx + φ_yy = 0.

Answer. At (2 m, 3 m): u = −6 m/s, v = −4 m/s, continuity gives 0 s⁻¹, vorticity is 0 s⁻¹, and φ = 2xy = 12 m²/s.

Exam traps

  • Using u = ∂ψ/∂x and v = ∂ψ/∂y instead of u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x. The signs and which derivative go with which component are fixed.
  • Writing u = +∂φ/∂x in one line and u = −∂φ/∂x in another. Choose the minus convention and keep it.
  • Assuming every flow has a velocity potential. Rotational flow, such as a forced vortex, has no φ even though it may have a stream function.
  • Forgetting that ψ in this form needs two-dimensional incompressible flow. A general 3-D flow does not have one scalar ψ.
  • Giving ψ or φ units of m/s. Both are m²/s.

2.8 Flow nets

A flow net is a mesh of two line families. Streamlines show the direction of flow, and equipotential lines join points of equal velocity potential. In a two-dimensional irrotational flow the two families cross at right angles, and the mesh cells are drawn as curvilinear squares. The mesh turns a hard field problem into a sketch: line spacing gives speed, and the number of channels gives discharge per unit depth. Engineers use flow nets to estimate flow past a body, flow through a contraction in a wide channel, and seepage under a dam.

You need Chapter 2.7 for the stream function ψ and velocity potential φ, and Chapter 2.3 for streamlines.

Streamlines and equipotential lines

A streamline is a curve whose tangent at every point is the velocity direction. Along one streamline the stream function ψ is constant. The stream function exists for any two-dimensional incompressible flow, as Chapter 2.7 explains. An equipotential line joins points with the same velocity potential φ. The velocity potential exists only when the flow is irrotational. In many flow-net problems the flow is both incompressible and irrotational, so both families exist.

At a point, velocity is tangent to the streamline and normal to the equipotential line. Those two directions are at right angles. Therefore streamlines and equipotential lines meet at 90°. This right-angle rule is the main check on a hand-drawn net. If a crossing is not square, the sketch is wrong.

v = Δψ / Δn

v is the speed in m/s; Δψ is the change in stream function between two neighbouring streamlines, in m²/s; Δn is the perpendicular distance between them, in m.

For the potential lines, v = Δφ / Δs, where Δφ is the change in velocity potential and Δs is the distance between neighbouring equipotential lines. In a curvilinear square Δn ≈ Δs, so the changes in ψ and φ across one cell have the same size.

Drawing the net

Start from the boundaries. A solid wall is a streamline because no fluid crosses it. A reservoir face is an equipotential because the total head is constant along it. Fix these lines before drawing any interior line.

  1. Draw the boundaries: solid walls as streamlines, reservoir faces as equipotentials.
  2. Choose one increment and use it for both families: Δψ = Δφ.
  3. Sketch streamlines at equal steps of ψ and equipotentials at equal steps of φ with that same increment.
  4. Adjust the net by adding or removing whole streamlines and whole equipotentials, which changes the increments together, until every cell is a curvilinear square: the average distance between streamlines equals the average distance between equipotentials.
  5. Count the flow channels N_f and the potential drops N_d.

The cells need not be perfect squares. A curvilinear square has four right-angle corners and an average width equal to its average length. In a narrow passage the cells become small, which correctly shows fast flow. Iterate: moving one line changes the neighbouring cells, so redraw until the square condition holds everywhere.

q = Nf Δψ

q is the discharge per unit depth in m²/s (equivalently m³/s per metre of depth); N_f is the number of flow channels between the two boundary streamlines; Δψ is the change in stream function between neighbouring streamlines in m²/s.

Reading discharge, velocity and head

Between two neighbouring streamlines the discharge per unit depth is Δψ. With N_f channels between the two boundary streamlines, q = N_f Δψ. The unit is square metres per second (equivalently cubic metres per second per metre of depth). This is the flow rate through a slice one metre thick. Count channels as spaces between streamlines, not the streamlines themselves.

Velocity at a point is inversely proportional to the spacing between streamlines. Close lines mean a small Δn and a large v. This is why the net crowds near a sharp corner or a sudden contraction. The same crowding appears between equipotential lines.

Δh = H / Nd

Δh is the head drop between neighbouring equipotential lines in m; H is the total head difference between the two boundary equipotentials in m; N_d is the number of equipotential divisions.

Uses include sketching flow past a cylinder or an airfoil, flow through a sudden contraction in a wide rectangular channel or duct (a pipe contraction is axisymmetric and needs its own treatment), and seepage under a dam. In seepage, the net gives flow rate and pore pressure; a soil mechanics course takes that calculation further. Some syllabi call φ lines equipotential lines and ask only for the number of flow channels and potential drops.

Worked example

Question. In a flow net, the stream function changes by 0.05 m²/s between neighbouring streamlines. There are 10 flow channels between the two boundary streamlines, and 20 equipotential divisions for a total head difference of 2 m. Find (a) the discharge per unit depth between the boundary streamlines and (b) the head drop between neighbouring equipotential lines.

  1. Write the data: Δψ = 0.05 m²/s, N_f = 10, N_d = 20, H = 2 m.
  2. Discharge per unit depth: q = N_f Δψ = 10 × 0.05 = 0.5 m²/s (that is 0.5 m³/s through each 1 m thick slice).
  3. Head drop per equipotential division: Δh = H / N_d = 2 / 20 = 0.1 m.

Answer. The discharge is 0.5 m²/s (0.5 m³/s per metre of depth), and the head drop between neighbouring equipotential lines is 0.1 m.

Exam traps

  • Drawing rectangular cells instead of curvilinear squares. The square condition is average width equals average length, with right-angle corners.
  • Counting the boundary streamlines as flow channels. N_f is the number of spaces between streamlines, not the number of streamlines.
  • Treating the head drop per equipotential division as a velocity. Δh is in metres of head, not m/s.
  • Confusing ψ and φ increments. Δψ gives discharge between streamlines; Δφ gives velocity through v = Δφ / Δs, and only for irrotational flow.
  • Forgetting that q is per unit depth. A two-dimensional flow net gives q in m²/s, which is the flow rate through a slice one metre thick.

2.9 Free and forced vortex flow

A free vortex has v θ falling as 1/r: fast near the axis, slow far out, no outside torque. A forced vortex turns like a wheel, driven by a torque. Both appear in drains, stirred cups, cyclone separators and pump casings. This chapter gives velocity, pressure and free-surface shape for each.

You need vorticity and irrotational flow from Chapter 2.6, and the stream function from Chapter 2.7.

Free vortex: v θ = K/r

Open a tank drain and let the stirring die. The swirl becomes steady: a particle at radius r circles with tangential velocity v θ that drops as r grows. No torque acts, so each ring keeps its angular momentum.

v θ = K / r

v θ = tangential velocity (m/s), r = radius (m), K = vortex constant (m²/s).

Angular momentum per unit mass r v θ equals K everywhere. The vortex is free because no external torque maintains it.

Γ = 2 π K

Γ = circulation (m²/s), the line integral of velocity round a closed curve. For a circle, Γ = v θ × 2πr = 2πK, same at every radius.

Vorticity, the spin of an element about its own centre, is zero except on the axis. Streamlines are circles but elements do not rotate, as Chapter 2.6 explained: shear between rings cancels the turning about the axis.

p = p∞ − ρ K² / (2 r²)

p = pressure at radius r (Pa), p∞ = pressure far out, ρ = density (kg/m³). Pressure falls inward; the inward pressure force supplies centripetal acceleration.

h = K² / (2 g r²)

h = depth below far-field level (m), g = 9.81 m/s². The surface is a hyperboloid: deep funnel at the axis, flat far out.

Forced vortex: v θ = ω r

Stir tea. The paddle drags water round; viscosity carries motion outward until the whole body turns together like a wheel. Each particle takes the same time for one turn: rigid-body rotation. The impeller supplies torque to overcome wall friction and losses; adjacent rings do not slide in steady rotation.

v θ = ω r

ω = angular speed (rad/s), r = radius (m), v θ = tangential velocity (m/s). Velocity grows linearly with radius, opposite to a free vortex.

Each element spins about its centre while going round the axis, so vorticity is 2ω everywhere.

p = p₀ + ρ ω² r² / 2

p₀ = pressure on the axis (Pa), ρ = density (kg/m³). Pressure rises outward.

h = ω² r² / (2 g)

h = height above the axial level at radius r (m). The surface is a paraboloid, a bowl rising to the wall.

The fluid lifted at the wall comes from the middle: the axial level drops as the rim rises above the rest level.

Rankine vortex, and where you meet both

A real drain swirl is mixed. Near the axis viscosity forces solid-body turning; outside a small core it is a free vortex. The Rankine model matches a forced core of radius r c to a free outer flow with K = ω r c². Velocities and slopes agree at r c; curvature changes. Only a few syllabi teach this.

A stirred vessel has a forced vortex near the impeller and a free vortex near the wall. A cyclone separator uses the free outer vortex to throw dust to the wall. A centrifugal pump casing holds a free vortex, so pressure rises smoothly as water slows toward delivery (Unit 7, Centrifugal and Reciprocating Pumps).

Takeaways

  1. The Eulerian view describes the velocity field at fixed points; the Lagrangian view follows individual particles; both give the same physics.
  2. A system is a fixed mass; a control volume is a fixed region in space; the Reynolds transport theorem connects them.
  3. Steady means no change with time at a point; uniform means no change with place at an instant.
  4. Streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines are different curves; they coincide only in steady flow.
  5. A fluid particle's acceleration has a local part ∂V/∂t and a convective part (V·∇)V.
  6. The continuity equation expresses conservation of mass for a control volume.
  7. Vorticity ∇×V measures the spin of a fluid element; curved streamlines can still be irrotational.
  8. The stream function exists for any 2-D incompressible flow; the velocity potential exists only for irrotational flow.
  9. A flow net gives velocity from the spacing of streamlines and discharge from the number of flow channels.
  10. A free vortex has vθ = K/r and zero vorticity away from the centre; a forced vortex has vθ = ωr and uniform vorticity.

Mass balance: rate of mass out − in = −∂m_cv/∂t; Integral form: ∮ρV·n dA + ∂/∂t ∫ρ dV = 0; Differential form: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρV) = 0; Steady: ∇·(ρV) = 0; Incompressible 2-D: ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0.

Analyze A velocity field is given by u = 4x, v = −4y. Determine whether the flow is irrotational. If so, find the velocity potential and the stream function. 13 marks

Check continuity: ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 4 − 4 = 0; Vorticity: ∂v/∂x − ∂u/∂y = 0 − 0 = 0, irrotational; Velocity potential: u = ∂φ/∂x → φ = 2x² + f(y); v = ∂φ/∂y → f'(y) = −4y → φ = 2x² − 2y²; Stream function: u = ∂ψ/∂y → ψ = 4xy + g(x); v = −∂ψ/∂x → g'(x) = 0 → ψ = 4xy; Check: φ and ψ satisfy Laplace equation.

Evaluate Explain the physical meaning of local and convective acceleration. Use the examples of flow in a nozzle and flow in a pipe bend to discuss when each term is important. 13 marks

Local: ∂V/∂t, important in unsteady flow (starting pump); Convective: (V·∇)V, important when velocity changes along path; Nozzle: steady flow, convective acceleration dominates; Pipe bend: direction changes, convective acceleration normal to flow; Unsteady start-up: local term significant.

Glossary

Circulation
The line integral of velocity around a closed curve: Γ = ∮V·dl.
Control volume
A fixed region in space used for mass and momentum balances.
Convective derivative
The change due to motion through a spatial gradient: (V·∇).
Equipotential line
A line on which the velocity potential is constant.
Flow net
A mesh of streamlines and equipotential lines.
Fluid particle
A very small mass of fluid that moves as a single unit.
Forced vortex
A vortex with vθ = ωr and uniform vorticity, like a rotating rigid body.
Free vortex
A vortex with vθ = K/r and zero vorticity away from the axis.
Irrotational flow
Flow with zero vorticity everywhere.
Laminar flow
Smooth, layered flow with no macroscopic mixing.
Local derivative
The rate of change at a fixed point: ∂/∂t.
Material derivative
The rate of change following a fluid particle: D/Dt = ∂/∂t + V·∇.
Non-uniform flow
Flow in which velocity varies from point to point.
Pathline
The trajectory of a single fluid particle.
Potential flow
An irrotational, incompressible flow that can be described by a velocity potential.
Steady flow
Flow in which velocity at a fixed point does not change with time.
Streakline
The locus of particles that have passed through a fixed point.
Stream function
A scalar ψ with u = ∂ψ/∂y, v = −∂ψ/∂x for 2-D incompressible flow.
Stream tube
A tube formed by streamlines; no flow crosses its walls.
Streamline
A curve everywhere tangent to the instantaneous velocity.
Turbulent flow
Chaotic flow with random eddies and fluctuations.
Two-dimensional flow
Flow in which velocity depends on two space coordinates and time.
Uniform flow
Flow in which velocity is the same everywhere at a given instant.
Unsteady flow
Flow in which velocity at a fixed point changes with time.
Velocity potential
A scalar φ with V = ∇φ for irrotational flow.
Vorticity
The curl of velocity, ∇×V; twice the angular velocity of a fluid element.
