3.1 Reynolds transport theorem

The laws of mechanics are written for a fixed mass of fluid. A pipe, a nozzle or a pump casing is easier to draw as a fixed region in space. The Reynolds transport theorem joins the two views: it turns the rate of change of a property of a fixed mass into a rate of change inside a chosen region plus the net rate carried out across its boundary. Mass, momentum, angular momentum and energy conservation all fall out of it by choosing one property. Pump design, jet impact on a Pelton bucket and the force on a pipe bend all rest on it.

You need Unit 2: the system and control volume distinction, the continuity equation, and streamlines.

System, control volume, and the bridge between them

A system is a fixed mass of fluid — a named set of particles that we follow. It moves, stretches and changes shape, but no mass crosses its boundary. Newton's second law, d(mV)/dt = ΣF, is written for exactly this kind of body.

A control volume is a fixed region in space. Its boundary is the control surface, drawn once and left in place. Fluid enters and leaves through it. A pipe section, a nozzle, a pump casing, the gap between two turbine blades — all are drawn this way, because a fixed region is easy to mark on a drawing and easy to instrument.

The difficulty: physics speaks about the system, the geometry speaks about the control volume. The Reynolds transport theorem is the bridge. It says the rate of change of a property of the system equals the rate of change of that property inside the control volume plus the net rate at which the property is carried out across the control surface.

Two words before the formula. An extensive property B grows with the amount of fluid present: mass, momentum, energy. Its intensive value is b = B/m, the same property per kilogram. With density ρ in kg/m³, the amount of B inside a small volume dV is ρb dV.

Statement of the theorem

Take any control volume that coincides with the system at the instant considered. Then:

dB/dt = ∂/∂t ∫<sub>CV</sub> ρb dV + ∫<sub>CS</sub> ρb (V·n) dA

B is the extensive property of the system (kg, kg·m/s, J); b = B/m is that property per unit mass; ρ is density in kg/m³; V is the fluid velocity vector in m/s; n is the unit normal to the control surface, pointing outward from the control volume; dV is a volume element in m³ and dA an area element in m². The first integral runs over the control volume, the second over its surface.

First term, the storage term: how fast the amount of B inside the region is growing. It is a partial derivative because the region is fixed while ρ and b may change with time at each point.

Second term, the flux term: ρb is the amount of B per unit volume, V·n is the volume leaving per unit area per second, so the product integrated over the surface is the net rate at which B is carried out. Where flow leaves, V·n is positive. Where flow enters, V·n is negative, so an inflow subtracts.

At the chosen instant the control surface and the system boundary are the same surface, so both sides describe the same fluid.

Steady form and the four special cases

For steady flow nothing inside changes with time and the storage term vanishes:

dB/dt = Σ ρ b (V·n) A,  and with one inlet 1 and one outlet 2:  dB/dt = ṁ(b₂ − b₁)

ṁ = ρQ is the mass flow rate in kg/s, Q is discharge in m³/s, A is the area of a section in m², and b₁, b₂ are the values of b at inlet and outlet.

  • Put b = 1: B is mass, dB/dt = 0, so ṁ₂ = ṁ₁. This is the continuity equation.
  • Put b = V: B is momentum, dB/dt = ΣF, the force on the fluid. Worked out in 3.6.
  • Put b = r × V, where r is the position vector from a chosen point: B is angular momentum and the result gives torque about that point.
  • Put b = e = u + V²/2 + gz, where u is internal energy per kg, V²/2 kinetic energy per kg and gz potential energy per kg: B is energy.

Some syllabi call it the Reynolds transport equation, or present it as Leibniz's rule applied to a volume whose boundary moves with the fluid. If the control volume itself moves or deforms, replace V by the relative velocity W = V − Vcv inside the surface integral. Shrinking the control volume to a thin streamtube gives Euler's equation.

Worked example

Question. Water (density 1000 kg/m³) flows steadily through a fixed control volume. At inlet section 1 the area is 0.01 m² and the velocity is 2 m/s. At outlet section 2 the area is 0.02 m². Find the discharge, the outlet velocity, the net rate at which momentum leaves the control volume, and the force acting on the fluid.

  1. Discharge from the inlet: Q = A₁V₁ = 0.01 × 2 = 0.02 m³/s.
  2. Continuity for steady flow gives V₂ = Q/A₂ = 0.02 / 0.02 = 1 m/s.
  3. Flow is steady, so the storage term ∂/∂t ∫ρb dV is zero and only the surface flux survives.
  4. Take b = V and the outward normal at each section. Momentum flux out = ρQ(V₂ − V₁) = 1000 × 0.02 × (1 − 2) = −20 N.
  5. The negative sign means the net momentum flux is directed upstream; equivalently, 20 N of momentum per second leaves in the negative direction.
  6. From the momentum form, ΣF on the fluid = momentum out − momentum in = −20 N, so the force on the fluid is 20 N pointing upstream, that is against the flow direction.

Answer. Q = 0.02 m³/s, V₂ = 1 m/s, net momentum flux = −20 N (20 N upstream), and the force on the fluid is 20 N acting upstream.

Exam traps

  • Using the acceleration of the system in place of the surface flux integral. The theorem needs ρb(V·n) integrated over the surface, not a material derivative written at a point.
  • Forgetting that n points outward. An inlet gives V·n < 0, so inflow enters the sum with a minus sign; the commonest sign error in the unit.
  • Applying the steady form while the amount inside the control volume is changing, as in a tank being filled or a surge travelling down a pipe.
  • Mixing up B and b. Using momentum in kg·m/s where the per-unit-mass value V in m/s was needed, or the reverse, throws the answer off by a factor of ṁ.
  • Writing the storage derivative as a total derivative. Inside a fixed region ρ and b vary with position as well as time, so it must be ∂/∂t.

The control volume is the tank. The storage term ∂/∂t ∫ρb dV is non-zero because the mass and momentum inside grow or shrink with time. The surface terms are the inflow and the outflow. The surface of the control volume is moving, so the outward flux uses the velocity of the fluid relative to the surface, W = V − Vcv, in place of V. The storage term is unchanged in form because it is evaluated at the same instant in a fixed frame. With this replacement, b = r × V gives the torque transmitted to the fluid, as used in Unit 7 for pump work.

3.2 Euler's equation of motion along a streamline

Along a single streamline in an inviscid fluid, pressure and weight are the only forces on a small fluid element, and they exactly produce its acceleration. Newton's second law written on that element gives Euler's equation. Engineers use it to find how pressure changes when velocity changes in a nozzle, a pipe transition or a venturimeter, before adding real losses. It is the differential statement that later integrates into Bernoulli's equation, so it explains why a faster stream has lower pressure.

You need: Unit 2 on streamlines and fluid acceleration, and Unit 1 on pressure and viscosity; the control-volume idea appears in Reynolds transport theorem.

The element and the forces

A streamline is a curve whose tangent at every point is the direction of the velocity at that instant. In steady flow the velocity at a fixed point does not change with time. An inviscid fluid has no shear stress, so only pressure and weight act on a fluid particle. Take a small cylindrical element along one streamline: length ds, cross-section dA, density ρ. Let θ be the angle between the streamline and the vertical, and let z be elevation upward, so dz = ds cos θ. Pressure p acts on the upstream face and p + dp on the downstream face.

The pressure force along the streamline is p dA − (p + dp)dA = −dp dA. The weight is ρg dA ds downward; its component along the streamline is −ρg dA ds cos θ. The mass is ρ dA ds. The acceleration along the streamline is dV/dt. A particle moves ds in time dt, so dV/dt = (dV/ds)(ds/dt) = V dV/ds. Newton's second law along the streamline gives:

−dp dA − ρg dA ds cos θ = ρ dA ds V dV/ds

p is pressure (Pa); ρ is density (kg/m³); g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²); dA is cross-sectional area (m²); ds is element length (m); V is speed along the streamline (m/s); θ is angle from the vertical.

Divide by ρ dA ds and use dz/ds = cos θ. The result is Euler's equation along a streamline: dp/ρ + g dz + V dV = 0. Here dp is pressure change (Pa) over ds, dz is elevation change (m) and dV is speed change (m/s). Some books measure θ from the horizontal; then sin θ replaces cos θ, and the final equation is the same.

Three forms and the assumptions

On a horizontal streamline dz = 0, so Euler's equation becomes:

dp/ds = −ρ V dV/ds

dp/ds is the pressure gradient along the streamline (Pa/m); ρ is density (kg/m³); V is local speed (m/s); dV/ds is the velocity gradient (s⁻¹).

Where velocity rises, pressure falls. Across a curved streamline there is also a normal balance. With n measured outward from the centre of curvature, r the radius of curvature and V the local speed: dp/dn = ρ V²/r. Pressure rises outward; only a few syllabi include this normal form.

Assumptions: steady flow; inviscid fluid (no shear); the two points lie on the same streamline; gravity is the only body force. The differential form dp/ρ + g dz + V dV = 0 allows variable density because ρ remains inside dp/ρ. Constant density is needed only when the equation is integrated to Bernoulli in Bernoulli's equation. Euler's equation has no loss term and no pump or turbine work term, so do not apply it across a lossy fitting, a pump or a turbine.

Worked example

Question. Water flows along a horizontal streamline. The speed rises from 2 m/s to 3 m/s over a distance of 0.5 m. Density is 1000 kg/m³. Find the pressure gradient at the mid-point and the pressure change over that distance.

  1. For a horizontal streamline dz = 0. Euler's equation gives dp/ds = −ρV dV/ds.
  2. The velocity gradient is dV/ds = (3 − 2)/0.5 = 2 s⁻¹.
  3. At the mid-point V = (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5 m/s.
  4. dp/ds = −1000 × 2.5 × 2 = −5000 Pa/m. The negative sign means pressure falls in the flow direction.
  5. Over 0.5 m, using the mid-point gradient, Δp ≈ (dp/ds)Δs = −5000 × 0.5 = −2500 Pa.
  6. Exact check because dV/ds is constant: Δp = −ρ(V₂² − V₁²)/2 = −1000(9 − 4)/2 = −2500 Pa.

Answer. The pressure gradient at the mid-point is −5000 Pa/m; over 0.5 m the pressure change is −2500 Pa, that is, a fall of 2500 Pa.

Exam traps

  • Using Euler across streamlines: the normal balance is dp/dn = ρV²/r, not the along-streamline equation.
  • Dropping g dz on a sloping streamline; elevation change must be included, and sin/cos must match the angle definition.
  • Applying Euler through a pump, turbine, valve or long pipe where work or friction acts; Euler has no such terms.
  • Confusing Euler with Bernoulli: Euler is differential and allows variable density; Bernoulli needs integration and usually constant density.
  • Sign slip: on a horizontal streamline a speed rise gives a negative pressure gradient, so pressure falls, not rises.

3.3 Bernoulli's equation and its applications

Bernoulli's equation is the energy statement for steady, inviscid, incompressible flow along a streamline. It says pressure head plus elevation head plus velocity head stays constant from point to point. Each term is a length in metres of the flowing fluid. Engineers use it to find pressure changes in pipes, discharge from tanks, siphon flow and the reading of a pitot tube. It is the first tool to reach for when a flow problem gives two sections and no friction.

You need: Euler's equation along a streamline and the continuity equation from Unit 2.

The three heads

Take a streamline from point 1 to point 2. Bernoulli's equation is:

p₁/γ + z₁ + V₁²/(2g) = p₂/γ + z₂ + V₂²/(2g)

p is pressure in Pa (gauge or absolute, same at both points); γ = ρg is specific weight in N/m³; z is elevation above a fixed datum in m; V is velocity along the streamline in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

The ratio p/γ is the pressure head. z is the elevation head. V²/(2g) is the velocity head. Their sum H is the total head. Every term is in metres of the flowing fluid. The pair p/γ + z is the piezometric head; a piezometer or manometer tapping reads it.

In the figure, a horizontal converging pipe has total head 5 m. At the wide end the velocity head is small (about 0 m on the scale of the sketch) and the pressure head is about 5 m. At the narrow end the velocity head rises to 2 m while the pressure head falls to 3 m. The pressure falls because the fluid speeds up; the total head stays at 5 m.

If a streamline is brought to rest at a point, V = 0 and the pressure becomes p₀ = p + ρV²/2. This p₀ is the stagnation pressure. A pitot tube measures it, as Chapter 3.4 shows.

Where the equation comes from

Start from Euler along a streamline: (1/ρ)(dp/ds) + g(dz/ds) + V(dV/ds) = 0. Here s is distance along the streamline, V is speed, and dV/ds is the acceleration along it. Multiply by ds: dp/ρ + g dz + V dV = 0.

For constant density ρ, integrate from point 1 to point 2: (p₂ − p₁)/ρ + g(z₂ − z₁) + (V₂² − V₁²)/2 = 0. Rearrange and divide by g. You get Bernoulli's equation in head form.

The steady energy equation for unit weight of fluid is the same three-head sum, plus pump head h_p on the inlet side and loss h_L on the outlet side. Set h_p = 0 and h_L = 0 and you recover Bernoulli.

The assumptions are strict and must be checked before you use the equation.

  • Steady flow: no change with time at a fixed point.
  • Incompressible flow: ρ is constant.
  • Inviscid flow: no friction inside the fluid.
  • Along one streamline: do not jump between streamlines in a rotational flow.
  • No pump, turbine or other work device between 1 and 2.
  • Continuous flow: no cavitation or air pocket between the points.

Tank, siphon, pump and loss

Torricelli's law is Bernoulli with two simplifications. A tank has a free surface at point 1 and a small hole at point 2, a depth h below the surface. Both points are at atmospheric pressure, and the surface velocity is nearly zero. Bernoulli gives h = V₂²/(2g), so:

V₂ = √(2gh)

V₂ is ideal jet velocity in m/s; h is depth of the hole below the free surface in m; g = 9.81 m/s².

A real jet is slower than √(2gh) because of friction in the hole and the approach. The coefficient of velocity Cv = Vactual/Videal corrects for this; the contraction of the jet is corrected separately by the coefficient of contraction Cc, and the discharge coefficient is Cd = Cc × Cv. Chapter 3.5 covers these.

A siphon is a pipe that rises above the supply level and then falls. Apply Bernoulli from the upper reservoir surface to the crest. The pressure at the crest is p_crest = p_atm + ρg(z_surface − z_crest) − ρV²/2. If the crest is too high, p_crest falls below the vapour pressure and the siphon breaks. Siphon flow is treated with pipe losses in Unit 4.

With a pump or a loss between the two points, add the pump head and subtract the loss:

p₁/γ + z₁ + V₁²/(2g) + h_p = p₂/γ + z₂ + V₂²/(2g) + h_L

h_p is pump head in m; h_L is head loss in m; both in metres of the flowing fluid.

This form is the working equation for pipe systems. Unit 4 gives Darcy–Weisbach and minor losses. Pump and turbine heads belong to Units 6 and 7. The energy line and hydraulic gradient line are drawn in Unit 4, not here.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at 0.05 m³/s through a pipe of 15 cm diameter at a pressure of 150 kPa gauge. The pipe then rises 4 m and narrows to 10 cm diameter. Losses are neglected. Find the pressure at the upper section.

  1. Section 1: D₁ = 0.15 m, r₁ = 0.075 m, A₁ = π×0.075² = 0.01767 m². V₁ = Q/A₁ = 0.05/0.01767 = 2.83 m/s.
  2. Section 2: D₂ = 0.10 m, r₂ = 0.05 m, A₂ = π×0.05² = 0.007854 m². V₂ = 0.05/0.007854 = 6.37 m/s.
  3. Take datum at section 1: z₁ = 0 m, z₂ = 4 m. p₁ = 150000 Pa gauge. ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s².
  4. Bernoulli from 1 to 2: p₂/γ + z₂ + V₂²/(2g) = p₁/γ + z₁ + V₁²/(2g). Multiply by γ: p₂ = p₁ + ρg(z₁ − z₂) + ρ(V₁² − V₂²)/2.
  5. Substitute: p₂ = 150000 − 1000×9.81×4 + 500×(8.01 − 40.58). The bracket is −32.57 m²/s². So p₂ = 150000 − 39240 − 16285 = 94475 Pa.
  6. In kPa: p₂ = 94.5 kPa gauge.

Answer. The pressure at the upper section is about 94.5 kPa gauge.

Exam traps

  • Applying one Bernoulli equation across a pump, a turbine or a loss without adding h_p or h_L. The plain equation has no work device and no friction.
  • Mixing gauge and absolute pressure, or writing p/ρ instead of p/γ. p/ρ has units J/kg, not metres.
  • Using Bernoulli across streamlines in a rotational flow such as a forced vortex. It holds along one streamline; the constant can differ from streamline to streamline.
  • Forgetting the sign of z. A rise of 4 m subtracts ρg×4 from the pressure; a fall adds it.
  • Using the average velocity in V²/(2g) for a real pipe profile. The kinetic energy correction factor α is needed when the profile is far from uniform.

3.4 Venturimeter, orificemeter and pitot tube

Three common flow meters answer one question: how fast is the fluid moving, or how much passes each second? Each creates a pressure difference, measures it with a manometer, and converts it using Bernoulli plus continuity. The venturimeter and orificemeter measure discharge in a pipe. The pitot tube measures velocity at one point. You meet them in water supply lines, pump testing, aeroplane airspeed indicators and industrial piping.

You need: Bernoulli's equation from 3.3 and the continuity equation from Unit 2.

Venturimeter: throat, head and discharge

Venturimeter. A venturimeter is a pipe fitting with a converging cone, a short throat, and a diverging cone. The inlet area is A₁ (m²) and the throat area is A₂ (m²). The fluid accelerates into the throat, so its pressure falls. Pressure tappings at the inlet and throat connect to a U-tube manometer. The manometer reads the pressure difference.

p₁/(ρg) + V₁²/(2g) = p₂/(ρg) + V₂²/(2g)

p₁ and p₂ are pressures at inlet and throat (Pa); V₁ and V₂ are mean velocities (m/s); ρ is density (kg/m³); g = 9.81 m/s². The elevation terms cancel for a horizontal pipe.

Continuity gives A₁V₁ = A₂V₂. Replace V₁ by (A₂/A₁)V₂ in Bernoulli and solve for V₂. The ideal discharge is Q = A₂V₂. Real flow has friction and eddies, so multiply by a discharge coefficient Cd. The result is:

Q = Cd A₁ A₂ √(2gh) / √(A₁² − A₂²)

Q is discharge (m³/s); Cd is the discharge coefficient (about 0.95 to 0.98 for a clean venturimeter); A₁ and A₂ are inlet and throat areas (m²); h is the pressure head difference in metres of the flowing liquid; g = 9.81 m/s².

The head h must be in metres of the flowing liquid. A mercury U-tube on a water pipe does not read h directly. If the manometer deflection is x (m), then:

h = x(Sm/S − 1)

x is the manometer deflection (m); Sm is the specific gravity of the manometer liquid (mercury = 13.6); S is the specific gravity of the pipe liquid (water = 1.00); h is in metres of pipe liquid.

The diverging cone slows the fluid gently and recovers much of the pressure, so the permanent loss is small. This is why venturimeters are used for accurate measurement in water supply and pump testing, despite being larger and costlier than an orifice plate.

Orificemeter: a plate and a vena contracta

An orificemeter is a thin plate with a central hole, clamped between two pipe flanges. The hole area is A₀ (m²) and the pipe area is A₁ (m²). The jet contracts after the hole to a smaller area called the vena contracta. Pressure tappings are placed upstream and near the plate or vena contracta. The same Bernoulli and continuity steps apply, but the contracted area is not known. It is absorbed into the discharge coefficient.

Q = Cd A₀ √(2gh) / √(1 − (A₀/A₁)²)

Q is discharge (m³/s); Cd is about 0.6; A₀ is orifice area (m²); A₁ is pipe area (m²); h is head difference in metres of flowing liquid. An equivalent form is Q = Cd A₀A₁√(2gh)/√(A₁² − A₀²).

Use the same manometer conversion as for the venturimeter: h = x(Sm/S − 1). Cd is about 0.6, much lower than for a venturimeter, because the vena contracta and the sudden expansion create eddies. The permanent pressure loss is larger. The meter is cheap, compact, and easy to fit between flanges, so it is common in steam, air and water lines where high accuracy is not needed.

Pitot tube: velocity at one point

Pitot tube. A pitot tube is a small tube bent to face the flow. At the open nose the fluid comes to rest. That point is a stagnation point. Its pressure is the stagnation pressure p₀ (Pa). A static tapping gives the undisturbed static pressure p (Pa). Apply Bernoulli along the streamline from the undisturbed fluid to the nose:

V = √(2(p₀ − p)/ρ) = √(2gh)

V is local velocity (m/s); p₀ is stagnation pressure (Pa); p is static pressure (Pa); ρ is density (kg/m³); h is the manometer head difference in metres of flowing liquid.

A real tube has a coefficient Cv about 0.98: V = Cv√(2gh). A pitot-static tube combines the two ports: one faces the flow, the other has side holes for static pressure. Connect the two to a U-tube and the reading is the dynamic pressure directly. Aeroplanes use this as an airspeed indicator; wind tunnels and ducts use it for local velocity.

In a pipe, a pitot tube gives the velocity at its nose, not the mean velocity. The reading is largest at the centreline. To find discharge, traverse the tube across the section, measure V at several radii, and integrate, or use a calibration factor. Treating one centreline reading as the mean velocity overestimates Q.

Worked example

Question. A venturimeter has a pipe diameter of 25 cm and a throat diameter of 12.5 cm. It carries water. A mercury manometer connected to the inlet and throat reads 20 cm. Take Cd = 0.98 and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the discharge in m³/s and in L/s.

  1. Convert diameters to metres: D₁ = 0.25 m, D₂ = 0.125 m. Radii: r₁ = 0.125 m, r₂ = 0.0625 m.
  2. A₁ = π r₁² = π × 0.125² = 0.04909 m².
  3. A₂ = π r₂² = π × 0.0625² = 0.01227 m².
  4. Manometer conversion: x = 0.20 m, Sm = 13.6 for mercury, S = 1.00 for water. h = x(Sm/S − 1) = 0.20 × (13.6 − 1) = 2.52 m of water.
  5. Velocity head term: √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 2.52) = 7.03 m/s.
  6. Area terms: A₁² − A₂² = 0.04909² − 0.01227² = 0.002410 − 0.0001506 = 0.0022594 m⁴. √(A₁² − A₂²) = 0.04753 m². Also A₁A₂ = 0.04909 × 0.01227 = 0.0006023 m⁴.
  7. Q = Cd A₁A₂√(2gh) / √(A₁² − A₂²) = 0.98 × 0.0006023 × 7.03 / 0.04753 = 0.0873 m³/s.
  8. Convert to litres per second: 0.0873 m³/s × 1000 = 87.3 L/s.

Answer. The discharge is Q = 0.0873 m³/s, which is 87.3 L/s.

Exam traps

  • Using the throat area A₂ alone in place of the combined term A₁A₂/√(A₁² − A₂²). The throat area gives only the velocity, not the discharge.
  • Taking the manometer deflection x as the head h. For mercury in water, x = 0.20 m gives h = 2.52 m, not 0.20 m.
  • Treating a pitot tube reading as the mean velocity. It is the local velocity at the nose, usually the maximum at the centreline.
  • Using Cd = 0.98 for an orificemeter. Its Cd is about 0.6 because of the vena contracta; a venturimeter's Cd is 0.95 to 0.98.
  • Forgetting to halve the diameter when finding area. A 25 cm pipe has radius 0.125 m, not 0.25 m.

3.5 Orifices, mouthpieces, notches and weirs

A hole in a tank, a short tube pushed into that hole, a V-shaped cut in a channel wall: all three ask the same question, how much flows out? Bernoulli gives the ideal answer from the head alone. The real answer differs only by a coefficient, and that coefficient splits into a contraction part and a velocity part. This is how canals, irrigation outlets and rooftop tanks are gauged, and how a notch of known shape becomes a cheap flow meter.

You need Bernoulli's equation from 3.3 and the continuity equation from Unit 2, Fluid Kinematics.

The orifice and its three coefficients

An orifice is a hole in the wall or bottom of a tank. A sharp-edged orifice is cut in a thin plate, so the jet touches the plate only along a line. Put point 1 on the free surface and point 2 in the jet just outside the hole. Both are at atmospheric pressure, and the tank is large, so the surface velocity is nearly zero. Bernoulli then gives the ideal jet speed.

V = √(2gH)

V is the ideal jet speed in m/s, g = 9.81 m/s² is the gravitational acceleration, and H is the head of water above the centre of the orifice in m.

The real jet is slower because of friction, and thinner than the hole. Streamlines keep converging for a short distance outside the hole, so the jet is narrowest a little downstream. That section is the vena contracta. Cc = ac/a is the coefficient of contraction, jet area over hole area a, about 0.62 for a sharp edge. Cv = Vactual/V is the coefficient of velocity, about 0.98. Their product is the coefficient of discharge, Cd = Cc Cv, about 0.62.

Q = Cd a √(2gH)

Q is the discharge in m³/s, a is the area of the hole in m², Cd = Cc Cv ≈ 0.62, and H is the head above the centre of the hole in m.

A small orifice has H more than about five hole diameters, so the head is nearly the same at the top and bottom of the hole. A large orifice does not, and its discharge is integrated over the height: Q = (2/3) Cd b √(2g) (H23/2 − H13/2), where b is the width of the hole and H1, H2 are the depths from the surface to the top and bottom edges.

Mouthpieces: a short tube on the same hole

A mouthpiece is a short tube, two or three diameters long, fixed to the hole. An external mouthpiece projects outside the tank. An internal or re-entrant mouthpiece projects inside.

The external cylindrical mouthpiece has Cd ≈ 0.82, well above the 0.62 of the bare hole. The vena contracta forms inside the tube, and the space between it and the outlet runs below atmospheric pressure. That suction pulls extra water through, provided the tube runs full. If the pressure at the vena contracta falls to vapour pressure, air enters and the coefficient drops back to about 0.62.

The Borda mouthpiece is an internal tube whose jet does not touch the tube walls, so there is no suction behind the jet and Cd ≈ 0.5. A converging mouthpiece recovers pressure along its length and gives a discharge coefficient much closer to 1.

Notches and weirs: integrating over the depth

A notch is a cut in the side of a tank or channel, with its top edge open to the air. A weir is a crest built across an open channel with water flowing over it. A sharp-crested weir is a thin plate with a sharp upstream edge, and the same theory covers it.

Take a rectangular notch of crest length L with head H over the crest. A horizontal strip at depth h below the surface, of thickness dh, passes water at speed √(2gh) through area L dh, so dQ = Cd L √(2gh) dh. Integrating h from 0 to H gives the working formula.

Q = (2/3) Cd L √(2g) H3/2

Q is the discharge in m³/s, Cd ≈ 0.6 for a sharp-crested weir, L is the crest length in m, g = 9.81 m/s², and H is the head over the crest in m.

A triangular or V-notch of full angle θ has strip width 2(H − h) tan(θ/2) at depth h. The same integration now runs into a 5/2 power.

Q = (8/15) Cd √(2g) tan(θ/2) H5/2

θ is the full angle of the notch, H is the head over the vertex in m, and the other symbols are as above.

The higher power suits a V-notch to small flows, because the head does not become too tiny to read. A rectangular weir suits large flows.

End contraction: if the crest is shorter than the channel, the jet draws in at each end and the effective length becomes L − 0.1nH, where n counts the contracting ends, 0 for a suppressed weir spanning the full width and 2 for a crest clear of both sides. Velocity of approach: if the approach velocity in the channel is Va, its head ha = Va²/2g adds to H, so replace H by H + ha. The Francis formula folds the coefficient into one number for a suppressed rectangular weir: Q = 1.84 L H3/2 in SI units, with Q in m³/s, L in m and H in m; with approach velocity the head term becomes (H + ha)3/2 − ha3/2. Trapezoidal and Cipolletti weirs have sloped sides that keep the coefficient steady as the head changes.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular sharp-crested weir spans the full width of a channel, so there are no end contractions. The crest length is 3 m and the head over the crest is 0.40 m. Take Cd = 0.62 and neglect the velocity of approach. (a) Find the discharge. (b) The same 3 m crest is now installed so that one end is clear of the side wall (one end contraction); the crest length is still 3 m. Find the new discharge at the same head.

  1. √(2g) = √(2 × 9.81) = √19.62 = 4.429 m0.5/s.
  2. H3/2 = 0.403/2 = 0.40 × √0.40 = 0.40 × 0.6325 = 0.2530 m3/2.
  3. (2/3) Cd = 0.6667 × 0.62 = 0.4133.
  4. (a) Q = 0.4133 × 3 × 4.429 × 0.2530 = 1.240 × 4.429 × 0.2530 = 5.492 × 0.2530 = 1.389 m³/s.
  5. (b) One end contraction: Leff = L − 0.1nH = 3 − 0.1 × 1 × 0.40 = 3 − 0.04 = 2.96 m.
  6. Q = 1.389 × (2.96 / 3) = 1.389 × 0.9867 = 1.371 m³/s.

Exam traps

  • Head read in the wrong place. For a weir, read H three to four H upstream of the crest, where the surface is still flat. For an orifice, read H to the centre of the hole.
  • Forgetting the end contraction L − 0.1nH, or using n = 2 for a suppressed weir that touches both walls. A suppressed weir has n = 0.
  • Borrowing a coefficient across devices. Orifice Cd ≈ 0.62, external mouthpiece ≈ 0.82, sharp-crested weir ≈ 0.6. Each belongs to its own device and edge condition.
  • Mixing Cc and Cd. Cd = Cc × Cv is smaller than either one. Writing it upside down as Cd = Cv / Cc gives 0.98 / 0.62 ≈ 1.58, which is impossible: actual discharge can never exceed ideal discharge, so Cd can never exceed 1.
  • Dropping the velocity of approach in a narrow channel. The formula then uses H + ha, with ha = Va²/2g, not H alone.

3.6 Linear and angular momentum equations

Newton's second law for a flowing fluid says the net force on a chosen control volume equals the rate at which momentum leaves minus the rate at which it enters. The same idea with a moment arm gives torque. This chapter applies the steady linear and angular momentum equations to pipe bends, nozzles, sudden enlargements and sprinklers. These tools size pipe supports, thrust blocks and nozzle anchors, and they check forces that Bernoulli alone cannot give.

You need Reynolds transport theorem and the continuity equation from Unit 2.

Steady linear momentum equation

Choose a control volume (CV), a fixed region in space. The fluid inside changes with time, but the CV boundary stays put. Reynolds transport theorem with property velocity V gives the force equation. For steady flow the time derivative vanishes.

ΣF = ṁ(V₂ − V₁), ṁ = ρQ

ΣF is the vector sum of forces on the fluid in the CV, in N; ṁ is mass flow rate in kg/s; ρ is density in kg/m³; Q is volume flow rate in m³/s; V₁ and V₂ are mass-average velocities at inlet and outlet in m/s.

The forces are of three kinds. Pressure acts on every cut surface of the CV. Body force, usually weight, acts on the fluid mass. Reaction from a solid wall acts where the fluid touches the bend, vane or nozzle. The equation uses components: ΣFx = ṁ(V2x − V1x), and likewise for y and z.

  • Net force is on the fluid, not on the pipe.
  • Outflow momentum is positive; inflow momentum is negative.
  • At an inlet, pressure force points opposite the outward normal; for flow in +x at an inlet, pA acts in +x.

A common check: if velocity does not change, as in a straight pipe of constant area, the momentum flux term is zero. Then ΣF = 0, so pressure forces and wall force balance. This is why a straight pipe support carries little axial force from steady flow, while a bend support carries a large one.

Bends, nozzles and sudden enlargements

For a pipe bend, draw the CV from section 1 to section 2, cutting the pipe. Use continuity to get V₁ = Q/A₁ and V₂ = Q/A₂. If pressures are not given, use Bernoulli when losses are neglected. Write x and y momentum. Solve for the wall force on the fluid. Reverse its sign to report the force of the fluid on the bend.

  1. Mark inlet 1 and outlet 2, choose +x and +y, and list p₁A₁ and p₂A₂ with arrows.
  2. Compute A, V, ṁ = ρQ, and each momentum flux ρQV.
  3. Write ΣFx = ṁ(V2x − V1x) and ΣFy = ṁ(V2y − V1y).
  4. Find wall force on fluid, then multiply by −1 for force on the bend.

A nozzle accelerates fluid. The pressure force at the inlet is large and points downstream; the nozzle wall pushes back. The thrust on the nozzle is not just ρQV₂; the pressure term p₁A₁ usually dominates. A sudden enlargement slows the fluid. Momentum gives the pressure rise on the downstream face, while Bernoulli would overpredict it because of losses. The drag on a body can be measured from the momentum defect in its wake: the force equals the rate at which axial momentum is missing downstream, ρ∫u(V − u)dA, where V is the free-stream speed, u the local axial velocity in the wake, and dA a small area of the wake section.

Angular momentum and torque

ΣT = ṁ(r₂ × V₂ − r₁ × V₁)

ΣT is torque on the fluid in N·m; r is position vector from the chosen axis in m; V is velocity in m/s; × is vector cross product; the sign follows the right-hand rule.

Angular momentum per unit mass is r × V. Its rate of change equals torque. Only the tangential component of velocity contributes to torque about an axis, because the radial component is parallel to r. For a rotor, the torque on the fluid is positive when the fluid leaves with more angular momentum than it enters.

Lawn sprinkler: water enters at the centre with no angular momentum and leaves one nozzle at radius r = 0.15 m with tangential speed V = 4 m/s. With Q = 0.001 m³/s and ρ = 1000 kg/m³, T = ρQrV = 1000 × 0.001 × 0.15 × 4 = 0.6 N·m. The water gains this torque; the sprinkler arm feels 0.6 N·m in the opposite direction and turns. For an accelerating control volume, only a few syllabi ask for the extra local term ∂/∂t ∫ρV dV and relative velocities; the steady equations above cover most machines and pipe problems.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at Q = 0.1 m³/s through a horizontal 90° reducing bend. The inlet diameter is D₁ = 20 cm and the outlet diameter is D₂ = 10 cm. The outlet discharges to atmosphere. Neglect losses. Find the force of the water on the bend as a vector, its magnitude, and its angle to the inlet axis. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³.

  1. A₁ = πD₁²/4 = π(0.20)²/4 = 0.031416 m². A₂ = π(0.10)²/4 = 0.007854 m².
  2. V₁ = Q/A₁ = 0.1/0.031416 = 3.183 m/s. V₂ = Q/A₂ = 0.1/0.007854 = 12.732 m/s.
  3. Bernoulli from 1 to 2, horizontal and p₂ = 0: p₁/ρ + V₁²/2 = 0 + V₂²/2, so p₁ = ρ(V₂² − V₁²)/2 = 500(162.11 − 10.13) = 75,990.9 Pa.
  4. Let inlet velocity be +x and outlet velocity be +y. Wall force on fluid plus pressure at inlet: Fx + p₁A₁ = ṁ(0 − V₁) = 1000×0.1×(−3.183) = −318.3 N.
  5. Fx = −318.3 − p₁A₁ = −318.3 − 75,990.9×0.031416 = −318.3 − 2387.3 = −2705.6 N.
  6. In y: Fy = ṁ(V₂ − 0) = 1000×0.1×12.732 = 1273.2 N. No outlet pressure term because p₂ = 0.
  7. These are forces of the wall on the fluid. Reverse signs for force of water on bend: (2705.6, −1273.2) N.
  8. Magnitude = √(2705.6² + 1273.2²) = 2990.2 N. Angle = tan⁻¹(1273.2/2705.6) = 25.2° below the inlet axis.

Exam traps

  • Using Q in place of ṁ = ρQ, which gives an answer in m⁴/s² instead of newtons.

  • Putting the pressure force in the wrong direction. At an inlet, pA acts opposite the outward normal, which is often downstream.

3.7 Navier-Stokes equations and some exact solutions

The Navier–Stokes equations are Newton’s second law written for every small element of a viscous fluid. Pressure, gravity and viscous friction produce the acceleration of the element. The equations are nonlinear, so no general solution exists. A few simple flows can be solved exactly by dropping terms that vanish; these give velocity profiles, maximum and mean speeds, and discharge for flows between plates and in pipes. Engineers use them for lubrication, viscometers, microchannels and as the starting point of computational fluid dynamics.

You need: Newton’s law of viscosity from Unit 1, the velocity field and continuity from Unit 2, and control-volume momentum from 3.6.

The stress on a small element

Take a small rectangular element of fluid. On each face the surrounding fluid pushes inward with pressure p and also drags along the face with viscous shear stress τ. For a Newtonian fluid τ = μ du/dy in simple shear. The net force is the pressure force −∇p per unit volume plus the viscous force μ∇²V per unit volume, where V is the velocity vector, p is pressure, ρ is density, g is gravitational acceleration and μ is dynamic viscosity. Gravity adds ρg per unit volume.

ρ(∂V/∂t + V·∇V) = −∇p + ρg + μ∇²V

V = (u, v, w) is the velocity vector in m/s; ρ is density in kg/m³; p is pressure in Pa; g is gravitational acceleration in m/s²; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s. The terms are local acceleration ∂V/∂t, convective acceleration V·∇V, pressure force −∇p, gravity force ρg, and viscous force μ∇²V, all per unit volume.

The left side is mass per unit volume times acceleration. The terms in order are local, convective, pressure, gravity and viscous. The convective term contains products of velocity and velocity gradients. That product makes the equation nonlinear, and nonlinear equations do not have a general formula for all flows. Exact solutions exist only when most terms are zero or constant.

∇·V = 0

∇·V is the divergence of velocity in s⁻¹. This is the continuity equation for an incompressible fluid; it states that volume is conserved.

Fully developed flow between parallel plates

Call the flow direction x and the direction across the gap y. The plates are at y = 0 and y = h, both infinite in z, so nothing changes with z. Fully developed means the velocity profile no longer changes with x: ∂u/∂x = 0. Continuity for incompressible flow in two dimensions is ∂u/∂x + ∂v/∂y = 0. With ∂u/∂x = 0, ∂v/∂y = 0, so v is constant across the gap. At both solid walls v = 0, hence v = 0 everywhere. The flow is parallel to the plates.

For steady, fully developed flow the left side of the Navier–Stokes equation is zero. The x equation reduces to 0 = −dp/dx + μ d²u/dy². Integrate twice and apply no slip. For plane Poiseuille flow, both plates fixed, u(0) = u(h) = 0:

u = (1/(2μ))(−dp/dx)(hy − y²)

u is velocity in m/s at height y in m; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; dp/dx is pressure gradient in Pa/m; h is the gap in m.

The maximum is at y = h/2: u_max = (−dp/dx)h²/(8μ). The mean is (2/3)u_max. Discharge per metre width is q = mean × h = (−dp/dx)h³/(12μ). For Couette flow, the upper plate moves at speed U and the pressure gradient is zero. With u(0) = 0 and u(h) = U, u = Uy/h, the mean is U/2, and the shear stress is τ = μU/h. Pipe flow and other exact solutions

For a circular pipe of radius R with axis x, fully developed laminar flow gives the Hagen–Poiseuille profile u(r) = (1/(4μ))(−dp/dx)(R² − r²). The maximum is at the centre, u_max = (−dp/dx)R²/(4μ), and the mean is half the maximum. The discharge is Q = πR⁴(−dp/dx)/(8μ). The derivation and pipe losses are in Unit 4.

u(r) = (1/(4μ))(−dp/dx)(R² − r²)

u(r) is velocity in m/s at radius r in m; R is pipe radius in m; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; dp/dx is pressure gradient in Pa/m.

Stokes flow past a small sphere gives drag F = 6πμRU for very slow flow, where U is the sphere speed and R its radius; it is one exact solution of the full equations with the convective term neglected. These solutions are used in viscometers, lubrication films and microfluidic channels. They are exact only for laminar, fully developed, simple geometry flows; they are not a general answer for a pump, a valve or a car body.

Worked example

Question. Oil of dynamic viscosity 0.05 Pa·s flows steadily and fully developed between two fixed parallel plates 5 mm apart. The pressure falls by 1200 Pa per metre in the flow direction and the flow is laminar. Find the maximum velocity, the mean velocity and the discharge per metre width.

  1. List the data: h = 5 mm = 5 × 10⁻³ m, μ = 0.05 Pa·s, and the pressure drop per metre is −dp/dx = 1200 Pa/m because pressure falls in the flow direction.
  2. For plane Poiseuille flow the maximum velocity is u_max = (−dp/dx)h²/(8μ).
  3. Substitute: u_max = 1200 × (5 × 10⁻³)² / (8 × 0.05) = 1200 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ / 0.4 = 0.03 / 0.4 = 0.075 m/s.
  4. For this parabolic profile the mean is two-thirds of the maximum: mean = (2/3) × 0.075 = 0.05 m/s.
  5. Discharge per metre width is q = mean × h = 0.05 × 5 × 10⁻³ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s per metre width.

Answer. u_max = 0.075 m/s, mean velocity = 0.05 m/s and discharge = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s per metre width.

Exam traps

  • Dropping terms without saying why: for fully developed flow ∂u/∂x = 0 and v = 0; for Couette flow dp/dx = 0; for Stokes flow the convective term is neglected because the Reynolds number is very small.
  • Losing the sign: for flow in the +x direction, pressure decreases, so dp/dx is negative. Use (−dp/dx) as a positive driving quantity.
  • Mixing maximum and mean: between fixed plates the mean is (2/3)u_max; in a circular pipe the mean is (1/2)u_max.
  • Using an exact solution outside its assumptions: laminar, fully developed, constant μ, simple geometry. A pipe entrance, a valve or a pump is not covered.
  • Writing Euler instead of Navier–Stokes: Euler has no viscous term and applies to inviscid flow; Navier–Stokes keeps μ∇²V.

3.8 Introduction to compressible flow

Below about Mach 0.3 a gas behaves as if its density is fixed, and Bernoulli's equation is enough. Above that, density changes with pressure, so a pressure drop also changes temperature and density. Compressible flow replaces Bernoulli with relations in the Mach number M and the ratio of specific heats γ. Aircraft wings, steam nozzles in turbines, gas pipelines and engine intakes all use these relations. This chapter builds the speed of sound, stagnation properties and the nozzle area rule. Shocks and flow with friction are left for later.

You need: Bernoulli and the energy equation from 3.3, and density and compressibility from Unit 1.

Mach number and the speed of sound

Sound is a small pressure wave. Its speed in a perfect gas is c = √(γRT). Here c is the speed of sound in m/s, γ is the ratio of specific heats cp/cv, R is the gas constant in J/(kg·K), and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. For air γ = 1.4 and R = 287 J/(kg·K). The Mach number is the flow speed divided by this wave speed: M = V/c, with V in m/s. At 300 K, c = √(1.4 × 287 × 300) = 347 m/s, so a flow at 694 m/s has M = 2.

The size of M sets the method. M below 0.3 is treated as incompressible, M between 0.3 and 1 is subsonic, M = 1 is sonic, and M above 1 is supersonic. The 0.3 limit is practical: density changes are small enough that incompressible Bernoulli from 3.3 stays close.

Bring a moving gas to rest adiabatically and its temperature rises to T₀. Bring it to rest isentropically (adiabatically and reversibly) and its pressure rises to p₀. An irreversible deceleration such as a shock gives a lower pressure than p₀. These stagnation values are written with subscript 0. For a perfect gas the relations are:

T₀ = T(1 + (γ − 1)M²/2)

T₀ is stagnation temperature in K; T is static temperature in K; γ is cp/cv; M is Mach number.

p₀/p = (1 + (γ − 1)M²/2)^{γ/(γ−1)}

p₀ is stagnation pressure; p is static pressure, in the same units; γ is cp/cv; M is Mach number.

For air the bracket is 1 + 0.2M² and the pressure exponent is γ/(γ−1) = 3.5. The same energy balance gives h₀ = h + V²/2 = constant for adiabatic flow with no shaft work. Here h is enthalpy per kg and V²/2 is kinetic energy per kg. In an insulated nozzle T₀ stays fixed while the static temperature T falls as the gas speeds up.

Area, velocity and choking

For steady flow in a duct of area A, continuity is ρAV = constant, with ρ the density in kg/m³ and V the speed in m/s. Differentiate and divide by ρAV: dρ/ρ + dA/A + dV/V = 0. The inviscid momentum equation without gravity is dp + ρV dV = 0. For an isentropic change dp = c² dρ. Combining these gives the area–velocity relation:

dA/A = (M² − 1) dV/V

A is duct area in m²; dA is a small change in area; V is speed in m/s; dV is a small change in speed; M is Mach number.

Read the sign. At M below 1, dA and dV have opposite signs: a converging duct speeds the gas up. At M above 1, dA and dV have the same sign: a diverging duct speeds the gas up. At M = 1, dA = 0, so the area is minimum. That minimum is the throat.

A/A* = (1/M) × [(2/(γ+1))(1 + (γ−1)M²/2)]<sup>(γ+1)/(2(γ−1))</sup>

A* is the throat area in m², the area at M = 1; A is the duct area in m² where the Mach number is M; γ is cp/cv; M is Mach number.

For air, γ = 1.4, this becomes A/A* = (1/M)[(2/2.4)(1 + 0.2M²)]³.

A converging nozzle can accelerate a gas only to M = 1 at its throat. If the back pressure is lowered further, the throat stays at M = 1 and the nozzle is choked. The mass flow then depends on the upstream stagnation pressure and temperature and on the throat area, not on the back pressure. To reach M above 1, add a diverging section after the throat. This is a converging–diverging nozzle, or de Laval nozzle. Steam turbine stages and rocket engines use it.

For M above 1, pressure disturbances cannot move ahead of the flow. They stay inside a Mach cone of half-angle μ = sin⁻¹(1/M), with μ in degrees or radians matching the inverse sine.

What to use, and what to leave out

Treat a vapour near saturation as compressible because its density changes strongly, but do not use the perfect-gas relations on it; use steam tables instead. The perfect-gas relations here need a gas that stays a gas, such as air. Use the compressible relations when M is above 0.3, or when the pressure ratio across a device is large enough to change density. Use incompressible Bernoulli when M is below 0.3. The error in that approximation grows roughly as M², so at M = 0.3 it is already a few percent.

The exponent 3.5 in p₀/p is for air with γ = 1.4. For another gas use γ/(γ−1): for steam with γ = 1.33 the exponent is 4, not 3.5. The stagnation temperature formula does not need the gas constant, but the speed of sound does.

Do not put static T or p into a formula that asks for stagnation values. Do not use p₀/p across a shock or in a pipe with friction; a normal shock is irreversible, so p₀ falls even though T₀ stays constant. Fanno flow with friction and Rayleigh flow with heat transfer need their own tables and are outside this introduction.

Worked example

Question. Air with γ = 1.4 and R = 287 J/(kg·K) is at 300 K and flows at Mach 2 in an isentropic duct. Find the speed of sound, the flow speed, the stagnation temperature, the pressure ratio p₀/p and the area ratio A/A*.

  1. Speed of sound: c = √(γRT) = √(1.4 × 287 × 300) = √120540 = 347.2 m/s.
  2. Flow speed: V = M c = 2 × 347.2 = 694.4 m/s.
  3. Stagnation temperature: T₀ = T(1 + 0.2M²) = 300 × (1 + 0.2 × 4) = 300 × 1.8 = 540 K.
  4. Pressure ratio: p₀/p = (1 + 0.2M²)^{3.5} = 1.8^{3.5} = 7.824, which rounds to 7.82.
  5. Area ratio (air, γ = 1.4): A/A* = (1/M)[(2/2.4)(1 + 0.2M²)]³ = (1/2)[(2/2.4)(1.8)]³ = 0.5 × 1.5³ = 1.6875, which rounds to 1.69.

Answer. c = 347.2 m/s, V = 694.4 m/s, T₀ = 540 K, p₀/p = 7.82, and A/A* = 1.69. The stagnation pressure is 7.82 times the static pressure.

Exam traps

  • Using static temperature in T₀ = T(1 + 0.2M²) when the question asks for stagnation temperature. The T in that formula is the static temperature of the moving gas.
  • Using incompressible Bernoulli at Mach 2. Density changes by a large factor; use the Mach-number relations.
  • Taking the exponent 3.5 as universal. It is γ/(γ−1), equal to 3.5 only for air with γ = 1.4.
  • Expecting a converging nozzle to give supersonic exit flow. It chokes at M = 1 in the throat; supersonic flow needs a diverging section after the throat.
  • Using p₀/p across a shock or a long frictional pipe. In both a normal shock and an adiabatic constant-area pipe with friction, p₀ falls and T₀ stays constant; so p₀/p from the isentropic relation is invalid in both.

Takeaways

  1. The Reynolds transport theorem is the single machine that converts every law written for a moving lump of fluid into a law usable on a fixed region in space.
  2. Euler's equation is just Newton's second law written along a streamline, and Bernoulli's equation is its integral when density is constant.
  3. Pressure head, elevation head and velocity head are all lengths in metres, so Bernoulli is a statement about a total head that stays constant.
  4. A venturimeter, an orificemeter and a pitot tube are the same idea: one measured pressure difference, one known area, one discharge or velocity.
  5. Every real flow device is handled by adding a single measured coefficient (Cd, Cc, Cv) to the ideal Bernoulli result.
  6. The momentum equation answers force questions that Bernoulli can not: it survives losses, so it is the tool for bends, vanes and nozzles.
  7. The Navier–Stokes equations are solved exactly only after deleting the terms that vanish by the geometry of the problem, as in plane Poiseuille flow.
  8. Above M ≈ 0.3 the density of a gas changes across a device and the incompressible relations must be replaced by Mach-number relations in γ.

Glossary

Cc
Coefficient of contraction, the ratio of the vena-contracta area to the orifice area.
Cd
Coefficient of discharge, the ratio of actual to ideal discharge, always less than 1.
Circulation
The line integral of velocity around a closed curve, Γ = ∮V·ds; it is zero in irrotational flow.
Continuity equation
Mass is conserved: for steady flow through a stream tube, ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂, and Q = AV for an incompressible fluid.
Control surface
The imaginary closed boundary that separates the chosen control volume from its surroundings.
Control volume
A fixed region of space chosen for analysis, through which fluid may flow.
Cv
Coefficient of velocity, the ratio of the actual jet velocity to the ideal √(2gh).
Eulerian description
Describing a flow by the velocity and pressure fields at fixed points in space, rather than by following individual particles.
Head
Energy per unit weight of fluid, expressed as a length in metres; for example V²/2g is the velocity head.
Hydraulic gradient
The line joining the piezometric heads p/ρg + z along a pipe, ignoring the velocity head.
Mach number
The ratio of flow speed to local speed of sound, M = V/√(γRT).
Momentum flux
The rate at which momentum crosses a surface, equal to ρAV² for uniform flow through area A at velocity V.
Mouthpiece
A short tube fitted to an orifice that changes the coefficient of discharge by controlling the vena contracta.
Navier–Stokes equations
The momentum equations for a Newtonian fluid, containing the viscous term μ∇²V.
Notch
An opening in a tank or channel wall with the free liquid surface as its upper edge, used to measure discharge in open channels.
Orifice
A small opening, usually in the base or side of a tank, through which fluid discharges and forms a jet.
Piezometric head
The sum p/ρg + z at a point, measured by a piezometer tapping.
Pitot tube
A bent tube with an open end facing the flow that measures stagnation pressure and hence local velocity.
Reynolds transport theorem
The identity that equates the rate of change of a property of a system to the storage rate inside a control volume plus the net flux out of it.
Stagnation point
A point in the flow where the velocity is zero and the pressure is the stagnation pressure.
Stream function
A scalar ψ whose derivatives give the velocity components in two-dimensional incompressible flow, so that ψ = constant along a streamline.
Total head
The sum p/ρg + z + V²/2g, constant along a streamline in ideal steady flow.
Velocity of approach
The small velocity of the liquid surface in the channel feeding a notch; its head Va²/2g is added to the measured head.
Vena contracta
The smallest cross-section of a jet just downstream of an orifice, where the streamlines become parallel again.
Venturimeter
A converging–throat–diverging meter that measures pipe discharge from the pressure difference between the inlet and the throat.
Weir
A notch built across an open channel, usually rectangular or triangular, over which the water spills.
