7.1 Classification of pumps

A pump is a machine that adds energy to a liquid and moves it from a low level to a higher level, or into a pressurised line. Every pump does this in one of two ways: a spinning impeller flings the liquid outward, or a moving boundary traps a fixed volume of liquid and pushes it out. The first family is rotodynamic, the second is positive displacement. The classification matters because the two families behave differently when the head changes, when the delivery valve is shut, and when the pump is started. Only a few universities teach this family tree in detail.

You need: pressure, gauge pressure and head from Unit 1, Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics. No pump theory is assumed.

Two ways to add energy

A pump takes mechanical energy from a motor or engine and gives it to a liquid as pressure energy and velocity energy. The liquid enters at the suction side, where the pressure is low, so atmospheric pressure or a supply tank pushes water up the suction pipe. It leaves at the delivery side, into the delivery pipe. The head between these two sides is the head the pump must produce.

A rotodynamic pump has a spinning impeller with curved vanes. The vanes give the liquid a high velocity, and the casing turns that velocity into pressure. The liquid flows through continuously; nothing is trapped in a closed space.

A positive-displacement pump has a moving boundary — a piston, a plunger, a gear or a vane — that traps a fixed volume of liquid and forces it into the delivery pipe. Its ideal flow depends only on geometry and speed.

Q = Vd N

Q is ideal discharge in m³/s; Vd is the volume trapped per revolution in m³/rev; N is shaft speed in rev/s. For a rotodynamic pump no such fixed Vd exists, because the flow changes with head.

The pump family tree

The figure shows the whole classification in one picture. Two branches, and each branch splits again.

  • Centrifugal: liquid leaves the impeller radially. Medium to high head with large flow; the workhorse of buildings and industry. See 7.2.
  • Axial flow: the impeller is a propeller and the liquid moves along the shaft. Very large flow at low head — irrigation and flood pumping.
  • Mixed flow: partly radial, partly axial, used where flow is large and head is moderate.
  • Reciprocating (piston or plunger): high pressure, small flow. See 7.7.
  • Rotary (gear, vane, screw): steady flow at high pressure, good for oil and viscous liquids.

The behavioural split matters more than the names. A rotodynamic pump's flow falls as the head rises, so it can run with the delivery valve shut. A positive-displacement pump pushes the same volume whatever the head, so shutting its valve raises pressure until a joint or pipe fails.

Pumps you meet in India

  • Deep-well (borewell) pump: a narrow, vertical multistage centrifugal pump. Several impellers sit one above the other on one shaft and each adds head, which a single impeller cannot do from deep down a Chennai borewell. The motor sits under water, below the pump.
  • Jet pump: a centrifugal pump on the ground with an ejector (nozzle and venturi) down the well. Part of the water is sent back down to draw more water up; used for shallow wells.
  • Gear pump: two meshing gears trap oil between the teeth and the casing. The usual lubricating-oil pump in a workshop.
  • Vane pump: sliding vanes in a rotor, used in hydraulic power packs, tractor hydraulics and power steering.
  • Hand pump: a village hand pump is a reciprocating plunger pump — small flow, high pressure from manual effort.

Worked example

Question. A pump for an office water tank has a rotating impeller with curved vanes. It delivers 20 L/s of water at 1450 rpm. (a) Place it in the pump family tree, using the volume handled per revolution as your reason. (b) Name a positive-displacement pump used as a small high-pressure hand pump.

  1. Convert the data: Q = 20 L/s = 20/1000 = 0.020 m³/s, and N = 1450/60 = 24.17 rev/s.
  2. Curved vanes on a spinning impeller put the pump on the rotodynamic branch; the flow leaves radially, so the sub-branch is centrifugal.
  3. Test it numerically. If the pump were a displacement pump, its trapped volume would be Vd = Q/N = 0.020/24.17 = 8.28 × 10⁻⁴ m³ per revolution, that is 0.83 L per revolution.
  4. A positive-displacement pump of a size that fits a building traps millilitres to a few tens of millilitres per revolution; a hand-pump cylinder holds a fraction of a litre per stroke. 0.83 L per revolution is far too large, so the pump cannot be positive displacement.
  5. Part (b): the village hand pump is a reciprocating plunger pump — a plunger in a cylinder, worked by a lever.

Answer. The pump is centrifugal (rotodynamic branch): 20 L/s at 1450 rpm means 0.83 L per revolution, far beyond a positive-displacement pump of that size. The hand pump is a reciprocating plunger pump.

Exam traps

  • Calling a centrifugal pump a positive-displacement pump. It is rotodynamic, and its flow falls as the head rises.
  • Starting a positive-displacement pump with the delivery valve closed. The pressure has nowhere to go; a centrifugal pump is safe at shut-off, a gear or plunger pump is not.
  • Treating suction and delivery as directions of rotation. They are the two sides of the pump: inlet and outlet.
  • Calling a submersible borewell pump reciprocating because it 'pushes' water up a long pipe. It is a multistage centrifugal pump.
  • Putting the gear pump on the rotodynamic branch. Gear and vane pumps are rotary positive displacement, because they trap fixed volumes between moving parts.

7.2 Centrifugal pump: components and working

A centrifugal pump is a rotodynamic pump: a spinning impeller throws water outward, and a widening casing slows that water so its speed becomes pressure. One shaft and one impeller move large flows smoothly, so you meet the pump in apartment overhead tanks, irrigation pumpsets, borewell submersibles, power-plant cooling lines and coffee machines. This chapter names the parts, follows the water from sump to tank, and shows why the casing must be full of water before the motor starts.

You need pressure and its variation with depth (Unit 1), Bernoulli's equation (Unit 3), and the pump grouping in 7.1.

The parts, and the job each one does

A centrifugal pump has two moving parts: the impeller and its shaft. Everything else guides water or holds pressure.

  • Suction pipe: carries water from sump to impeller under partial vacuum, so joints must be airtight.
  • Foot valve and strainer: the foot valve is a one-way valve at the bottom of the suction pipe; water enters upward and cannot run back, so the casing stays full between starts. The strainer keeps out sand and leaves.
  • Impeller and eye: a disc with curved vanes. The eye is the hole at its centre; water enters along the axis and leaves at the outer edge, radially.
  • Casing: an airtight shell that collects water and guides it to the delivery pipe.
  • Shaft, gland and stuffing box: the shaft brings power from the motor; the gland seals the gap where it passes through the casing while letting it turn.

An open impeller has vanes on one plate with no cover discs: it passes muddy water without choking, at lower efficiency. A semi-open impeller has one cover disc. A closed impeller traps water between two cover discs, gives the best efficiency, and suits clean water.

A volute casing is a spiral whose flow area grows towards the delivery branch. A vortex chamber is a circular casing of constant area with a separate diffuser pipe. A diffuser ring is fixed guide vanes around the impeller that slow the water; diffuser and multistage pumps use it.

Working, step by step

  1. Prime: fill the casing and suction pipe with water; the foot valve holds it in.
  2. Start with the delivery valve closed. The impeller spins in the water-filled casing.
  3. The vanes push water outward; a low-pressure region forms at the eye.
  4. Atmospheric pressure on the sump pushes water up the suction pipe. The pump does not suck; the atmosphere pushes.
  5. The casing slows the water: velocity head falls, pressure head rises. Open the delivery valve slowly.

p_eye = p_atm − ρ g (H_s + h_fs)

p_eye is absolute pressure at the eye (Pa); p_atm is atmospheric pressure (about 101 325 Pa at sea level); ρ is density (1000 kg/m³ for water); g = 9.81 m/s²; H_s is suction lift from sump surface to pump centreline (m); h_fs is head lost in strainer, foot valve and suction pipe (m).

Priming is not optional. A dry casing holds air, and the same head in metres of air gives a pressure smaller than in metres of water by the density ratio, about 1.2/1000 — far too small to lift water. A dry pump runs hot and delivers nothing.

Water leaves the vane tips at V₁, often 10 to 25 m/s. That speed is useless in a pipe, where friction loss grows with V². The casing diffuses it: area increases, V falls to V₂, and Bernoulli gives the pressure rise.

p₂ = p₁ + ½ρ(V₁² − V₂²) − ρ g h_L

p₁, V₁ are at the impeller outlet; p₂, V₂ at the delivery branch; ρ is density; h_L is head lost inside the casing. The casing adds no energy; it only converts velocity head into pressure head.

Why the suction side decides the layout

Atmospheric pressure at sea level balances 10.33 m of water — the absolute ceiling on suction lift. The strainer, foot valve and pipe friction take 1 to 3 m, so a real pump sits within about 6 to 7 m above the sump level.

So the pump sits near the sump, not near the tank. Pushing water on the delivery side is easy; pulling it on the suction side is limited by the atmosphere. A Chennai apartment pump sits at ground level beside the sump and pushes to the roof tank.

A deep borewell breaks the limit by going down instead of pulling up. A multistage pump keys many impellers on one shaft in one casing; each stage passes water to the next, so each adds head. At ground level it is a deep-well pump; lowered under water it is a submersible. Use a closed impeller for clear water, an open one for sandy water, a single volute for large flow at low head, and several stages for small flow at high head.

7.3 Heads, efficiencies and work done by the impeller

A centrifugal pump impeller adds energy to water. The energy per unit weight added by the impeller is the Euler head. Only part of it appears as useful pressure rise across the pump. Hydraulic losses inside the impeller and casing — vane and casing friction, shock at entry and exit, and eddy formation — reduce the head per unit weight. Leakage lowers the delivered discharge; mechanical losses waste part of the shaft power. Neither lowers the head per unit weight. This chapter separates Euler head, manometric head and the main efficiencies. It gives the velocity-triangle formula for impeller work and shows why manometric head is smaller than Euler head. Only a few universities teach the full derivation.

You need: 7.2 for the impeller and casing, Unit 6 for velocity triangles and Euler's turbomachine equation, and Unit 3 for Bernoulli's equation.

Euler head from velocity triangles

Use subscript 1 at inlet and 2 at outlet. u is blade speed, V is absolute water velocity, Vw is its whirl (tangential) component, and Vf is its flow (radial) component. A pump impeller is a control volume.

H<sub>e</sub> = (u<sub>2</sub>V<sub>w2</sub> − u<sub>1</sub>V<sub>w1</sub>)/g

He is Euler head in m; u is blade speed in m/s; Vw is absolute whirl velocity in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

Angular momentum gives torque T = ρQ(r2Vw2 − r1Vw1). Power is Tω = ρQ(u2Vw2 − u1Vw1). Divide by weight flow ρgQ: work per second per unit weight is He. Some syllabi call this work done by the impeller.

For a normal pump, inlet whirl is small: Vw1 ≈ 0. Then He = u2Vw2/g. Use absolute whirl Vw2, not blade speed alone.

Heads and efficiencies

Suction head Hs is the static lift on the suction side; delivery head Hd is the static lift on the delivery side. Pipe friction adds hfs and hfd. The manometric head is the useful head across the pump:

H<sub>m</sub> = H<sub>s</sub> + H<sub>d</sub> + h<sub>fs</sub> + h<sub>fd</sub> + V<sub>d</sub>²/(2g)

Hm is manometric head in m; Hs and Hd are static lifts; hfs and hfd are friction heads in suction and delivery pipes; Vd is delivery velocity in m/s.

Across flanges, Hm = (pd/γ + Vd²/2g + zd) − (ps/γ + Vs²/2g + zs), where p is pressure, γ = ρg, z is elevation and V is velocity. Hydraulic losses inside the impeller and casing make Hm less than He, so Hm < He and ηman < 1.

η<sub>man</sub> = gH<sub>m</sub>/(u<sub>2</sub>V<sub>w2</sub> − u<sub>1</sub>V<sub>w1</sub>) = H<sub>m</sub>/H<sub>e</sub>

ηman = Hm/He is the manometric efficiency; some syllabi call this the hydraulic efficiency of the pump, while others use hydraulic efficiency for the impeller-only ratio. Other symbols as above.

Mechanical efficiency ηmech = power to impeller / shaft power. Volumetric efficiency ηvol = Qdelivered/Qimpeller. Overall efficiency ηo = ρgQdeliveredHm/Pshaft = ηmanηmechηvol.

7.4 Minimum starting speed; pumps in series and parallel

A centrifugal pump can spin without lifting water. It starts delivering only when the head created by its impeller at shut-off reaches the manometric head. That speed is the minimum starting speed. Designers also combine pumps: in series to add head, in parallel to add flow. The reasoning is like pipes in series and parallel, but now the curves belong to pumps. Borewell booster sets, multi-stage water supply pumps and fire-fighting headers use these ideas.

You need: heads and manometric efficiency from 7.3, and the idea of pipes in series and parallel from Unit 4.

Minimum starting speed

At standstill the impeller cannot push water into a delivery pipe that already needs a pressure head. The pressure head the pump must produce is the manometric head Hm. With the delivery valve closed, flow Q is zero; this is shut-off. The impeller still creates a head because it spins the water. At the outlet the peripheral velocity is u = πDN/60, where u is in m/s, D is the impeller outlet diameter in m and N is the speed in rpm.

For the ideal shut-off model, the head imparted by the impeller is He = u²/g. Manometric efficiency is ηman = Hm/He, so He = Hm/ηman. Put the two expressions for He equal and solve for N.

N<sub>min</sub> = (60/(πD))√(gH<sub>m</sub>/η<sub>man</sub>)

Nmin is the minimum starting speed in rpm; D is the impeller outlet diameter in m; Hm is the manometric head in m; g = 9.81 m/s²; ηman is the manometric efficiency, dimensionless. Use ηman = 1 if efficiency is not used.

Below Nmin the impeller head is less than Hm, so the pump cannot deliver water. At Nmin it just starts delivering. Real pumps need a little more speed to cover losses and clearance. This estimate is used while selecting a motor for a pump that must start against a closed delivery valve.

Pumps in series

In series, the delivery of pump 1 is connected to the suction of pump 2. The same discharge Q passes through both pumps. Each pump adds head at that Q. The total head is H = H1 + H2 at the same Q.

For two identical pumps, H = 2H1(Q). This is how a multi-stage pump works: each stage adds head while the flow stays the same. Use series when the system needs high head and the flow is low, such as lifting water from a deep borewell or feeding a boiler.

The second pump and its casing must withstand the higher pressure produced by the first pump. Series connection does not add flows. Do not add Q1 + Q2 here; the flow is common.

Pumps in parallel and comparison

In parallel, both pumps draw from a common suction header and deliver into a common delivery header. The head H is the same across each pump. The flows add: Q = Q1 + Q2 at the same H. For identical pumps, Q = 2Q1(H). Use parallel operation for high flow at moderate head, such as a city water booster or a fire-fighting header.

Worked example

Question. A centrifugal pump has the characteristic H = 40 − 0.25Q, with H in m and Q in L/s. Two identical pumps are connected in series and in parallel. Find the head at Q = 40 L/s for one pump, two pumps in series, and two pumps in parallel.

  1. One pump at Q = 40 L/s: H = 40 − 0.25 × 40 = 40 − 10 = 30 m.
  2. Two pumps in series carry the same Q = 40 L/s. Each pump develops 30 m, so Hseries = 30 + 30 = 60 m. Using the series curve: H = 80 − 0.5 × 40 = 80 − 20 = 60 m.
  3. Two pumps in parallel have the same head. The total Q = 40 L/s splits equally between identical pumps, so each pump passes Qeach = 20 L/s. Each head is H = 40 − 0.25 × 20 = 40 − 5 = 35 m. Using the parallel curve: H = 40 − 0.125 × 40 = 40 − 5 = 35 m.

Answer. At Q = 40 L/s the head is 30 m for one pump, 60 m for two pumps in series and 35 m for two pumps in parallel.

Exam traps

  • In series the flow is the same and heads add; in parallel the head is the same and flows add. Do not add both head and flow.
  • The minimum-speed formula uses D in metres and N in rpm. Using D in mm, or using angular speed in rad/s, gives a wrong answer. Convert ηman from percent to decimal.
  • At Q = 0 the pump still has shut-off head. Zero flow does not mean zero head. Minimum starting speed is where impeller head equals manometric head.
  • For identical pumps in parallel at total Q, each pump takes Q/2 only when the curves are identical. Unequal pumps split according to the common head.
  • Series pumps do not double flow; parallel pumps do not double head. The examiner's favourite question asks for both at one Q.

7.5 NPSH and cavitation

Cavitation is the formation and violent collapse of vapour bubbles inside a pump. It starts when the local absolute pressure at the impeller eye falls to the liquid's vapour pressure. NPSH, net positive suction head, is the pressure margin above vapour pressure at the pump inlet. Available NPSH is what the installation gives; required NPSH is what the maker demands. Keeping available above required prevents noise, pitting and head loss. The idea is used wherever pumps lift hot water: power plants, process plants and high-rise building services.

You need: 7.3 for pump heads, Unit 1 for absolute pressure, and Unit 4 for pipe friction losses.

Why bubbles form at the eye

A centrifugal pump lowers pressure at the impeller eye to draw liquid in (7.2). If that absolute pressure reaches the vapour pressure pv of the liquid, the liquid boils locally. Vapour bubbles form. They travel with the flow into regions of higher pressure and collapse suddenly. Each collapse sends a small pressure wave into the metal. Thousands of collapses per second give crackling noise, vibration and pit marks, called cavitation erosion. The pump head and flow then fall because the bubbles occupy volume and disturb the flow. Cavitation is not the same as air entrainment: air comes from outside, vapour comes from the liquid itself. The risk rises with hot water because pv rises with temperature, and with a long suction pipe because friction lowers pressure. Absolute pressure is the only pressure that decides boiling; gauge pressure can be negative and still be above absolute zero. Use absolute values throughout this chapter.

NPSH available

NPSH available is the total absolute head at the pump suction flange above the vapour pressure, expressed in metres of the pumped liquid.

NPSH<sub>A</sub> = p<sub>atm</sub>/(ρg) − p<sub>v</sub>/(ρg) − H<sub>s</sub> − h<sub>fs</sub>

patm is absolute atmospheric pressure at the sump surface, in Pa; pv is absolute vapour pressure of the liquid at its temperature, in Pa; ρ is liquid density, in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s²; Hs is suction lift, the height of the pump centreline above the sump surface, in m, positive for lift; hfs is friction and minor loss in the suction pipe, in m of liquid.

Each pressure divided by ρg becomes a head in metres. The first term is the atmospheric head pushing liquid into the pipe. The second is the vapour pressure head — the absolute pressure at which the liquid boils at its temperature — which must be subtracted because it is unavailable as suction margin. The last two are losses that reduce pressure at the eye. For a pump below the sump (flooded suction), Hs is negative and helps. NPSHA cannot be negative in a real installation: if the formula gives a negative number, the liquid would already be vapour at the eye, so the pump cavitates or loses suction. A small positive value is therefore essential.

NPSH required and safe running

NPSH required is the NPSH at which the pump head falls by a stated small amount (commonly 3%) in the maker's test, taken as the onset of cavitation; it already includes the eye and inlet losses of that pump. It comes from tests and is printed on the pump curve. The condition for safe running is NPSHA ≥ NPSHR. If NPSHA is only slightly larger, keep a margin of about 0.5 m to 1 m for transients and fouling. NPSHR rises with flow and with speed. A pump that is safe at design flow may cavitate if it is run at a higher flow than design (for example with the discharge valve fully open on a steep system curve) or at a higher speed. Throttling the discharge reduces flow and usually reduces NPSHR, though running far below design flow brings its own problems such as recirculation. Raising suction lift or suction friction lowers NPSHA; heating the water lowers NPSHA because pv rises; running the pump faster raises NPSHR. Some texts define the Thoma cavitation factor σ = NPSHA/H, where H is pump head, with a critical value σc = NPSHR/H at which cavitation begins; σ is a maker's index, not a replacement for the NPSH comparison. The full NPSH required curve against flow belongs with the performance curves in 7.6. In the worked example, the installation gives 4.71 m and the pump is safe only if its NPSHR is at most 4.71 m.

Worked example

Question. A pump draws water at 30°C from a sump whose surface is 4 m below the pump centreline. The suction pipe friction loss is 1.2 m. Atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa and vapour pressure at 30°C is 4.2 kPa. Find NPSH available. Density of water is 996 kg/m³.

  1. Convert pressures to Pa: p_atm = 101 kPa = 101000 Pa; p_v = 4.2 kPa = 4200 Pa.
  2. Atmospheric head = p_atm/(ρg) = 101000/(996 × 9.81) = 10.337 m.
  3. Vapour head = p_v/(ρg) = 4200/(996 × 9.81) = 0.430 m.
  4. Suction lift H_s = 4 m, positive because the sump surface is below the pump centreline. Suction loss h_fs = 1.2 m.
  5. NPSH_A = 10.337 − 0.430 − 4 − 1.2 = 4.707 m.

Answer. NPSH available is about 4.71 m; the pump is safe only if its NPSH required is 4.71 m or less.

Exam traps

  • Using gauge pressure in place of absolute pressure. The formula needs absolute p_atm and p_v; a gauge reading can mislead.
  • Sign of H_s. Suction lift is positive and subtracted; a flooded suction (sump above pump) gives a negative H_s and adds to NPSH_A.
  • Forgetting unit conversion. 101 kPa is 101000 Pa; using kPa directly with ρg in Pa gives a wrong head.
  • Treating negative NPSH_A as a reserve. A negative value means the liquid is already at vapour pressure, so cavitation is certain.
  • Swapping NPSH_A and NPSH_R. Available comes from the installation; required comes from the pump maker.

7.6 Performance and characteristic curves

A pump is sold with a curve, not a single flow. At each flow Q the same pump gives a different head H, draws different power P and runs at different efficiency η. Plot these against Q and you get the performance curves. They exist because a pipe system also demands more head as flow rises. Where the pump curve meets the system curve is the duty point, the flow the pump will actually deliver. Every selection — a 1 hp domestic booster, a 50 kW irrigation set, a data-centre cooling pump — is a search for a safe duty point.

You need: 7.3 heads and efficiencies, 7.4 series and parallel, 7.5 NPSH, and see Unit 6, Specific speed and unit quantities, for the turbine analogy.

The four curves on one sheet

The main curve is head against flow, H(Q). It falls: a centrifugal impeller gives its highest head at zero flow, called shut-off head, and less head as flow rises toward the run-out end. The falling shape is why a fixed speed cannot fix both flow and head.

Efficiency η(Q) starts low, rises to a peak near the design flow, then falls. The peak is the best efficiency point, BEP. The rated point is the buyer's guarantee point, usually near BEP: a stated Q, H, η and shaft power P.

Shaft power P(Q) for a centrifugal pump usually rises with Q. At shut-off it is low but not zero, because disc friction and leakage remain. The motor must cover run-out power, not only duty power.

Required NPSH, NPSHR(Q), generally rises with Q. Available NPSH must stay above it, or cavitation occurs (see 7.5).

System curve and duty point

System head has two parts: static head Hst, which is lift plus pressure difference and does not change with Q; and friction head, which grows roughly as Q². Write Hsys = Hst + kQ², where k is a constant for that pipe layout, in s²/m⁵ when Q is in m³/s and H in m.

Plot Hsys on the same axes as pump head. The crossing satisfies Hpump(Q) = Hsys(Q). That Q and H are the duty point. It is the flow the pump can hold steadily with that pipe system.

A valve opening lowers k and moves the duty to higher Q. A valve closing raises k and moves it to lower Q. The rated point and the duty point can differ.

Specific speed and the affinity laws

Ns = N√Q ÷ H3/4

Ns is pump specific speed; N is shaft speed in rpm; Q is flow in m³/s at best efficiency; H is head in m at the same point.

Ns is dimensional; it is not a dimensionless number. Low Ns (often below 30) means a narrow radial-flow impeller. Medium values give mixed flow. High values (often above 80) give axial flow. The bands vary by maker, so use Ns for selection, not as a strict law. Turbine specific speed in Unit 6 is a different definition; do not mix the two.

Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁;  H₂/H₁ = (N₂/N₁)²;  P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³

Q is flow, H is head, P is shaft power, and N is speed. The laws apply to the same pump and the same impeller diameter, while efficiency stays roughly unchanged.

Centrifugal and reciprocating shapes

A reciprocating pump traps a fixed volume each stroke. Its flow is almost constant and its head varies with load, so its H(Q) curve is nearly vertical. A centrifugal pump is the opposite: flow changes a lot while head falls. Reciprocating flow pulses; an air vessel smooths it (see 7.8). Slip is in 7.7.

Worked example

Question. A pump test at 1450 rpm gives head 40 m at 0 L/s, 38 m at 10 L/s, 34 m at 20 L/s, 28 m at 30 L/s and 20 m at 40 L/s. The system head is 12 m at 0 L/s, 13 m at 10 L/s, 16 m at 20 L/s, 21 m at 30 L/s and 28 m at 40 L/s. Plot both curves and estimate the duty flow and head.

  1. At each listed Q, find the difference Hpump − Hsys. At Q = 0 L/s: 40 − 12 = 28 m. At 10 L/s: 38 − 13 = 25 m. At 20 L/s: 34 − 16 = 18 m. At 30 L/s: 28 − 21 = 7 m. At 40 L/s: 20 − 28 = −8 m.
  2. The difference changes sign between 30 L/s and 40 L/s, so the duty point lies between them.
  3. Use linear interpolation between the two points. The drop over 10 L/s is 7 − (−8) = 15 m. The duty flow is Qd = 30 + 10 × (7 ÷ 15) = 34.67 L/s.
  4. Find head on the pump curve between 30 L/s and 40 L/s. It falls by 8 m over 10 L/s, so Hd = 28 − 8 × (4.67 ÷ 10) = 24.27 m. The system curve gives the same value: 21 + 7 × (4.67 ÷ 10) = 24.27 m.
  5. Round to practical readings: duty flow ≈ 34.7 L/s, duty head ≈ 24.3 m.

Answer. The duty point is about 34.7 L/s at 24.3 m head.

Exam traps

  • The duty point is where pump head equals system head, not the peak-efficiency point unless the system curve also passes there.
  • In Ns use Q in m³/s, not L/s, and H in m; 35 L/s is 0.035 m³/s.
  • Affinity laws apply to the same pump and impeller diameter, not to two different pump models.
  • Shut-off head is at zero flow; it is not the rated head or the duty head.
  • A centrifugal pump's power usually rises with flow; do not size the motor from shut-off power alone.

7.7 Reciprocating pump: working principle and slip

A reciprocating pump is a positive-displacement machine: a piston slides inside a cylinder, traps a fixed volume of water, and pushes it out through a one-way valve. Because the volume per stroke is fixed by the cylinder size, the ideal flow depends only on geometry and running speed. Real flow always differs a little, and the gap between ideal and real is called slip. You meet these pumps in hand pumps, pressure washers, boiler feed and high-pressure jetting, where a small steady flow at a high head is wanted.

You need: crank-and-connecting-rod motion from Unit 2, Fluid Kinematics, and pressure ideas from Unit 1, Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics.

Parts and the two strokes

Four parts do the work: a cylinder, a piston or plunger inside it, a crank turned by a motor, and a connecting rod that turns the crank's circle into the piston's back-and-forth motion. Two one-way valves sit on the cylinder head: a suction valve from the suction pipe and a delivery valve to the delivery pipe. Pressure difference alone opens and closes them; no cam touches them.

  1. Suction stroke: the crank pulls the piston outward, cylinder pressure falls below atmospheric, the suction valve lifts, and water rises up the suction pipe.
  2. Delivery stroke: the crank pushes the piston inward, the suction valve shuts, pressure rises above the delivery pressure, the delivery valve lifts, and water is pushed into the delivery pipe.

A piston carries rings and seals against the bore. A plunger is a plain solid ram sliding through a packed gland; plunger pumps are picked for very high heads such as boiler feed. In a single-acting pump water touches one face only, giving one suction and one delivery stroke per revolution. In a double-acting pump water touches both faces, giving two deliveries per revolution and a steadier flow; the piston rod takes up area ar on one face, so that side sweeps less volume.

Theoretical discharge from geometry

Volume swept in one stroke is A L. A single-acting pump delivers that volume N/60 times a second, since N is in revolutions per minute and one revolution gives one delivery.

Qth = A L N / 60

Qth is the ideal discharge in m³/s; A is the piston face area = πD²/4 in m², with D the bore in m; L is the stroke = 2 × crank radius in m; N is the crank speed in rpm; 60 converts minutes to seconds.

Qth = (2A − ar) L N / 60

ar is the rod cross-section area = πdr²/4 in m², with dr the rod diameter in m; per revolution one face sweeps A L and the rod face sweeps (A − ar)L, so the sum is (2A − ar)L. Double-acting pump.

The flow is not steady. Piston speed follows the crank and peaks at mid-stroke, so the instantaneous discharge rises and falls twice per revolution in a double-acting pump and once in a single-acting one. That unevenness is why air vessels are fitted (see 7.8).

Slip and coefficient of discharge

Slip is what geometry promises minus what the meter measures.

slip = Q<sub>th</sub> − Q<sub>act</sub> ,   C<sub>d</sub> = Q<sub>act</sub> / Q<sub>th</sub>

Qact is the measured discharge in m³/s; Cd is the coefficient of discharge, dimensionless, also called volumetric efficiency; percentage slip = (Qth − Qact)/Qth × 100, so slip fraction = 1 − Cd.

Positive slip is normal: Qact is less than Qth because water leaks past valves and rings, valves lag before seating, dissolved air cushions the stroke, and at high speed the cylinder does not fill completely.

Negative slip means Qact beats Qth. It happens when the suction pipe is long and the pump runs slowly: the moving water column has enough inertia to keep entering after the piston has begun its delivery stroke, so a little extra water spills through the delivery valve. A pump with a head on its suction side can also show it.

Work, power and the indicator diagram

The indicator diagram plots cylinder pressure against the volume swept over one revolution. For an ideal pump with no friction and no acceleration, the suction stroke is a flat line at suction pressure and the delivery stroke a flat line at delivery pressure; the rectangle between them is the work per stroke.

W = ρ g A L (h<sub>d</sub> + h<sub>s</sub>) ,   P = ρ g Q<sub>th</sub> (h<sub>d</sub> + h<sub>s</sub>) / 1000

W is work per stroke in J; P is the ideal power in kW; ρ = 1000 kg/m³ is water density; g = 9.81 m/s²; hd is the delivery height above the pump centre line in m; hs is the suction lift below the pump centre line in m; both are positive lengths. Cylinder pressure is (patm − ρg hs) during suction and (patm + ρg hd) during delivery, so the pressure difference across the stroke is ρg(hd + hs). If elevations are instead taken as signed heights from the pump axis, with the suction level negative, the same bracket reads (hd − hs); say which convention you use.

In a real pump the suction line sags below its ideal level at the start of the stroke, because the water column must be accelerated and suction-pipe friction must be beaten; the delivery line rises above its ideal for the same reason. Both lines are wavy. The suction line dips lowest and the delivery line peaks highest near the start of each stroke, where the water column's acceleration is greatest; at mid-stroke the acceleration is zero, so the lines come back closest to the ideal levels, held apart only by friction. Valve seating takes time, so the corners are rounded. If suction-side pressure falls to the vapour pressure of water, the column separates from the piston: separation, the reciprocating pump's cavitation (see 7.5), which gives a knock and a shock when the column rejoins.

Worked example

Question. A single-acting reciprocating pump has a cylinder bore of 180 mm, a stroke of 300 mm and runs at 60 rpm. Its measured discharge is 6.5 L/s. Find the theoretical discharge, the coefficient of discharge and the percentage slip.

  1. Convert to SI: D = 180 mm = 0.180 m, L = 300 mm = 0.300 m, N = 60 rpm, Qact = 6.5 L/s = 0.0065 m³/s.
  2. Piston area A = πD²/4 = π(0.180)²/4 = 0.025447 m².
  3. Volume swept per stroke = A L = 0.025447 × 0.300 = 7.6341 × 10⁻³ m³.
  4. Theoretical discharge Qth = A L N/60 = 7.6341 × 10⁻³ × 60/60 = 7.634 × 10⁻³ m³/s = 7.634 L/s.
  5. Coefficient of discharge Cd = Qact/Qth = 0.0065 / 0.007634 = 0.851.
  6. Slip = Qth − Qact = 7.634 − 6.5 = 1.134 L/s.
  7. Percentage slip = 1.134 / 7.634 × 100 = 14.86 %. The slip is positive, so the pump delivers less than its geometry promises.

Answer. Qth = 7.634 L/s, Cd = 0.851 and slip = 1.134 L/s, that is 14.86 % of the theoretical discharge.

Exam traps

  • Using N in rpm without dividing by 60 makes Qth sixty times too large; the 60 is part of the formula.
  • Slip is theoretical minus actual. Positive slip means the actual discharge is smaller, so do not flip the order.
  • A double-acting pump uses (2A − ar), not 2A: the rod area is subtracted once, on the rod side only.
  • Stroke L is twice the crank radius, and both D and L must be in metres before squaring or multiplying.
  • Cd and slip fraction add to one, so a Cd of 0.92 and 8 % slip are the same statement (and a Cd of 0.88 is 12 % slip).

7.8 Air vessels in reciprocating pumps

A reciprocating pump pushes water in bursts, so the delivery pipe sees a fast flow near mid-stroke and almost no flow near the dead centres. An air vessel is a closed branch on the pipe with trapped air. It stores water when the piston is fast and returns it when the piston is slow. The delivery flow becomes steadier and less energy is lost to pipe friction. You see air vessels on hand pumps, reciprocating feed pumps and old boiler-feed sets. This chapter finds the work saved against delivery friction for a single-acting pump.

You need: 7.7 for crank, piston and ideal flow, and Unit 4 for pipe friction.

Why the flow pulses

In a single-acting pump the crank turns at constant angular speed ω, in rad/s. Let r be crank radius, in m; L = 2r is stroke, in m; A is piston area, in m²; and θ is crank angle from inner dead centre, in radians. The piston velocity is approximately v_p = ω r sin θ. The delivery flow is Q = A v_p = A ω r sin θ, in m³/s. It is zero at θ = 0 and θ = π, and maximum at mid-stroke.

Friction head in the delivery pipe follows Darcy–Weisbach: h_f = f L_p v²/(2 g D), where f is friction factor, L_p is pipe length, v is pipe velocity, D is pipe diameter and g is gravitational acceleration. Since v is proportional to Q, h_f varies as sin² θ. Let h_f,max be the friction head at peak delivery velocity. Then during delivery h_f = h_f,max sin² θ. The mean of sin² θ is 1/2, but work uses the integral of sin³ θ, not the mean head. That is the source of the 2/3 factor.

Q = A ω r sin θ; h_f = h_f,max sin² θ

Q is delivery flow in m³/s; A is piston area in m²; ω is crank angular speed in rad/s; r is crank radius in m; θ is crank angle in radians; h_f is instantaneous friction head in m; h_f,max is friction head at peak delivery velocity in m.

Air vessel: location and action

An air vessel is a closed chamber connected by a branch pipe, as the figure shows. It holds trapped air above water. On the delivery side it is fitted close to the pump, before the long delivery pipe. On the suction side it can be fitted close to the pump on the suction pipe. The suction vessel reduces the acceleration head needed to start and stop the water column each stroke. The delivery vessel smooths the flow and cuts friction work.

  • Fast part of stroke: piston speed is high, Q is above mean, water rises in the vessel and compresses the trapped air.
  • Slow part of stroke: piston speed is low, Q is below mean, compressed air pushes stored water back into the delivery pipe.
  • Large vessel: air volume is big, so pressure change is small. Delivery-pipe flow stays nearly constant at the cycle mean Q_mean = A L N/60, where N is pump speed in rpm.

For a large delivery air vessel, the delivery pipe carries Q_mean all through the cycle. The friction head is then constant at h_f,max/π². Why π²? For single-acting simple harmonic motion, Q_mean = Q_max/π, and friction head goes as Q². A small vessel cannot hold pressure constant, so this result does not apply.

Work saved against delivery friction

For one delivery stroke, without an air vessel, integrate power ρ g Q h_f over the stroke. The result for a single-acting pump is:

W_f = (2/3) ρ g A L h_f,max

W_f is friction work per stroke in J; ρ is water density in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration in m/s²; A is piston area in m²; L is stroke in m; h_f,max is peak friction head in m.

With a large air vessel the flow is steady at Q_mean and the friction head is h_f,max/π². The work per stroke becomes:

W_f,av = ρ g A L h_f,max / π²

W_f,av is friction work per stroke with a large air vessel in J; the other symbols are as above.

Saved work = W_f − W_f,av = (2/3 − 1/π²) ρ g A L h_f,max ≈ 0.565 ρ g A L h_f,max. The saving is about 84.8% of the no-vessel friction work. The air vessel does not change the useful pumping work; it changes only the friction loss in the pipe. It also protects the pipe from water hammer by reducing sudden velocity changes.

Worked example

Question. A single-acting reciprocating pump has cylinder area 0.03 m², stroke 0.30 m and speed 60 rpm. The maximum friction head in the delivery pipe is 6 m. Find the work done against friction per stroke without an air vessel and with a large air vessel, and the percentage saved. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Given: A = 0.03 m², L = 0.30 m, N = 60 rpm, h_f,max = 6 m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s². Volume per stroke = A L = 0.03 × 0.30 = 0.009 m³.
  2. Common group ρ g A L h_f,max = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.03 × 0.30 × 6 = 529.74 J.
  3. Without air vessel: W_f = (2/3) × 529.74 = 353.16 J per stroke.
  4. With large air vessel: W_f,av = 529.74 / π² = 529.74 / 9.8696 = 53.67 J per stroke.
  5. Saved work = 353.16 − 53.67 = 299.49 J per stroke.
  6. Percentage saved = (299.49 / 353.16) × 100 = 84.8%.

Answer. The work against delivery friction is 353.16 J per stroke without an air vessel and 53.67 J with a large air vessel; the saving is 299.49 J per stroke, about 84.8%.

Exam traps

  • Do not use mean friction head in the without-air-vessel work. h_f varies as sin² θ, so the factor is 2/3, not 1/2.
  • Do not apply the π² formula to a small air vessel or to the suction pipe. It is the large-delivery-vessel result for single-acting flow.
  • Keep h_f,max as the peak friction head. If the data gives an average head, convert using the definition in the question; do not guess.
  • The 84.8% is the fraction of friction work saved, not the fraction of total pump power saved.
  • A double-acting pump has two delivery strokes per revolution. The 2/3 and 1/π² factors change; use them only for single-acting unless the double-acting case is derived.

Remember What is an air vessel? 2 marks

A closed chamber with trapped air, connected by a branch to a pump pipe. It stores water at high flow and returns it at low flow.

Understand Why does delivery friction head vary as sin² θ in a single-acting pump? 3 marks

Delivery flow Q varies as sin θ. Pipe friction head varies as v², and v is proportional to Q, so h_f varies as sin² θ.

Analyze A pump has an air vessel on delivery but none on suction. Explain why delivery friction falls but suction acceleration head remains. 4 marks

The delivery vessel keeps delivery-pipe flow nearly constant, so delivery friction is steady and low. The suction pipe still has to accelerate and decelerate its water column every stroke, so suction acceleration head is unchanged.

A large trapped-air volume changes little in pressure when water enters or leaves. The air cushion can absorb the fast-stroke excess and supply the slow-stroke deficit without a large pressure swing.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern reciprocating pumps often use bladder or diaphragm dampers instead of open air vessels, because trapped air can dissolve into water and the cushion can be lost. Takeaways

  1. Pumps add energy to water; rotodynamic pumps use a spinning impeller, while positive-displacement pumps trap and push fixed volumes.
  2. A centrifugal pump first creates velocity head in the impeller and then converts most of it into pressure head in the volute or diffuser.
  3. Manometric head is less than the head produced by the impeller because hydraulic, mechanical and volumetric losses consume part of the input.
  4. A centrifugal pump starts lifting water only when the impeller head exceeds the manometric head; this condition fixes the minimum starting speed.
  5. Pumps in series add their heads at the same discharge; pumps in parallel add their discharges at the same head.
  6. Cavitation begins when local pressure falls to vapour pressure; NPSH is the safety margin, and reducing suction lift helps protect the pump.
  7. Performance curves show that head falls and power usually rises as discharge increases; the duty point is where the pump curve meets the system curve.
  8. Reciprocating pump flow is fixed by cylinder volume and speed; slip is the small difference between ideal and actual discharge.
  9. Air vessels smooth the pulsating flow of a reciprocating pump and save part of the work lost to pipe friction.

Glossary

Air vessel
A closed chamber of compressed air connected to a pump pipe to smooth pulsating flow.
Cavitation
Boiling and collapse of liquid in low-pressure zones, causing erosion, noise and vibration.
Delivery pipe
The pipe from the pump outlet to the destination.
Duty point
The operating point where the pump characteristic curve meets the system curve.
Friction head
The head lost to friction in suction and delivery pipes and fittings.
Hydraulic efficiency
The ratio of actual head developed to the ideal head from the impeller.
Impeller
The rotating wheel with vanes that transfers shaft work to the water in a centrifugal pump.
Indicator diagram
A pressure-volume diagram of the cylinder of a reciprocating pump.
Manometric efficiency
The ratio of manometric head to the head produced by the impeller.
Manometric head
The useful head developed by a pump, measured between suction and delivery gauge points.
Mechanical efficiency
The ratio of power delivered to the water to the shaft power input.
NPSH
Net positive suction head: absolute pressure head at pump inlet minus vapour pressure head, in metres.
Positive-displacement pump
A pump that traps a fixed volume of liquid and forces it into the delivery pipe.
Priming
Filling the pump casing and suction pipe with water before starting, to remove air.
Pump
A machine that adds energy to a liquid to move it from a lower level or pressure to a higher one.
Rotodynamic pump
A pump in which a rotating impeller continuously adds energy to flowing liquid.
Slip
The difference between ideal and actual discharge of a reciprocating pump, usually given as a percentage.
Specific speed
The speed of a geometrically similar pump that would deliver unit discharge against unit head.
Static head
The vertical height difference between the sump water level and the delivery level.
Suction pipe
The pipe from the sump to the pump inlet, carrying water at pressure below atmospheric.
System curve
A graph of the head required by a piping system against the discharge through it.
Vapour pressure
The pressure at which a liquid boils at a given temperature.
Velocity head
The head equivalent to the kinetic energy of water, equal to V squared divided by 2g.
Volumetric efficiency
The ratio of actual discharge to the discharge passing through the impeller, allowing for leakage.
Volute casing
A spiral casing of increasing area around an impeller that slows the water and raises its pressure.
