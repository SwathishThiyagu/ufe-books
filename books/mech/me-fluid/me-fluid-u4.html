4.1 Reynolds experiment; laminar and turbulent flow

A dye streak in a glass pipe is the simplest test of flow character. At low speed the dye stays a thin straight line: laminar flow, where fluid layers slide past each other. At high speed the dye breaks into swirls and clouds: turbulent flow, where particles mix across the pipe. The Reynolds number Re tells you which regime to expect before you open the valve. It is used to size water mains, oil pipelines, blood-flow models and chemical reactors.

You need: viscosity and density from Unit 1, and the laminar–turbulent classification from Unit 2.

The Reynolds experiment

Reynolds' apparatus makes the flow pattern visible. A constant-head tank holds water at a fixed level, so the pressure at the pipe inlet does not change with time. A glass tube of diameter D is connected to the tank. A control valve at the outlet sets the mean velocity V. A small dye reservoir feeds a fine needle at the tube entrance. The dye is a coloured liquid with nearly the same density as water. A measuring jar collects the outflow so you can find discharge Q and then V = Q / (πD²/4).

  1. Fill the tank and let the water settle.
  2. Open the valve slightly until water flows steadily.
  3. Release a thin dye thread into the glass tube.
  4. Record the dye shape and measure Q with the jar and a stopwatch.
  5. Raise the valve opening in small steps and repeat.

Reynolds number and critical values

The flow regime is fixed by one dimensionless group.

Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν

ρ is density in kg/m³; V is mean velocity in m/s; D is pipe diameter in m; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; ν = μ/ρ is kinematic viscosity in m²/s.

Re has no units. The same pipe can be laminar or turbulent: increase V or D and Re rises; increase viscosity and Re falls. For pipe flow, common textbook values are lower critical Re ≈ 2000 and upper critical Re ≈ 4000. Below 2000 the flow is laminar. Between 2000 and 4000 it is transitional; some syllabi call this the critical zone. Above 4000 it is turbulent. The exact switch depends on entrance disturbances, vibration and surface finish, so treat 2000 and 4000 as guides, not sharp walls.

What the dye shows

At low Re the dye thread stays straight for the whole length of the tube. A particle of dye stays in its own layer; this is laminar flow. The velocity profile is a paraboloid: zero at the wall and maximum at the centre. The Hagen–Poiseuille result for this profile is in Chapter 4.2. As Re enters the critical zone, the dye line starts to wave. Small disturbances grow. At high Re the dye mixes across the pipe within a short distance and forms a blurred cloud. This is turbulent flow. The time-averaged velocity profile is flatter in the middle and steeper near the wall, because eddies carry momentum across the pipe. The wall layer still has low speed, but the core moves almost as a block. The dye test is qualitative; Re is the quantitative test. Use Re to classify, then use the correct friction law in later sections.

Worked example

Question. Water (ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s) flows through a 50 mm diameter glass pipe. The mean velocity is 0.06 m/s. Find Re and classify the flow. Repeat for 0.20 m/s.

  1. Convert diameter: D = 50 mm = 0.05 m. Kinematic viscosity ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s.
  2. Use Re = VD/ν for V = 0.06 m/s: Re = 0.06 × 0.05 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 0.003 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 3000.
  3. Re = 3000 lies between 2000 and 4000, so the flow is transitional.
  4. Use Re = VD/ν for V = 0.20 m/s: Re = 0.20 × 0.05 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 0.010 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 10000.
  5. Re = 10000 is above 4000, so the flow is turbulent.

Answer. At 0.06 m/s, Re = 3000 and the flow is transitional; at 0.20 m/s, Re = 10000 and the flow is turbulent.

Exam traps

  • Using pipe radius in place of diameter. Re uses D, the full diameter. A 50 mm pipe has D = 0.05 m, not 0.025 m.
  • Mixing μ and ν. μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; ν = μ/ρ is kinematic viscosity in m²/s. Use the formula that matches the data.
  • Forgetting unit conversion. Convert mm to m before dividing by ν in m²/s.
  • Treating Re = 2000 as an exact switch. The critical zone is about 2000 to 4000; disturbances shift the change.
  • Giving units to Re. It is dimensionless; do not write Re = 3000 m/s.

4.2 Laminar flow in circular pipes: Hagen-Poiseuille equation

Oil in a thin pipe, blood in a capillary, water in a drip line: at low speed the fluid moves in orderly layers. The layer touching the wall sticks; the centre moves fastest. A force balance on a cylinder of fluid gives the shear stress, and Newton's law of viscosity gives the velocity profile. For a circular pipe this leads to the Hagen-Poiseuille equation. It answers the practical question: for a given flow rate, what pressure drop and head loss must a pump supply?

You need: viscosity and Newton's law of viscosity from Unit 1; continuity, laminar flow and Reynolds number from Unit 2 and 4.1; Bernoulli head from Unit 3.

Force balance on a fluid cylinder

Take a horizontal pipe of diameter D and radius R = D/2. Consider a cylinder of fluid of radius r and length L, coaxial with the pipe. The flow is steady and fully developed, so this fluid does not accelerate. Upstream pressure is p1; downstream pressure is p2. The pressure force forward is (p1 − p2)πr² = Δp πr². The shear stress τ on the curved side acts backward over area 2πrL, giving force τ2πrL. No other axial force acts.

Δp πr² = τ 2πrL → τ = (Δp/L)(r/2)

Δp = p1 − p2 is the pressure drop over length L (Pa); r is the radial distance from the centre (m); τ is the shear stress at radius r (Pa).

At the wall r = R, the wall shear stress is τw = (Δp/L)(R/2) = ΔpD/(4L). Because τ is proportional to r, shear is zero at the centre and maximum at the wall. This linear shear is the key to the whole problem.

Velocity profile and the Hagen-Poiseuille equation

Newton's law of viscosity for this radial profile is τ = −μ du/dr. The minus sign appears because u falls as r rises. Put the force-balance result in place of τ: −μ du/dr = Δp r/(2L). Rearrange to du/dr = −Δp r/(2μL).

Integrate: u = −Δp r²/(4μL) + C. At the wall r = R, no slip gives u = 0, so C = ΔpR²/(4μL). Hence u(r) = Δp(R² − r²)/(4μL). The centreline velocity is umax = ΔpR²/(4μL).

u(r) = 2V[1 − (r/R)²]

u(r) is velocity at radius r (m/s); V = Q/(πD²/4) is the mean velocity (m/s); R = D/2 is the pipe radius (m); r is radial distance from the centre (m).

The mean velocity is half the centreline value, V = umax/2. The profile is a paraboloid of revolution: zero at the wall, 2V at the centre. The figure plots u/V against r/R.

Integrating the profile over the area gives Q = ∫₀ᴿ u 2πr dr = πΔpR⁴/(8μL) = πΔpD⁴/(128μL). Rearranged, Δp = 128μLQ/(πD⁴). In terms of mean velocity, Δp = 32μLV/D².

h<sub>f</sub> = Δp/(ρg) = 32μLV/(ρgD²) = 128νLQ/(πgD⁴)

hf is the head loss (m); ρ is density (kg/m³); g = 9.81 m/s²; μ is dynamic viscosity (Pa·s); ν = μ/ρ is kinematic viscosity (m²/s); other symbols as above.

Assumptions and the D⁴ rule

  • Steady, laminar, incompressible flow.
  • Fully developed flow: the profile does not change along the pipe.
  • No slip at the wall; horizontal pipe.
  • Newtonian fluid with constant μ.

The equation is exact for these conditions, not an approximation. In a pipe, laminar flow normally means Re = ρVD/μ below about 2000 (see 4.1). For an upward incline at angle θ, replace Δp by Δp − ρgL sinθ.

Because Q ∝ D⁴, diameter dominates. A 10% larger diameter gives about 46% more discharge at the same pressure drop. This is why a slightly blocked fuel line or capillary changes flow sharply.

A capillary-tube viscometer uses this equation backwards. Measure Q for known Δp, L and D, then μ = πΔpD⁴/(128LQ). The tube must be narrow and the flow slow, so Re stays small.

Worked example

Question. Oil with dynamic viscosity μ = 0.10 Pa·s and density ρ = 900 kg/m³ flows through a horizontal pipe of diameter 20 mm and length 10 m. The discharge is Q = 1.0×10⁻⁵ m³/s. Check that the flow is laminar, then find the pressure drop, the head loss and the wall shear stress.

  1. Convert: D = 20 mm = 0.020 m, R = 0.010 m, L = 10 m. Area A = πD²/4 = π(0.020)²/4 = 3.1416×10⁻⁴ m².
  2. Mean velocity V = Q/A = 1.0×10⁻⁵/(3.1416×10⁻⁴) = 0.0318 m/s.
  3. Reynolds number Re = ρVD/μ = 900×0.0318×0.020/0.10 = 5.73. Re < 2000, so the flow is laminar.
  4. Pressure drop Δp = 128μLQ/(πD⁴). Numerator = 128×0.10×10×1.0×10⁻⁵ = 1.28×10⁻³. Denominator = π(0.020)⁴ = 5.027×10⁻⁷. Δp = 1.28×10⁻³/5.027×10⁻⁷ = 2546.5 Pa = 2.55 kPa.
  5. Head loss hf = Δp/(ρg) = 2546.5/(900×9.81) = 0.288 m.
  6. Wall shear τw = ΔpD/(4L) = 2546.5×0.020/(4×10) = 1.27 Pa.

Answer. V = 0.0318 m/s, Re = 5.73 (laminar), Δp = 2546.5 Pa ≈ 2.55 kPa, hf = 0.288 m and τw = 1.27 Pa.

Exam traps

  • Using D instead of R in u(r) = Δp(R² − r²)/(4μL). The radius appears squared, and D = 2R, so substituting the diameter gives a profile four times too large (umax = ΔpD²/(4μL) instead of ΔpR²/(4μL)).
  • Calling V the centreline velocity. The centreline velocity is umax = 2V; the mean is V = Q/(πD²/4).
  • Forgetting the fourth power. Q ∝ D⁴, so a 10% diameter error gives about a 46% discharge error.
  • Mixing Δp (Pa) with hf (m). hf = Δp/(ρg); in the head form use ν = μ/ρ.
  • Applying the equation to turbulent or developing flow. It needs steady, fully developed laminar flow.

4.3 Darcy-Weisbach equation and friction factor

The Darcy-Weisbach equation is the standard way to calculate friction loss in a pipe. It says the head lost to friction is a multiple of the velocity head. The multiple contains pipe length, diameter and a dimensionless friction factor. Engineers use it to size water mains, design pump systems and check whether a gravity line can deliver the flow demanded. It works for laminar and turbulent flow, provided the correct friction factor is used.

You need Reynolds number from Unit 2 and the laminar pipe result in 4.2.

Head loss as a velocity head

Real water loses pressure as it moves along a pipe. The Darcy-Weisbach equation writes that loss as a head hf in metres of the flowing fluid:

hf = f (L/D)(V²/(2g))

hf is head loss in m; f is the Darcy friction factor, dimensionless; L is pipe length in m; D is internal diameter in m; V is mean velocity in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

The group V²/(2g) is the velocity head, in metres. The ratio L/D counts how many diameters long the pipe is. A long, narrow pipe has a large L/D and loses more head. The factor f carries the effect of viscosity and wall roughness. For a horizontal pipe, the pressure drop is Δp = ρ g hf, where ρ is density in kg/m³ and Δp is in Pa.

Use the equation for steady, incompressible flow in a constant-diameter pipe. With f fixed, hf rises as V². In laminar flow f itself falls as 1/V, so hf rises only as V. In turbulent flow f changes slowly with V, so hf is close to V².

The Darcy friction factor

The symbol f above is the Darcy friction factor, also called the Darcy-Weisbach friction factor. It is dimensionless. For fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe, f = 64/Re, where Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν is the Reynolds number. Here μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s and ν = μ/ρ is kinematic viscosity in m²/s. This laminar result holds up to Re ≈ 2000.

For turbulent flow, f depends on Re and on relative roughness ε/D. Here ε is the absolute roughness of the pipe wall in m; ε/D is dimensionless because both lengths are in m. The Colebrook-White equation gives the turbulent value:

1/√f = −2 log₁₀( ε/(3.7D) + 2.51/(Re√f) )

f is the Darcy friction factor; ε is absolute roughness in m; D is diameter in m; Re is Reynolds number; log₁₀ is base-10 logarithm.

The equation is implicit: f appears on both sides. Solve it by iteration, or read the Moody chart in 4.4. An explicit approximation is f ≈ 0.25/[log₁₀(ε/(3.7D) + 5.74/Re0.9)]² for 5000 < Re < 10⁸.

Warning for exams: the Fanning friction factor fF is one quarter of the Darcy factor, fF = f/4. In Fanning form, hf = 4 fF (L/D)(V²/2g). If a chart or formula gives fF, multiply by 4 before putting it into the Darcy-Weisbach equation.

Chezy's equation and the same friction factor

Chezy's equation is another friction formula, common in open-channel flow: V = C√(Rh S). Here V is mean velocity in m/s, C is the Chezy coefficient, with units m1/2/s in SI, Rh is hydraulic radius in m, and S is the energy slope, dimensionless. For a full circular pipe, Rh = A/P = (πD²/4)/(πD) = D/4, where A is area in m² and P is wetted perimeter in m.

Compare with Darcy-Weisbach. Write S = hf/L and substitute hf = f(L/D)(V²/2g): S = f V²/(2gD). Rearrange to V = √(8g/f) √(Rh S) using D = 4Rh. Therefore C = √(8g/f), and f = 8g/C². The two formulas carry the same information for a full pipe; only the coefficient changes.

In practice, C is not constant: it depends on roughness and Reynolds number. Darcy-Weisbach is preferred for pipe design because f has a clear link to roughness and Re. Chezy remains useful for channels, sewers and rough estimates.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at V = 2.0 m/s through a 100 mm diameter, 50 m long pipe. Take f = 0.02, ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the head loss and the pressure drop.

  1. Convert diameter: D = 100 mm = 0.100 m. Given L = 50 m, V = 2.0 m/s, f = 0.02.
  2. Compute velocity head: V²/(2g) = 2.0²/(2 × 9.81) = 4.00/19.62 = 0.2039 m.
  3. Compute length ratio: L/D = 50/0.100 = 500.
  4. Apply Darcy-Weisbach: hf = f (L/D)(V²/2g) = 0.02 × 500 × 0.2039 = 2.039 m.
  5. Compute pressure drop: Δp = ρ g hf = 1000 × 9.81 × 2.039 = 2.00 × 10⁴ Pa = 20.0 kPa.

Answer. The head loss is about 2.04 m and the pressure drop is about 20.0 kPa.

Exam traps

  • Darcy versus Fanning: fDarcy = 4 fFanning. Using the Fanning value in Darcy-Weisbach underestimates head loss by a factor of 4.
  • f = 64/Re is only for laminar flow. Do not use it for turbulent pipe flow, even as a quick check.
  • Relative roughness ε/D is dimensionless. Keep ε and D in the same unit; do not put ε in mm and D in m.
  • In laminar flow hf ∝ V, not V². The V² proportionality assumes f is constant.
  • hf is metres of the flowing fluid. Pressure drop needs Δp = ρg hf; head alone is not pressure.

4.4 Moody diagram

A Moody diagram is a chart of the Darcy friction factor f against Reynolds number Re, drawn on log-log axes, with one curve for each relative roughness ε/D. It exists because the Colebrook-White equation gives f only implicitly: f appears on both sides, so hand calculation needs trial and error. The chart turns that iteration into one visual read. Designers use it to size water mains, plant piping and air ducts, and to check friction head loss in the Darcy-Weisbach equation before choosing a pump.

You need 4.1 Reynolds experiment for Re and the laminar-turbulent split, and 4.3 Darcy-Weisbach equation for f and head loss.

Axes and curves

The horizontal axis is Reynolds number Re on a log scale, usually from 10³ to 10⁷. The vertical axis is the Darcy friction factor f on a log scale, usually 0.008 to 0.1. Each curve is one value of relative roughness ε/D. Here ε is the average height of the pipe-wall roughness in metres, D is the internal diameter in metres, and ε/D is dimensionless. A smooth drawn tube is near ε/D = 0; a rusty steel main is nearer 0.01.

Some syllabi call it Moody's chart or Moody chart. The chart is a picture of the Colebrook-White equation, not a separate law. It applies to steady, fully developed, turbulent flow in a circular pipe. It says nothing about fittings, and nothing about laminar flow beyond one straight line.

The five regions

Below Re ≈ 2000 the flow is laminar and every roughness curve collapses onto one straight line: f = 64/Re. On log-log paper this line falls with slope −1. Roughness has no effect because the viscous layer fills the pipe. Use 4.2 Hagen-Poiseuille here, not the turbulent curves.

Between Re ≈ 2000 and 4000 the flow is in the critical zone. Friction is uncertain and can jump between laminar and turbulent values. Designers avoid sizing a pipe to run in this band.

Above Re ≈ 4000 the curves separate. The lowest curve is the smooth pipe, ε/D = 0; for Re below about 10⁵ the Blasius form f = 0.316 Re^(−1/4) gives it. As Re rises the curves fan out: this is the transitionally rough zone, where both Re and ε/D matter. At high Re each curve flattens into the fully rough zone, where f depends only on ε/D and is nearly constant. There the viscous sublayer is thinner than the roughness bumps, so viscosity stops shielding them.

Reading the chart

  1. Compute Re = VD/ν, with V the mean velocity in m/s, D the diameter in m and ν the kinematic viscosity in m²/s.
  2. Compute ε/D, using the same unit for roughness and diameter.
  3. If Re < 2000, use f = 64/Re. If 2000 < Re < 4000, redesign the pipe.
  4. For Re > 4000, enter the chart at Re on the horizontal axis, move vertically to the curve for ε/D, then read f on the vertical axis.
  5. Confirm the read value with the Haaland approximation.

The chart is a picture of the Colebrook-White equation, which is implicit:

1/√f = −2 log₁₀( ε/(3.7D) + 2.51/(Re√f) )

f is the Darcy friction factor, ε the roughness height (m), D the pipe diameter (m), Re the Reynolds number, and log₁₀ the base-10 logarithm. The equation is implicit in f, so it is solved by iteration.

Haaland's explicit form, used for the check:

1/√f = −1.8 log₁₀[ (ε/(3.7D))^1.11 + 6.9/Re ]

symbols are as above. Haaland's form is explicit: put Re and ε/D on the right and get f directly, within about 2% of Colebrook-White.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at V = 0.5 m/s through a pipe of diameter D = 200 mm and length L = 100 m. The kinematic viscosity is ν = 1×10⁻⁶ m²/s and the relative roughness is ε/D = 0.002. Read the friction factor from a Moody diagram and find the friction head loss h_f. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Compute Re = VD/ν = 0.5 × 0.2 / (1×10⁻⁶) = 1×10⁵. This is well above 4000, so the flow is turbulent and the chart applies.
  2. Relative roughness is already given: ε/D = 0.002.
  3. Enter the chart at Re = 10⁵, move up to the ε/D = 0.002 curve, and read f ≈ 0.025. The Haaland check gives 0.0250, so the read value is sound.
  4. Write the Darcy-Weisbach equation: h_f = f (L/D)(V²/(2g)).
  5. Compute L/D = 100/0.2 = 500.
  6. Compute V²/(2g) = (0.5)²/(2 × 9.81) = 0.25/19.62 = 0.01274 m.
  7. Multiply: h_f = 0.025 × 500 × 0.01274 = 0.159 m, that is about 0.16 m.

Answer. The friction factor is f ≈ 0.025 and the friction head loss is h_f ≈ 0.16 m of water.

Exam traps

  • Using ε in mm but D in m, so ε/D comes out 1000 times too large. Convert both to the same unit first.
  • Using the laminar line f = 64/Re for a turbulent flow. That line belongs only below Re ≈ 2000.
  • Confusing the Darcy friction factor with the Fanning friction factor. The chart gives Darcy; Fanning is one quarter of it.
  • Reading a curve for the wrong ε/D instead of interpolating between the two nearest curves.
  • Designing a pipe to run in the critical zone 2000 < Re < 4000, where the chart gives no dependable value.

4.5 Major and minor losses in pipes

A real pipe never has only straight pipe. Water enters from a tank, passes elbows and valves, and leaves into another tank. Friction along the straight length causes the major loss. Each fitting causes a local disturbance and a minor loss. Engineers add both to get the total head the pump must supply. The same numbers decide pump size, pipe diameter and whether a short suction line will cavitate. This chapter gives the Darcy-Weisbach major loss, the K-coefficient minor loss, and the total-loss rule.

You need the Darcy-Weisbach equation from 4.3 and the Bernoulli equation from Unit 3.

Two losses, one total

The Darcy-Weisbach equation gives the loss in a straight constant-diameter pipe. It is the major loss because a long line has far more straight pipe than fittings. Loss is a head in metres of the flowing fluid.

hf = f (L/D) V²/(2g)

hf is friction head loss in m; f is the Darcy friction factor, dimensionless; L is straight pipe length in m; D is internal diameter in m; V is mean velocity in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

A fitting turns or changes the flow area. Extra turbulence converts mechanical energy into heat. This local loss is the minor loss. Its coefficient K is dimensionless and depends on fitting shape and, weakly, on Reynolds number.

hm = K V²/(2g)

hm is minor head loss in m; K is the fitting loss coefficient, dimensionless; V is the local mean velocity in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

Add all losses for the system. Use the velocity of each segment; use one velocity only when the diameter is the same. Total head loss is the sum of major and minor losses.

H<sub>L</sub> = Σ [f L V²/(2gD)] + Σ [K V²/(2g)]

HL is total head loss in m; the first sum covers straight segments; the second covers fittings; each term uses the local V and D.

Common loss coefficients

Handbooks list K for standard fittings. Values below are typical for turbulent water flow; use manufacturer data for design. Exit loss is exactly one velocity head because all kinetic energy is lost in the receiving tank.

FittingTypical K
Sharp entrance0.5
Exit into large tank1.0
90° elbow0.9
Gate valve, fully open0.2
Globe valve, fully open10

A sudden expansion has a standard result from momentum balance: hex = (V1 − V2)²/(2g), where V1 is the mean velocity in the smaller upstream pipe in m/s, V2 is the mean velocity in the larger downstream pipe in m/s, and hex is expansion head loss in m. For a sudden contraction, K is based on the downstream velocity and is about 0.5 for a sharp edge.

Equivalent length converts a fitting into straight pipe of the same loss. Set K V²/(2g) = f (Le/D) V²/(2g), so Le = K D/f. Here Le is equivalent length in m, D is pipe diameter in m, f is the Darcy friction factor and K is the fitting coefficient. A valve with K = 2.5 in a 50 mm pipe with f = 0.03 adds Le = 2.5 × 0.05/0.03 = 4.17 m.

When minor losses matter

A long pipe has large L/D. Major loss grows with L/D, while minor loss stays a fixed number of velocity heads. Minor losses can be dropped when ΣK is small compared with fL/D. With typical ΣK of 5–10 and f ≈ 0.02–0.03, L/D must be a few thousand before minor losses drop below 10% of the major loss. For a short pipe, pump suction, manifold or laboratory rig, fittings can dominate. Never drop minor losses just because the word 'minor' appears.

  • Draw the line from start tank to end tank and number every straight run and fitting.
  • Find diameter, length and mean velocity in each run.
  • Find f from the Moody diagram using Reynolds number and relative roughness (see 4.4).
  • Add K for every fitting; use the local velocity for each.
  • Add major and minor losses. The pump head must cover this total plus any lift and pressure difference.

The total energy line falls by each loss; the hydraulic gradient line sits one velocity head below it. See 4.6 for the drawing.

Worked example

Question. A 50 mm diameter pipe is 20 m long and carries water at a mean velocity of 1.2 m/s. The Darcy friction factor is 0.03. The line has a sharp entrance (K = 0.5), two 90° elbows (K = 0.9 each), one valve (K = 2.5) and an exit into a tank (K = 1.0). Find the velocity head, major loss, minor loss, total head loss and the equivalent length of all fittings. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Data: D = 50 mm = 0.05 m; L = 20 m; V = 1.2 m/s; f = 0.03; g = 9.81 m/s².
  2. Velocity head: V²/(2g) = 1.2²/(2 × 9.81) = 1.44/19.62 = 0.07339 m.
  3. Major loss: h_f = f (L/D) V²/(2g) = 0.03 × (20/0.05) × 0.07339. 20/0.05 = 400; 0.03 × 400 = 12; h_f = 12 × 0.07339 = 0.8807 m.
  4. Sum K: entrance 0.5 + two elbows 2 × 0.9 = 1.8 + valve 2.5 + exit 1.0 gives ΣK = 5.8.
  5. Minor loss: h_m = ΣK V²/(2g) = 5.8 × 0.07339 = 0.4257 m.
  6. Total head loss: H_L = h_f + h_m = 0.8807 + 0.4257 = 1.3064 m, about 1.306 m.
  7. Equivalent length: L_e = ΣK D/f = 5.8 × 0.05/0.03 = 9.667 m.

Answer. The total head loss is 1.306 m of water; the fittings are equivalent to 9.667 m of the same pipe.

Exam traps

  • Using one velocity head for all fittings when diameters change. Each K must multiply the local V²/(2g).
  • Adding K values for a sudden expansion. Use h = (V₁ − V₂)²/(2g) with the two pipe velocities.
  • Thinking minor losses are always small. In a short pipe or pump suction, ΣK can exceed fL/D.
  • Using mm for D in fL/D or in L_e = KD/f. Convert D to metres first: 50 mm is 0.05 m, so 20/0.05 = 400 and 5.8 × 0.05/0.03 = 9.667 m.
  • Mixing head loss in metres with pressure drop in Pa. Multiply head by ρg when pump data is in pressure.

4.6 Hydraulic gradient and total energy lines

Water in a pipe carries three heads: elevation, pressure and velocity. Plot their sum along the pipe and you get the total energy line (TEL). Plot elevation plus pressure only and you get the hydraulic gradient line (HGL). The two lines make losses visible: every friction length and every fitting pulls the TEL down, and the HGL sits exactly one velocity head below it. Engineers use these lines to check pressures, find where a pipe may go below atmospheric, and size pumps.

You need the Bernoulli equation from Unit 3 and the loss formulas from 4.5 Major and minor losses in pipes.

Two heads at every section

At any section of a pipe, define the total head H as the mechanical energy per unit weight of water, measured in metres. The elevation z is measured in m above a chosen datum. The gauge pressure is p, in Pa. The mean velocity is V, in m/s. Water density is ρ, in kg/m³, and gravitational acceleration is g = 9.81 m/s².

H = z + p/(ρg) + V²/(2g)

H is the total head in m; z is elevation in m; p is gauge pressure in Pa; ρ is density in kg/m³; g is 9.81 m/s²; V is mean velocity in m/s. The group V²/(2g) is the velocity head in m.

The pressure head p/(ρg) is the height of a water column that the gauge pressure can support. Add z to it and you get the piezometric head h. This is the level a vertical piezometer tube would show above the datum. It contains no kinetic energy.

h = z + p/(ρg)

h is the piezometric head in m; z is elevation in m; p is gauge pressure in Pa; ρ is density in kg/m³; g is 9.81 m/s².

The TEL is a plot of H against distance along the pipe. The HGL is a plot of h against the same distance. At every section, TEL − HGL = V²/(2g). The vertical gap is the velocity head. Use gauge pressure, not absolute pressure. Absolute pressure would add the atmospheric head, about 10.3 m of water, to both lines and leave the gap unchanged.

How the lines behave

Both lines fall in the direction of flow when there is no pump. Friction and fittings remove mechanical energy, so H decreases. The slope of the TEL over a straight uniform pipe is h_f/L, where h_f is the friction head loss in m and L is the length in m. This slope is dimensionless. A minor loss at a valve or bend appears as a local vertical drop.

The HGL usually falls too, but it can rise. In a diffuser, the pipe area increases and V falls. The velocity head V²/(2g) becomes smaller, so the gap between TEL and HGL shrinks. If the TEL falls only a little due to loss, the HGL rises. The TEL itself still falls unless a pump adds energy.

At a reservoir surface, V is almost zero and p is zero gauge. So TEL = HGL = reservoir surface elevation. At a free outlet into air, p = 0 gauge, so HGL = z at the outlet. The TEL is then one velocity head above the outlet elevation. At a high point of a siphon, the HGL can fall below the pipe, meaning the pressure is below atmospheric.

The figure uses the worked example. At A, HGL = 9.077 m and TEL = 9.192 m. At B, HGL = 8.558 m and TEL = 8.673 m. The vertical gap is 0.115 m at both sections because the diameter and velocity do not change.

Pressure checks from the HGL

The HGL is a pressure map. At any point, the pressure head is the vertical distance from the pipe centreline up to the HGL. If the HGL is above the pipe, the pressure is positive. If the HGL falls below the pipe, the gauge pressure is negative, so the pressure is below atmospheric. That can collapse a thin pipe or draw air in through a joint.

The slope of the HGL is often called the hydraulic gradient. For a uniform pipe with constant velocity head, the HGL and TEL are parallel, so both have slope h_f/L. Where the velocity changes, the slopes differ. A pump raises both lines by the pump head. A turbine lowers both lines by the head extracted. Pump and turbine jumps are treated in Units 6 and 7.

To draw the lines, mark the total head at each known section, join them with straight segments, and put the HGL one velocity head below. Add a vertical step at each minor loss. Check every reservoir, free outlet and high point. This sketch catches impossible pressures before you calculate pipe sizes.

Worked example

Question. Water flows at V = 1.5 m/s in a 100 mm diameter pipe. At section A, z = 5.0 m and p = 40 kPa gauge. At section B, 30 m downstream, z = 5.5 m and p = 30 kPa gauge. Find the HGL and TEL at A and B, and the head loss between them. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Convert pressures: pA = 40 kPa = 40000 Pa and pB = 30 kPa = 30000 Pa.
  2. Pressure head at A: pA/(ρg) = 40000/(1000 × 9.81) = 4.077 m.
  3. HGL at A: hA = zA + pA/(ρg) = 5.0 + 4.077 = 9.077 m.
  4. Velocity head: V²/(2g) = 1.5²/(2 × 9.81) = 2.25/19.62 = 0.115 m.
  5. TEL at A: HA = hA + V²/(2g) = 9.077 + 0.115 = 9.192 m.
  6. Pressure head at B: pB/(ρg) = 30000/(1000 × 9.81) = 3.058 m.
  7. HGL at B: hB = zB + pB/(ρg) = 5.5 + 3.058 = 8.558 m.
  8. TEL at B: HB = hB + V²/(2g) = 8.558 + 0.115 = 8.673 m.
  9. Head loss: hL = HA − HB = 9.192 − 8.673 = 0.519 m.

Answer. HGL at A = 9.077 m and TEL at A = 9.192 m; HGL at B = 8.558 m and TEL at B = 8.673 m; head loss between A and B = 0.519 m.

Exam traps

  • Putting the HGL above the TEL. The TEL always includes the velocity head, so it must be at least one velocity head above the HGL.
  • Using absolute pressure in p/(ρg). The head formula uses gauge pressure; absolute pressure adds the same atmospheric head to every section.
  • Forgetting that the HGL can rise in a diffuser. A falling velocity head shrinks the gap, so the HGL may rise even while the TEL falls.
  • Writing the slope of the TEL as h_f/L for a pipe with changing diameter. That slope is valid for a uniform pipe; use the actual head drop between sections otherwise.
  • Drawing a straight line through a reservoir or free outlet without fixing the level. At a reservoir surface TEL = HGL = surface elevation; at a free outlet p = 0 gauge.

4.7 Pipes in series and parallel; equivalent pipes

Real pipelines rarely run as one straight pipe. A pump feeds branches that split and rejoin, or lengths of different diameter joined end to end. This chapter gives two rules. In series, every pipe carries the same discharge and the head losses add. In parallel, the same head loss drives every branch and the discharges add. With these rules a whole network becomes one equivalent pipe: a single length and diameter that gives the same total discharge for the same total head loss. That is how designers size mains and check whether a branch gets enough flow.

You need: Darcy-Weisbach friction factor, major and minor losses, and HGL and TEL.

Series pipes: one discharge, losses add

Two pipes in series are joined end to end. The same discharge Q passes through both, because no fluid is stored or lost at the joint. The total head loss is the sum of the losses in each pipe plus any minor loss at the joint: H = Σhfi + Σhmi. For straight pipes, Darcy-Weisbach gives hf = f(L/D)(V²/2g) = 8fLQ²/(π²gD⁵). Here f is the friction factor (dimensionless), L is length (m), D is diameter (m), Q is discharge (m³/s), V is mean velocity (m/s), and g = 9.81 m/s².

For the same f and a chosen equivalent diameter De, the equivalent length Le satisfies Le/De⁵ = Σ(Li/Di⁵). Note the fifth power: a short narrow pipe can add more loss than a long wide one. Series equivalent length is not a simple sum of lengths.

Parallel pipes: one head loss, discharges add

Two pipes in parallel leave a node A and rejoin at node B. Both see the same pressure difference, so the head loss from A to B is the same through each branch: hf1 = hf2 = H. The total discharge is the sum: Q = Q₁ + Q₂ + ...

For the same f, hf = 8fLQ²/(π²gD⁵) gives Q ∝ √(D⁵/L). Thus Q₁/Q₂ = √[(D₁⁵/L₁)/(D₂⁵/L₂)]. If all parallel pipes have the same D and f, 1/√Le = Σ1/√Li. A longer branch carries less flow, not more. Use HGL and TEL to see that both branches start and end at the same energy levels.

Equivalent pipe: one pipe replacing the network

An equivalent pipe is a single pipe that carries the same total Q for the same total head loss H as the original system. To find it:

  1. Replace each series run by one length using Le/De⁵ = ΣLi/Di⁵ at a chosen De.
  2. Replace parallel branches by one length using 1/√Le = Σ1/√Li when D and f are equal.
  3. If diameters or f differ, write hf = 8fLQ²/(π²gD⁵) for each branch, set head losses equal in parallel, add Q in parallel, and solve.

Include minor losses by adding their hm = K(V²/2g) to the branch head loss, or by an equivalent length Le = KD/f. Check units: L and D in metres, Q in m³/s.

Worked example

Question. Two parallel pipes connect the same two nodes. Pipe 1: L₁ = 100 m, D₁ = 0.10 m. Pipe 2: L₂ = 200 m, D₂ = 0.10 m. Both have f = 0.02. The total flow is Q = 0.03 m³/s. Find the flow in each pipe and the equivalent length at D = 0.10 m. Neglect minor losses.

  1. For parallel pipes, hf1 = hf2. With hf = 8fLQ²/(π²gD⁵) and equal f, D, g: L₁Q₁² = L₂Q₂².
  2. So Q₁/Q₂ = √(L₂/L₁) = √(200/100) = √2 = 1.414.
  3. Total Q = Q₁ + Q₂ = Q₂(1.414 + 1) = 2.414Q₂. Thus Q₂ = 0.03/2.414 = 0.0124 m³/s.
  4. Q₁ = 1.414 × 0.0124 = 0.0176 m³/s. Check: 0.0176 + 0.0124 = 0.0300 m³/s.
  5. Equivalent length for equal D and f: 1/√Le = 1/√100 + 1/√200 = 0.1000 + 0.0707 = 0.1707 m-1/2. So Le = 1/(0.1707)² = 34.3 m.

Answer. Q₁ = 0.0176 m³/s, Q₂ = 0.0124 m³/s, and the equivalent length is Le = 34.3 m at D = 0.10 m.

Exam traps

  • In series, adding discharges is wrong: every series pipe has the same Q.
  • In parallel, adding head losses is wrong: every branch has the same hf between the two nodes.
  • For a series equivalent length use Le/De⁵ = ΣLi/Di⁵, not Le = ΣLi.
  • Use D in metres and Q in m³/s in hf = 8fLQ²/(π²gD⁵); mixing mm and m gives large errors.
  • A longer parallel branch carries less flow when D and f are equal, because Q ∝ 1/√L.

4.8 Water hammer and siphons

A valve slams shut at the end of a long pipe. Water cannot stop instantly; it piles against the valve and a pressure wave races back up the pipe. That surge is water hammer, strong enough to split a main. The same physics, reversed, explains the siphon: a pipe climbs over a crest where pressure drops below atmospheric and delivers water to a lower level with no pump. Both occur in hydropower, building supply, canal irrigation and long pumping mains.

You need: bulk modulus and compressibility (see Unit 1, Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics), Bernoulli's equation (see Unit 3, Fluid Dynamics and Flow Measurement) and the 4.6 Hydraulic gradient and total energy lines.

Water hammer: the surge after a fast closure

In steady flow the valve pressure follows the hydraulic gradient. Shut the valve quickly and water at the valve stops. Water behind still moves at V and slams into the stopped slice. Pressure jumps, the pipe wall stretches, and a compression wave runs back to the reservoir at wave speed a.

At the reservoir the wave reflects as a low-pressure wave and returns. Pressure swings above and below steady value, decaying as friction eats energy. Old plumbing knocks for this reason; the name comes from the noise and the hammer-blow force on bends and valves.

Closure speed sets surge size. Let L be pipe length in m and Tc the closing time in s. If Tc < 2L/a, the wave returns after closure: rapid closure, full surge. If Tc ≥ 2L/a, the reflected wave arrives during closing and cancels part of the rise: slow closure, smaller surge.

Wave speed and the Joukowsky pressure rise

a = √(K/ρ) ÷ √(1 + K D / (E t))

a = wave speed in m/s; K = bulk modulus of water, about 2.2 GPa; ρ = density of water, 1000 kg/m³; D = pipe internal diameter in m; E = Young's modulus of pipe material in Pa (about 200 GPa for steel); t = wall thickness in m. K D/(E t) compares water squeeze with pipe stretch.

A rigid pipe (E → ∞) gives a = √(K/ρ) ≈ 1483 m/s. A thin steel wall lowers a to about 1200 m/s; a thick wall or concrete raises it.

Δp = ρ a V

Δp = surge pressure above steady pressure, in Pa; ρ = water density, 1000 kg/m³; a = wave speed from the first formula, m/s; V = velocity change in m/s, equal to full flow velocity for rapid closure. Valid only for rapid closure.

In head form ΔH = aV/g. A 1236 m/s wave with V = 2.0 m/s gives ΔH = 252 m. The surge adds to steady pressure, so design pressure is psteady + Δp.

Four defences: close slowly so Tc ≥ 2L/a; fit a surge tank, a vertical standpipe near the valve; fit an air vessel, a trapped air pocket that cushions the surge; add a relief valve.

The siphon: lifting water with low pressure

A siphon carries water over an obstacle higher than both free surfaces, from an upper reservoir to a lower one, with no pump. Some syllabi spell it syphon. Layout: up leg, crest, down leg.

Driving head is the level difference H between free surfaces, not crest height. Bernoulli from upper surface (1) to outlet (2): H = hL + V²/2g, with hL total pipe loss in m and V outlet velocity in m/s. Gravity pulls the longer, heavier down leg and drags the up leg along.

p<sub>c</sub>/ρg = p<sub>atm</sub>/ρg − z<sub>c</sub> − V<sub>c</sub>²/(2g) − h<sub>L,up</sub>

pc = absolute pressure at crest, Pa; patm = atmospheric pressure, about 101.3 kPa; zc = crest height above upper free surface, m; Vc = velocity at crest, m/s; hL,up = friction loss in up leg, m.

The three terms after the atmospheric term are subtracted, so crest pressure is below atmospheric.

Crest pressure cannot fall below vapour pressure, about 2.3 kPa absolute at 20 °C (0.24 m of water absolute). Setting pc = pvap gives maximum crest height roughly 10.3 − 0.24 − Vc²/2g − hL,up metres, in practice 6 to 8 m. Higher and water boils at the crest, the column breaks, and the siphon stops. This is the same cavitation that damages pump inlets (see Unit 7, Centrifugal and Reciprocating Pumps).

  • Priming: pipe must be full of water with no air pocket. Air at crest expands instead of pulling, column separates, nothing flows. Fill the pipe, or use a small pump or valve to drive air out.
  • Uses: emptying a sump or fish tank, drawing canal water over a field bund, taking water from a dam reservoir over the dam body, an emergency drain that needs no power.

Worked example

Question. A steel pipe of internal diameter D = 0.20 m, wall thickness t = 5 mm and length L = 500 m carries water at V = 2.0 m/s. Take E = 200 GPa, K = 2.2 GPa and ρ = 1000 kg/m³. A valve closes rapidly. Find wave speed, Joukowsky pressure rise, equivalent head and the closure time below which closure is rapid.

  1. Water alone: K/ρ = 2.2×10⁹/1000 = 2.2×10⁶ m²/s², so √(K/ρ) = 1483.2 m/s.
  2. Pipe group: K D/(E t) = (2.2×10⁹ × 0.20)/(200×10⁹ × 0.005) = 0.44.
  3. Wave speed: a = 1483.2/√1.44 = 1236 m/s.
  4. Critical closure time: 2L/a = 2 × 500/1236 = 0.809 s. Closure is rapid only below 0.809 s.
  5. Surge pressure: Δp = ρ a V = 1000 × 1236 × 2.0 = 2.472×10⁶ Pa = 2.472 MPa.
  6. Equivalent head: Δp/(ρg) = 2.472×10⁶/(1000 × 9.81) = 252.0 m.

Answer. a = 1236 m/s, Δp = 2.472 MPa (252.0 m of water head); rapid only when the valve shuts in under 0.809 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A siphon lifts water over a crest 6.0 m above the upper reservoir. Velocity at crest is 1.5 m/s and up-leg loss is 0.8 m. Take patm = 101.3 kPa and ρ = 1000 kg/m³. Find absolute crest pressure and state whether the siphon works.

  1. Velocity head: V²/2g = 1.5²/(2 × 9.81) = 0.115 m.
  2. Head removed: zc + V²/2g + hL,up = 6.0 + 0.115 + 0.8 = 6.915 m.
  3. pc = 101300 − 1000 × 9.81 × 6.915 = 33.5 kPa absolute.
  4. This is far above vapour pressure (about 2.3 kPa at 20 °C), so the column stays unbroken and the siphon works.

Answer. Crest pressure is about 33.5 kPa absolute, well above vapour pressure, so the siphon runs without cavitation.

Exam traps

  • Using Δp = ρaV for slow closure. Joukowsky holds only when Tc < 2L/a; for slow closure use roughly Δp ≈ 2ρLV/Tc.
  • Taking a = 1483 m/s for every pipe. That is the rigid-pipe value; a steel pipe gives 1236 m/s, so rigid value overestimates surge by about 20 %.
  • Forgetting surge adds to steady pressure. Design pressure is steady plus surge, and a 252 m surge dwarfs friction head.
  • Believing a siphon can lift water to any height under 10.3 m. Crest pressure must stay above vapour pressure; velocity head and up-leg friction leave about 6 to 8 m.
  • Thinking siphon discharge depends on crest height. Driving head is the difference between free surface levels; crest height only controls pressure there.
  • Sign slip: gauge pressure at the crest is negative, so crest pressure is below atmospheric, not above it.

Analyze How does an air vessel reduce water hammer in a pumping main? 6 marks

Trapped air acts as a spring. Extra water pushed by the surge compresses it, and later the air pushes water back, so velocity changes gradually instead of instantly. Wave amplitude falls and pump discharge is smoothed.

Not for the exam. Real surge analysis uses the method of characteristics, which marches the wave along the pipe in short time steps and adds friction at each step; Joukowsky is only a first estimate. Takeaways

  1. Reynolds number decides the flow regime: low Re gives a straight dye thread, high Re gives a blurred cloud, and the change happens around 2000–4000.
  2. Laminar pipe flow has a parabolic velocity profile, u_max = 2V, and the Hagen–Poiseuille result that discharge grows as D⁴ and falls as 1/μ.
  3. The Darcy–Weisbach equation converts wall friction into a head loss proportional to f, L, V² and 1/D.
  4. The friction factor is not a constant: it depends on Re in smooth pipes, on ε/D in fully rough pipes, and on both in between, which is what the Moody diagram shows.
  5. Real systems lose head both along the pipe and at every entry, bend, valve and exit, so minor losses can dominate in short, crowded pipework.
  6. The total energy line always falls in the direction of flow by the head loss, never rises except at a pump; the HGL sits one velocity head below it.
  7. Series pipes share discharge and add head losses; parallel pipes share head loss and add discharges; either way the system reduces to one equivalent pipe.
  8. Fast valve closure stores kinetic energy as a pressure wave, so the surge head cV/g can be many times the working head; a siphon works only while the crest pressure stays above vapour pressure.

Glossary

Boundary shear stress
The shear stress at the pipe wall, τ₀ = fρV²/8, which balances the pressure force in fully developed flow.
Cavitation
Formation and violent collapse of vapour bubbles when local pressure falls below the vapour pressure, damaging the pipe or impeller.
Discharge
The volume of fluid passing a section per unit time, Q = AV, in m³/s.
Dynamic viscosity
The property μ, in Pa·s, that measures a fluid's resistance to shear, equal to shear stress divided by velocity gradient.
Eddy
A rotating pocket of fluid in turbulent flow that carries momentum across streamlines and increases mixing.
Friction slope
The head loss per unit length, i = h_f/L, dimensionless.
Head loss
The energy per unit weight, in metres of flowing fluid, lost to friction and fittings between two sections.
Kinematic viscosity
ν = μ/ρ in m²/s, the dynamic viscosity divided by density.
Laminar sublayer
The very thin layer of laminar-flowing fluid next to a wall in turbulent flow; roughness smaller than it does not affect f.
Mean velocity
The discharge divided by the cross-sectional area, V = Q/A, in m/s.
Newtonian fluid
A fluid in which shear stress is directly proportional to velocity gradient, so μ is constant at a given temperature.
No-slip condition
The rule that fluid in contact with a solid surface has the same velocity as the surface, so velocity is zero at a fixed pipe wall.
Penstock
The large pipe that carries water from a reservoir or surge tank down to a hydro turbine.
Piezometric head
p/(ρg) + z, in metres, the sum of pressure head and elevation head at a section.
Priming
Filling a siphon or pump with liquid and removing air so that the pressure difference can act on a continuous liquid column.
Roughness height
The equivalent sand-grain height ε, in metres, representing the size of wall irregularities in turbulent flow.
Shear stress
Tangential force per unit area, in Pa, acting between fluid layers or between fluid and wall.
Siphon
A pipe or duct that lifts liquid above its source level and discharges it at a lower level, driven by the head difference.
Surge tank
An open or closed tank on a pipeline that absorbs the pressure wave of water hammer.
Transition zone (pipe flow)
The Reynolds number band, roughly 2000 to 4000, where the flow alternates between laminar and turbulent.
Velocity head
The term V²/(2g) in metres, the height to which the kinetic energy of the flow could lift the fluid.
Water hammer
Pressure fluctuation in a pipe caused by a rapid change of flow velocity.
