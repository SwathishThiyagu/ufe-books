6.1 Impact of jets on vanes

A jet that strikes a vane changes direction, and its momentum changes. The vane feels a force. The force is largest when the jet is turned back on itself, and it falls when the vane moves away because the jet meets the vane more slowly. This chapter derives that force for flat plates, curved vanes and a wheel with many vanes. The results feed directly into the Pelton wheel and every machine that uses a jet. Some syllabi call this impact of jet on flat and curved vanes.

You need: the linear momentum equation from Unit 3, Fluid Dynamics and Flow Measurement, and the definitions of density and area from Unit 1, Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics.

A stationary plate stops the jet

Water leaves a nozzle as a jet of area a (m²) and speed V (m/s). The mass reaching a stationary vane per second is ṁ = ρaV, where ρ is density (kg/m³) and ṁ is mass flow rate (kg/s). Take the jet direction as positive. The vane changes the jet's velocity, so the jet's momentum changes. The force on the vane is equal and opposite to the force that changes the jet's momentum.

For a flat plate held normal to the jet, the water leaves along the plate. It has no velocity left in the jet direction. Every kilogram loses velocity V, so the force is:

F = ρaV²

F is the force on the vane (N); ρ is density (kg/m³); a is jet area (m²); V is jet speed (m/s).

For a curved vane that turns the jet through angle θ, the leaving velocity has component V cos θ in the original direction. θ is the deflection angle, measured from the old jet direction to the new one. The force is:

F = ρaV²(1 − cos θ)

θ is the angle through which the jet is deflected; cos θ uses the same angle unit as θ.

Put θ = 90° for a flat plate: cos 90° = 0, so F = ρaV². Put θ = 180° for a hemispherical cup: cos 180° = −1, so F = 2ρaV². The cup gets twice the force of a flat plate from the same jet. This is why turbine buckets are curved to turn water back, not just sideways. The force acts on the vane; the water feels the same force in the opposite direction.

A moving vane sees a slower jet

Now the vane moves away from the nozzle at speed u (m/s) along the jet. If u < V, the jet meets the vane at relative speed V − u. The mass striking one moving vane per second is ρa(V − u), not ρaV. After the turn, the relative speed is still V − u when friction is neglected. The change of absolute velocity in the jet direction is (V − u)(1 − cos θ). Hence:

F = ρa(V − u)²(1 − cos θ)

u is vane speed (m/s); V − u is the relative speed at entry and exit (m/s); other symbols are as before.

For a moving flat plate, θ = 90°, so F = ρa(V − u)². If u = V, the jet never catches the plate, so F = 0. If u = 0, the formula returns the stationary result.

Work done per second is P = Fu, where P is power in watts (W). Vane efficiency is η = P/(½ρaV³). The denominator is the kinetic energy of the jet passing per second. For a single vane this η uses the whole jet's kinetic energy ½ρaV³ as the input; it is a convention, not the energy of the water that actually strikes the vane. For one 180° curved vane, P = 2ρa u(V − u)². This is zero at u = 0, rises to a peak at u = V/3, and returns to zero at u = V. A single vane is therefore not best at half speed; it is best at one-third speed.

Many vanes use the whole jet

In a wheel, one vane follows another. The jet is always hitting some vane, so all the water from the nozzle is deflected. The mass striking per second is the full ρaV. At entry the relative speed is V − u; at exit it is V − u in a new direction. The force is:

F = ρaV(V − u)(1 − cos θ)

F is the force on the series of vanes (N); the mass flow is the full jet mass flow ρaV.

Work per second is P = Fu = ρaV(V − u)(1 − cos θ)u. Divide by ρaV³ and put x = u/V. Then P/(ρaV³) = (1 − cos θ)x(1 − x). For a 180° bucket, this is 2x(1 − x), a parabola. It peaks at x = 0.5, so u = V/2. That is the ideal bucket speed for a Pelton wheel; 6.4 uses this result. Vane efficiency is η = P/(½ρaV³); for the series this becomes 2(1 − cos θ)x(1 − x).

For u = 0 the series formula becomes ρaV²(1 − cos θ), the stationary curved vane. For θ = 90° it becomes ρaV(V − u), the flat-plate series result. The series arrangement gives more work than one vane because the full jet mass flow ρaV is deflected, while a single vane is struck only by ρa(V − u).

Worked example

Question. A jet of water 40 mm in diameter leaves a nozzle at V = 25 m/s and strikes one curved vane. The vane moves in the jet direction at u = 10 m/s. The vane deflects the jet through θ = 120°. Friction is neglected and ρ = 1000 kg/m³. Find the force on the vane and the work done per second.

  1. Diameter d = 40 mm = 0.04 m. Jet area a = πd²/4 = π(0.04)²/4 = 1.257 × 10⁻³ m².
  2. Relative speed at inlet Vr = V − u = 25 − 10 = 15 m/s.
  3. For one moving vane, F = ρaVr²(1 − cos θ).
  4. cos 120° = −0.5, so 1 − cos 120° = 1.5.
  5. F = 1000 × 1.257 × 10⁻³ × 15² × 1.5 = 424 N.
  6. Work per second P = Fu = 424 × 10 = 4240 W = 4.24 kW.

Answer. The force on the vane is about 424 N and the work done per second is 4.24 kW.

Exam traps

  • Using the absolute jet speed V instead of the relative speed V − u for a moving vane. The jet meets the vane at V − u.
  • Dropping the (1 − cos θ) factor. A flat plate has θ = 90° and factor 1; a 180° cup has factor 2.
  • Mixing the single-vane and series formulas. One moving vane uses mass flow ρa(V − u); a series of vanes uses ρaV.
  • Forgetting that diameter is not area. Convert 40 mm to 0.04 m and then use a = πd²/4.
  • Reporting the force on the water as the force on the vane. The two are equal in size and opposite in direction.

6.2 Velocity triangles and Euler's turbomachine equation

A turbine runner is a wheel with blades. Water enters at one radius and leaves at another. The blade moves, so the water's velocity seen from the ground and its velocity seen from the blade are different. A velocity triangle draws all three velocities at inlet and at outlet. Euler's turbomachine equation turns the change in the water's angular momentum into head and work. Every Pelton, Francis and Kaplan design uses these two triangles. The same equation, with signs reversed, gives the head a pump adds.

You need: the angular momentum equation from Unit 3, and relative velocity over a moving vane from 6.1.

The three velocities at one point

At any point on a runner blade, three velocities meet. The absolute velocity V is the water's velocity measured by a stationary observer, in m/s. The blade speed u is the velocity of the runner at that radius, in m/s. The relative velocity V_r is the water's velocity seen by someone sitting on the blade, in m/s. These three form a closed triangle: V = u + V_r as vectors. The blade speed follows the runner size and speed:

u = πDN/60

u is blade speed in m/s; D is the diameter at that point in m; N is runner speed in rpm.

Split V into two perpendicular parts. The whirl velocity V_w is along the direction of blade motion. The flow velocity V_f is at right angles to u, through the blade passage. Then V_w = V cos α and V_f = V sin α, where α is the angle between V and u. At inlet use subscript 1; at outlet use subscript 2 and draw a second triangle. The blade angles decide the shape. Water should enter without shock, so V_r1 should line up with the inlet blade angle. Figure 1 shows the inlet triangle for the worked example below.

Euler's turbomachine equation

Unit 3 gives the angular momentum equation: torque on the fluid equals the rate of change of its angular momentum. For a runner, T = ρQ(r₁V_w1 − r₂V_w2), where T is torque in N·m, ρ is density in kg/m³, Q is volume flow in m³/s, r is radius in m, and V_w is whirl velocity in m/s. The runner turns at angular speed ω = 2πN/60 rad/s, so power is P = Tω. Since u = ωr, P = ρQ(u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2). Divide by weight flow ρQg to get head. This is Euler's turbomachine equation:

H_E = (u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2)/g

H_E is Euler head in m of fluid; u is blade speed in m/s; V_w is whirl velocity in m/s; g is 9.81 m/s²; subscript 1 is inlet, 2 is outlet.

w = u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2

w is work per unit mass in J/kg.

The equation is a difference, so the sign of V_w2 matters. If the outlet whirl is in the same direction as u₂, subtract. If the runner turns the water so that V_w2 points opposite to u₂, put V_w2 as negative; the two terms add. A good turbine tries to leave the water with little or no whirl, V_w2 ≈ 0, giving H_E = u₁V_w1/g. Never use the full V in place of V_w.

Degree of reaction and the pump sign

Degree of reaction R tells how much of the head is converted in the runner by falling pressure rather than by changing kinetic energy. R = (static pressure drop in the runner)/(total head across the runner). For an impulse turbine the pressure is the same on both sides of the runner, so R = 0. For a reaction turbine the pressure falls across the runner, so 0 < R < 1; a Francis runner typically has R around 0.5. Francis and Kaplan are reaction machines; see 6.5. A Pelton wheel is impulse with R = 0; see 6.4.

Euler's equation above is written for a turbine: the fluid gives energy to the runner, so work is positive out. For a pump the runner gives energy to the fluid. The same equation is used with inlet and outlet signs reversed: H_pump = (u₂V_w2 − u₁V_w1)/g. A pump therefore needs outlet whirl larger than inlet whirl. The full pump triangles, minimum starting speed and NPSH are in Unit 7.

Worked example

Question. At the inlet of a turbine runner the blade speed is u₁ = 20 m/s. Water enters with absolute velocity V₁ = 25 m/s at 30° to the direction of blade motion. Find V_w1, V_f1 and V_r1. If the outlet whirl is zero, find the work per unit mass.

  1. Resolve V₁ along and across the blade motion: V_w1 = V₁ cos 30° = 25 × 0.8660 = 21.65 m/s.
  2. V_f1 = V₁ sin 30° = 25 × 0.5000 = 12.5 m/s.
  3. Relative velocity from the triangle: V_r1 = √(V_f1² + (V_w1 − u₁)²) = √(12.5² + (21.65 − 20)²) = √(156.25 + 2.72) = √158.97 = 12.61 m/s ≈ 12.6 m/s.
  4. Outlet whirl V_w2 = 0. Work per unit mass w = u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2 = 20 × 21.65 − u₂ × 0 = 433 J/kg.

Answer. V_w1 = 21.65 m/s, V_f1 = 12.5 m/s, V_r1 ≈ 12.6 m/s, and with zero outlet whirl the work per unit mass is 433 J/kg.

Exam traps

  • Using V instead of V_w in Euler's equation. Only the whirl component creates torque about the axis.
  • Putting V_w2 as positive when the outlet whirl is opposite to u₂. Opposite whirl is negative, so the two terms add.
  • Mixing head and work. Euler head is in metres; work per unit mass is in J/kg. Divide by g to go from J/kg to m.
  • Calling degree of reaction zero for a Francis runner. Impulse means R = 0; Francis is reaction, 0 < R < 1.
  • Forgetting that u changes with radius. Use u at the inlet radius for u₁ and at the outlet radius for u₂.

6.3 Classification of hydraulic turbines

Hydro sites differ in head and discharge. Three questions classify a turbine: does pressure fall inside the runner, which way does water move, and how much head is available. Engineers shortlist a runner type before calculation. Only a few universities teach this as a stand-alone topic.

You need velocity triangles and Euler's equation (6.2) and Bernoulli's equation from Unit 3, Fluid Dynamics and Flow Measurement.

Impulse against reaction: what the pressure does

An impulse turbine uses a nozzle to turn the whole head into a free jet at atmospheric pressure. The jet strikes buckets on a wheel spinning in air. Pressure at bucket inlet equals pressure at outlet, both atmospheric. Only jet speed and direction change, as in 6.1.

A reaction turbine has a flooded runner. Water enters at high pressure and pressure falls across the runner. Energy comes partly from falling pressure and partly from velocity change. The draft tube (6.6) lets a reaction runner sit above tailwater.

The separating number is degree of reaction:

R = (p₁ − p₂) / (ρ g H)

p₁ and p₂ are static pressures at runner inlet and outlet (Pa); ρ is water density (1000 kg/m³); g = 9.81 m/s²; H is the head across the runner, total head at inlet minus total head at outlet at the same datum (m). R = 0 for an impulse runner. R > 0 for reaction, near 0.5 for a Francis and higher for a Kaplan.

High head gives a fast jet from a small nozzle; an open wheel is cheap. Low head needs large discharge, which only a flooded runner with large flow area can pass.

Flow direction through the runner

The second label is the water path inside the runner.

  • Tangential: jet along the rim in the wheel plane. Pelton.
  • Radial: enters at the outer edge, moves inward. Older designs.
  • Mixed: enters radially, leaves axially. Francis.
  • Axial: parallel to the shaft. Kaplan.

Pressure and flow direction are independent labels. Francis is reaction and mixed; Kaplan is reaction and axial. A closed casing does not make a machine impulse — only pressure drop across the runner decides.

Head bands and jet count

Head is the third filter. Bands are rules of thumb; exact boundaries differ, but the order does not.

At fixed head, jet speed V = √(2gH), so one nozzle passes limited discharge. A multi-jet Pelton has two to six nozzles around one wheel. Jets raise discharge, not head.

Worked example

Question. A canal site has H = 25 m, Q = 40 m³/s, overall efficiency 0.90. Choose a family and find shaft power in MW.

  1. H = 25 m is low head; Q = 40 m³/s is large discharge, so choose Kaplan (axial reaction). Pelton needs high head; Francis is medium head.
  2. P = ρ g Q H η, with P shaft power in W.
  3. Water power = 1000 × 9.81 × 40 × 25 = 9.81 MW.
  4. Shaft power = 9.81 × 0.90 = 8.829 MW.

Answer. Kaplan suits H = 25 m and Q = 40 m³/s. Shaft power at η = 0.90 is 8.829 MW, about 8.83 MW. Specific speed would confirm — see 6.7.

Exam traps

  • Calling Francis impulse because it has a casing. Casing proves nothing; pressure drop across runner decides.
  • Saying a reaction runner has no pressure change. Pressure falls across runner; that fall gives much energy.
  • Swapping labels: Francis is reaction/mixed, Kaplan reaction/axial. Neither is tangential.
  • Thinking more jets make Pelton reaction. Jets only raise discharge at same head.
  • Writing R = 1 for every reaction turbine. R = 0 for impulse; reaction lies between 0 and 1.

6.4 Pelton wheel: working, work done and efficiencies

A Pelton wheel is a high-head impulse turbine. A nozzle turns the pressure of water in a long penstock into one or more free jets. The jet strikes bucket-shaped vanes on a runner and is turned through almost 180°. The runner spins, the shaft drives a generator, and the water leaves at atmospheric pressure. Pelton units suit heads from roughly 100 m to over 1000 m, as in Himalayan run-of-river plants. The whole design chases one condition: bucket speed about half jet speed.

You need 6.1 force on a moving curved vane, 6.2 Euler head, and Bernoulli for the nozzle from Unit 3.

Parts and working

Water from the reservoir enters a penstock, a large pipe that carries it down to the powerhouse. At the end, a nozzle converts the pressure head H into a high-speed free jet. A spear inside the nozzle moves in and out; pushing it forward reduces the jet area and flow, which is how the unit is controlled. For a jet of area a, Q = aV1, and Bernoulli with a nozzle coefficient Cv gives V1 = Cv√(2gH). Cv is about 0.97 to 0.99.

The jet hits the runner, a wheel carrying buckets around its rim. Each bucket is a double cup with a central ridge; the ridge splits the jet into two halves, so the two axial thrusts cancel. A bucket turns the water through about 165°, not 180°, so the outgoing water does not hit the back of the following bucket. The runner is inside a casing that keeps splashing in and leads water to the tailrace. A small brake nozzle can be used to stop the unit.

With D runner diameter and d jet diameter, the jet ratio is m = D/d. The number of buckets is often estimated as Z = 15 + D/(2d); only a few syllabi ask for this. The speed ratio is φ = u/V1, where u is bucket speed at the pitch circle. Pelton wheels run best near φ = 0.46, a little below 0.5 because of bucket friction and the 165° turn. Large power needs several jets on one runner, because one jet can only pass so much water.

Velocity triangles, work and maximum efficiency

Take the jet direction as positive x. V1 is jet speed and u is bucket speed, both in m/s. Relative inlet speed is Vr1 = V1 − u. At outlet, let the bucket deflect the relative velocity through angle δ, measured from the inlet relative direction. Friction reduces it to Vr2 = kVr1, where k is the bucket friction factor, about 0.9.

At outlet, the absolute whirl component is Vw2 = u + kVr1 cos δ. For δ = 165°, cos δ is negative, so Vw2 is small and often slightly negative; the water leaves with a small backward whirl. The inlet whirl is Vw1 = V1. Euler's equation gives work per unit weight of water:

<i>w</i> = (<i>u</i>/<i>g</i>)(<i>V</i><sub>w1</sub> − <i>V</i><sub>w2</sub>) = (<i>u</i>/<i>g</i>)[<i>V</i><sub>1</sub> − <i>u</i> − <i>k</i>(<i>V</i><sub>1</sub> − <i>u</i>)cos <i>δ</i>]

w is work per unit weight in metres of water; u and V1 are in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s²; k is the bucket friction factor; δ is the deflection angle. The force on the buckets is F = ρQ(Vw1 − Vw2) in newtons, with ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³.

Put k = 1 and δ = 180° for an ideal hemispherical bucket. Then Vw2 = 2u − V1 and w = 2u(V1 − u)/g. This is a parabola in u; it is zero at u = 0 and u = V1, and maximum at u = V1/2. The maximum work per unit weight is V1²/(2g). Hydraulic efficiency is ηh = w/H. With V1 = Cv√(2gH), ηh = 2u(V1 − u)/(gH); at u = V1/2, ηh = Cv². The force on the bucket is largest at u = 0, but no work is done there. For power and efficiency, u ≈ V1/2 is the target.

Efficiencies and multi-jet units

Mechanical efficiency ηm = shaft power / hydraulic power at the runner. It accounts for bearing friction, windage, and gland losses. Overall efficiency ηo = ηh × ηm; volumetric leakage is negligible in a Pelton wheel. The shaft power is P = ρgQHηo. Input water power is ρgQH. If only hydraulic efficiency is given, P = ρgQw is the hydraulic power.

At a high-head site, one jet may be too small for the power wanted. Several jets can strike the same runner at different points, all in one casing. Multi-jet Pelton turbines reach hundreds of megawatts. They also allow a smaller runner for the same power. A vertical shaft arrangement is common for large units.

Worked example

Question. A Pelton wheel works under a net head H = 400 m with a 0.15 m jet. Take Cv = 0.98, speed ratio φ = 0.46, bucket deflection δ = 165°, bucket friction factor k = 0.9, and ρ = 1000 kg/m³. Find the hydraulic efficiency and the shaft power, taking mechanical efficiency as 1.0.

  1. Jet speed: V1 = Cv√(2gH) = 0.98√(2 × 9.81 × 400) = 86.8 m/s.
  2. Bucket speed: u = φV1 = 0.46 × 86.8 = 39.9 m/s.
  3. Jet area: a = πd²/4 = π(0.15)²/4 = 0.01767 m². Flow: Q = aV1 = 0.01767 × 86.8 = 1.534 m³/s.
  4. Relative speeds: Vr1 = V1 − u = 86.8 − 39.9 = 46.9 m/s. Vr2 = kVr1 = 0.9 × 46.9 = 42.2 m/s.
  5. Outlet whirl: Vw2 = u + Vr2 cos δ = 39.9 + 42.2 cos 165° = 39.9 − 40.8 = −0.8 m/s.
  6. Work per unit weight: w = (u/g)(V1 − Vw2) = (39.9/9.81)(86.8 + 0.8) = 356.8 m.
  7. Hydraulic efficiency: ηh = w/H = 356.8/400 = 0.892.
  8. Power: P = ρgQw = 1000 × 9.81 × 1.534 × 356.8 = 5.37 × 10⁶ W = 5.37 MW.

Answer. The hydraulic efficiency is about 0.892 (89.2%) and the shaft power is about 5.37 MW when mechanical losses are neglected.

Exam traps

  • Do not put δ = 165° into Vw2 = u − k Vr1 cos δ. When δ is the deflection from the inlet relative direction, use Vw2 = u + k Vr1 cos δ; the cosine is then negative, so Vw2 is small and often negative.
  • The maximum efficiency condition u = V1/2 is not the maximum force condition. Force on the bucket is largest at u = 0, but the bucket does no work when it does not move.
  • Use the net head at the nozzle, after penstock friction, not the gross reservoir level. A few metres lost in the penstock changes the jet speed and the power.
  • V1 = Cv√(2gH), not √(2gH). Cv is about 0.97 to 0.99, so leaving it out makes V1 and the power too high.
  • ρgQw is hydraulic power. Multiply by mechanical efficiency to get shaft power; do not call it shaft power when ηm is less than 1.

6.5 Francis and Kaplan turbines

A Pelton wheel takes its energy from a jet of water in air. A Francis or Kaplan turbine takes it from water that stays under pressure inside the machine. The runner stays full of water, the pressure falls as the water crosses it, and a draft tube slows the leaving water and recovers that velocity head as pressure. Francis suits medium heads. Kaplan suits low heads with very large discharges. Most hydel stations, from a 6 m weir to a 200 m dam, use one of these two machines.

You need: 6.2 velocity triangles and Euler's equation, and 6.1 relative velocity.

A reaction turbine keeps the runner full

In a Pelton wheel the whole head becomes kinetic energy in the nozzle, and the buckets work in air at atmospheric pressure. In a reaction turbine the water enters the runner still under pressure and leaves it at a lower pressure, still moving. Pressure and velocity both change across the runner, so the machine reacts to both. The degree of reaction R compares the two.

R = (pressure head drop in the runner) ÷ (total head across the runner)

R is the degree of reaction (no unit); both heads are in metres of water. For a well-designed Francis runner R is about 0.5, so the guide vanes turn half the head into velocity and the runner takes the other half as a pressure drop.

The water path is fixed by the casing and the vanes around the runner.

  1. Spiral casing (scroll): its area shrinks as it goes round, so water reaches the stay ring all round the circle at nearly the same speed.
  2. Stay vanes: fixed vanes that guide the water from the spiral casing to the guide vanes with an even, steady flow, and support the top cover and stay ring.
  3. Guide vanes: adjustable vanes that set the direction and the amount of water entering the runner.
  4. Runner: the pressure drop and the work happen here.
  5. Draft tube: slows the leaving water and recovers head; its theory is in 6.6.

The guide vanes are the throttle. Opening them passes more water; closing them cuts the discharge and the power. No spear or nozzle is needed, because the water is already under pressure. This gives smooth governing, but it also ties discharge to load, which is why a reaction turbine is less forgiving at part load than a Pelton wheel.

Francis: radial in, axial out

A Francis runner is a ring of curved vanes held between a top crown and a bottom band. Water enters at the outer edge, moves inward across the vanes, and leaves at the inner edge near the shaft. It enters radially and leaves axially, so the flow is called mixed flow. A large unit may carry a few hundred cubic metres per second under a head of tens of metres.

The work comes from the change in angular momentum. Euler's equation for the runner is:

H_E = (u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2) ÷ g

H_E is the Euler head in m; u₁ and u₂ are the tangential runner speeds at inlet and outlet in m/s; V_w1 and V_w2 are the whirl components of the absolute velocity at inlet and outlet in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

A Francis runner is shaped so the water leaves with almost no whirl, V_w2 ≈ 0 at the design point. All the angular momentum is then given up and H_E = u₁V_w1 ÷ g. The guide vane angle α is measured between the absolute velocity and the wheel tangent; the runner blade angle β₁ is measured between the relative velocity and the tangent. They are different angles, set by different things.

At part load the guide vanes close, V_w1 falls, and the outlet whirl is no longer zero, so the Euler head drops faster than the discharge. Francis efficiency stays high, above 90% in large units, only between roughly 70% and 100% of design flow. Below that the flow leaves the blade surfaces, losses climb, and efficiency falls quickly.

Kaplan: a propeller for low heads

A Kaplan turbine is a propeller turbine with adjustable runner blades. Water flows straight along the shaft axis, with no radial component. The head is low, below about 30 m, and the discharge is very large: what a low head cannot give in pressure it must give in volume. The runner carries only a few blades, so it can pass that volume without choking.

The blade speed is u = πDN ÷ 60, where u is the blade speed in m/s, D is the diameter of that blade section in m (D = 2 × radius of the section), and N is the runner speed in rpm. The radius changes from hub to tip, so u grows from root to tip. The blade must therefore be twisted: set steeply near the hub and flatter near the tip, so each section meets the water at its own correct angle. That twist is why a Kaplan blade looks like a ship's propeller.

A Kaplan turbine is double-regulated: the governor moves the guide vanes and the runner blade angle together. As load falls, the blades turn to keep V_w2 near zero, so efficiency stays high down to about 30% of full load. A fixed-blade propeller turbine is efficient only near its design point, which is why the adjustable version is the one used where load changes through the day.

Worked example

Question. A Francis turbine runner has a tangential speed at inlet u₁ = 20 m/s and an inlet whirl component V_w1 = 18 m/s. At outlet the whirl component is zero. The net head at the runner inlet, after casing, stay vane and guide vane losses are removed, is 45 m. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find (a) the Euler head and (b) the hydraulic efficiency.

  1. Write Euler's equation for the runner: H_E = (u₁V_w1 − u₂V_w2) ÷ g.
  2. Outlet whirl is zero, so V_w2 = 0 and the second term u₂V_w2 vanishes whatever u₂ is.
  3. u₁V_w1 = 20 × 18 = 360 m²/s².
  4. H_E = 360 ÷ 9.81 = 36.70 m. This is the head the runner actually converts.
  5. Hydraulic efficiency is the Euler head divided by the net head at the runner inlet: η_h = H_E ÷ H_net = 36.70 ÷ 45.
  6. η_h = 0.815, that is 81.5%. The other 18.5% is friction and eddy loss inside the runner, plus the exit kinetic energy that the draft tube does not recover. The draft tube sits after the runner, so its loss lowers the overall efficiency, not the head at the runner inlet.

Answer. Euler head H_E = 36.7 m and hydraulic efficiency η_h = 81.5%.

Exam traps

  • Using the guide vane angle α in place of the runner blade angle β₁. α lies between the absolute velocity and the tangent; β₁ lies between the relative velocity and the tangent.
  • Putting the gross head into the hydraulic efficiency. Use the net head at the runner inlet, after casing, stay vane and guide vane losses. An efficiency above 100% means the head used was too small — for example the head measured after the runner, or a head with the same losses subtracted twice.
  • Treating V_w2 = 0 as always true. It holds at the design point only; at part load the leaving whirl comes back and the Euler head falls.
  • Saying a Kaplan is governed by the guide vanes alone. It is double-regulated: the runner blade angle moves with the vane opening.
  • Swapping the runners: Francis is mixed flow, radial in and axial out; Kaplan is axial throughout, with twisted adjustable blades.

6.6 Draft tube theory

A draft tube is a short diverging pipe that connects the runner exit of a reaction turbine to the tailrace. It exists because the water leaves a Francis or Kaplan runner fast, and that speed is wasted if the water simply spills into the tailwater. The tube slows the water and turns most of that kinetic energy back into pressure. This lets the turbine sit above the tailwater and still use nearly all the available head. Every hydro station with a reaction turbine uses one.

You need Bernoulli's equation from Unit 3 and the runner-exit velocity triangle from 6.2; the pressure behaviour across a reaction runner is in 6.5.

Why a draft tube is needed

In a reaction turbine the runner is full of water. The water leaves the runner at section 2 with velocity V₂. It must reach the tailrace, section 3, where the pressure is atmospheric, p_atm, and the velocity is V₃. Let the runner exit be H_s above the tailwater. H_s is called the suction head or setting of the turbine.

If the runner discharged straight into the air, the exit pressure would be atmospheric. Then the whole velocity head V₂²/2g would be lost. If the same runner is lifted above the tailwater to save civil cost, the pressure at exit falls below atmospheric. The draft tube is the diverging passage that makes this safe and useful.

p₂/(ρg) + V₂²/2g + H_s = p_atm/(ρg) + V₃²/2g + h_f

p₂ is absolute pressure at runner exit; ρ is water density; g = 9.81 m/s²; V₂ and V₃ are mean velocities at runner exit and tailrace; H_s is runner-exit height above tailwater; h_f is head lost in the tube.

p₂/(ρg) = p_atm/(ρg) − H_s − (V₂² − V₃²)/2g + h_f

the term (V₂² − V₃²)/2g is the velocity head the tube can convert; h_f raises p₂ slightly because friction uses up some energy.

The pressure p₂ is below p_atm by almost H_s plus that velocity head. p₂ must stay above the vapour pressure of water. If it falls to vapour pressure, the water boils, bubbles collapse, and the runner and tube are damaged. This is cavitation. The condition sets the maximum suction head for a given flow and temperature.

Recovery and efficiency

Continuity gives A₂V₂ = A₃V₃. A₂ and A₃ are the inlet and outlet areas. If the tube diverges, A₃ > A₂ and V₃ < V₂. The ideal pressure head recovered from the velocity change is Δh = (V₂² − V₃²)/2g. Put x = A₂/A₃. Then V₃ = xV₂ and Δh = (V₂²/2g)(1 − x²). A very large outlet makes x small and Δh close to V₂²/2g, but too fast a divergence separates the flow.

η_d = actual pressure head recovered ÷ ((V₂² − V₃²)/2g)

η_d is draft tube efficiency; actual pressure head recovered is in metres of water; the denominator is the ideal velocity-head difference in metres.

actual recovery = η_d (V₂² − V₃²)/2g

head lost = (1 − η_d)(V₂² − V₃²)/2g; real tubes have η_d between about 0.8 and 0.9.

Do not use a ratio of pressures. Both numerator and denominator are heads in metres. Efficiency does not fix the shape. The area ratio fixes the ideal recovery; the cone angle and surface finish fix how much of that ideal you get. The figure shows the ideal curve and a real tube with η_d = 0.85.

Types and the suction-head limit

Three shapes are common. The conical draft tube is a straight cone. It is simple and has low loss, so it is used when the runner axis is vertical and there is room below. The elbow draft tube turns the flow through a bend and then expands horizontally. It saves excavation depth and is common in large Francis stations. The Moody spreading tube has a central cone inside a wider passage; it spreads the flow without a sharp wall angle. Some syllabi ask for these names; the idea is the same: slow the water gently.

H_s,max ≈ (p_atm − p_vap)/(ρg) − (V₂² − V₃²)/2g

p_vap is vapour pressure of water; the first term is about 10 m at 20 °C; the second term is the velocity head converted in the tube; subtracting it shows that a larger velocity-head recovery reduces the allowable suction head.

At 20 °C, p_atm − p_vap is about 101 − 2.3 = 98.7 kPa, so the first term is about 10 m of water. A real setting is much smaller, often 2 to 6 m, because losses, transients and cavitation safety margins use up the rest. Higher tailwater in monsoon raises p₃ and helps; low tailwater in summer lowers p₃ and makes cavitation more likely. That is why the same turbine can cavitate in one season and not in another.

Worked example

Question. A draft tube has inlet diameter 0.6 m and outlet diameter 1.2 m. It carries Q = 1.5 m³/s. Its efficiency is 0.85. Find the ideal velocity-head difference, the head actually recovered, and the head lost.

  1. Inlet area A₂ = πD₂²/4 = π(0.6)²/4 = 0.2827 m².
  2. Outlet area A₃ = πD₃²/4 = π(1.2)²/4 = 1.1310 m².
  3. Inlet velocity V₂ = Q/A₂ = 1.5/0.2827 = 5.305 m/s.
  4. Outlet velocity V₃ = Q/A₃ = 1.5/1.1310 = 1.326 m/s.
  5. Inlet velocity head V₂²/2g = 5.305²/(2×9.81) = 1.434 m.
  6. Outlet velocity head V₃²/2g = 1.326²/(2×9.81) = 0.0896 m.
  7. Ideal difference Δh = 1.434 − 0.0896 = 1.345 m.
  8. Actual recovery = η_d Δh = 0.85 × 1.345 = 1.143 m.
  9. Head lost = (1 − 0.85) × 1.345 = 0.202 m.

Answer. The ideal velocity-head difference is 1.345 m, the tube recovers 1.143 m, and 0.202 m is lost.

Exam traps

  • Draft tube efficiency is a ratio of heads, not pressures. Write actual recovered head divided by (V₂² − V₃²)/2g. A pressure ratio gives a different number.
  • Subtract V₃²/2g before applying η_d. The tube cannot recover the exit velocity head V₃²/2g; that water still leaves with speed.
  • Sign of H_s: the runner exit is above tailwater, so p₂ is below atmospheric. Adding H_s instead of subtracting it makes suction look like a pressure rise.
  • Do not call the draft tube a pump. It has no moving part and adds no energy; it only trades velocity head for pressure head.
  • A very large area ratio looks good on the ideal curve but real flow separates. Keep the cone angle small; the installed recovery is below the ideal curve.

6.7 Specific speed and unit quantities

A 300 mm model in a laboratory and a 3 m runner at a hill site cannot be compared by their speeds or discharges, because head and size are mixed into every reading. Unit quantities divide the head out: unit speed, unit discharge and unit power say what the same machine would do under exactly one metre of head. Specific speed divides out size too, leaving one number that fixes the type of turbine. Manufacturers, tender documents and electricity utilities use it before any machine is drawn.

You need: the Pelton wheel in 6.4, the Francis and Kaplan runners in 6.5, and the similarity laws and model studies of Unit 5, Boundary Layer and Dimensional Analysis.

Unit quantities: dividing out the head

A turbine is a shape, not a size. Two runners are geometrically similar when every length of one is the same multiple of the matching length of the other: same vane angles, same number of buckets, same proportions. Inside one such family every velocity in the machine is proportional to √(2gH), with g = 9.81 m/s² and H the net head in metres.

The runner speed u, the jet speed and the flow speed all rise as √H. Discharge is area × velocity, and area goes as D², where D is the runner diameter in metres. Three measured quantities therefore range roughly as N ∝ √H/D, Q ∝ D²√H and P ∝ D²H^{3/2}, with P the shaft power.

N₁ = N/√H Q₁ = Q/√H P₁ = P/H^{3/2}

N is the actual speed in rpm, N₁ the unit speed in rpm; Q is the actual discharge in m³/s, Q₁ the unit discharge in m³/s; P is the shaft power in kW, P₁ the unit power in kW; H is the net head in metres.

Read them as a what-if. N₁ is the speed this machine would run at under exactly 1 m of head, Q₁ the discharge it would then pass, P₁ the power it would then give. They also tell you how one machine behaves at a new head: raise the head from H to 4H and the speed doubles, the discharge doubles, and the power becomes 4^{1.5} = 8 times.

They do not remove size. A 2 m runner and its half-scale model have different unit quantities even when the shapes match, because N₁ ∝ 1/D, Q₁ ∝ D² and P₁ ∝ D². Only the head has been divided out.

Specific speed: one number for the type

Size is still inside the unit quantities. Divide it out and one number remains, the specific speed. Start from the similarity rules: Q ∝ D²√H and P ∝ QH ∝ D²H^{3/2}, so D ∝ √P / H^{3/4}. Speed goes as N ∝ √H/D, which gives N ∝ H^{5/4}/√P. Rearranged, N√P/H^{5/4} is the same for every machine of the family, whatever its size.

N_s = N√P / H^{5/4}

N_s is the specific speed, quoted in rpm, but it is not a running speed; only the form ω√Q/(gH)^{3/4} is fully dimensionless. N is the actual running speed in rpm; P is the shaft power in kW; H is the net head in metres.

Some books define specific speed from discharge instead. Then N_s = N√Q / H^{3/4}, with Q in m³/s and H in metres. The two definitions give different numbers for the same machine and are not interchangeable, so always state which one you used. The discharge form is the usual one for pumps, see Unit 7, Centrifugal and Reciprocating Pumps.

Dividing the units out as well gives a dimensionless shape number, ω√Q/(gH)^{3/4}, with ω the angular speed in rad/s. It carries the same information with no units attached.

TurbineN_s (rpm, power form)Head it suits

The number is a shape label. A high N_s means a short, fat runner that passes a large discharge at low head; a low N_s means a tall, narrow runner working on a small discharge at high head. That is why the type follows from N_s alone, before any drawing is made.

Scaling a machine with these numbers

A tender fixes the head the site gives and the power the grid needs. The specific speed then fixes the speed and the shape family, and the unit quantities fix the size.

  1. Note the head H and the power P the site can supply.
  2. Read a trial N_s from the band table for that head; the type of turbine follows.
  3. Find the speed from N = N_s H^{5/4} / √P, then round it to a synchronous value such as 3000/n rpm for a 50 Hz supply (3000, 1500, 1000, 750 ...).
  4. Fix the runner size from a similar model: N₁ ∝ 1/D and Q₁ ∝ D², so D grows as the square root of the discharge per metre of head.

In the worked example, 5000 kW at 300 rpm under 80 m gives N_s = 88.66, inside the Francis band, with unit speed 33.54 rpm, unit discharge 0.805 m³/s and unit power 6.988 kW. Scale-up is not free: a model and its prototype share N_s only while they stay truly similar, and efficiency rises with size, so the predicted prototype power is corrected upward. Comparison of running points is in 6.8.

  • Using the power form N_s = N√P/H^{5/4} and the discharge form N_s = N√Q/H^{3/4} in the same answer. They give different numbers; state the definition you used before substituting.
  • Feeding P in watts or H in centimetres into a formula built for kW and metres. 5000 kW is not 5000; it is 5 000 000 W, and the answer changes by a factor of about 31.6.
  • Writing H^{3/2} where H^{5/4} belongs, or squaring H in the specific speed. The 5/4 exponent cannot be found by cancelling units, because N_s in this metric form is not dimensionless. Get it by combining N ∝ √H/D with P ∝ D²H^{3/2}, or simply remember it. Only ω√Q/(gH)^{3/4} is dimensionless.
  • Forgetting the square root: N√P means N times √P. With P = 5000 kW, √P = 70.71, so the numerator is 300 × 70.71 = 21213. Writing √(N P) would wrongly give √1500000 = 1224.7.
  • Calling N_s a running speed. A turbine with N_s = 88.66 rpm usually runs at 300 rpm or more; the unit rpm appear in the formula only as bookkeeping.

Check yourself

Remember Define specific speed of a turbine from power, and give its units. 2 marks

N_s = N√P / H^{5/4}, with N in rpm, P the shaft power in kW and H the net head in metres. It is quoted in rpm, though it is a shape number, not a real speed.

Remember Write the unit power formula and state the unit of each symbol. 2 marks

P₁ = P/H^{3/2}, with P in kW, H in metres and P₁ in kW.

Understand Specific speed is the same for a model and its geometrically similar prototype, but unit speed is not. Why? 3 marks

Specific speed divides out both head and size, so only the shape survives. Unit speed still contains the diameter, because N₁ ∝ 1/D: a half-scale runner turns twice as fast per metre of head.

Apply A turbine runs at 150 rpm under a net head of 25 m and delivers 800 kW. Find its specific speed on the power definition and name the type. 4 marks

√P = √800 = 28.28; H^{5/4} = 25 × 25^{0.25} = 25 × 2.2361 = 55.90; N_s = 150 × 28.28 / 55.90 = 75.9 rpm. That is in the Francis band, 60 to 300.

Apply The turbine of the previous question is run at the same setting but with the head raised from 25 m to 36 m. Find the new speed and new power. 3 marks

Speed rises as √H: N₂ = 150 × √(36/25) = 150 × 1.2 = 180 rpm. Power rises as H^{3/2}: P₂ = 800 × (36/25)^{1.5} = 800 × 1.728 = 1382.4 kW.

Analyze Unit A runs at 500 rpm, 100 kW, 200 m head. Unit B runs at 200 rpm, 2000 kW, 15 m head. Compute both specific speeds and say which is the reaction machine with a draft tube. 5 marks

Unit A: √100 = 10, H^{5/4} = 200 × 3.7606 = 752.1, N_s = 500 × 10 / 752.1 = 6.6 rpm. Unit B: √2000 = 44.72, H^{5/4} = 15 × 1.9680 = 29.52, N_s = 200 × 44.72 / 29.52 = 303.0 rpm. Unit B is in the Kaplan band and is the reaction machine.

Analyze Two turbines of identical shape and head run at different speeds. Which quantity is unchanged, and what does that tell you about using specific speed to compare them? 4 marks

Both are geometrically similar, so their specific speeds are equal; only the sizes and speeds differ. The constant N_s is exactly the point: it labels the shape family and the type of turbine, and size or speed changes leave it alone.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Model acceptance tests for large hydro units follow IEC 60193, which uses the dimensionless specific speed and efficiency scale-up rules of this kind. Modern design uses CFD to shape a runner once N_s is fixed, because N_s settles the meridional proportions before any blade is drawn. Pumps borrow the same number, and the suction specific speed predicts cavitation limits on the inlet side.

6.8 Turbine efficiencies, performance and selection

A turbine's four efficiencies show where the water power goes: hydraulic losses in the runner, leakage past seals, and mechanical friction in bearings. They multiply, never add. The site fixes head and discharge, while the turbine curve fixes efficiency at that flow. Selection matches the two. A small hydro plant at a canal drop uses this when summer flow falls and the chosen turbine must still run well.

You need: work done and velocity triangles from 6.4, losses in 6.5, and specific speed from 6.7.

The four efficiencies

Water power entering the turbine is Pw = ρgQH. Here ρ is density in kg/m³, g is 9.81 m/s², Q is discharge in m³/s and H is net head in m.

η<sub>h</sub> = (u₁V<sub>w1</sub> − u₂V<sub>w2</sub>) / (gH)

ηh is hydraulic efficiency; u₁ and u₂ are runner speeds at inlet and outlet in m/s; Vw1 and Vw2 are whirl velocities in m/s.

Hydraulic efficiency measures energy transferred to the runner. Volumetric efficiency accounts for water that leaks past without doing work. Mechanical efficiency accounts for bearing and seal friction.

η<sub>v</sub> = (Q − q) / Q     η<sub>m</sub> = P<sub>s</sub> / P<sub>r</sub>

q is leakage in m³/s; Ps is shaft power; Pr is runner power.

η<sub>o</sub> = η<sub>h</sub>η<sub>v</sub>η<sub>m</sub> = P<sub>s</sub> / (ρgQH)

ηo is overall efficiency. Multiply the three factors; adding them gives a meaningless number above 1.

Curves and the efficiency hill

A main curve is drawn at fixed head: hold H, change the guide-vane opening, and record discharge and efficiency. An operating curve holds speed fixed, as a grid-connected machine does, and lets head and load vary. A Muschel curve plots efficiency against speed ratio (or unit speed) for one fixed guide-vane opening at constant head, so a family of them shows how the best operating point shifts as the opening changes. The iso-efficiency hill collects many such tests; the hill's peak is the design point.

Part-load behaviour follows from velocity triangles. A Kaplan turbine has movable runner blades and guide vanes, so it re-angles the flow into the runner as Q falls and keeps the velocity triangles close to their best shape. Pelton buckets are fixed; at low Q the jet is thin and windage and bearing losses dominate, so efficiency falls quickly. Francis sits between the two.

Selection and governing

Select in this order. Head first: high head above about 300 m points to Pelton; medium head from about 30 m to 300 m to Francis; low head below about 30 m to Kaplan. Discharge then sets the size. Specific speed confirms the family.

Ns = N√P / H5/4

N is speed in rpm; P is shaft power in kW; H is head in m. Low Ns suits Pelton, medium Francis, high Kaplan.

Rotational speed must match the generator. For 50 Hz, N = 120f/Pp rpm, where Pp is the number of poles. A 10-pole machine runs at 600 rpm. Reaction turbines also need enough submergence to avoid cavitation; the setting is checked against the manufacturer's limit, with details in Unit 7.

A governor holds speed by moving guide vanes, or a Pelton deflector, when load changes. It should correct frequency without chasing every small fluctuation.

Worked example

Question. A turbine passes Q = 5 m³/s under a net head H = 60 m. Its hydraulic efficiency is 0.92, volumetric efficiency is 0.96 and mechanical efficiency is 0.97. Take ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s². Find the overall efficiency and the shaft power.

  1. Overall efficiency: η_o = η_h η_v η_m = 0.92 × 0.96 × 0.97 = 0.856704.
  2. Water power: P_w = ρgQH = 1000 × 9.81 × 5 × 60 = 2,943,000 W = 2.943 MW.
  3. Shaft power: P_s = η_o P_w = 0.856704 × 2,943,000 = 2,521,279.872 W = 2.521 MW.

Answer. The overall efficiency is 0.8567 and the shaft power is 2.521 MW.

Exam traps

  • Adding η_h, η_v and η_m instead of multiplying them.
  • Using hydraulic efficiency alone as the overall efficiency.
  • Reading a constant-speed operating curve as if it were a constant-head main curve.
  • Using the design-point efficiency when the turbine runs at part load.
  • Mixing watts and kilowatts, or dropping ρ and g when computing water power.

Check yourself

Remember State the four efficiencies of a turbine and the relation between them. 2 marks

Hydraulic η_h, volumetric η_v, mechanical η_m and overall η_o = η_h η_v η_m.

Understand Why are the efficiencies multiplied rather than added? 3 marks

Each is a fraction of the power remaining after the previous loss. Multiplication chains the fractions: 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81, not 1.8.

Apply A turbine passes 2 m³/s under 40 m head with overall efficiency 0.85. Find the shaft power. Use ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s². 4 marks

Analyze Explain why a Kaplan turbine keeps high efficiency at part load while a Pelton wheel does not. 4 marks

Analyze A site has H = 150 m and Q = 2 m³/s. Which turbine family fits, and what else must be checked before ordering? 4 marks

Medium head points to Francis. Check specific speed, synchronous speed, part-load efficiency and the cavitation submergence limit.

A contour plot of equal overall efficiency over a map of head, discharge or speed. Its peak is the design point.

A main curve is measured at constant head while opening or discharge changes. An operating curve is measured at constant speed while head and load vary.

Takeaways

  1. A jet force comes from momentum change and falls as the vane speed approaches the jet speed.
  2. Euler's equation links turbine work to the change of angular momentum and the inlet and outlet velocity triangles.
  3. Hydraulic turbines are classified by pressure action, flow direction and available head.
  4. The Pelton wheel is an impulse turbine for high head, with best work near bucket speed equal to half jet speed.
  5. Francis and Kaplan turbines are reaction turbines; pressure falls across the runner and a draft tube recovers exit kinetic energy.
  6. A draft tube allows the runner to sit above tailwater while recovering velocity head as pressure.
  7. Unit quantities and specific speed compare turbines without carrying size and head into every number.
  8. Overall efficiency is the product of hydraulic, mechanical and volumetric efficiencies, and the operating point is where the turbine curve meets the site conditions.

Glossary

Absolute velocity
Velocity of water measured from the fixed casing, denoted V.
Bucket
Curved cup on a Pelton runner that turns the jet and takes its momentum.
Cavitation
Formation and collapse of vapour bubbles in low-pressure zones, causing erosion and noise.
Discharge
Volume of water passing a section per second, measured in m³/s.
Draft head
Height of runner exit above tailrace; it affects pressure at runner exit.
Draft tube
Diverging passage that connects runner exit to tailrace and recovers velocity head.
Euler head
Head equivalent of work done by angular momentum change, H_e = (u₁V_u1 − u₂V_u2)/g.
Flow ratio
Ratio of flow velocity to runner speed at inlet of a reaction turbine.
Guide vane
Stationary vane that controls flow and gives whirl before a reaction runner.
Head
Energy per unit weight of water available to the turbine, measured in metres.
Hydraulic efficiency
Ratio of work delivered to the runner to energy supplied by water.
Impulse turbine
Turbine in which pressure stays constant across the runner and the jet is free.
Mechanical efficiency
Ratio of shaft power to runner power after bearing and seal losses.
Overall efficiency
Ratio of shaft power to water power, equal to the product of the three efficiencies.
Reaction turbine
Turbine in which pressure falls across the runner and the runner is full of water.
Relative velocity
Velocity of water measured from the moving runner, denoted V_r.
Runner
Rotating part carrying buckets or blades that receives energy from water.
Specific speed
Speed of a geometrically similar turbine that would give unit power under unit head.
Speed ratio
Ratio of runner speed to jet speed, u/V, about 0.5 for a Pelton wheel.
Tailrace
Channel that carries water away from the turbine after the draft tube.
Tangential velocity
Runner speed at a point, u = πDN/60 for diameter D and speed N.
Unit discharge
Discharge of a turbine under 1 m head, Q_u = Q/√H.
Unit power
Power of a turbine under 1 m head, P_u = P/H3/2.
Unit speed
Speed of a turbine under 1 m head, N_u = N/√H.
Velocity triangle
Diagram of V, u and V_r at inlet or outlet of a runner.
Volumetric efficiency
Ratio of discharge through the runner to total discharge supplied.
