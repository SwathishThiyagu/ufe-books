1.1 Fluid as a continuum; ideal and real fluids

A fluid is a substance that flows: it cannot hold a fixed shape and cannot resist a shear force when at rest. Real fluids are made of molecules in constant motion, with empty space between them. Engineers avoid tracking every molecule. When the region of interest is much larger than the mean free path (which is itself much larger than the molecular spacing in a gas), we replace the crowd of molecules with a smooth continuous medium, a continuum, and speak of properties at a point. The same idea lets us sort fluids into ideal models (no viscosity) and real fluids (viscosity present).

You need only school physics: force, area, and the fact that matter is made of molecules.

Molecules, gaps and the continuum

A fluid is a substance that cannot resist a shear force when at rest: put a shear on it and it keeps deforming. Gases and liquids both do this.

At the molecular scale a fluid is mostly empty space. In air at 20 °C and 1 atm, a molecule travels on average about 6.8×10⁻⁸ m between collisions. This distance is the mean free path, written λ. In a liquid the molecules are packed much closer, so λ is of the order of the molecular spacing, about 10⁻¹⁰ m.

The body length L is the smallest length over which you need the answer: the diameter of a sensor opening, the gap in a valve, the width of a microchannel.

The ratio λ/L decides whether the continuum idea works. If λ/L is very small, a molecule collides with other molecules far more often than it crosses the body, so the crowd behaves like a smooth medium. If λ/L is not small, molecules cross the body without colliding and the continuum fails. Some syllabi write Kn = λ/L and call it the Knudsen number.

Kn = λ / L

λ is the mean free path (m), the average distance a molecule travels between collisions; L is the smallest body length of interest (m); Kn is the Knudsen number, dimensionless.

  1. Find λ for the gas from its pressure and temperature.
  2. Find L, the smallest length in the problem.
  3. Compute Kn = λ/L.
  4. Treat the fluid as a continuum if Kn is below about 0.01. Above about 0.1, use rarefied-gas methods.

The averaging volume and the continuum point

A continuum point means a small volume, not a mathematical point. Pick a point P in the fluid. Draw a small volume ΔV around P. Average the mass of the molecules inside, and divide by ΔV to get a density. Now shrink ΔV. While ΔV is still large compared with the molecular spacing, the average mass divided by ΔV stays steady — this steady value is the density at P. Only when ΔV shrinks to the averaging volume, small enough that individual molecules entering and leaving start to matter, does the value start to jump about. Use the steady plateau value as the property at P; the smallest volume that still sits on the plateau is the averaging volume.

The averaging volume contains many molecules, yet it is tiny compared with the body. For air at ordinary conditions, a cube of side 1 μm holds about 2.5×10⁷ molecules, enough for a smooth average.

Once every point has a property, you can write density as ρ(x, y, z, t) and pressure as p(x, y, z, t). These are field variables. Calculus then applies, and you can differentiate and integrate them. This is the whole reason the continuum is useful: it turns a problem about 10²³ molecules into a problem about a few smooth functions.

Ideal and real fluids

An ideal fluid is a model with viscosity μ = 0. It cannot support a shear stress, even when moving. It is also taken as incompressible (constant density) and without surface tension. Some syllabi call an ideal fluid inviscid. An ideal fluid is not a fluid with zero density; it has mass and inertia.

A real fluid has μ > 0. It resists shear, and Newton's law of viscosity links shear stress to velocity gradient (see 1.3). Real liquids also have surface tension (see 1.5), and real fluids are compressible to some degree.

Why keep the ideal model? Because it makes the mathematics simple. Bernoulli's equation and potential flow assume an ideal fluid; over short distances and at low viscosity, water and air follow those results closely. The difference appears in pipe friction, drag, and pump losses, which are real-fluid effects (see Unit 4, Viscous Flow Through Pipes).

Worked example

Question. A pressure sensor has a circular opening of diameter 1 mm. Air at 20 °C and 1 atm has mean free path λ = 6.8×10⁻⁸ m. Compare the opening with the mean free path, compute the Knudsen number, and decide whether the air behaves as a continuum at the sensor scale.

  1. Record the data: opening diameter D = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m; mean free path λ = 6.8×10⁻⁸ m.
  2. Compare the opening with the mean free path: L/λ = 1×10⁻³ / 6.8×10⁻⁸.
  3. Compute: L/λ = 1.470588×10⁴ ≈ 1.47×10⁴.
  4. Convert to the usual Knudsen number: Kn = λ/L = 1 / (L/λ) = 6.8×10⁻⁵.
  5. Compare with the continuum threshold: Kn = 6.8×10⁻⁵ is far below 0.01, equivalently L/λ ≫ 100.
  6. Conclusion: the air is a continuum at the sensor opening.

Answer. L/λ ≈ 1.47×10⁴ and Kn ≈ 6.8×10⁻⁵; because Kn ≪ 0.01, the air behaves as a continuum at the 1 mm sensor opening.

Exam traps

  • Ideal fluid is not zero density. Ideal means μ = 0 and usually incompressible. It still has mass, weight and inertia.
  • Ideal fluid and ideal gas are different models. An ideal gas obeys pV = nRT and can have viscosity.
  • The continuum is a length-scale assumption, not a universal truth. A 1 mm sensor in air sees a continuum; a 100 nm channel in the same air does not.
  • Mean free path λ changes with pressure. At fixed temperature λ ∝ 1/p. Do not use the sea-level value at 10 km altitude.
  • Unit slip in Kn: 1 mm = 10⁻³ m and 1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m. Kn is dimensionless; do not write it as 0.01 m.

1.2 Properties: density, specific weight, specific gravity, compressibility

Density is the mass a fluid packs into one cubic metre. Everything else in this chapter is built from it: multiply by g and you get specific weight, divide by water's density and you get specific gravity, and squeeze the fluid to get bulk modulus and compressibility. Engineers use these numbers daily — an oil supplier quotes specific gravity, a dam designer needs specific weight, a hydraulic press designer needs to know how much the oil shrinks under pressure. Get density right and the rest is arithmetic.

You need: Ch 1.1 for the continuum idea and the meaning of a fluid.

Mass density and specific volume

Mass density is the mass of fluid in unit volume. It answers one question: how much matter sits inside one cubic metre?

ρ = m / V

ρ (Greek rho) is mass density in kg/m³; m is the mass in kg; V is the volume in m³.

Specific volume is the same idea turned upside down: the volume that one kilogram occupies. Steam tables and refrigeration work use it more than density.

v = 1 / ρ = V / m

v is specific volume in m³/kg; ρ is density in kg/m³.

Water at 4 °C has ρ = 1000 kg/m³. Mercury has ρ = 13600 kg/m³. Air at 20 °C and normal atmospheric pressure has ρ ≈ 1.2 kg/m³. Air is roughly 800 times lighter than water, which is why a balloon of air feels empty but a balloon of water does not.

Density of a gas falls when it expands and rises when it is compressed. Density of a liquid changes very little, so most liquid problems treat ρ as a constant. Water at the bottom of a 100 m dam has a density only about 0.05 % higher than at the surface (a rise of roughly 0.5 kg/m³ on 1000 kg/m³). Even at 1 km depth the rise is only about 0.5 %.

Specific weight and specific gravity

Specific weight is the weight of fluid in unit volume. Weight is a force, so specific weight is a force per volume, not a mass per volume.

γ = ρ g

γ (Greek gamma) is specific weight in N/m³; ρ is density in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s².

Do not swap the two. Density is in kg/m³ and is a property of the fluid alone. Specific weight is in N/m³ and depends on where you are: the same water on the Moon has the same density but about one-sixth the specific weight, because g changes.

Specific gravity is a pure ratio. It compares the fluid with water at 4 °C, so it has no unit at all.

SG = ρ / ρ_water = γ / γ_water

SG is specific gravity (dimensionless); ρ_water = 1000 kg/m³; γ_water = 9810 N/m³.

Specific gravity is what the petrol pump, the coolant bottle and the battery shop actually talk about. Petrol has SG about 0.72, so one litre has a mass of about 0.72 kg and weighs about 0.72 × 9.81 ≈ 7.1 N. Mercury has SG 13.6, which is why a 760 mm mercury column balances the whole atmosphere. Battery acid is checked by SG because a charged battery's acid is denser than a discharged one.

Bulk modulus and compressibility

Bulk modulus measures how strongly a fluid resists being squeezed. Apply a pressure rise, watch the volume shrink, and the ratio tells you the stiffness.

K = − V dp / dV = ρ dp / dρ

K is the bulk modulus in Pa (or N/m²); dp is the pressure rise; dV is the change in volume; V is the original volume. The minus sign keeps K positive because volume falls as pressure rises.

For finite changes the same relation is written K ≈ − Δp / (ΔV/V). The fraction ΔV/V is the volumetric strain, so K is pressure divided by strain — exactly like Young's modulus for a solid, but for volume instead of length.

Compressibility is simply the reciprocal. It tells you how much volume shrinks per unit pressure.

β = 1 / K = − (1/V) dV / dp

β (Greek beta) is compressibility in Pa⁻¹ (or m²/N); K is bulk modulus in Pa.

Water has K ≈ 2.2 × 10⁹ Pa, so β ≈ 4.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ Pa⁻¹. A pressure rise of one atmosphere (about 10⁵ Pa) shrinks water by only about 0.005 %. That is why liquids are called incompressible in normal work. Mercury is stiffer still; air has K ≈ 10⁵ Pa, about four orders of magnitude (roughly 20 000 times) softer than water.

A gas has no single K. Squeeze it slowly and heat escapes, so the process is isothermal and K = p for an ideal gas. Squeeze it fast and heat stays in, so the process is adiabatic and K = γp, where γ is the ratio of specific heats (1.4 for air). Always state the process. See Unit 3 for compressible flow, where these ideas become essential.

1.3 Viscosity and Newton's law of viscosity

When you stir honey, the spoon feels a drag; water gives less. That drag comes from viscosity, the fluid property that resists one layer sliding over the next. Newton's law of viscosity puts this into numbers: shear stress equals dynamic viscosity times velocity gradient. The law explains why oil films carry load in bearings, why pumping heavy oil costs more, and why a thin gap between moving parts matters. It also sets up laminar pipe flow and boundary layers, studied later.

You need: density and specific weight from Ch 1.2, and the continuum idea from Ch 1.1.

Shear stress and the sliding layers

A fluid at rest cannot resist a shear force: it flows. The resistance appears only when one layer of fluid slides over another. Picture two parallel plates with fluid between them. The lower plate is fixed and the upper plate moves with speed U. The fluid touching each plate sticks to it, which is the no-slip condition. So the fluid at the lower plate has velocity u = 0, and the fluid at the upper plate has u = U. Across the gap t the velocity changes from 0 to U.

τ = μ du/dy

τ is the shear stress on a layer in Pa; μ is the dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; du/dy is the velocity gradient in s⁻¹, the change in velocity u over a distance y measured across the layers.

Shear stress is force per area parallel to the surface: τ = F/A. Here F is the force pulling the upper plate and A is the plate area in m². Newton's law of viscosity says the shear stress is proportional to the velocity gradient for a Newtonian fluid. The constant μ is the dynamic viscosity, with SI unit Pa·s, also written N·s/m². For a thin film with a straight-line velocity profile, du/dy = U/t. The figure plots this law for μ = 0.2 Pa·s: a straight line through the origin whose slope is the viscosity.

Dynamic and kinematic viscosity

Dynamic viscosity μ measures how strongly a fluid resists shear. Kinematic viscosity ν is defined by dividing μ by density. Dividing by density removes the inertia of the fluid, so ν appears in formulas that compare viscous forces with inertial forces, such as the Reynolds number.

ν = μ/ρ

ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; ρ is density in kg/m³ from Ch 1.2.

Values help. Water at 20°C has μ ≈ 1.0×10⁻³ Pa·s. Air at 20°C has μ ≈ 1.8×10⁻⁵ Pa·s. Gases are far less viscous than liquids. Temperature changes μ. For a liquid, μ falls as temperature rises: hot engine oil flows more easily than cold oil. For a gas, μ rises with temperature because faster molecules exchange momentum more strongly. A Newtonian fluid has a constant μ at a fixed temperature; non-Newtonian fluids do not, as Ch 1.4 explains.

Solving a thin-film problem

Use these steps for a plate-and-film problem.

  1. Sketch the two plates. Mark the fixed surface with u = 0 and the moving surface with u = U.
  2. Convert the gap t to metres and the area A to m².
  3. For a thin film with steady laminar flow and no pressure gradient, take the velocity profile as a straight line, so du/dy = U/t.
  4. Compute shear stress from τ = μ du/dy.
  5. Compute the force on the plate from F = τA. On the moving plate the force acts opposite to its motion.

Assumptions matter. The simple result du/dy = U/t holds for a thin film, steady flow, laminar flow, and no pressure gradient along the plates. If the gap is wide or pressure drives the flow, du/dy varies with y; then use the local gradient. The law also starts the study of laminar pipe flow in Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. An oil film of area 0.5 m² lies between a fixed lower plate and a moving upper plate. The gap is 1 mm and the moving plate speed is 0.4 m/s. The oil has dynamic viscosity μ = 0.2 Pa·s. Find the velocity gradient, the shear stress in the oil, and the force needed to keep the plate moving.

  1. Write the data: U = 0.4 m/s, t = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m, A = 0.5 m², μ = 0.2 Pa·s.
  2. The no-slip condition makes the fluid velocity zero at the fixed plate and 0.4 m/s at the moving plate. For a thin film the profile is a straight line, so du/dy = U/t = 0.4 / (1×10⁻³) = 400 s⁻¹.
  3. Apply Newton's law: τ = μ du/dy = 0.2 × 400 = 80 Pa.
  4. Shear force on the plate: F = τA = 80 × 0.5 = 40 N.

Exam traps

  • Using the plate speed U in place of the velocity gradient du/dy = U/t. Shear stress depends on how fast velocity changes across the gap, not on the plate speed alone.
  • Mixing dynamic viscosity μ in Pa·s with kinematic viscosity ν in m²/s. Newton's law uses μ; if you only know ν, first compute μ = νρ.
  • Forgetting to convert the gap from millimetres to metres. A gap of 1 mm is 1×10⁻³ m; using 1 in place of 0.001 makes du/dy a thousand times too small.
  • Using area in cm² without converting to m². Force in newtons needs shear stress in Pa and area in m².
  • Assuming du/dy = U/t when the gap is wide or pressure changes along the plates. The local gradient then varies across the gap, and the straight-line result is no longer valid.

1.4 Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids

Some fluids resist sliding with the same viscosity at every speed. Water and air behave this way. Others become thinner or thicker as they are sheared, and some refuse to move until the stress crosses a threshold. The difference matters when you size a pipe for toothpaste, choose a pump for paint, or model blood flow. Newtonian fluids follow a straight-line flow curve. Non-Newtonian fluids do not. This chapter draws those curves, writes the power-law and Bingham models, and shows how to compute an apparent viscosity for each.

You need: Ch 1.3 for Newton's law of viscosity, shear stress and the shear rate du/dy.

Newtonian fluids: a straight flow curve

In a Newtonian fluid, shear stress is directly proportional to shear rate. The flow curve of τ against du/dy is a straight line through the origin. The constant slope is the dynamic viscosity μ. Double the shear rate and the shear stress doubles; μ does not change.

τ = μ (du/dy)

τ is shear stress in Pa; μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; du/dy is the velocity gradient, also called shear rate, in s⁻¹.

The apparent viscosity at a chosen shear rate is the viscosity a fluid would need to produce the measured shear stress at that shear rate: μ_app = τ/(du/dy). For a Newtonian fluid this gives μ_app = μ at every shear rate. Water, air, mercury and light machine oils are common Newtonian examples. Temperature changes μ, but it does not bend the line.

In a simple shear layer, doubling the speed of one plate over another doubles du/dy and doubles τ. The viscosity remains the same. That is the test: measured μ_app stays constant over the tested range.

Power law: shear-thinning and shear-thickening

Many non-Newtonian fluids fit a power law over a range of shear rates: τ = K(du/dy)^n. K is the consistency index in Pa·s^n; n is the flow behaviour index, dimensionless. When n = 1, K equals μ and the fluid is Newtonian.

τ = K(du/dy)^n

τ is shear stress in Pa; K is the consistency index in Pa·s^n; n is the dimensionless flow behaviour index; du/dy is shear rate in s⁻¹.

If n < 1, the fluid is pseudoplastic or shear-thinning. Its apparent viscosity μ_app = K(du/dy)^(n−1) falls as shear rate rises. Long molecules line up with the flow, or loose clusters break apart, so the fluid slides more easily. Paint, polymer solutions and blood show this behaviour.

If n > 1, the fluid is dilatant or shear-thickening. Its apparent viscosity rises with shear rate. Particles that were free to slide past one another jam together, and the liquid between them cannot lubricate the motion. Cornflour suspended in water and some concentrated slurries behave this way. A quick stir feels stiffer than a slow stir.

μ_app = τ/(du/dy) = K(du/dy)^(n−1)

μ_app is apparent viscosity in Pa·s at the chosen shear rate.

n is not a viscosity. It is the exponent that sets the shape of the curve. K sets the level, and its units change with n, so compare K values only when n is the same. To classify measured data, compute μ_app at several shear rates. Constant μ_app means Newtonian. Falling μ_app means pseudoplastic; rising μ_app means dilatant. A log-log plot of τ against du/dy gives slope n and intercept log K.

Bingham plastics and time-dependent fluids

A Bingham plastic does not flow until the shear stress exceeds a yield stress τ_y. Below τ_y it behaves like a solid or moves as a solid plug. Above τ_y, the flow curve is a straight line with slope μ_p, the plastic viscosity: τ = τ_y + μ_p(du/dy). Toothpaste from a tube, drilling mud and sewage sludge are common examples.

τ = τ_y + μ_p(du/dy)

τ_y is the yield stress in Pa; μ_p is the plastic viscosity in Pa·s.

The apparent viscosity of a Bingham plastic is μ_app = τ_y/(du/dy) + μ_p. It falls as shear rate rises, even though μ_p is constant. At very low shear rates, just above yield, μ_app is large. The yield stress must be included; setting τ_y = 0 turns the model into a Newtonian fluid with viscosity μ_p.

Ketchup and tomato sauce fit neither simple model well. They need a yield stress before they start to move, and their apparent viscosity still falls as shear rate rises after that. Such fluids are fitted with a yield-stress power law, the Herschel–Bulkley model τ = τ_y + K(du/dy)^n, where τ_y is the yield stress in Pa, K is the consistency index in Pa·s^n and n is the dimensionless flow behaviour index. Setting n = 1 gives a Bingham plastic; setting τ_y = 0 gives a simple power law.

Some fluids also depend on time. A thixotropic fluid becomes thinner after it is sheared for a while at a constant shear rate; its gel structure breaks down. Paint and drilling mud are examples. A rheopectic fluid becomes thicker with continued shear; some inks and gypsum pastes are examples. These names describe time dependence, not the shape of the τ against du/dy curve.

Worked example

Question. At a shear rate du/dy = 100 s⁻¹, four fluids are compared. Fluid A is Newtonian with μ = 0.08 Pa·s. Fluid B is pseudoplastic with K = 0.5 Pa·s^n and n = 0.6. Fluid C is dilatant with K = 0.02 Pa·s^n and n = 1.4. Fluid D is Bingham with τ_y = 4 Pa and μ_p = 0.04 Pa·s. Find τ and μ_app for each fluid.

  1. For every fluid, μ_app = τ/(du/dy) = τ/100.
  2. A: τ = μ(du/dy) = 0.08×100 = 8 Pa. μ_app = 8/100 = 0.08 Pa·s.
  3. B: 100^0.6 = 15.849. τ = 0.5×15.849 = 7.92 Pa. μ_app = 7.92/100 = 0.0792 Pa·s.
  4. C: 100^1.4 = 630.96. τ = 0.02×630.96 = 12.62 Pa. μ_app = 12.62/100 = 0.1262 Pa·s.
  5. D: τ = τ_y + μ_p(du/dy) = 4 + 0.04×100 = 4 + 4 = 8 Pa. μ_app = 8/100 = 0.08 Pa·s.
  6. B is shear-thinning because n < 1. C is shear-thickening because n > 1. D has a yield stress of 4 Pa.

Answer. A: τ = 8 Pa, μ_app = 0.08 Pa·s; B: τ = 7.92 Pa, μ_app = 0.0792 Pa·s; C: τ = 12.62 Pa, μ_app = 0.1262 Pa·s; D: τ = 8 Pa, μ_app = 0.08 Pa·s.

Exam traps

  • Calling n a viscosity. n is dimensionless and only shapes the curve; K carries the units Pa·s^n.
  • Dropping the yield stress in a Bingham plastic. At du/dy = 0 the correct stress is τ_y, not zero.
  • Using a single μ_app in a pipe-loss formula. μ_app changes with shear rate, so choose the value at the pipe's shear rate.
  • Swapping pseudoplastic and dilatant. n < 1 is shear-thinning; n > 1 is shear-thickening.
  • Confusing thixotropic with pseudoplastic. Thixotropy depends on shearing time; pseudoplasticity depends on shear rate.

1.5 Surface tension and capillarity

A liquid surface stores energy because molecules at the surface are pulled inward. This stored energy acts like a stretched sheet and is called surface tension. It creates a pressure jump across curved interfaces such as droplets and soap bubbles. It also pulls liquid up or pushes it down in narrow tubes, an effect called capillarity. Engineers meet it in rain drops, inkjet printing, soil water, wicks, manometer tubes and lab pipettes. At small scales it can dominate gravity.

You need density and specific weight from Ch 1.2; pressure in a static fluid is covered in Ch 1.6.

Surface tension: force per unit length

Inside a liquid, each molecule is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbours. At the free surface there are no molecules above, so the net pull is inward. The surface therefore behaves like a stretched sheet. Surface tension σ is the force per unit length along any line drawn on the surface.

σ = F / L

σ is surface tension in N/m; F is the force in N acting along a line of length L in m on the surface.

The same σ is the surface energy per unit area, in J/m². Values are small but not negligible at small scales. For water at 20 °C, σ ≈ 0.072 N/m; for mercury, σ ≈ 0.48 N/m. Surface tension falls as temperature rises because faster molecular motion weakens the inward pull. Soap lowers the surface tension of water. Small droplets become spherical because a sphere has the least surface area for a given volume.

Pressure jump across a curved surface

A curved liquid surface is stretched, so it pushes inward. For a droplet of radius R in a gas, the pressure inside is higher than outside.

Δp = pin − pout = 2σ / R

Δp is the pressure difference in Pa; σ is surface tension in N/m; R is the droplet radius in m.

A soap bubble in air has two surfaces, an inner one and an outer one, so the pressure jump doubles.

Δp = 4σ / R

for a soap bubble in air; R is the bubble radius in m. A bubble inside a liquid has one surface, so use 2σ/R.

Smaller R gives a larger Δp. A water droplet of radius 1 mm has Δp = 2 × 0.072 / 0.001 = 144 Pa; one of radius 0.1 mm has Δp = 1440 Pa. This is why tiny droplets resist being squashed.

Contact angle and capillary rise

The contact angle θ is the angle, inside the liquid, between the tube wall and the tangent to the liquid surface at the contact line. If θ < 90°, the liquid wets the wall and the meniscus is concave; water on clean glass has θ ≈ 0°. If θ > 90°, it does not wet the wall and the meniscus is convex; mercury on glass has θ ≈ 140°.

In a capillary tube of internal radius r, surface tension pulls the liquid up along the contact line. The raised liquid column has weight, and the two balance.

  1. The vertical component of surface tension per unit length is σ cosθ.
  2. The contact line length is 2πr, so the upward force is 2πrσ cosθ.
  3. The weight of the raised column is ρgπr²h.
  4. Equate and cancel πr: 2σ cosθ = ρgrh.
  5. Solve for height: h = 2σ cosθ / (ρgr).

h = 2σ cosθ / (ρgr)

h is capillary rise in m; σ is surface tension in N/m; θ is contact angle in degrees; ρ is liquid density in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration in m/s²; r is internal tube radius in m.

If θ > 90°, cosθ is negative, so h is negative: the liquid is depressed. Mercury sinks in a narrow glass tube. The same formula gives the depression. Use the internal radius, not the diameter. A narrower tube gives a larger rise because h is proportional to 1/r.

Worked example

Question. A clean glass capillary tube of internal radius 0.5 mm is dipped in water. Take σ = 0.072 N/m, θ = 0°, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s². Find the capillary rise h in metres and centimetres.

  1. Convert the radius: r = 0.5 mm = 0.5 × 10⁻³ m = 5 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  2. The contact angle is 0°, so cosθ = cos0° = 1.
  3. Numerator: 2σ cosθ = 2 × 0.072 × 1 = 0.144 N/m.
  4. Denominator: ρgr = 1000 × 9.81 × 5 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.905 N/m².
  5. h = 0.144 (N/m) / 4.905 (N/m²) = 0.02936 m ≈ 0.0294 m.
  6. In centimetres: h = 0.0294 × 100 = 2.94 cm.

Answer. The water rises by h ≈ 0.0294 m, or 2.94 cm, in the 0.5 mm radius tube.

Exam traps

  • Using 2σ/R for a soap bubble. A soap film has two surfaces, so the pressure jump is 4σ/R.
  • Using diameter in place of radius. The formula needs the internal radius r; halve the diameter first.
  • Putting θ in the cosine without checking its meaning. θ is measured inside the liquid, and cosθ is negative for mercury, giving a depression.
  • Forgetting that σ falls with temperature. A value of 0.072 N/m is for water near 20 °C, not for hot water.
  • Ignoring capillarity in narrow manometer tubes. A small tube can shift the reading; wide tubes reduce this error.

1.6 Pressure: absolute, gauge and variation with depth

Pressure tells how hard a fluid pushes on each unit of area. It is force per area acting perpendicular to a surface. In a static fluid, pressure rises steadily with depth because each layer carries the weight of fluid above it. This one idea sizes water tanks, dams, submarine hulls, pipeline test pressures and the reading on a tap gauge. Engineers use absolute pressure for gas laws, gauge pressure for instruments, and pressure head for pumps and pipes. This chapter builds those three views and the simple depth rule that connects them.

You need: density and specific weight from Ch 1.2; force and weight ideas from basic mechanics.

Pressure, absolute and gauge

Pressure p is normal force F divided by area A: p = F/A. The force must be perpendicular to the surface; a tilted force contributes only its normal part. The unit is pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m². A kilopascal is 1000 Pa, and 1 bar is 100 kPa. Pressure at a point is a scalar: no direction. The force it produces is normal to the chosen surface.

p = F/A

p is pressure in Pa; F is normal force in N; A is area in m².

Two references are used. Absolute pressure p_abs is measured from perfect vacuum. Gauge pressure p_gauge is measured from local atmospheric pressure p_atm. The link is p_abs = p_atm + p_gauge, so p_gauge = p_abs − p_atm. A tyre gauge and a pipeline gauge usually show gauge pressure. A vacuum gives negative gauge pressure because p_abs < p_atm; the vacuum pressure is the magnitude of that negative value.

Pressure variation with depth

Take a vertical column of static fluid of uniform density ρ (kg/m³), cross-section A, from depth h to the free surface. Its weight is W = ρgAh, with g = 9.81 m/s². The pressure p0 at the free surface acts on the top face with force p0A, and the surrounding fluid at depth h pushes up with force pA. Equilibrium gives pA = p0A + ρgAh. Divide by A: p = p0 + ρgh. Here p is pressure at depth h, p0 is pressure at the free surface, ρ is density, g is gravity, and h is depth measured downward.

p = p0 + ρgh

p is pressure at depth h in Pa; p0 is pressure at the free surface in Pa; ρ is density in kg/m³; g is 9.81 m/s²; h is depth below the free surface in m.

At an open surface p0 = p_atm. Then gauge pressure is p_gauge = p − p_atm = ρgh. For water, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, so each metre adds 9.81 kPa. At 5 m, gauge pressure is 49.05 kPa. In a sealed tank p0 may differ from atmosphere; use the full p0 + ρgh.

Pressure head is pressure written as a column height of the same fluid: h = p/(ρg). It has units of metres. A pump developing 49.05 kPa in water has a pressure head of 5 m of water. Pressure head compares pressures without carrying pascals through every line.

Isobars are lines of constant pressure. In a static fluid of uniform density they are horizontal, because h is constant on a horizontal line. The free surface is the top isobar. Pressure also falls with elevation z: p2 − p1 = −ρg(z2 − z1), with z measured upward.

Effective gravity and sign care

Some syllabi add a tank that accelerates uniformly. In horizontal acceleration a, the free surface tilts and is perpendicular to an effective gravity vector g_eff. Pressure still rises along g_eff, so replace g by g_eff in the depth rule. Here h is measured along g_eff, perpendicular to the isobars, not vertically. Only a few universities ask for the full treatment; the static case is the main exam target.

g_eff = √(g² + a²), tan θ = a/g

g_eff is effective gravity magnitude in m/s²; g is 9.81 m/s²; a is horizontal acceleration in m/s²; θ is the angle of the free surface to the horizontal.

Use depth h positive downward inside one continuous liquid of density ρ. Within that same liquid, h is negative only when the chosen reference level lies below the point, for example a pipe branch above the tank surface; then p = p0 + ρgh gives a pressure below p0, which is negative gauge pressure only if p0 is atmospheric. The equation applies only to that liquid. Never use the liquid density for the gas space above the free surface: there the pressure is essentially p0. In a sealed vessel p0 is the pressure at the reference level, not automatically atmosphere. Keep p_atm out of gauge calculations; add it only for absolute pressure. For pressure head, state whether p is gauge or absolute. Mixing them changes the answer by about 10.3 m of water at standard atmosphere.

  1. Write the data and convert all lengths to metres and pressures to pascals.
  2. Choose the reference: use p0 = p_atm for absolute pressure, or p0 = 0 for gauge pressure in an open tank.
  3. Take h positive downward from the free surface.
  4. Compute p = p0 + ρgh.
  5. If pressure head is asked, use h = p/(ρg) with the same pressure type.

Worked example

Question. At a point 5 m below the free surface of a tank of water open to atmosphere, find the gauge pressure, the absolute pressure and the pressure head. Take p_atm = 101.3 kPa, ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Depth h = 5 m. The tank is open, so the pressure at the free surface is p0 = p_atm = 101.3 kPa.
  2. Gauge pressure: p_gauge = ρgh = 1000 × 9.81 × 5 = 49,050 Pa = 49.05 kPa.
  3. Absolute pressure: p_abs = p_atm + p_gauge = 101.3 + 49.05 = 150.35 kPa.
  4. Gauge pressure head: h = p_gauge/(ρg) = 49,050/(1000 × 9.81) = 5 m of water.

Answer. Gauge pressure is 49.05 kPa, absolute pressure is 150.35 kPa, and the gauge pressure head is 5 m of water.

Exam traps

  • Adding atmospheric pressure when the question asks for gauge pressure; in an open tank the p_atm term cancels.
  • Measuring h upward from the bottom; h is depth downward from the free surface.
  • Setting p0 = 0 in a sealed tank; p0 is the pressure at the reference surface.
  • Using absolute pressure in a pressure-head calculation when gauge head is asked; state the reference.
  • Treating pressure as a vector or using total force instead of normal force per area.

1.7 Pressure measurement using manometers

A manometer measures pressure by balancing it against a liquid column. The rule is simple: in the same continuous fluid at the same level, pressure is the same. Follow the tube from the unknown pressure to the open end. Add ρgh when the path goes down; subtract ρgh when it goes up. This turns a pressure into a height you can see. Manometers check pipe pressure, filter drop and laboratory drafts.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for gauge pressure and p = ρgh.

Balance a column, read a pressure

A manometer is a transparent tube with a liquid. The unknown pressure pushes one liquid surface. The other surface is open, or held at a known pressure. The liquid settles when the pressure difference is balanced by the weight of the liquid column. The working rule is: in one continuous static fluid, two points at the same horizontal level have the same pressure.

A piezometer is the simplest manometer. It is a vertical tube open at the top, fixed to a pipe. The liquid rises to height h above the pipe centre. Then p = ρgh, where p is gauge pressure at the pipe centre in Pa, ρ is liquid density in kg/m³, g is gravitational acceleration in m/s² and h is height in m. A piezometer needs a liquid, so it cannot measure gas pressure in a pipe. It also needs a tall tube for high pressure.

The U-tube removes these limits. A heavy liquid such as mercury sits in a U. One limb connects to the pipe; the other is open. The pressure in the pipe moves the mercury. The height difference h is the reading. The U-tube can measure gas pressure and higher pressures because mercury is dense.

p_A + ρ_w g h₁ − ρ_Hg g h = p_atm

p_A is the pressure at A; ρ_w and ρ_Hg are the densities of water and mercury; h₁ is the downward water column; h is the upward mercury column; p_atm is the open-end pressure. For gauge pressure, set p_atm = 0.

Five common arrangements

  • Piezometer. A single vertical tube open to air. It uses the pipe liquid only. p = ρgh. It suits low pressures in liquids, not gases, and not negative gauge pressures that would draw air in.
  • U-tube manometer. A U-shaped tube with a heavy gauge liquid, often mercury. One limb connects to the point; the other is open. It reads gas or liquid pressure. The height difference is h.
  • Differential U-tube. Two limbs connect to two points. It reads p₁ − p₂. When both legs have the same liquid above mercury and the tappings are level, p₁ − p₂ = (ρ_Hg − ρ_f) g h. ρ_f is the fluid in the pipes.
  • Inverted U-tube. A light fluid, such as air or oil, is trapped at the top. It suits small pressure differences between liquids. A heavy fluid would fall and destroy the trapped layer.
  • Inclined manometer. The tube is tilted at angle θ to the horizontal. The scale reads length L along the tube. The vertical height is h = L sin θ. For the same h, L = h / sin θ, so a small θ gives a long, easy-to-read scale. This is called increased sensitivity.

The sign path method

The manometer equation is not a formula to memorise for each shape. It is a path sum. Use it for any U-tube, inverted tube or inclined tube. Write pressures in Pa. Convert heights to m. Convert densities to kg/m³. Keep the same g for both legs.

  1. Start at one end with p_A. Use gauge pressure if the question asks gauge.
  2. Move along one continuous fluid path.
  3. When the path goes down by a vertical height z, add ρgz.
  4. When it goes up by z, subtract ρgz. ρ is the density of the fluid in that part.
  5. At the same horizontal level in the same fluid, cross without adding anything: the pressure is equal.
  6. At an open end, write p_atm. For gauge pressure, put p_atm = 0.
  7. Solve the resulting equation for the unknown.
  8. Check the sign: a pipe pressure above atmosphere must give a positive gauge answer.

The path can start at either end. Starting at the open end is allowed; the signs reverse. If a limb has two fluids, change density at the interface, not at the same-level crossing.

Worked example

Question. A horizontal water pipe has a U-tube mercury manometer. The water column from the pipe centre to the left mercury surface is h₁ = 0.40 m. The right mercury surface is h = 0.15 m above the left mercury surface and is open to atmosphere. ρ_water = 1000 kg/m³, ρ_Hg = 13,600 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s². Find the gauge pressure in the pipe in Pa and kPa.

  1. Take gauge pressure at the open right surface as 0.
  2. Start at the pipe centre A with pressure p_A.
  3. Go down the water column h₁ = 0.40 m: pressure rises by 1000 × 9.81 × 0.40 = 3,924 Pa.
  4. Cross the mercury at the left-surface level. The right limb at that level has the same pressure.
  5. Go up mercury h = 0.15 m: pressure falls by 13,600 × 9.81 × 0.15 = 20,012.4 Pa.
  6. At the open surface, gauge pressure is 0. So p_A + 3,924 − 20,012.4 = 0.
  7. p_A = 20,012.4 − 3,924 = 16,088.4 Pa = 16.09 kPa gauge.

Answer. The gauge pressure in the pipe is 16,088.4 Pa, which is about 16.09 kPa.

Exam traps

  • Down add, up subtract, from the starting pressure. Reversing the path reverses every sign; do not mix.
  • Use gauge zero at an open end for gauge pressure. Do not add 101.3 kPa unless absolute pressure is asked.
  • A fluid contributes ρgh only along its own continuous column. Crossing at the same level in the same fluid adds nothing.
  • In an inclined tube the scale reads L, not h. Convert with h = L sin θ.
  • In a differential manometer, cancel the common fluid only when both legs have the same fluid and the tappings are at the same level; otherwise use the full path.

1.8 Hydrostatic forces on plane and curved surfaces

Pressure in a static liquid increases with depth. A submerged gate, tank wall or dam face therefore carries a pressure that changes from point to point. Design needs one resultant force and the point where it acts, because a gate tips about a hinge or a dam overturns about its toe. For a plane surface the pressure distribution reduces to one resultant: F = pc A, where pc is the pressure at the centroid. The force acts at the centre of pressure, which lies below the centroid. This chapter also splits the force on a curved surface into a horizontal part from the projected area and a vertical part from the liquid weight.

You need: pressure variation with depth for p = ρgh, and manometer rules for same-level pressure.

Force on a plane surface

Take a flat area A inside a liquid of density ρ. The free surface is the top liquid level. Let θ be the angle between the plane and the horizontal. Measure y along the plane from the free surface. A small strip at y has vertical depth h = y sin θ. Its pressure is p = ρ g h, so the force on it is dF = p dA.

F = ρ g hc A = pc A

F is total hydrostatic force in N; ρ is liquid density in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s²; hc is vertical depth of the centroid of A in m; A is area in m²; pc is pressure at the centroid in Pa.

The centroid is the geometric centre of the area. The formula works because pressure varies linearly with depth, so the average pressure over A equals the pressure at the centroid.

The resultant does not act at the centroid. It acts at the centre of pressure, deeper than the centroid because lower strips carry more pressure. Along the plane, ycp = yc + Ic/(A yc).

y<sub>cp</sub> = y<sub>c</sub> + I<sub>c</sub>/(A y<sub>c</sub>)

ycp is distance along the plane from the free surface to the centre of pressure in m; yc is distance along the plane to the centroid in m; Ic is second moment of area about the centroidal axis parallel to the free surface in m⁴; A is area in m².

For a vertical rectangle with its top edge at the free surface, yc = h/2 and Ic = b h³/12. Put them in the formula: ycp = h/2 + (b h³/12)/(b h × h/2) = 2h/3. The force acts at two-thirds of the height from the top.

The pressure prism is another picture. Plot pressure p = ρ g h normal to the surface. The volume of this prism equals the total force. Its centroid lies on the line of action. For a rectangle with top at the free surface, the pressure triangle has centroid at 2h/3 from the top.

Force on a curved surface

A curved surface has pressure normal at every point, so the directions differ. Split the resultant into a horizontal component and a vertical component. Find each component by a simple rule, then combine them as vectors.

The horizontal component on a curved surface equals the force on the vertical projection of the surface. Project the curved area onto a vertical plane. Let Ap be that projected area and hc its centroid depth. Then FH = ρ g hc Ap. The line of action is the centre of pressure of the projected area.

F<sub>H</sub> = ρ g h<sub>c</sub> A<sub>p</sub>

FH is horizontal force component in N; Ap is projected vertical area in m²; hc is vertical depth to the centroid of Ap in m.

The vertical component equals the weight of the liquid that would stand above the curved surface up to the free surface. If liquid is below the surface, use an imaginary free surface. Let V be the volume between the surface and the free surface. Then FV = ρ g V. The line of action passes through the centroid of that volume.

FV = ρ g V

FV is vertical force component in N; V is volume of liquid above the surface up to the free surface in m³.

Combine: F = √(FH² + FV²), and tan θ = FV/FH, where θ is the angle of the resultant to the horizontal. The line of action comes from the two components: FH acts at the centre of pressure of the projected vertical area, and FV acts through the centroid of the liquid volume between the surface and the free surface. Combine the two to locate the resultant. Liquid above pushes down; liquid below pushes up.

For a curved gate holding water, compute FH from the projected area and FV from the water volume above. Take moments about a hinge to find the opening force. This is the standard route for radial gates, tank ends and dam spillway crests.

Worked example

Question. A vertical rectangular gate is 2 m wide and 3 m high. Its top edge is at the water free surface. Find the total hydrostatic force and the centre of pressure. Use ρ = 1000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Area A = b h = 2 m × 3 m = 6 m². Width b = 2 m, height h = 3 m.
  2. Centroid depth hc = h/2 = 1.5 m because the top is at the free surface.

  6. yc = hc = 1.5 m for a vertical gate. ycp = yc + Ic/(A yc) = 1.5 + 4.5/(6 × 1.5) = 1.5 + 0.5 = 2.0 m from the top.

  • Using centroid depth hc as the centre of pressure. The force equals pc A, but it acts lower, at ycp.
  • Mixing y along an inclined plane with vertical depth h. Use h = y sin θ; the formula for ycp uses y, not h.

1.9 Buoyancy, floatation and metacentric height

Push a wooden block under water and it springs back up. The fluid pushes up with a force equal to the weight of the fluid the block pushes aside. That upward force is buoyancy. It keeps ships, boats and floating covers above water. This chapter finds that force, sets the condition for floating, and measures stability with metacentric height. Stability asks a second question: after a small tilt, does the body return upright or capsize?

You need: density and specific weight, pressure variation with depth, and hydrostatic forces on surfaces.

Archimedes principle and floatation

Archimedes principle: a body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid receives an upward force equal to the weight of fluid it displaces. The pressure on the lower face of a submerged body is larger than on the upper face, so the pressure forces add to an upward resultant.

Fb = ρ g V

Fb is the upward buoyant force in N; ρ is fluid density in kg/m³; g is gravitational acceleration, 9.81 m/s²; V is displaced volume in m³.

A floating body is in vertical equilibrium when its weight equals buoyancy: W = m g = ρ g V, so V = m/ρ. Here m is body mass in kg and V is the volume below the waterline, not the whole volume of the body.

Draft d is the depth of the lowest point below the waterline. For a body with vertical sides and waterplane area Aw, V = Aw d, so d = V/Aw. Center of buoyancy B is the centroid of displaced volume. For a rectangular box of draft d, KB = d/2, where KB is height of B above the bottom.

Metacenter and metacentric height

Tilt a floating body by a small angle θ. The displaced volume keeps the same size but changes shape, so B moves sideways. Draw a vertical line through the new B. It cuts the body's original centreline at M, the metacenter. For small θ, M stays fixed.

The distance BM follows BM = I/V. I is the second moment of waterplane area about the axis of tilt, in m⁴; V is displaced volume in m³; BM is in m. For a rectangle of length L and width b, tilting about the long axis, I = L b³/12. The width b is across the tilt, not the length.

BM = I / V, GM = BM − (KG − KB)

I is second moment of waterplane area about the tilt axis in m⁴; V is displaced volume in m³; KG and KB are heights of G and B above the bottom in m; GM is metacentric height in m.

Measure heights from the bottom: KB to B, KG to centre of gravity G. Then BG = KG − KB. The metacentric height is GM = BM − BG = BM − (KG − KB). If G is below B, BG is negative and GM is larger.

Stability rule for small tilts: GM > 0 stable, body returns; GM = 0 neutral; GM < 0 unstable, body heels further. A positive GM means G is below M. This rule uses small angles only.

One experimental check rocks the body and times small oscillations. The period T = 2π √(k²/(g GM)), where k is radius of gyration about the roll axis. A longer period means smaller GM.

Solving a stability problem

Follow a fixed order. Use displaced volume everywhere; never the full volume of the body. Keep kg, m and s consistent.

  1. Find displaced volume V = m/ρ from the floating condition.
  2. Find draft d = V/Aw and KB, the centroid height of the displaced volume.
  3. Read KG from the mass distribution; compute BG = KG − KB.
  4. Find I about the roll axis; compute BM = I/V.
  5. Compute GM = BM − BG. State stable if GM > 0.

A wide, shallow barge has large I and small V, so BM is large. That is why barges and pontoons are stiff. A tall narrow body has small I and large KG, so GM can fall below zero. A GM in metres is read directly as a stability margin.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular barge is 4 m wide and 10 m long. Its mass is 20,000 kg. It floats in fresh water of density 1000 kg/m³. Its centre of gravity is 1.0 m above the bottom. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the draft, the height of the centre of buoyancy, BM, GM, and state whether it is stable.

  1. Floating: weight = buoyancy, so m g = ρ g V and V = m/ρ = 20,000/1000 = 20 m³.
  2. Waterplane area Aw = 4 × 10 = 40 m². Draft d = V/Aw = 20/40 = 0.5 m.
  3. For a box, KB = d/2 = 0.25 m. Given KG = 1.0 m, so BG = KG − KB = 1.0 − 0.25 = 0.75 m.
  4. Tilt is about the long axis, so b = 4 m and L = 10 m. I = L b³/12 = 10 × 4³ /12 = 10 × 64 /12 = 53.3333 m⁴.
  5. BM = I/V = 53.3333/20 = 2.6667 m.
  6. GM = BM − BG = 2.6667 − 0.75 = 1.9167 m. GM > 0, so stable.

Answer. Draft 0.5 m, KB 0.25 m, BG 0.75 m, BM 2.6667 m, GM 1.9167 m; the barge is stable.

Exam traps

  • Using the body's full volume in Fb = ρgV or in BM = I/V. Use only the displaced volume below the waterline.
  • Reversing BG. With heights from the bottom, BG = KG − KB, and GM = BM − BG. If G is above B, BG is positive.
  • Taking b as the length in I = L b³/12. b is the width across the tilt axis.
  • Applying GM to a large heel. GM and the fixed metacenter are small-angle ideas.
  • Mixing up B and G. B is the centroid of displaced fluid; G is the mass centroid of the body.

Takeaways

  1. A fluid can be modelled as a continuum when the flow scale is far larger than the molecular spacing, so density and velocity can be assigned to a point.
  2. Density, specific weight and specific gravity are simple ratios of mass, weight and water density; bulk modulus and compressibility describe volume change under pressure.
  3. Viscosity converts velocity differences between layers into shear stress; Newton's law of viscosity is τ = μ du/dy.
  4. Newtonian fluids have constant viscosity, while non-Newtonian fluids change apparent viscosity with shear rate.
  5. Surface tension causes a pressure jump across a curved interface and produces capillary rise or fall in narrow tubes.
  6. In a static fluid, pressure increases linearly with depth, and gauge pressure is absolute pressure minus atmospheric pressure.
  7. A manometer uses the same-fluid-same-level rule: equal levels in the same continuous fluid have equal pressure.
  8. The resultant force on a plane submerged surface is pressure at the centroid times area, and it acts at the centre of pressure.
  9. Buoyant force equals the weight of displaced fluid; a floating body is stable when its metacentric height is positive.
  10. For a curved submerged surface, horizontal and vertical force components can be found separately; the vertical component equals the weight of the displaced liquid column.

Glossary

Absolute pressure
Pressure measured from absolute zero, equal to gauge pressure plus atmospheric pressure.
Bulk modulus
The pressure increase needed to produce a unit fractional decrease in volume.
Buoyancy
The upward force exerted by a fluid on a body immersed in it, equal to the weight of displaced fluid.
Capillarity
The rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube caused by surface tension and contact angle.
Centre of pressure
The point where the resultant hydrostatic force acts on a submerged surface.
Compressibility
The reciprocal of bulk modulus; fractional volume change per unit pressure change.
Contact angle
The angle between the liquid surface and the solid wall at the contact line.
Continuum
A model in which fluid properties are assumed to vary smoothly from point to point, ignoring individual molecules.
Density
Mass per unit volume of a fluid, measured in kg/m³.
Dynamic viscosity
The fluid property that relates shear stress to velocity gradient, measured in Pa·s.
Gauge pressure
Pressure measured relative to atmospheric pressure, equal to absolute pressure minus atmospheric pressure.
Hydrostatic force
The force exerted by a static fluid on a submerged surface, equal to pressure at the centroid times area for a plane surface.
Kinematic viscosity
Dynamic viscosity divided by density, measured in m²/s.
Manometer
An instrument that measures pressure by balancing it against a column of liquid.
Metacentre
The point where the vertical line through the new centre of buoyancy meets the original vertical centreline after a small tilt.
Metacentric height
The distance between the metacentre and the centre of gravity of a floating body.
Newtonian fluid
A fluid in which shear stress is directly proportional to shear rate at a given temperature.
Non-Newtonian fluid
A fluid in which the relation between shear stress and shear rate is not a straight line through the origin.
Piezometer
A simple vertical tube open to atmosphere, used to measure pressure in a liquid pipeline.
Shear stress
Tangential force per unit area acting between fluid layers.
Specific gravity
The ratio of a fluid's density to the density of water at 4 °C.
Specific weight
Weight per unit volume of a fluid, equal to ρg and measured in N/m³.
Stable equilibrium
A floating body condition in which a small tilt produces a restoring moment; metacentric height is positive.
Surface tension
The force per unit length acting along a liquid surface, tending to reduce surface area.
Vacuum pressure
The amount by which pressure is below atmospheric pressure; it is a negative gauge pressure.

The rate at which velocity changes with distance perpendicular to the flow direction.
