5.1 Boundary layer concepts; growth over a flat plate

Hold a flat plate edge-on in a stream of water. The fluid touching the plate sticks to it, so the velocity there is zero, while a few millimetres away the fluid moves at the full stream speed U. That thin slowed region is the boundary layer, and its thickness δ grows from zero at the leading edge. Knowing δ tells you where viscous friction acts and how much skin-friction drag a ship hull, an aircraft wing or a turbine blade will feel.

You need viscosity, Newton's law of viscosity and the no-slip condition from Unit 1 (Fluid Properties and Fluid Statics), and the Reynolds number from Unit 4 (Viscous Flow Through Pipes).

The layer where velocity rises from zero

At a solid surface the fluid in contact with it does not slide. The fluid velocity at the wall equals the wall velocity. For a stationary flat plate this is u = 0 at y = 0, where u is the velocity at height y above the plate. This no-slip condition comes from viscosity: friction between fluid layers drags the fluid next to the wall to rest.

Far above the plate the fluid moves at the free-stream velocity U. Between wall and free stream, u rises from 0 to U. The rise happens in a thin band called the boundary layer. Its thickness δ is the height y where u = 0.99U. The 99% is a convention — the layer has no sharp outer edge, and u only approaches U asymptotically. Some syllabi call δ the nominal thickness and the whole picture Prandtl's boundary layer.

Near the wall the gradient du/dy is large, so the viscous shear stress τ = μ du/dy matters (μ is dynamic viscosity in Pa·s). Outside the layer du/dy is tiny and the flow behaves as inviscid. This split — a thin viscous layer inside an inviscid outer flow — is what makes drag and lift calculable.

Scale check: water at 20 °C has ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s. At U = 0.5 m/s and x = 0.5 m the layer is only 5 mm thick, as the worked example shows, while the plate may be hundreds of millimetres long. δ ≪ x.

Growth over a flat plate

δ is zero at the leading edge x = 0 and thickens downstream. For laminar flow over a smooth flat plate, δ = 5x/√Rex, with Rex = Ux/ν. The formula is a fit to Blasius's exact series solution. Only a few universities derive the series; most ask you to use the formula.

δ = 5x / √Re<sub>x</sub>    and    Re<sub>x</sub> = Ux / ν

δ is boundary layer thickness in m; x is distance from the leading edge in m; U is free-stream velocity in m/s; ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s; Rex is the local Reynolds number, dimensionless.

δ grows as √x, not in a straight line. But δ/x = 5/√Rex falls as x rises: at 4× the distance, δ is only 2× larger, so the plate outruns its own layer. Profiles at different x are similar in shape — plot u/U against y/δ and they collapse onto one curve. That self-similarity is why one formula covers the whole plate.

Past a critical Reynolds number Rex ≈ 5×10⁵ the layer turns turbulent. Eddies appear, the profile becomes fuller near the wall, and the layer then thickens faster than the laminar √x law. The thickness is continuous through transition — it does not jump. A correlation often quoted for the turbulent part is δ ≈ 0.38x/Rex1/5. That correlation describes a layer that has been turbulent all the way from the leading edge, so it overpredicts δ just downstream of transition. To use it just after transition, measure x from a virtual origin picked so the curve meets the laminar thickness at xc. For a smooth plate in a quiet stream transition is fairly sharp near 5×10⁵; roughness or a disturbed stream can bring it down to about 3×10⁵. Use 5×10⁵ unless told otherwise.

The transition distance follows from Rec = Uxc/ν, so xc = Rec ν/U. In the worked example xc = 1.0 m, twice the station where δ was found, confirming that δ there was genuinely laminar.

Where the layer matters

Skin-friction drag on a hull, a wing or a blade is the wall shear stress τw integrated over the surface, with τw = μ(du/dy) at the wall — set entirely by the profile inside the layer. Because the layer is thin, an inviscid solution gives the pressure field and the layer gives the friction. The layer also decides where the flow separates when pressure rises downstream (see 5.4).

Worked example

Question. Water at 20 °C (ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s) flows at U = 0.5 m/s over a smooth flat plate. (a) Find the boundary layer thickness at x = 0.5 m from the leading edge. (b) Find the distance x_c where the layer becomes turbulent, taking Re_c = 5×10⁵.

  1. Local Reynolds number: Re_x = Ux/ν = (0.5 m/s)(0.5 m)/(1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s) = 0.25/1.0×10⁻⁶ = 2.5×10⁵.
  2. Compare: Re_x = 2.5×10⁵ is below Re_c = 5×10⁵, so the layer at x = 0.5 m is laminar and the laminar formula applies.
  3. Thickness: δ = 5x/√Re_x = 5(0.5 m)/√(2.5×10⁵) = 2.5/500 m = 0.005 m = 5 mm.
  4. Transition: x_c = Re_c ν/U = (5×10⁵)(1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s)/(0.5 m/s) = 0.5/0.5 m = 1.0 m.

Answer. δ = 5 mm = 0.005 m at x = 0.5 m, and the layer becomes turbulent at x_c = 1.0 m.

Exam traps

  • Taking δ at u = 0 — that is only the wall itself. δ is where u = 0.99U.
  • Using the plate length L in Rex when the question asks for the local thickness at distance x. Rex uses the local x.
  • Confusing ν and μ: Rex = Ux/ν = ρUx/μ. Putting μ where ν belongs makes Rex too small by a factor ρ (about 1000 for water), and makes δ too large by a factor √ρ (about 32).
  • Assuming δ grows in a straight line. δ ∝ √x, so δ/x falls as x rises; doubling x multiplies δ by only 1.41.
  • Treating the 99% thickness as a physical edge. The layer has no sharp boundary and u only approaches U asymptotically.
  • Applying δ = 0.38x/Rex1/5 right after transition and expecting a jump. δ is continuous at xc; the turbulent correlation needs a virtual origin there.

5.2 Displacement, momentum and energy thickness

The fluid slowed inside a boundary layer carries less mass, less momentum and less energy than the same height of free-stream fluid. Rather than compare whole velocity profiles, engineers squeeze each deficit into one length in metres: displacement thickness δ*, momentum thickness θ and energy thickness δE. These lengths turn a complicated profile into three numbers. They feed wall-shear formulas, drag estimates and boundary-layer control. A pipeline designer or a wing analyst uses them to answer: how much does this layer thicken the body, and how much drag or energy loss does it cause?

You need: 5.1 for the boundary layer and the velocity profile u(y) inside it.

Three deficits, three lengths

Inside the layer the velocity u rises from 0 at the wall to the free-stream value U. Take y as height above the wall and δ as the boundary-layer thickness where u ≈ U. The same height of free-stream fluid would carry more mass, more momentum and more kinetic energy. Each deficit is written as the free-stream value multiplied by a length.

δ* = ∫₀^δ (1 − u/U) dy

u is the local velocity in m/s at height y in m; U is the free-stream velocity in m/s; δ is the boundary-layer thickness in m; δ* is the displacement thickness in m.

δ* is the height by which the wall would have to move outward to give the same mass-flow deficit. The missing mass flow per unit width is ρUδ*, with ρ the density in kg/m³. It is a fictitious distance, not the visible edge of the layer.

θ = ∫₀^δ (u/U)(1 − u/U) dy

θ is the momentum thickness in m; other symbols are as above. The missing momentum flux per unit width is ρU²θ.

θ is the height of free-stream fluid whose momentum equals the momentum lost by the slow layer. It is the key length in drag calculations because wall shear feeds momentum loss.

δE = ∫₀^δ (u/U)(1 − (u/U)²) dy

δE is the energy thickness in m; some syllabi write δ** for it. The missing kinetic-energy flux per unit width is (1/2)ρU³δE.

The shape factor H = δ*/θ is a dimensionless number. A profile that stays slow over most of the layer (laminar) has a large mass deficit compared with its momentum deficit, so H is high. A fuller profile near the wall, as in turbulent flow, has lower H. For the Blasius laminar layer on a flat plate, δ*/δ ≈ 0.34, θ/δ ≈ 0.13 and H ≈ 2.59.

Integrals for two profiles

For a straight-line profile u/U = y/δ, put η = y/δ. Then dy = δ dη and η runs from 0 to 1. The three integrals become simple polynomials.

δ* = δ∫₀¹(1 − η)dη = δ/2; θ = δ∫₀¹ η(1 − η)dη = δ/6; δ<sub>E</sub> = δ∫₀¹ η(1 − η²)dη = δ/4

η is the dimensionless height y/δ; the results are in metres when δ is in metres.

For the 1/7 power law u/U = η^(1/7), used as a rough model of a turbulent layer, the integrals give δ* = δ/8, θ = 7δ/72 ≈ 0.0972δ and δE = 7δ/40 = 0.175δ. Then H = (δ/8)/(7δ/72) = 9/7 ≈ 1.29. A real turbulent layer has H around 1.3 to 1.4, so the simple power law is reasonable but not exact.

Momentum thickness and wall shear

The momentum integral equation links θ to the shear stress at the wall τw in N/m². For a flat plate with zero pressure gradient, τw = ρU² dθ/dx, where x is distance along the plate in m. The derivative dθ/dx has units of m/m = dimensionless, so the right side has units kg/m³ × m²/s² = kg/(m·s²) = N/m².

This equation is useful because θ grows along the plate as the layer thickens. Measure or compute θ(x), differentiate it, and you get τw(x) without finding the slope of the velocity profile at the wall. Integrate τw over the plate area and you get the friction drag. That step is in 5.4.

When pressure changes along the surface, an extra term appears; the simple form above is for a flat plate at zero pressure gradient. For the straight-line profile example, θ = δ/6, so if δ grows with x, τw is proportional to dδ/dx.

Worked example

Question. A boundary layer of thickness δ = 10 mm has the straight-line profile u/U = y/δ. Find δ*, θ, δE and H.

  1. δ* = ∫ from y = 0 to 10 mm of (1 − y/10) dy = [y − y²/20] from 0 to 10 = 10 − 5 = 5.0 mm.
  2. θ = ∫ from y = 0 to 10 mm of (y/10)(1 − y/10) dy = [y²/20 − y³/300] from 0 to 10 = 5 − 3.333 = 1.667 mm ≈ 1.67 mm.
  3. δE = ∫ from y = 0 to 10 mm of (y/10)(1 − y²/100) dy = [y²/20 − y⁴/4000] from 0 to 10 = 5 − 2.5 = 2.5 mm.
  4. H = δ*/θ = 5.0/1.667 = 3.0.

Answer. δ* = 5.0 mm, θ = 1.67 mm, δE = 2.5 mm and H = 3.0.

Exam traps

  • Do not write H = θ/δ*. The shape factor is H = δ*/θ, dimensionless, and is greater than 1 for a real layer.
  • Energy thickness uses (u/U)(1 − (u/U)²), not (1 − u/U). For the linear profile this gives 0.5δ instead of 0.25δ, exactly twice too large; for the 1/7 power law it gives 0.125δ instead of 0.175δ. Either way the number is wrong, so use the correct integrand.
  • δ* is not the same as δ. δ is where u reaches U; δ* is a fictitious extra wall distance. Keep all three thicknesses in metres.
  • Integrate up to y = δ, or beyond where u = U, and use the free-stream U in every ratio. Stopping at an arbitrary height leaves part of the deficit uncounted.
  • All three integrands are positive because u ≤ U. A negative thickness means u/U was inverted or the limits were swapped.

5.3 Laminar and turbulent boundary layers; laminar sublayer

A boundary layer begins laminar and turns turbulent past a critical Reynolds number. In the turbulent part the velocity profile is fuller, the layer is thicker, and the wall shear is larger. Even then, a very thin laminar sublayer clings to the wall. It holds only about a fifth of the velocity rise from zero to the free-stream speed U, but there the gradient is steepest and the whole wall shear passes through viscosity. These facts let engineers estimate skin friction on wings, ship hulls, car bodies and long pipes, and explain why a turbulent layer transfers heat better but costs more drag.

You need 5.1 for growth and the critical Reynolds number, and 5.2 for the profile integrals.

Laminar layer turns turbulent

A boundary layer starts at the leading edge with a laminar profile. Fluid moves in smooth layers. At a distance x from the leading edge, the local Reynolds number tells which regime is present.

Rex = Ux/ν

Rex is the local Reynolds number, dimensionless; U is the free-stream velocity in m/s; x is distance from the leading edge in m; ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s.

Below Rex ≈ 5×10⁵ the layer is normally laminar. Above it, small disturbances grow and the layer becomes turbulent. The transition point is xc ≈ 5×10⁵ν/U.

The two profiles look different. The laminar profile is rounded: velocity rises slowly from the wall and the layer stays thin. The turbulent profile is fuller. A useful engineering fit for its outer part is the 1/7 power law.

u/U = (y/δ)1/7

u is velocity at height y above the wall in m/s; U is free-stream velocity in m/s; y is height above the wall in m; δ is boundary layer thickness in m. This law fails very near the wall.

δ<sub>lam</sub> = 5x/√Re<sub>x</sub>    δ<sub>turb</sub> = 0.37x/Re<sub>x</sub><sup>1/5</sup>

δlam is laminar boundary layer thickness in m; δturb is turbulent boundary layer thickness in m; x is distance from the leading edge in m; Rex is Ux/ν, dimensionless.

The turbulent layer is thicker. At the same x and U it grows like Rex−1/5, while the laminar layer grows like Rex−1/2. At x = 2 m with ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s (so Rex = 2×10⁶), the laminar estimate is 7.1 mm and the turbulent estimate is 41 mm. A fuller profile carries more momentum close to the wall, so the wall shear and skin friction are higher. This is why a turbulent layer removes heat faster but costs more drag.

The laminar sublayer near the wall

No-slip still holds at a turbulent wall: u = 0 at y = 0. Very close to the wall, turbulent eddies are suppressed by viscosity. This thin sheet is the laminar sublayer. Above it is a buffer layer where neither the viscous law nor the turbulent law fits. Above that is the turbulent core.

y+ = y u*/ν

y+ is the dimensionless wall coordinate; y is height above the wall in m; u* is friction velocity in m/s; ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s. The viscous sublayer is often taken below y+ ≈ 5; the buffer layer lies roughly between 5 and 30.

The friction velocity is a measure of wall shear. For a flat plate it is found from the skin friction coefficient.

u<sub>*</sub> = U√(C<sub>f</sub>/2)    δ<sub>s</sub> ≈ 11.6ν/u<sub>*</sub>

u* is friction velocity in m/s; U is free-stream velocity in m/s; Cf is the local skin friction coefficient at the same station, dimensionless; δs is laminar sublayer thickness in m; ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s.

Textbooks quote the sublayer edge either at y+ ≈ 5 (purely viscous part) or at y+ ≈ 11.6, where the linear law meets the logarithmic law of the wall. The common engineering formula δs ≈ 11.6ν/u* uses the second convention. With the first convention δs ≈ 5ν/u*; for the numbers below that gives 0.11 mm instead of 0.26 mm.

For U = 15 m/s and Cf = 0.004, u* = 0.67 m/s and δs = 0.26 mm. The whole boundary layer at the same condition is about 41 mm, over 150 times thicker. Larger Cf raises u*, raises wall shear τw = ρu*², and squeezes the sublayer thinner. The universal velocity profile in wall units and Prandtl's mixing length explain these layers further; only a few universities teach them.

Worked example

Question. Air with ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s flows at U = 15 m/s over a 2 m long flat plate. Find the transition point, the boundary layer thickness at the trailing edge, and the laminar sublayer thickness there. Use Rec = 5×10⁵ and Cf = 0.072/ReL1/5 for the turbulent plate.

  1. Transition point: xc = Recν/U = 5×10⁵ × 1.5×10⁻⁵ / 15 = 0.5 m. The layer is laminar for the first 0.5 m and turbulent after that.
  2. At the trailing edge x = 2 m: Rex = Ux/ν = 15 × 2 / 1.5×10⁻⁵ = 2.0×10⁶. Since 2.0×10⁶ > 5×10⁵, the layer is turbulent there.
  3. Turbulent thickness: Rex1/5 = (2.0×10⁶)1/5 = 18.2. δ = 0.37x/Rex1/5 = 0.37 × 2 / 18.2 = 0.041 m = 41 mm.
  4. Laminar formula if wrongly used: δ = 5x/√Rex = 5 × 2 / √(2.0×10⁶) = 10 / 1414 = 0.0071 m = 7.1 mm. The laminar estimate is about six times too small.
  5. Skin friction: for plate length L = 2 m, ReL = 2.0×10⁶, so Cf = 0.072/ReL1/5 = 0.072/18.2 = 0.00396. This Cf is the mean value for the whole plate; using it as the local value at x = 2 m is an approximation and slightly overstates the local wall shear.
  6. Friction velocity: u* = U√(Cf/2) = 15 × √(0.00396/2) = 15 × √0.00198 = 15 × 0.0445 = 0.67 m/s.
  7. Sublayer thickness (edge at y+ ≈ 11.6): δs = 11.6ν/u* = 11.6 × 1.5×10⁻⁵ / 0.67 = 2.6×10⁻⁴ m = 0.26 mm.

Answer. Transition occurs at x = 0.5 m; at the trailing edge Rex = 2.0×10⁶, δ = 41 mm, and the laminar sublayer is 0.26 mm thick.

Exam traps

  • Using the laminar formula after transition: at Rex = 2×10⁶ it gives 7.1 mm instead of the turbulent 41 mm.
  • Confusing the laminar sublayer with the whole boundary layer. The sublayer is about 0.26 mm here; the layer is 41 mm.
  • Mixing Rex with ReL in Cf. Both are dimensionless, but x is the local distance and L is the full plate length.
  • Using U instead of u* in y+ or in the sublayer formula. u* is much smaller, about 0.67 m/s here against 15 m/s.
  • Putting dynamic viscosity μ in Reynolds number. Re uses kinematic viscosity ν in m²/s; μ has units Pa·s.

5.4 Boundary layer separation, drag and lift

A boundary layer normally clings to a surface and grows quietly along it. If the pressure starts rising in the direction of flow, the slow fluid near the wall runs out of momentum, stops, and turns back. The layer lifts off and leaves a churning wake behind it. That breakaway is separation. It matters wherever fluid meets a solid body: a bus on a highway, a chimney in monsoon wind, a bend in a water main, a cricket ball swung through the air, a wing at high angle of attack. Separation raises drag and can kill lift.

You need: 5.1 for how the layer grows, 5.3 for transition, and 5.2 for momentum thickness.

Why the layer breaks away

Pressure changes along a surface are set by the outer flow and pass almost unchanged through the thin layer. On the front of a cylinder or an aerofoil the flow speeds up and pressure falls: dp/dx < 0, called a favourable pressure gradient. Past the widest point the flow slows and pressure rises: dp/dx > 0, an adverse pressure gradient.

Rising pressure pushes fluid backwards, against the direction of flow. Fluid near the wall has already lost most of its momentum to friction. It cannot climb the pressure hill. It slows, stops, and then reverses. The layer lifts off the surface, and a wake with a recirculating eddy forms behind the body.

Watch the velocity profile. Near the leading edge the profile is full and steep at the wall, so the wall shear is large. Inside the adverse region the profile leans over and the slope at the wall falls. At the separation point the slope is exactly zero. Downstream it is negative, so the fluid touching the wall moves upstream.

τ<sub>w</sub> = μ (du/dy)<sub>y = 0</sub> = 0 at separation

τw is the wall shear stress in N/m²; μ is the dynamic viscosity in Pa·s; u is the velocity at height y above the wall in m/s; y is measured in m. Separation needs dp/dx > 0 upstream of this point.

  1. Find the outer pressure distribution and hence dp/dx along the surface.
  2. Solve the layer, or the momentum integral, for u(y) at each station x.
  3. Locate the station where (du/dy) at the wall reaches zero; that is the separation point.
  4. Beyond it the layer equations fail; the wake must be measured or computed by CFD.

Friction drag, form drag and the coefficients

The fluid pushes on the body in two ways: shear along the surface, and pressure normal to it. Summing the shear gives friction drag Df. Summing the pressure gives pressure drag; after separation the wake holds low pressure, so the net pressure force points downstream. That part is form drag Dp. Total drag D = Df + Dp, in N.

C<sub>D</sub> = D / (½ ρ U² A)   and   C<sub>L</sub> = L / (½ ρ U² A)

CD and CL are the drag and lift coefficients, both dimensionless; D is drag in N and L is lift in N, the force perpendicular to the free stream; ρ is density in kg/m³; U is free-stream speed in m/s; A is a reference area in m², the frontal area for a bluff body and the plan area for a wing.

A flat plate parallel to the flow does not separate, so Dp is nearly zero and all the drag is friction. The average skin friction coefficient uses the Reynolds number over the whole plate, ReL = UL/ν.

C<sub>f</sub> = 1.328 / √Re<sub>L</sub>  (laminar)    C<sub>f</sub> = 0.072 / Re<sub>L</sub><sup>1/5</sup>  (turbulent)

Cf is the average skin friction coefficient, dimensionless; L is plate length in m; ν is kinematic viscosity in m²/s. The laminar form holds up to ReL of about 5×10⁵; the turbulent form is used above it. Then D = Cf × ½ρU² × Awetted, with Awetted the total wetted area in m².

Delaying separation, and where lift comes from

Separation costs a low-pressure wake, which is expensive. Five practical remedies exist.

  • Streamlining: shape the rear so the pressure rise is spread over a long distance, giving the slow fluid room to recover. The classic teardrop.
  • Suction: draw the slowest fluid out through a slot, so fresh outer air reaches the wall.
  • Blowing: a small tangential jet near the wall injects momentum into the sluggish layer.
  • Vortex generators: small vanes that shed vortices which mix fast outer air down into the layer.
  • Trip wires and roughness: force turbulent transition early, because a turbulent layer carries more momentum near the wall and resists the pressure rise. Golf ball dimples work this way.

Lift comes from circulation. A body with circulation carries fluid faster over one side and slower over the other. By Bernoulli's equation the fast side has lower pressure, so a net force acts perpendicular to the free stream: that is lift. A wing gets its circulation from its camber and its angle of attack. Raise the angle too far and the adverse gradient on the upper surface becomes too strong, the layer separates, lift collapses and drag jumps. That is a stall.

Worked example

Question. Air (ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, ν = 1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s) flows at U = 10 m/s along a smooth flat plate 2 m long and 1 m wide, both faces exposed to the air. Exam traps

  • A flat plate has two wetted sides. Using A = bL alone halves the drag; write A = 2bL when both faces touch the fluid.
  • The average coefficient needs ReL = UL/ν; the local skin friction at a distance x needs Rex = Ux/ν. Mixing the two gives a wrong Cf.
  • Separation is not transition. Transition is laminar turning turbulent; separation is wall shear falling to zero under an adverse gradient.
  • Only dp/dx > 0 causes separation. A falling pressure gradient (dp/dx < 0) accelerates the fluid and keeps the layer attached. Watch the sign.
  • Cf for a plate multiplies the wetted area; CD for a bluff body multiplies the frontal area. Do not swap the two areas.

5.5 Dimensions, units and dimensional homogeneity

Every physical quantity can be written as a product of powers of a few fundamental dimensions: mass M, length L and time T. This idea exists so that an equation can be checked without solving it. A correct equation must have the same dimensions on both sides. Engineers use this check on pump power, pipe friction, drag and flow-meter formulas before trusting a calculation. It also finds the units of a constant, converts between unit systems, and prepares the ground for the dimensionless groups used in model studies.

You need: Unit 1 for the units of density, viscosity and pressure.

Dimensions and units

A dimension says what kind of quantity you have. A unit says how much of it you measure. In mechanics three fundamental dimensions are enough: mass M, length L and time T. In SI their base units are kilogram (kg), metre (m) and second (s). Temperature adds a fourth dimension in thermodynamics, but this chapter stays with M, L and T.

Some books use force F, length L and time T as fundamentals. The two systems connect by F = MLT⁻². A force of 1 N is 1 kg·m/s².

Prefixes scale a unit by powers of ten: kilo (k) = 10³, mega (M) = 10⁶, milli (m) = 10⁻³, micro (μ) = 10⁻⁶. So 1 kPa = 10³ Pa and 1 mm = 10⁻³ m. Density is kg/m³, pressure is N/m² = Pa, and dynamic viscosity is Pa·s. Write units with every number; a bare 9.81 can be m/s² or N/kg.

Dimensional homogeneity has two rules. Every term added or subtracted in a correct equation has the same dimensions, and both sides match. The argument of sin, cos, exp or log is a pure number. A dimensionless constant has dimensions M⁰L⁰T⁰ = 1.

Three uses of homogeneity

First, check an equation. For s = ut + ½at², the term ut has LT⁻¹ × T = L; the term ½at² has LT⁻² × T² = L; both match s, which is L. The ½ is dimensionless.

Second, find the dimensions of an unknown constant. In v = k√(p/ρ), v is LT⁻¹ and p/ρ is (ML⁻¹T⁻²)/(ML⁻³) = L²T⁻², whose square root is LT⁻¹. Therefore k is dimensionless.

Third, convert between unit systems. Replace each unit by its dimension and cancel; the numerical factor follows from the unit sizes. For viscosity, 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s, so 8 poise = 0.8 Pa·s.

Dividing a governing equation by a reference quantity makes every term dimensionless. Only a few universities teach this step in this unit; the full group-building method is in Rayleigh's method and the Buckingham Pi theorem.

5.6 Rayleigh's method and Buckingham Pi theorem

Once you have decided which variables matter, dimensions alone fix the form of the relation between them, up to an unknown function. Rayleigh's method guesses a power product for the quantity you want and fixes the powers so units match. Buckingham's Pi theorem handles many variables and gives n − m independent dimensionless groups. Engineers use both before experiments: drag on a sphere, pressure drop in a pipe, flow over a weir.

You need: 5.5 for fundamental dimensions, units and the homogeneity rule.

Rayleigh's method: one power product

Rayleigh's method starts from one guess: the quantity you want is a constant times a product of powers of the other variables.

F = C D^a V^b ρ^c μ^d

F is the dependent variable (drag force, N); C is a dimensionless constant; D is diameter (m), V is velocity (m/s), ρ is density (kg/m³), μ is viscosity (Pa·s); a, b, c, d are unknown exponents.

  1. List the n variables that matter, including the one you want.
  2. Write the dependent variable as C times each independent variable raised to an unknown power.
  3. Replace every variable by its dimensions in M, L and T.
  4. Equate exponents of M, L and T on the two sides. The constant C is dimensionless, so it adds no exponents to the right-hand side.
  5. Solve the exponent equations and put the exponents back.

The catch: three equations (M, L, T) but one unknown per independent variable. With three independent variables the system closes. With four, one exponent stays free and the answer contains an unknown function — Rayleigh's method has quietly turned into the Pi theorem. With more variables use Buckingham.

Buckingham's Pi theorem

If a phenomenon depends on n dimensional variables that together involve m fundamental dimensions, the relation between them reduces to n − m independent dimensionless groups.

π₁ = φ(π₂, π₃, …, πₙ₋ₘ)

π₁ is the group containing the dependent variable; φ is an unknown function; the other π's are groups built from the independent variables.

m is not automatically 3. Count the dimensions actually present in your list. A problem where only length and time appear has m = 2; a problem listing only lengths has m = 1.

  1. List all n variables.
  2. Write the dimensions of each in M, L, T.
  3. Count m, the fundamental dimensions actually present.
  4. Number of groups = n − m.
  5. Choose m repeating variables.
  6. Build each group as the repeating variables raised to unknown powers, times one leftover variable.
  7. Solve the exponents so the group is dimensionless; check every group and the count.
  • Between them, the repeating variables must contain all m dimensions present.
  • No combination of them may be dimensionless by itself: ρ, V and μ fail, because ρVD/μ is already dimensionless.
  • Never include the dependent variable among them.
  • Pick simple ones: one length (D), one velocity (V), one density (ρ).

Reading the groups

The algebra gives groups; the physics comes from naming them. In the sphere problem, F/(ρV²D²) has the form of a drag coefficient — force divided by area, then by ρV² — and differs from the standard C_D only by a constant factor (8/π). The second group, μ/(ρVD), is 1/Re, the ratio of viscous to inertial effects. Naming groups belongs to 5.7.

Same method on pressure drop in a pipe. Δp depends on D, L, V, ρ and μ: n = 6, m = 3, so three groups. With D, V, ρ repeating: π₁ = Δp/(ρV²), π₂ = L/D, π₃ = μ/(ρVD). Hence Δp/(ρV²) = φ(L/D, Re). That is the Darcy–Weisbach shape, with the friction factor inside φ. Dimensional analysis predicted the form; experiment supplies φ.

  • Each group must be dimensionless — check it before you leave the hall.
  • Exactly n − m groups, no more, no fewer.
  • Different students may choose different repeating variables and get different-looking groups; the count is fixed and the sets are related by rearrangement. Choose the set whose groups you can name.

5.7 Dimensionless numbers

A dimensionless number is a ratio of two forces or energies in a flow, or the square root of such a ratio. It has no units. When one such ratio is large, that force controls the flow. Engineers use these numbers to compare pipe flows, ship waves, droplets and aircraft without carrying units. The five common groups are Reynolds, Froude, Euler, Weber and Mach. Each answers a simple question: is inertia stronger than viscosity, gravity, pressure, surface tension or compressibility? The same numbers decide model tests, so a small model can predict a full-size machine.

You need 5.6 for where dimensionless groups come from and 5.5 for units.

What a dimensionless number means

A flow has several forces acting at once. Inertia is the tendency of moving fluid to keep going. Pick two forces. Divide one by the other. Both have the unit newton, so the newtons cancel. The result is a pure number. Its name records which pair you divided. A large value means the force on top is strong. A small value means the force below is strong. This is the whole habit behind dimensionless numbers.

Reading the numbers in practice

In a pipe, use the inside diameter for D. Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν. Re below about 2000 gives laminar flow. Re above about 4000 gives turbulent flow. Between them the flow is transitional. Small waves travel faster than the flow, so the flow can feel a downstream control. Fr = 1 is critical. Fr > 1 is supercritical. A hydraulic jump can form.

Euler number compares a measured pressure drop with the dynamic pressure ½ρV². A valve with large Eu has a large loss. Weber number matters when surface tension is not negligible: droplets, bubbles and thin jets. Mach number compares flow speed with sound speed c in the gas. Below Ma ≈ 0.3, density changes are small and the flow is treated as incompressible. Above that, use compressible flow relations; see Unit 3, Fluid Dynamics and Flow Measurement. Heat transfer has its own groups, such as Nusselt and Prandtl; they are defined in a later subject, not derived here.

A short procedure for any problem

  1. List every variable with its unit.
  2. Choose the length that matches the body: diameter for a pipe or sphere, chord for an aerofoil, distance x for a flat plate, hydraulic depth for an open channel.
  3. Check the physics: is there a free surface? is surface tension present? is the gas speed above Ma 0.3? Then pick Fr, We or Ma.
  4. Compute the number. Cancel the units. State the result as a pure number.

Pump specific speed is another dimensionless number, but it belongs with pumps; see Unit 7, Centrifugal and Reciprocating Pumps.

Worked example

Question. A 20 mm diameter sphere moves at V = 0.5 m/s through water with ρ = 1000 kg/m³, ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s and surface tension σ = 0.072 N/m. The pressure difference across it is measured as 200 Pa. Compute Re, We and Eu. Use D as the sphere diameter.

  1. Data: D = 20 mm = 0.020 m; V = 0.5 m/s; ρ = 1000 kg/m³; ν = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m²/s; σ = 0.072 N/m; Δp = 200 Pa.
  2. Reynolds number: Re = VD/ν = 0.5 × 0.020 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 0.010 / (1.0×10⁻⁶) = 1.0×10⁴. It has no unit.
  3. Weber number: We = ρV²D/σ = 1000 × (0.5)² × 0.020 / 0.072 = 1000 × 0.25 × 0.020 / 0.072 = 5 / 0.072 = 69.4 (rounded to one decimal). It has no unit.
  4. Euler number: Eu = Δp/(½ρV²) = 200 / (0.5 × 1000 × 0.25) = 200 / 125 = 1.6. It has no unit.
  5. Unit check: ρV² is in Pa; multiplying by D (m) gives Pa·m = N/m, the same unit as σ. So We = (N/m)/(N/m) is a pure number. Re is (m/s × m)/(m²/s), also unit-free. Eu is Pa/Pa.

Answer. Re = 1.0×10⁴, We = 69.4 and Eu = 1.6; all three are dimensionless.

Exam traps

  • Using the wrong length in Re: diameter for a pipe or sphere, chord for an aerofoil, distance x from the leading edge for a flat plate. A different D gives a different Re.
  • Using Froude number in a full pipe with no free surface. Gravity waves cannot form there, so Fr does not control the flow.
  • Forgetting the ½ in Euler number. Some books define Eu = Δp/(ρV²); with that definition every Eu is half the value used here (the value here is twice as large).
  • Mixing dynamic viscosity μ in Pa·s with kinematic viscosity ν in m²/s. Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν; use the matching form.
  • Treating Mach 1 as the start of compressibility. Density changes matter from about Ma 0.3, well below the speed of sound.

5.8 Similitude, model studies and distorted models

A model is a small copy of a real machine — a spillway, a ship, a pipe network. Testing the copy is cheap; testing the real thing is not. But a measurement on the model only predicts the prototype if the two flows are similar in shape, in velocity pattern and in force ratios. That set of conditions is called similitude. This chapter shows what each similarity fixes, how the scale ratios for velocity, discharge and force follow from the one dimensionless group you match, and why river and harbour models are deliberately built with two different length scales.

You need the named groups from 5.7, the idea of a π group from 5.6, and Reynolds number in pipes from Unit 4.

Three similarities, three jobs

The real machine is the prototype (subscript p). The test copy is the model (subscript m). Similitude is the set of conditions that lets a model reading be carried back to the prototype.

Geometric similarity. Every length in the model has the same ratio to the matching length in the prototype. Define the length ratio L_r = L_p / L_m, where L_p is a prototype length in metres and L_m the matching model length in metres. One number then fixes everything: area ratio = L_r², volume ratio = L_r³. The model must have the same shape, not merely the same proportions in one plane.

Kinematic similarity. Velocity at matching points must be in a fixed ratio V_r = V_p / V_m, and so must acceleration. Streamlines then look the same in both, and a particle takes time in the ratio T_r = L_r / V_r. Kinematic similarity is what makes a photograph of the model look like a photograph of the prototype.

Dynamic similarity. Forces at matching points must be in a fixed ratio. Inertia, viscous, gravity and surface-tension forces cannot be scaled independently, so the only clean way to say it is: every dimensionless force ratio must be equal in model and prototype. From 5.7 each group is exactly such a ratio, so matching the group that matters matches the force balance.

A model that matches all three is a true similitude. In practice you match one group — the one whose forces dominate — and this is called the model law.

Model laws: build the scale ratios from the group

Write the group for the model, set it equal to the group for the prototype, and solve for V_r. Everything else follows from geometry and from Q = A V.

Froude law: V_p / √(g L_p) = V_m / √(g L_m)  →  V_r = √L_r

V_r = V_p/V_m is the velocity ratio; L_r = L_p/L_m is the length ratio; g = 9.81 m/s² cancels because it is the same in both.

Q_r = L_r² × √L_r = L_r^(5/2) ,   F_r = ρ_r L_r² V_r² = L_r³ ,   T_r = √L_r

Q_r = Q_p/Q_m is the discharge ratio in m³/s per m³/s; F_r = F_p/F_m is the force ratio in N per N (force ratio, not the Froude number Fr); ρ_r = 1 when the same fluid is used; T_r = T_p/T_m is the time ratio.

Reynolds law, same fluid: V_r = 1 / L_r ,   Q_r = L_r ,   F_r = 1

Re = V L / ν must be equal; ν is the kinematic viscosity in m²/s. Since ν_r = 1, the velocity ratio inverts: the small model must run faster.

Group matchedTypical caseVelocity ratioDischarge ratio
Froude (Fr)ships, spillways, open channels, weirs√L_rL_r^(5/2)
Reynolds (Re)full pipes, submerged bodies, pumps1/L_rL_r
Mach (Ma)compressible gas flow, nozzles1L_r²
Weber (We)droplets, bubbles, thin films1/√L_rL_r^(3/2)

The Mach row assumes the same gas at the same temperature, so the sound speed c is unchanged and V_r = 1. The Weber row assumes the same liquid, so surface tension σ is unchanged.

Distorted models and scale effect

An undistorted model uses one length ratio for all three directions. A distorted model uses a different vertical scale L_vr and horizontal scale L_hr. River and harbour models are almost always distorted, because a 2 km reach that is 30 m deep at 1:1000 would be 2 m long and only 3 cm deep — too shallow for surface tension and viscosity to stay out of the way.

In a distorted model the Froude law is enforced on the vertical plane only, using L_vr. Gravity waves in the model still travel at √(g × depth), so the correct ratios are:

V_r = √L_vr ,   Q_r = L_hr × L_vr^(3/2) ,   S_r = L_vr / L_hr

L_vr is the vertical length ratio, L_hr the horizontal length ratio, Q_r the discharge ratio, and S_r = S_p/S_m the slope ratio. For L_vr = 100 and L_hr = 1000, S_r = 0.1, so the model slope is 10 times steeper than the prototype.

Scale effect is the leftover error. Matching Re and Fr together needs √L_r = 1/L_r, which is true only at L_r = 1 — full size. So the small groups are dropped and the resulting error is estimated and stated. In a 1:30 spillway model, surface tension and viscosity are negligible and Fr alone is enough.

Worked example

Question. A 1:30 scale model of a spillway is tested under Froude similarity. The model velocity is 2.0 m/s, the model discharge is 0.02 m³/s, and the measured force on the model is 15 N. Find the prototype velocity, discharge and force, and state how the times compare. The same water is used in both.

  1. Identify the law. A free-surface flow with gravity waves is controlled by Froude number, so Fr_p = Fr_m. The length ratio is L_r = L_p/L_m = 30, with no unit.
  2. Velocity ratio. V_r = √L_r = √30 = 5.477. Prototype velocity V_p = V_m × V_r = 2.0 × 5.477 = 10.95 m/s, about 10.9 m/s.
  3. Discharge ratio. Q_r = L_r^(5/2) = 30² × √30 = 900 × 5.477 = 4929.5. Prototype discharge Q_p = 0.02 × 4929.5 = 98.6 m³/s.
  4. Force ratio. F_r = ρ_r L_r² V_r². Same water gives ρ_r = 1, and V_r² = L_r, so F_r = L_r³ = 30³ = 27000. Prototype force F_p = 15 × 27000 = 405000 N = 405 kN.
  5. Time ratio. T_r = L_r / V_r = 30 / 5.477 = √L_r = 5.477. So one second in the model represents 5.48 s in the prototype; the model runs faster.

Answer. Prototype velocity 10.95 m/s, discharge 98.6 m³/s, force 405 kN, and prototype times are 5.48 times model times.

Exam traps

  • Scaling the force by L_r² instead of L_r³. Force is ρ A V², so both the area (L_r²) and the velocity squared (L_r² under Froude) scale, giving L_r³.
  • Applying the Froude law to a full pipe flow or a submerged body. A pipe with no free surface has no gravity waves; Reynolds number controls it, and V_r = 1/L_r, not √L_r.
  • Defining L_r both ways. Fix L_r = L_p/L_m once and keep it. A ratio written upside down inverts every answer.
  • In a distorted model, using Q_r = L_r^(5/2) with the vertical scale alone. The correct ratio is Q_r = L_hr × L_vr^(3/2), because the flow cross-section is width (horizontal scale L_hr) times depth (vertical scale L_vr), and velocity scales as √L_vr.
  • Forgetting the same-fluid assumption. Re similarity with a different fluid gives V_r = ν_r / L_r, not 1/L_r.

Remember Name the three similarities required for a model to predict its prototype, and state what each one fixes. 2 marks

Geometric fixes all length ratios (L_r, L_r², L_r³); kinematic fixes the velocity and acceleration ratios at matching points; dynamic fixes the force ratios, which means matching the controlling dimensionless group.

Understand Why can the same water model not satisfy Reynolds and Froude similarity at the same time? 3 marks

Froude needs V_r = √L_r and Reynolds with the same fluid needs V_r = 1/L_r. Equating them gives L_r = 1, that is, full size. For any real scale one group must be sacrificed, and the leftover error is the scale effect.

Apply A 1:50 Froude model of a weir shows a velocity of 1.5 m/s. Find the prototype velocity and the discharge ratio. 4 marks

V_r = √50 = 7.07, so V_p = 1.5 × 7.07 = 10.6 m/s. Q_r = 50^(5/2) = 2500 × 7.07 = 17678, so Q_p = 17678 Q_m.

Apply A 1:8 Reynolds model of an oil pipe uses the same oil. The prototype velocity is 1.2 m/s. Find the model velocity and the force ratio. 4 marks

V_r = 1/L_r = 1/8, so V_m = V_p / V_r = 1.2 × 8 = 9.6 m/s. With the same fluid F_r = ρ_r L_r² V_r² = 1 × 64 × (1/64) = 1, so model and prototype forces are equal.

Analyze A river model uses a vertical scale of 1:100 and a horizontal scale of 1:1000. Explain which length ratio sets the discharge ratio and why. 5 marks

The vertical scale sets velocity, because gravity waves travel at √(g × depth): V_r = √L_vr = √100 = 10. Discharge is flow area × velocity, and the flow area is width (horizontal scale L_hr) × depth (vertical scale L_vr). So Q_r = L_hr × L_vr × √L_vr = L_hr × L_vr^(3/2) = 1000 × 1000 = 10⁶. Using L_vr for both directions gives 100^(5/2) = 10⁵, which underestimates discharge by a factor of 10.

Analyze A 1:25 Froude model reads 20 N on a spillway gate. Estimate the prototype force, and say why the Froude law is still acceptable here even though viscous forces are not scaled. 5 marks

F_r = L_r³ = 15625, so F_p = 20 × 15625 = 312500 N = 312.5 kN. Viscous forces are small compared with gravity and inertia forces in a large spillway flow, so the dropped group causes only a small scale effect and the Froude law holds.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern practice validates a model law against computational fluid dynamics before a physical model is even built, and the two are compared to bound the scale effect. Takeaways

  1. Viscosity plus the no-slip condition forces a thin boundary layer to grow along a flat plate.
  2. The displacement, momentum and energy thicknesses turn three flow deficits into three lengths.
  3. Laminar flow near the leading edge gives way to turbulence above a critical Reynolds number, but a laminar sublayer remains at the wall.
  4. An adverse pressure gradient can stop and reverse the near-wall flow, causing separation, a wake and extra form drag.
  5. Dimensions are products of powers of M, L, T and Θ, and every correct equation is dimensionally homogeneous.
  6. Rayleigh's method solves exponents for a guessed power product; the Buckingham Pi theorem reduces many variables to n − m π groups.
  7. A dimensionless number is a force or energy ratio, and its size shows which physics controls the flow.
  8. Model testing needs geometric, kinematic and dynamic similarity, usually by matching the one controlling dimensionless group.

Glossary

Adverse pressure gradient
A pressure increase in the flow direction that slows the fluid near the wall.
Boundary layer
The thin region near a solid surface where viscous effects make the velocity change from zero at the wall to the free-stream value.
Boundary-layer separation
The detachment of the boundary layer from a surface when near-wall fluid reverses under an adverse pressure gradient.
Boundary-layer thickness
The distance δ from the wall where the velocity reaches about 0.99U∞.
Buckingham Pi theorem
The rule that n variables with m fundamental dimensions form n − m independent dimensionless groups.
Dimensional homogeneity
The requirement that all terms of a correct physical equation have the same dimensions.
Dimensionless number
A combination of variables with no net dimension, often a ratio of two forces.
Displacement thickness
The length δ* by which the wall would be shifted outward to give the same mass-flow deficit as the real boundary layer.
Distorted model
A model in which the length scale is different in different directions.
Dynamic similarity
The condition that forces at corresponding points in model and prototype are in a fixed ratio.
Energy thickness
The length δ_E that represents the loss of kinetic-energy flux in the boundary layer.
Form drag
Drag due to pressure differences around a body, mainly from the wake behind separation.
Free-stream velocity
The uniform velocity U∞ far from the body and outside the boundary layer.
Fundamental dimension
A base dimension such as mass M, length L, time T or temperature Θ.
Geometric similarity
The condition that model and prototype have the same shape with all lengths in one fixed ratio.
Kinematic similarity
The condition that velocities at corresponding points in model and prototype are in a fixed ratio.
Laminar boundary layer
A smooth boundary layer in which fluid particles move in orderly layers and viscous shear dominates.
Laminar sublayer
A very thin viscous layer next to the wall inside a turbulent boundary layer.
Momentum thickness
The length θ that represents the loss of momentum flux in the boundary layer.
No-slip condition
The condition that fluid in contact with a solid surface has the same velocity as the surface.
Rayleigh's method
A dimensional method that assumes a power-law product and solves the unknown exponents.
Shape factor
The ratio H = δ*/θ, which measures how full or how sluggish the velocity profile is.
Skin-friction drag
Drag due to viscous shear stress along the wetted surface.
Turbulent boundary layer
A boundary layer with chaotic eddies, strong mixing and a flatter mean velocity profile.
Wake
The low-pressure disturbed region downstream of a separated or bluff body.
