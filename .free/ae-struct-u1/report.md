- ch 3 [low] Section 1, third sentence of paragraph 2: The reason given for placing the box deep in the aerofoil — 'where the material is farthest from the twist centre' — is loose. - ch 3 [low] Section 2, closing paragraph: 'The leading edge and trailing edge are lighter structures behind and ahead of the spars' puts the two edges in the wrong order: the leading edge is ahead of the front spar, the trailing edge behind the rear spar. - ch 3 [low] Section 3, frame and longeron bullets: Fuselage stringers are never given a job. The longeron bullet calls longerons 'fewer and heavier than stringers', so stringers clearly exist in the layout, but the reader is never told what they do or how they differ from wing stringers.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: The counting rule is muddled. 'One rib closes each bay, plus one at the root' suggests 10 ribs from the bays plus one more, which only works because the tip rib is not mentioned. A student can follow the arithmetic but not the reason.
- ch 4 [high] Worked example 2, step 5 and answer: The chapter says the mass per unit area is unchanged and the gain is 'at the same mass' when the core goes from 10 mm to 20 mm. Doubling the core thickness doubles the core mass. For a 20 mm core the core is a real share of the panel weight, so the claim of no weight penalty is wrong.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q4: The question assumes a plain sheet of the same face material does not fail below yield, and the answer says it has no short-wave mode 'because it is thick enough or supported differently'. A thin plain sheet also buckles below yield, by overall (Euler) buckling. Only the mode differs: the sandwich face wrinkles locally into the core, the plain sheet buckles as a whole.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps, last bullet: Typing slip: 'Mixings units'.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, foam bullet: Polymethacrylimide (PMI) foam is grouped with 'cheap' foams. PMI is the most expensive core foam in common aircraft use, several times the price of PVC or polyurethane foam.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, face wrinkling bullet: 'Over a short wavelength, one or two cells long' suggests the honeycomb cell size sets the wrinkle wavelength. The wavelength is set by the balance between face bending stiffness and core stiffness, and is typically a few times the face thickness, which may be smaller or larger than a cell.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3 (Fatigue): The chapter says aluminium has a clear endurance limit. Aluminium alloys do not show a true endurance limit: their S-N curve keeps falling with cycles, so fatigue design must be at a specified life (e.g. 10⁶ cycles), not below an infinite-life stress. Steels, not aluminium, show a genuine endurance limit. The next sentence correctly denies an endurance limit for composites, which makes the aluminium claim look deliberate and misleading.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q8: The question and answer say high-strength steel has 'low specific strength'. The chapter's own table contradicts this: 300M gives σult/ρ = (1950 × 10⁶)/7850 ≈ 248 kN·m/kg, which is higher than 7075-T6 (203) and Ti-6Al-4V (214). Only CFRP beats it. So the premise of the question is factually wrong.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 (Titanium): Ti-6Al-4V is said to keep strength to roughly 400–500°C. The 500°C figure belongs to near-alpha titanium alloys (Ti-6242, IMI 834 class), not to the general-purpose Ti-6Al-4V, whose long-term service limit is usually quoted nearer 300–400°C.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q5: The question asks for two alloys and one use each, but the model answer lists three (2024, 7075, 6061). A student answering 'two' as asked would be marked against a three-item answer.
- ch 5 [low] Lead paragraph: The lead defines specific strength as σ/ρ (generic stress), while Section 1 and the formula box correctly define it as σult/ρ. A student skimming the lead may use yield or working stress.

- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q3: Q3 is the same problem as Worked example 2 with the same numbers (3 rivets, 5 mm, 180 MPa, 10.6 kN). Students can copy the worked answer instead of applying the method.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, inspection sentence: 'Fastener grip' is used without a definition, and a second-language reader on a phone has no way to guess it.
- ch 7 [low] Lead: The sentence 'The load factor n = L/W tells how many times the weight the lift reaches' is garbled and a student may not parse it.

- ch 7 [low] Section 1, bullet list, cabin pressure: 'internal pressure times fuselage area' is imprecise: on a frame the load is pressure × frame spacing × circumference, and on a bulkhead it is pressure × the area the bulkhead encloses. Taking the whole skin area gives a meaningless huge force.
- ch 7 [low] Figure, node 'b' note: The note '2.5 × 588.6 = 1.47 MN' gives 588.6 without a unit, while the result is in MN; a student copying the line cannot tell whether 588.6 is kN or N.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, last paragraph (gust boundary): The low-speed behaviour of the gust line is stated backwards. The gust line starts at n = 1 at V = 0 and rises linearly with V, while the stall line rises as V² from n = 0. So at V_S1 and just above it the gust line lies above the stall line, not inside it. A gust load factor in that band cannot be reached at all, because the wing stalls first; the stall line governs there. The figure in this same chapter shows exactly this (at V = 60 m/s the gust line is at n = 2.01 while the stall limit is n ≈ 1.10), so text and figure contradict each other.
- ch 8 [high] Figure (gust line series): The plotted gust line (60, 2.01) to (89, 2.5) lies above the stall line over its whole drawn length, and it meets n = 2.5 at V = 89 m/s while the stall line there allows only n = (89/57.14)² = 2.43. So the point where the gust line touches the flat top is outside the manoeuvre envelope: the aircraft cannot be at n = 2.5 at 89 m/s, because V_A is 90.4 m/s. A student reading the graph will place the gust corner below V_A, which is impossible.
- ch 8 [low] Figure (upper limit series) and Section 2: The top boundary is drawn flat at n = 2.5 to 120 m/s and then slopes straight down to n = 0 at V_D = 160 m/s. That implies the permitted load factor falls to zero at the dive speed. In practice the top stays at n_max up to V_C and then reduces to a lower value (2.0 for transport aircraft) at V_D, after which the envelope closes steeply to the negative limit. Section 2 also never says that the top is not flat all the way to V_D.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, gust formula: The formula given is the sharp-edged gust idealisation with no gust alleviation factor, but it is presented as the working design formula. Students will use it as the certification value, which it is not: the real increment is smaller because the aircraft is not a point mass and the lift build-up is unsteady.
- ch 8 [low] Quiz Q7: The answer stays vague ('can add a local load or a pitching load', 'can also trigger aeroelastic effects'). The standard reason is more definite: at V_D the aerodynamic forces are largest, and full deflection cases (control surface and tail loads) are separate design cases that can exceed the limit loads even when the aircraft load factor n stays below n_max.
- ch 9 [high] Section 3 (tail loads paragraph): It says the tail load makes a rear-fuselage bending moment that is 'largest near the tail attachment'. This is backwards. The tail load is applied at the tail, so the bending moment there is zero (or only T times the small height of the fin), and it grows linearly as you move forward, reaching its maximum at the wing attachment, which is exactly what the next sentence (M = T × l at the wing attachment) says. The two sentences contradict each other, and a student will draw the rear-fuselage bending moment diagram the wrong way round.
- ch 9 [high] Quiz Q7: The answer states 'The rear-fuselage bending moment is largest near the tail attachment'. Wrong. For a load applied at the tail, the moment is greatest where the fuselage is furthest from the load, i.e. at the wing attachment (240 kN·m here), and falls to zero towards the tail.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2 (elliptic distribution sentence): Minimum induced drag for an elliptic distribution holds for a given span as well as a given total lift. Without the span, the statement is incomplete.
- ch 9 [low] Figure caption and y-axis: Shear (kN) and moment (kN·m) are plotted on one axis and the caption calls the moment 'the larger quantity'. kN and kN·m are different quantities, so they cannot be compared as bigger or smaller. Students may read it as a physical statement.
- ch 9 [low] Lead and Section 1: 'Both are zero at the free tip and largest at the root' is safe for moment but not always for shear. A concentrated mass on the wing (an engine, a store, a landing gear) makes the net load w(x) locally very negative, so the shear diagram jumps downward there and the root shear need not be the largest value on the span.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, last paragraph of the fuel discussion: 'check the fuel cases your design cases require' repeats 'cases' and reads awkwardly for a second-language reader.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3 (tail load formula): In 'a tail load T at arm l from the wing attachment', the arm l is not defined with a unit, unlike the other symbols in the chapter.

The cabin gauge reading IS the differential pressure, so "the differential is much larger than the cabin gauge reading alone" cannot be true. The point being made is that the differential is much larger than it would be at sea level, or much larger than the outside ambient pressure.
