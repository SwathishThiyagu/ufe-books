## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 3, 'Line or edge' route: One edge gives only the line spread function, which is the PSF integrated in the direction perpendicular to the edge. Saying 'for a separable blur this gives h' hides that a second edge (or another measurement) in the perpendicular direction is needed to get the other factor of h. A student will think one edge is enough.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, step 3: The stated reason 'they sum to zero when consecutive angles differ by π/2' does not produce u = 128. At u = 128 the four vectors alternate 1, −1, 1, −1, so their spacing is π, not π/2. The equation 2πu/256 = 2πk/4 happens to give u = 128 anyway, but the sentence and the equation do not say the same thing, so a student following the sentence would find only u = 64 and 192.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1 (last paragraph of the model part) and Section 3 opening: 'If H is not known or cannot be estimated, the model gives you nothing' and 'Every restoration filter needs h or H as input' contradict the 'Beyond' note on blind deconvolution, which restores without a known H by estimating it jointly with f.
- ch 1 [low] Lead paragraph: CT is listed as working from this PSF-plus-additive-noise convolution model. CT reconstruction comes from a different forward model, line integrals of the object along rays (the Radon transform), not from blurring by a point spread function.
- ch 2 [high] Worked example 1, step 7 and answer (same assumption in Section 3, bullet 'For a Gaussian, report µ̂ and σ̂'): The patch mean of 12 grey levels is reported as the mean of the noise. Under the chapter's own model g = f + n, the average of a flat patch estimates f + µ, so 12 is the flat grey level (plus the noise mean), not µ. A noise distribution with mean 12 grey levels added to the image would put the true level near 0, which contradicts readings of 8 to 16. A student will carry away the idea that the noise mean is the patch brightness.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1 (patch size) versus Section 3, first bullet: Section 3 tells the reader to pick a region of at least 20×20 pixels, but the worked example estimates the density from a 3×3 patch of 9 pixels. The reader may think a 3×3 patch is normal practice.
- ch 2 [low] Figure caption and spec (Laplace series): The figure plots a Laplace density, but the chapter never defines Laplace and the table does not list it, so a student cannot tell what curve 'Laplace' is or why it is there.
- ch 2 [low] Beyond paragraph: BM3D is described as a 'modern learned denoiser'. BM3D is a patch-matching and collaborative-filtering method with fixed transforms and a hard/wiener thresholding rule; it is not learned from training data.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, order-statistic filters: The alpha-trimmed mean says d = MN−1 gives the median. That is only true when MN is odd, so exactly one value is left after trimming. For even MN two values remain and you would average them. The chapter gives the median rule only 'for odd MN' but does not repeat the condition here, so a student may apply MN−1 to an even window and expect a single middle value.
- ch 3 [low] Figure, 'outputs' row: The outputs row holds the three numbers 37.8, 11 and 132, but only the first cell has a pointer labelling it 'mean'. The cells for the median and the midpoint are unlabelled, so a reader scanning the figure cannot tell which number is which.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, adaptive median filter: z_min, z_med and z_max are defined as 'the smallest, median and largest values' without saying of what. In this filter they are recomputed from the current window each time the window grows, not taken once from the image or from the first 3×3 window. A student can read them as fixed values for the whole run.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, mean filters: The geometric mean is given as a formula but with no statement of what it is for. The other means in the section each get a use or a limitation (arithmetic blurs edges, harmonic suits salt, contraharmonic sign rule), so the geometric mean looks like a definition to memorise with no purpose.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, Butterworth bullet: The exponent is typed inline as "(D₀/Dk)2n". On a phone this reads as the ratio multiplied by 2n, which is not the Butterworth notch factor; the order n must appear as the exponent, as it does in the other two formulas where superscripts are used.
- ch 4 [low] Figure (Notch filtering block chain): The chain runs Noisy image → DFT → Log spectrum → Notch mask → Inverse DFT. Read as a data flow, it says the mask multiplies the log spectrum, which is exactly what the text forbids ("Do not multiply the log by H; multiply the complex F"). A student following the diagram will apply H to log(1+|F|) and get a wrong result.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps and Quiz Q6 (notch at the origin): "The image turns mid-grey" is loose. After the DC term is removed the spatial data have zero mean and contain negative values; on a plain 0–255 display those negatives are clipped to 0, so the picture goes mostly dark, not mid-grey. Mid-grey appears only if the zero-mean data are rescaled or offset by 128 for viewing.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, first sentence: The mean square error is written E[(F − F̂)²], but F and F̂ are complex spectra. Squaring a complex error is not a real error measure and leads to wrong algebra.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, no-noise limit: After setting Sn = 0, the chapter says that at H = 0, H* = 0, so the gain is 0. With Sn = 0 the Wiener bracket is 0/0 at H = 0, not 0. The zero-gain statement is valid only when the noise-to-signal term K = Sn/Sf is positive.
- ch 5 [high] Figure: The inverse-filter series omits the samples nearest the zeros of H (for example x ≈ 12.8125, 38.0125 and 63.2125). With y max 20, the plotted curve never shows the claimed blow-up; it peaks only near 19.7 and looks smooth across the first zero.
- ch 6 [high] Section 3, first bullet of the digital FBP recipe: The angular step is given as 'Δθ of about 1/N radians'. That is twice too fine and contradicts the chapter's own quiz Q6, which uses (π/2)N views over 180°, giving Δθ = 2/N radians. A student following the bullet gets 1/N = 0.0039 rad = 0.22° for N = 256 (about 804 views), not the 0.45° the quiz computes.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, first bullet ('Skipping the ramp filter'): It says unfiltered back-projection gives 'correct total, wrong pixels'. That is false and contradicts worked example 1, where the back-projection total is 10 against a true total of 2. Plain back-projection does not preserve the total; the 1/r spreading both smears the values and inflates them (in the continuous case the total even diverges).
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, fourth bullet (the ramp): Ram-Lak is listed among the windows ('It must be windowed (Ram-Lak, Shepp-Logan, Hamming)'), but Section 3 defines Ram-Lak as the pure ramp with a sharp cutoff at ω_c, not a window. The list reads as if Ram-Lak were a smoothing window like the others.
- ch 6 [low] Lead paragraph, last sentence: "Here restoration means building the image from its projections, not repairing a blurred one." The word restoration names a different operation in this subject, so the sentence can confuse a reader who has just met image restoration as deblurring.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, SNR line: The variance symbols are written flat as σf2 and σe2, so a student reads sigma times f times 2 instead of sigma-squared of the signal and of the error. This is the only formula in the section without the sub/sup tags used elsewhere.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q7 answer: MAX is squared in the formula but written flat as MAX2, which reads as MAX times 2 and gives the wrong PSNR if a student copies it into an exam.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The reason given, that a code averaging below H would 'leave some symbols indistinguishable', is not the standard argument. The real reason is that no uniquely decodable code can have an average length below H (source coding theorem, via the Kraft inequality); nothing about a short code makes symbols look alike.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, first trap: Saying 'not R = 75' clashes with the earlier sentence 'R = 0.75, so 75% of the original data was redundant'. Both look like the same statement to a student unless the unit is made explicit.
- ch 8 [high] Section 1, limits paragraph: The sentence "With two or more symbols, every code word has at least one bit, so L = 1 even when H is small" is false for three or more symbols. Only with exactly two symbols must both code words be 1 bit long, giving L = 1. With four symbols, for example, L can be 2 bits as in the worked example that follows, so a student reading this will expect L = 1 everywhere.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, and quiz Q5 answer: Saying a symbol with p = 0.9 "needs about 0.152 bits, not 1 bit" overstates it for a single symbol. 0.152 bits is the self-information of that symbol; arithmetic coding reaches that average cost only over a long message, and any real encoder must also spend bits to end the message.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, decoder paragraph: "Any number inside the final interval identifies the message" leaves out how the decoder knows the message has ended. Without a length or an end-of-message symbol, the decoder keeps mapping the number and emits extra symbols.
- ch 8 [low] Lead: The first sentence defines an entropy coder as one that gives short code words to likely symbols, then lumps run-length coding under "These coders". RLE does not shorten code words by probability; it replaces repeats by counts, and it can make a file bigger.
- ch 9 [high] Worked example 1, part (c) and steps 7–8: It quantises the flat block's pixel value 8 as if it were a DCT coefficient. In the chapter's N=2 orthonormal DCT, a flat block with pixel value 8 has DC coefficient F(0,0)=16, not 8. So 8/3→3 and decoded 9 is a coefficient comparison, not a grey-level reconstruction; the stated +1 grey-level error is wrong.
- ch 9 [high] Quiz Q4: The answer lists separability as a reason JPEG uses the DCT rather than the DFT. The DFT is also separable, so this is not a distinguishing reason; the quiz answer gives an invalid reason.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2, separability bullet: The bullet says separability costs '8 + 8 instead of 64 × 64 operations'. That is a wrong operation count. A 2-D separable DCT uses 16 one-dimensional 8-point transforms, each with many operations, not 16 operations; a naive direct 2-D DCT is about 64×64=4096 multiply-adds.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1 formula and Quiz Q1: The predictor formula sums over i only while j is described as the neighbour offset. Because j is not indexed by the summation, the formula is not well defined.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1 output: The output lists only two of the four DCT coefficients. F(1,0) and F(1,1) are missing, although part (a) asks for them and the answer says both are zero.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, video standards: It says every block is predicted from a motion-compensated region of an earlier frame. Intra-coded blocks are predicted spatially within the same frame, and B-frames can use reference frames later in display order.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, JPEG baseline: The baseline pipeline omits the DC coefficient's differential coding across blocks. Baseline JPEG codes DC as a difference from the previous block's DC, then Huffman-codes it; AC coefficients use zig-zag run-length coding.
