## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3, 2017/2021 bullet: 'SegWit (2017) ... fixes a signature-counting bug' names the wrong fix. SegWit's bug fix is transaction malleability: a third party could change a signature's encoding without invalidating the transaction, which changed the transaction id. SegWit also removed the quadratic time needed to hash a transaction with many signatures. 'Signature-counting bug' is not the standard term and teaches a wrong idea.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, 1990s bullet: The sentence lumps b-money with bit gold as 'puzzle-minted money on timestamped records'. b-money (1998) proposed work-minted money recorded in a ledger that every participant keeps; the timestamped, chained records are bit gold's idea, and bit gold's write-up is usually dated to the early 2000s, not the 1990s.
- ch 1 [low] Figure: The caption says 'Two decades of e-cash proposals', but the Ideas bar runs from 1980 to 2008, which is about twenty-eight years. The bar is also labelled '1980s' although it extends to 2008, so a student reading the chart thinks the 1980s lasted until 2008.
- ch 1 [low] Section 4: The five bullets (users, exchanges, miners, banks, government) begin with no sentence saying what they are a list of, so the reader has to guess that these are competing interests of the parties involved.
- ch 2 [high] Exam traps, bullet 1: The claim that writing the fee as a third output makes the transaction 'pay the miner twice or is invalid' is wrong. Inputs are fixed at 1.0 BTC in the worked example; if you add a 0.0001 BTC third output, the outputs now total 1.0 BTC, so the implied fee becomes zero. The transaction is still valid, but the miner receives nothing extra — the mistake is that the payer loses the fee amount to an output instead of leaving it as the difference.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, SegWit paragraph: 'Old nodes can still validate the base transaction' can mislead. Pre-SegWit nodes do not check the witness signatures at all; they see the witness program as an anyone-can-spend output, so they accept the transaction without really validating the spending condition.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q4: The question asks two things — why the coinbase is always first and why it is special — but the answer only covers what it does and the one-per-block rule. The reason it must be first (consensus identifies the coinbase by its position as the first transaction in the list) is missing.
- ch 3 [high] Section 4, witness scripts: The P2WPKH locking script is given as just OP_0. That is only the version byte of the witness program. On its own it is not a P2WPKH output and does not commit to any key.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, P2SH: The P2SH locking script is written as 'OP_HASH160  OP_EQUAL'. The pushed constant has been dropped, so the script shown is incomplete and would not run.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q5: The script in the question reads 'OP_DUP OP_HASH160  OP_EQUALVERIFY OP_CHECKSIG' with the hash constant missing, yet the answer mentions 'After pushing h'. The student cannot see which step pushes h.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q6: A bare multisig has no redeem script; the redeem script belongs to P2SH multisig. Asking what scriptSig pushes 'before the redeem data' mixes the two patterns and can confuse the student about which one is being spent.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q7: The answer contradicts Section 2 and itself: it first says a stale block's coinbase pays nothing, then adds that 'any coins that do arrive are locked for 100 blocks'. In Bitcoin a stale block's coinbase output is never part of the longest chain, so no coins 'arrive' to be locked. A student will read this as 'stale coins come but are frozen', which is the opposite of the intended point.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, expected-profit formula: C is defined only as 'electricity plus hardware cost, in rupees', with no time base, so the subtraction is not consistent with the per-attempt expectation (S + F) × (h ÷ H). Units are stated but the period is not.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, selfish mining: The threshold is presented as a single number. The original analysis gives about one third; later refinements show it can drop to about one quarter when other miners build on the attacker's block. Stating only 'roughly a third' without the caveat can mislead in a 6-mark answer.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, the E[Tk] formula, the confirmation table and Worked example steps 5-7: The chapter defines k confirmations as k blocks built after the block that holds the transaction, but then gives the mean wait as k × 10 min measured from the moment the customer pays. Under the chapter's own definition there is one extra block first (the block that includes the transaction), so from broadcast the mean wait is about (k+1) × 10 min, not k × 10 min. A student will use 10 min and 60 min in both senses without noticing the mismatch.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: 'it can reverse or censor its own transactions' reads as if a 51% attacker can only censor its own transactions. The section text correctly says the attacker 'can censor transactions' in general, so the trap contradicts the body and understates the power.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, closing paragraph: 'One confirmation removes the simple race and Finney cases for a non-miner' is ambiguous: 'for a non-miner' can be read as describing the attacker, and a Finney attack is by definition done by a miner.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q2: The chapter says there are three common address forms and that native SegWit addresses start with bc1. Since the Taproot upgrade, bc1p addresses (bech32m) are also common, and bc1 alone hides the difference between bc1q (P2WPKH/P2WSH) and bc1p (P2TR). A student who sees a bc1p address in a wallet will be confused.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, P2SH bullet: Calling P2SH 'the usual home of multisignature' is dated. Most new multisignature wallets use P2WSH (bc1q...) or Taproot, because those pay lower fees. P2SH is the classic form, not the current usual one.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, BIP39 bullet: '12 or 24 words' is incomplete: BIP39 defines 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24 words, and the wordlist is a fixed list of 2048 words in which each word is identified by its first four letters. Students may think any English words work.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 bullet 'The same words always regenerate the same keys' and the figure note: This is only true when the optional BIP39 passphrase (sometimes called the 25th word) is also the same. With a different passphrase the same words give a completely different tree, so a student could restore a phrase into a wallet and conclude the backup is broken.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, UTXO selection rules: 'smallest first to clear dust' mixes two ideas. Smallest-first consolidates many small UTXOs into fewer larger ones; dust means an output too small to be worth spending at current fees, so spending it does not 'clear' it economically.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, step 4 of the spend steps: 'Sign every input with the key for its address' is only right for single-key addresses. A P2SH or P2WSH multisignature input needs several signatures, and usually the redeem script or witness script, before it is valid.
- ch 6 [low] Lead, Section 1 and Quiz Q1: 'UTXOs locked to addresses' is loose. An output is locked to an output script; the address is only a human-readable encoding of that script. The statement is fine as shorthand, but in a chapter that defines every term a student may think the address itself does the locking.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, merged mining step list: The direction of merged mining is reversed. Namecoin is the auxiliary (child) chain and Bitcoin is the parent chain: the hash of the Namecoin block is committed inside the Bitcoin coinbase transaction, not the other way round. As written, the miner is asked to put the hash of a Bitcoin block B into a Namecoin header, but B's hash does not exist until B's nonce is found, so the stated steps cannot be carried out. Saying 'submit the same proof to Namecoin' also hides that the proof is the Bitcoin block header plus its coinbase.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, paragraph on transaction forms: 'Three transaction forms exist: transparent to transparent, transparent to shielded, and shielded to shielded' is wrong. Zcash has four forms; shielded to transparent (z to t) is missing, and it is exactly the form students confuse with full privacy.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, step 4 and Quiz Q3 answer: The ledger records only ZEC amounts; a rupee value never appears on chain. Saying 'the public sees ... the ₹6,000 equivalent' can make a student think the rupee figure is part of the transaction data.
- ch 8 [high] Quiz Q4 and its answer: The scenario says an exchange 'co-spends withdrawals from many customers in one transaction'. Withdrawals are outputs: the inputs in such a batch are the exchange's own hot-wallet coins, so clustering them correctly gives one owner (the exchange) and does not falsely merge customers. The described false cluster only happens the other way round, when the exchange sweeps many customers' deposit addresses together as inputs.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, CoinJoin paragraph: Calling CoinJoin simply 'trustless' hides a real point: most implementations use a coordinator. The coordinator cannot take the coins, but in unblinded designs it can learn which output belongs to which participant.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, second paragraph and Worked example 1: The 0.8 + 0.3 = 1.1 BTC example is written twice with identical numbers and nearly identical sentences, once in the body and again as the worked example.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, first line of the second paragraph: 'Two habits reduce privacy' then lists address reuse (a habit) and co-spending (an action in a single transaction, not a habit). The wording makes students think both are user habits.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, blocks, weight and fees: 'Users bid fees per virtual byte' is followed by 'Miners pick high-fee transactions first'. A student can read this as the largest absolute fee, which is wrong: a big transaction can pay a high total fee but a lower fee per virtual byte and be picked later.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, bullet on limited scripting: 'Bitcoin scripts are deliberately small' is unclear. Bitcoin's Script is limited in what it can express, not small in size; the usual points are no loops and not Turing-complete.
