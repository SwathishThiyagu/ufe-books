## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1, formula box and following sentence: The comparison sign is missing: the text reads 'If m  2j − 3 it has extra members and is statically indeterminate.' As printed the condition is incomplete and a student cannot tell which side of the count makes a truss indeterminate.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, after the m = 2j − 3 statement: Section 1 names only the over-count case. The under-count case (m < 2j − 3, a mechanism, the count used later in Quiz Q4) is never stated, so the word 'mechanism' appears in the quiz without a definition earlier.
- ch 2 [low] worked example, step 7 (check at joint C): Joint C also carries member CF, but the joint check lists only BC, CE and the roller. A student repeating the check at C finds a vertical term (CF) unaccounted for, and the claim that the forces 'balance in both directions' is only true because CF happens to be zero.
- ch 2 [low] exam traps, fifth item: The item contradicts the rule given in Section 2. Section 2 tells the student to take moments about the point where two unknown cut members meet; the trap then says that if two unknowns pass through the moment point 'the equation cannot find them', which reads like a warning against the recommended trick. Only the first clause (a point on all three unknown lines) is a genuine trap.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, units paragraph: 'lengths may stay in metres because they cancel in the angle ratios' is misleading. Lengths also appear as moment arms, where they do not cancel against an angle ratio; they combine with forces to give moments in kN·m.
- ch 3 [high] Worked example 1, step 2: The step says the 12 kN load and the link force at B have a resultant that 'is not along AB'. With the numbers of this very problem the link force on B is (−9, +12) kN, so the resultant with the (0, −12) kN load is (−9, 0) kN — exactly along AB. The stated reason for AB not being a two-force member is therefore false, though the conclusion (analyse AB as its own free body) is right.
- ch 3 [high] Worked example 1, step 8: Step 8 says the reaction at A 'does not point along AB's own axis'. AB is horizontal and the reaction came out as 9 kN horizontal, so it does lie along AB. The sentence contradicts the arithmetic two lines above and will confuse a student. The chapter's own three-force rule (Section 3) also proves the reaction must lie along AB: the 12 kN load and the link force both pass through B, so the third force, the reaction at A, must pass through B too.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps, third trap: 'Assuming a pin reaction is vertical, or lies along the member. Here A_y comes out exactly zero; a wrong assumption would have hidden that.' The two wrong assumptions are not equal: assuming the reaction lies along the member gives the correct answer in this problem, so nothing is exposed. Only the 'vertical reaction' assumption fails (it makes ΣFx impossible).
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 2 and quiz Q3: Example 2 is Example 1 rescaled, and quiz Q3 repeats Example 2 number for number; both examples again give A_y = 0. Every illustration in the chapter therefore shows a frame member with zero vertical reaction, so students never see the general case where the pin reaction has two nonzero components.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, third bullet (kinetic friction): The sentence is broken: "Usually μk s." The comparison symbol and the second symbol are lost, so the key fact that kinetic friction is smaller than limiting static friction never reaches the student, even though the rest of the chapter relies on it.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3, fourth bullet (compare P with Fmax): The static condition is garbled: "If P max, the body is static and F = P." The inequality sign and Fmax are missing, so the rule that decides every friction problem is unreadable.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q6 answer: The inequality is broken: "Unsafe. cos θ  N." The relation N = W cos θ < W is lost, so the reason the answer is unsafe (W overestimates N, hence overestimates Fmax) is not stated.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The wording is muddled ("surface peaks and bonds are broken or ride over each other less") and the claim that true contact area is lower during sliding is not what the standard explanation says; true contact area is close to W/H in both states.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, angle of repose paragraph: The sentence "If θ  φ, it slides unless P is applied" has lost its comparison sign, so the key condition is unreadable and a student cannot tell whether it means θ > φ or θ < φ.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, start of the P derivation: The equilibrium P + f = W sin θ only holds when P acts up the plane, but this direction is never stated, so the minus sign in P_min looks arbitrary.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 and Section 3, the P_min ≤ P ≤ P_max statement: When θ < φ (that is μ > tan θ), P_min = W sin θ − μW cos θ comes out negative. The claim "any P between P_min and P_max keeps the block in equilibrium" then suggests a negative force, which confuses students.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, final lines: The symbol φ appears in "Hence φ = tan⁻¹ μ" without being named as the angle of repose in that sentence.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, third paragraph: "The normal reaction is smaller than the weight because the slope tilts the block away from vertical" is vague; the real reason is that N is only the component of W perpendicular to the plane.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, formula for P: The sign of the N₁ sin α term is wrong. The block presses on the wedge's slope and its normal has a horizontal component N₁ sin α that pushes the wedge back out, so it adds to the push, not subtracts from it. With the minus sign, the load would help drive the wedge in, and the answer for P comes out far too small (115 N instead of about 403 N).
- ch 6 [high] Worked example 1, steps 7, 8 and answer: Step 7 writes P + N₁ sin 15° = f₁ cos 15° + f₂, which puts the sloping normal on the wrong side. Step 8 and the answer (P = 115.38 N) follow from that wrong equation. The correct push for α = 15°, μ = 0.25, W = 500 N is about 402.6 N.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q4, answer: The wedge horizontal equation given repeats the sign error: P = 0.25N₁ cos 15° + 0.25N₂ − N₁ sin 15°. The normal from the block on the sloping face adds to the push.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q5, answer: The answer keeps the raising-state values N₁ = 554.80 N and f₁ = 138.70 N, but with P removed the block tends to slide down the slope, so the friction on it reverses and the block vertical equation changes. Those numbers no longer satisfy N₁ cos 15° + f₁ sin 15° = 500 N, so the 9.62 N figure does not come from the removal state. The conclusion (self-locking) is still correct.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, self-locking test: The test paragraph says to set P = 0 in the wedge equilibrium equations but does not say that the friction senses must also reverse at the same time. A student who keeps the raising arrows gets a negative floor friction, finds no possible equilibrium and wrongly concludes the wedge is never self-locking.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1, given data: The example never says the wedge's own weight is neglected, yet the solution uses N₂ = W, which is only true if the wedge weight is ignored.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, bullet after the two minimum formulae: 'Raising the foot lowers cot θ and always helps' is not true as written. Moving the foot away from the wall, or lifting it onto a kerb or step so the ladder lies shallower, makes θ smaller, so cot θ grows and the required friction rises — the ladder gets worse, not better. Only making the ladder steeper (larger θ) helps, and the sentence never says that.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, first paragraph and free-body table: The letter P is used twice: once for the point where the person stands and once for the person's weight in newtons. A student can lose track of which P is 600 N and which P is a point on the ladder.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, 'Example data' bullets: The numbers 100, 600, 3/5, 0.3 and 800 are worked before the ladder they belong to is described; the 5 m, 200 N, 600 N, 3 m, μ = 0.3 data appear only in Worked example 1, which comes after. A reader meeting Section 3 first has no source for these numbers.
- ch 7 [low] Beyond, ladder safety rule: A foot set one quarter of the height from the wall gives tan θ = 4, so θ = 76°, not 75°. Saying 'roughly 75°' is loose for a book that has just stressed exact angles.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q2: The question does not say the ladder length stays the same, so a student may picture a longer ladder reaching the same point, in which case the foot moving out need not lower θ.
- ch 8 [high] Beyond section: The sentence lumps V-ribbed belts and toothed belts together and says both 'combine a large friction ratio with low slip'. A toothed (timing) belt does not work by friction at all: it drives by positive engagement of teeth with pulley grooves, so friction ratio ideas do not apply to it.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1 and Section 3, step 1: The formula T_tight/T_slack = e^{μθ} is presented as if it always holds. It is the limiting (maximum) ratio, reached only when the belt is on the point of slipping; a lightly loaded belt has a ratio smaller than e^{μθ}. The worked example quietly assumes impending slip. A student can read this as an equality for every running belt.
- ch 8 [low] Exam traps, fourth bullet: The bullet says 'Using β in degrees inside sin' is a mistake and advises 'use radians'. A sine of an angle can be taken in degrees; entering 20° while the calculator sits in radian mode is the actual error, and the more common slip is entering the full 40° instead of 20°.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3 and worked example steps 2 and 5: The exponents are written without superscript: 'e0.6283', 'e1.837', and worst 'e0.6283/0.3420', which can be read as (e^0.6283)/0.3420 = 5.48 instead of e^(0.6283/0.3420) = 6.279.
- ch 9 [high] Section 1 (paragraph after the tan α formula): The sentence "Putting the diameter d in place of r halves the torque and doubles the angle" is backwards and numerically wrong. With d = 2r, T = W d tan(α + φ) doubles the torque, and tan α = L/(2πd) is half of L/(2πr), so the lead angle is roughly halved, not doubled. A student who remembers this will get the sign of the error wrong in every numerical problem.
- ch 9 [high] Exam traps, first trap: "A double-start thread with 3 mm pitch has L = 6 mm, and using 3 mm halves the lead angle and the torque" — the torque claim is wrong. Taking 3 mm instead of 6 mm halves tan α (lead angle roughly halves: 1.82° becomes 0.91° for r = 25 mm), but the torque falls only slightly, by under 10% in a typical jack (tan(α + φ) goes 0.1827 → 0.166), because φ dominates the sum α + φ.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1 (step list): The step "Write the lead in metres: L = pitch × number of starts" mixes two operations. The product gives the lead in millimetres; the conversion to metres is a separate move, and a student may drop it.
