## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 2, fin spacing paragraph: The reason given for thin fins is backwards. Thin fins do not stop the metal from being the bottleneck; making a fin thinner increases its conduction resistance, so less heat reaches the fin tip. Fin thickness is kept small to save weight, cost and space while remaining thick enough to conduct heat to the tip; the real bottleneck is the air-side convection, not the metal.
- ch 1 [low] Lead: The lead says only about a quarter of the fuel energy reaches the crankshaft, but Section 1 then uses 30 percent brake power. A student may read the two numbers as contradicting each other.
- ch 1 [low] Lead: The lead says gensets use liquid cooling, while Section 2 lists small gensets and pump sets as typical air-cooled machines. Both are true, but stated absolutely they look inconsistent.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 1: The densities quoted are not standard steam-table values: water is about 992 kg/m³ at 40 °C and about 972 kg/m³ at 80 °C. 995 kg/m³ is closer to 30 °C and 977 kg/m³ to 75 °C. The method and arithmetic are correct, but a student who memorises these numbers will carry a small error forward.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: The trap 'A pressure cap raises the boiling point; it does not increase cooling capacity' sits awkwardly against Section 2, which says hotter coolant lets the radiator be smaller. A student will read the two as contradicting each other.
- ch 2 [low] Figure (thermosyphon loop): The blocks are laid out downwards with the jacket first, so the radiator appears below the jacket. The text and the aria label say hot water rises from the jacket to the radiator, so the picture contradicts the physics.
- ch 3 [low] Lead: "Friction takes a fixed slice of the power" says the friction share is constant. It is not: friction power is roughly a fixed fraction of indicated power only near full load, and at light load or idle it can be a very large share of indicated power (mechanical efficiency falls sharply). A student may write "friction is always 10-20 percent of IP".
- ch 3 [low] Section 1: "Two ratios summarise the loss" — the two quantities that follow are η_m (a true ratio) and fmep (a pressure in Pa or bar, not a ratio). The wording misdescribes fmep.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, fmep formula note: "N/120 is the number of cycles per second of a four-stroke engine" is true for one cylinder. In a multi-cylinder engine the whole engine completes (z × N/120) cycles per second, where z is the number of cylinders. The formula still gives the right answer only because V_d is the total swept volume; as written a student may think the two statements conflict.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, mist (petroil): "2 to 5 percent by volume" does not say percent of what. Students may read it as percent of the total fuel-air-oil charge, giving a wildly wrong oil quantity.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: "A four-stroke cannot use it, because petrol in the sump would wash oil off the cylinder wall" mixes two different effects and gives the wrong main reason. The real reason is that in a four-stroke the oil must be supplied from a separate sump, and petrol reaching the sump would thin and contaminate the sump oil. It also contradicts the exam trap, which gives dilution of sump oil as the reason.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q1: Section 1 says rubbing friction comes from four places (rings, skirt, bearings, valve train) and treats pumping as a separate loss, but Q1 asks for "four sources of friction power" and its answer groups rings with skirt and adds pumping as the fourth. The two counts do not match, which will confuse a student revising from the notes.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, last sentence: The closing line says additives change behaviour 'not its basic viscosity', but the same section has just explained that a VI improver raises hot viscosity — the very property a grade is built on. A student will read the two statements as contradictory.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, grade description (also Quiz Q2 answer): The W part of a grade is described only as a cold-cranking limit, and the 40 part only as a kinematic-viscosity band at 100 °C. A full SAE grade also fixes a low-temperature pumping (MRV) limit, a minimum kinematic viscosity at 100 °C for the W part, and a high-temperature high-shear (HTHS) minimum. Calling it a single cold-cranking limit understates the definition.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, worked example 1 and figure: The stated blower exit temperature of 340 K is physically impossible for adiabatic compression. For a pressure ratio of 1.6 from 300 K, the isentropic outlet temperature is 300 × 1.6^0.2857 ≈ 343 K, which is the minimum possible; a real blower (efficiency about 0.75) would give roughly 355 to 360 K. So 340 K implies an efficiency slightly above 100 percent.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, roots blower: A roots blower has two rotors, each carrying two or three lobes, not 'two meshing lobes'. The description also skips the key textbook point that a roots blower has no internal compression: it only displaces air, and the pressure rise happens when the air meets the higher pressure in the manifold.
- ch 5 [low] Section 4, fourth bullet (exhaust): 'Exhaust temperature rise' is not generally true. Exhaust mass flow rises with boost, but in CI engines the exhaust temperature usually falls because the excess-air ratio increases, and in SI engines it depends on fuel enrichment and charge cooling.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, closing sentences: 'The necessity is clear: more power density, altitude compensation and a smaller package. The limitations are also clear...' is vague and repeats the earlier bullets without adding information; a student may not see what 'necessity' refers to.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q6: The question asks for 'two effects of supercharging on SI engine knock', but the answer gives one effect (knock tendency rises) with two causes (end-gas pressure and temperature), which does not match the wording.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, heading and first line ('Anti-lag measures attack inertia and exhaust volume'): In standard automotive usage 'anti-lag' (ALS) means one specific system: retarded timing and extra fuel burnt late so the exhaust keeps the turbine spinning when the driver lifts off the throttle. Here the words are used for ordinary lag-reduction hardware (small housing, twin-scroll, light wheels, ball bearings), which a student may confuse with the two.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1, steps 1 to 3: The air flow is found using the full swept volume at ambient density, which silently assumes 100 percent volumetric efficiency and no boost. A student may take 0.060 kg/s as the real airflow of a boosted 2 litre engine, when a real naturally aspirated intake only traps about 80 to 90 percent of that, and a boosted one traps more.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, heat soak paragraph: The paragraph sits under a heading about matching, wastegate and anti-lag, but it is about shutdown care and oil coking. It reads as if heat soak is part of matching or anti-lag.
- ch 7 [low] Figure caption: The caption says the two curves 'meet near 1.3 bar at high speed', but the plotted data never meet: the fixed turbine peaks at 1.3 bar at 4000 rpm and drops to 1.2 bar at 5000 rpm, while the VGT stays above it (1.35 bar at 3000–4000 rpm, 1.3 bar at 5000 rpm). A student comparing text with plot will be confused.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, step 2 and Quiz Q3: The power 0.286 is written without a superscript: 'rp0.286', '1.60.286', '2.00.286 = 1.219'. Read on a phone this looks like a decimal number multiplied by 0.286 (1.60 × 0.286), which is a different quantity.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: 'It also raises exhaust temperature and flow for exhaust gas recirculation (EGR)' does not say what VGT actually does for EGR. The mechanism is that closing the vanes raises exhaust manifold pressure above the inlet manifold pressure, which pushes more exhaust gas through the EGR valve. 'Raises flow' could be read as raising the engine's exhaust mass flow, which it does not do.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, steps 3–5: The density ratios are computed as if pressure is unchanged from compressor outlet to intake manifold. A real charge-air cooler drops about 0.05–0.2 bar, so the 1.52 figure is optimistic. The assumption is never stated.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3 table, row 'Pistons, rings, gasket': 'Stronger, lower expansion' is unclear. Students will not know that 'lower expansion' means lower thermal expansion of the piston alloy, which allows tighter cold clearances and less piston slap at warm-up.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, fifth bullet: The wording 'not the ambient after the radiator' is confusing because the ambient air entering a front-mounted air-to-air cooler has not passed through any radiator.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, the trapping and scavenging efficiency formulas and the worked example step 2: The formulas give ηt and ηs as ratios of volumes, yet Vdel is stated to be measured at outside conditions while Vec is the cylinder volume at exhaust closure (inside the hot, higher-pressure cylinder). Strictly, trapping efficiency and scavenging efficiency are mass ratios, and volume ratios are valid only when all volumes are referred to the same pressure and temperature. The text does not say this, so a student may think volume ratios are exact here.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, first paragraph on cross scavenging: The paragraph explains the straight across path but leaves out the defining feature of cross scavenging: the piston crown has a deflector that turns the incoming charge upward so it loops over the burnt gas. Without mentioning it, a student may picture an unguided jet straight across the piston crown, which describes only the short-circuit path, not how the layout normally works.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3 comparison table, Uniflow row, 'Extra hardware' column: The table says uniflow needs 'blower and valve', but the text and quiz Q2 say uniflow exhaust can also be through ports uncovered by an opposed piston, with no valve at all. The table entry contradicts them.
- ch 8 [low] Beyond: 'Stratified charging' is a term used for direct-injection petrol engines, not for the small two-stroke charge-loss fix being described. The correct term for two-strokes (air-head or stratified scavenging) is different, and a student may look up the wrong idea.
