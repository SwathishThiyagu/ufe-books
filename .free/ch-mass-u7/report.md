## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 1, definition of Y: The sentence says Y is 'a pure number in kg water per kg dry air'. Calling it a pure number and then giving units contradicts itself and confuses students about whether Y is dimensionless.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, dew-point comfort example: The number is off: at 30 °C with 40 % RH, p_A = 1.70 kPa, and the saturation temperature at 1.70 kPa is about 15 °C, not 16 °C. Also a dew point of 15 °C is not high enough to stop sweat evaporating, so the example does not prove the 'sticky' claim.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, humid volume formula: The formula uses 22.414 m³/kmol, which is the molar volume at 101.3 kPa, so the result is correct only at that total pressure. At other pressures the volume is in error.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, percentage humidity paragraph: 'An examiner who writes RH when the calculation needs percentage humidity changes the answer' is confusing: the examiner sets the question, the student writes the answer.
- ch 1 [low] Figure, edge from node d to node e: The label 'add λ = 2500' suggests enthalpy is obtained by adding latent heat alone, leaving out the sensible term c_s T. A student following the arrows gets 23 kJ/kg instead of 63.9 kJ/kg.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q6 and its answer: The question asks for a case where the error is 'large', but the given case shows 18.9 % against 20 %, a gap of about 1 percentage point. The claim that the gap grows as the air dries is also misleading: RH and percentage humidity become equal again as p_A falls to zero, so the absolute gap peaks at intermediate humidity.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, bullet on reading properties from a point: "Read ... enthalpy along the local wet-bulb line" suggests enthalpy is constant along a wet-bulb line and can be read by scanning along it. The enthalpy line through the point is a separate line that only lies nearly along the wet-bulb line; along a constant wet-bulb line the enthalpy actually falls slightly. A student can misread this as the trap already warned about in a different form.
- ch 2 [low] Lead and Section 1: Wet-bulb temperature is listed as a property that the chart gives and is used throughout, but it is never defined anywhere in the chapter.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, step 3: The humid heat is taken as cs ≈ 1.04 kJ/(kg·K), but at Y = 0.009 kg/kg the formula in Section 1 gives cs = 1.00 + 1.88(0.009) ≈ 1.02. The slice 1.04 pushes the check to 0.0091, one unit in the last digit away from the stated 0.0092, so the reader sees a small mismatch inside the example.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q6, answer: The last sentence ('Further evaporative cooling would need unsaturated air to take more vapour, so T cannot fall below Tw') turns the reason around. The point is that at Tw the air is already saturated, so it can take no more vapour and no more latent heat can be drawn from it.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q3: The question gives Y = 0.010 kg/kg and then asks the student to 'estimate Y from the wet-bulb line and compare', which reads as if Y is unknown. The intent is to test whether Tw = 20 °C is the right wet-bulb temperature for the given state.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, closing sentence: 'In all methods, the driving force is the difference between the water vapour pressure at the water surface and the partial pressure of water vapour in the air' is false for steam injection, which is direct mixing of steam into the duct and has no water surface. Only the spray chamber and the wetted-surface humidifier work by evaporation from a surface.
- ch 4 [low] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 5: Step 3 computes Yout = 0.0142 kg/kg, then step 5 switches to 'the chart value Y = 0.014' and gets 3.4 kg/h. With the computed 0.0142 the condensate is 1000 × (0.0142 − 0.0106) = 3.6 kg/h. Two answers come out of the same example.
- ch 4 [low] Lead and figure: The humidity ratio Y appears in the lead and figure ('Y = 0.010') before it is defined. A student reading on a phone does not know whether it is kg water per kg dry air or per kg moist air.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, Compression: The sequence is muddled: 'Compress the moist air, cool it, and water condenses because the partial pressure of water vapour rises above its saturation value.' The rise of partial pressure happens during compression itself (saturation pressure depends only on temperature), and cooling afterwards only adds more condensation.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, Merkel equation formula: The integral is written KaV/L = ∫ dT/(h* − h) with h* and h in kJ/kg dry air and T in K (or °C). The water-side heat balance given two lines earlier is L c_p dT = G dh, so the water-side capacity rate c_p must appear: with enthalpies in kJ/kg and c_p = 4.187 kJ/(kg K), the plain integral gives units of K·kg/kJ and is not the dimensionless tower characteristic. The chapter also calls KaV/L dimensionless, which is only true with c_p included (or with h in kcal/kg, where c_p = 1 numerically).
- ch 5 [high] Worked example 1, steps 6 and answer: Step 6 computes only (10/6)(1/27.5 + 4/33.6 + 1/48.2) = 0.29 and ignores c_p. With the correct Merkel equation in kJ units the value is 4.187 × 0.29 = 1.23. The stated answer 'tower characteristic about 0.29' is therefore wrong by a factor of 4.187, and 0.29 is unrealistically small for a tower of this range.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, last paragraph: It says 'Higher air flow raises L/G'. L/G is water flow divided by dry-air flow, so more air lowers L/G, not raises it. This also contradicts Section 2, which correctly says more air per kg water lowers KaV/L.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q5: The stem asks what happens when L/G increases, but the answer describes 'more air per kg water', which is a decrease of L/G. The direction is also wrong: raising L/G (less air per kg water) raises the air enthalpy h at a given water temperature, lowers h* − h, raises the integral and so raises KaV/L. The answer inverts the physics and contradicts Section 2.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, four-point rule: The formula (Th − Tc)/4 Σ 1/(h* − h) is the Chebyshev four-point rule only when the four nodes are fixed; 'at the chosen nodes' leaves the student free to pick wrong points.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, HTU relation: HTU = L/(Ka) is dimensionally a volume, not a height, when L is the total water flow in kg/s and a is area per unit volume, because Z = HTU × NTU then returns V, not Z.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, tower types: The phrase 'mechanical-draught forced towers' mixes two names; the standard pair is forced draught and induced draught, both mechanical draught.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, hysteresis bullet: The conclusion drawn from hysteresis contradicts the fact stated in the same sentence. If the desorption curve lies above the adsorption curve, then at a fixed relative humidity the solid being dried (desorption) holds MORE water than the same solid taking up water (adsorption). A dried solid put back into humid air follows the lower adsorption branch, so it holds LESS water than it did while drying. The chapter says it holds more.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q4: The answer says rewetting traps water 'so at the same relative humidity the solid holds more water'. This reverses the isotherm: the wetting (adsorption) branch is the lower one, so the re-wetted solid holds less at a given relative humidity than it did during drying.
- ch 6 [low] Figure and its caption: The plotted curve is X* = 0.02·exp(0.04·RH). At RH = 40 % this gives 0.02·exp(1.6) = 0.0991 kg/kg, not the 0.10 kg/kg stated in the caption and used in the worked example, so the marker floats slightly above its own curve.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, equilibrium moisture paragraph: 'X* is zero for a perfectly non-hygroscopic solid such as clean sand at zero humidity' is misleading. At zero humidity every solid has X* = 0; the point about non-hygroscopic solids is that X* stays near zero at all humidities.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, mechanism of drying: The falling-rate period is described without naming the critical moisture content where it starts, or noting that it usually has two parts (a wet surface that dries patch by patch, then full internal control). Students meet Xc in the very next chapter.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, falling-rate time formula: The time formula does not match the rate law given just above it. With R = R_c(X − X*)/(X_c − X*), integrating (m_s/A)∫dX/R from X₂ to X_c gives a leading factor of (X_c − X*), not X_c. The X_c factor is only correct when X* = 0 (the line runs to the origin). As printed, the formula under-predicts or over-predicts the falling-rate time whenever X* ≠ 0, and this error flows into Worked example 1 and Quiz Q6.
- ch 7 [high] Worked example 1, steps 5 to 8 and answer: Steps 6 to 8 use the wrong prefactor X_c = 0.20 instead of (X_c − X*) = 0.15, so t_f = 7.32 h and the total 14.0 h are both wrong.
- ch 7 [high] Quiz Q6: The answer uses the prefactor X_c = 0.20 instead of (X_c − X*) = 0.16, so 5.82 h is wrong.
- ch 7 [low] Lead paragraph: "The area under 1/R between two moistures gives the drying time" drops the m_s/A factor, so read literally it gives the wrong number (and the wrong unit).
- ch 8 [high] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 6: The stated outlet humidity Y2 = 0.030 kg water/kg dry air is impossible for air entering at 60 °C with Y1 = 0.010. Air passing through a bed loses only sensible heat to evaporation, so its humid enthalpy stays roughly constant at 1.005(60) + 0.010(2501 + 1.86×60) ≈ 86 kJ/kg dry air. That limits the pickup to the adiabatic saturation point, about 27–28 °C with Y ≈ 0.023–0.024 kg/kg. Air at 35 °C and Y = 0.030 would carry about 112 kJ/kg, i.e. it would have gained heat that the bed does not supply. Step 6 also invents the 35 °C from nowhere, so the saturation check is done at a temperature the problem never gives and never justifies. The mass balance in steps 1–5 is correct, but the psychrometrics of the data are wrong.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, step 6: "the bed is not rewetting the solid" is confusing: the bed is the solid. It is the air that would condense water back onto the solid if it were saturated.
- ch 9 [high] Section 1, water-balance formula: The formula W = S[X1/(1-X1) - X2/(1-X2)] uses wet-basis fractions, but X1 and X2 are then defined as water-to-dry-solid ratios. Those definitions do not match. F and P in W = F x1 - P x2 are also undefined.
- ch 9 [high] Section 1, heat-duty equation and Worked example 2: Q is called heat supplied, but the equation contains only useful heat absorbed by solids and water. It omits heat carried away by exhaust air and shell losses. Worked example 2 then calls Q1/Q = 92.9% 'evaporation efficiency' and treats Q/0.90 as fuel duty, which contradicts the stated drying efficiency of 40-70% and the definition evaporation heat / heat supplied.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2, fan volumetric flow and Exam traps: The sentence 'Air expands as it cools and picks up water' is physically wrong. Cooling contracts gas; water pickup only slightly increases the humid volume. Using the hot inlet volume would usually oversize the fan, not undersize it.
- ch 9 [high] Section 3 and Exam traps, cocurrent efficiency: The text says cocurrent outlet gas leaves cool, so thermal efficiency is lower. A cooler exhaust would improve efficiency. In a cocurrent dryer the exhaust gas generally leaves warmer than in counter-current, because it leaves in contact with the already heated solids; that is why its efficiency is lower.
- ch 9 [high] Quiz Q8: The cause-and-fix direction for residence time is wrong. Too shallow a slope and too low an rpm would lengthen residence time, not shorten it. Raising slope or rpm would shorten residence time further.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, step 9 and answer: Step 9 says use L = 8 m, but the answer reports shell length 7.8 m.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, paste dryers: A spray dryer is not the usual choice for a paste; it needs a pumpable, atomizable feed. A paste is normally handled by a drum dryer or another paste dryer.
