## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3, second paragraph (PM from a frequency modulator): The reason given is wrong. It says a frequency modulator fed with dm/dt gives PM "because the phase becomes proportional to dm/dt". In a frequency modulator the phase is proportional to the integral of its input; the input is dm/dt, so the phase becomes proportional to m(t). That is why the result is PM.
- ch 1 [high] Exam traps, second bullet: The unit statement is wrong. Dropping the 2π from 2πkf∫m dt does not turn hertz into radians per second. kf·m has units of hertz and ∫dt of seconds, so kf∫m dt is a phase measured in cycles; the 2π converts cycles to radians. Without it the phase argument is 2π times too small, and the deviation computed from it is also 2π times too small.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: "The message can enter through φ(t) directly (PM) or through its derivative (FM)" is ambiguous. "Its" can be read as the derivative of the message, which would contradict the lead, where FM is tied to the integral of the message.
- ch 1 [low] Figure and its caption: The plotted quantity is not named precisely. The y axis says "deviation / Δf" and both curves stay within ±1, but a reader cannot tell whether the curves show phase deviation or instantaneous-frequency deviation. For a PM tone the phase deviation would be a cosine, while the plotted PM curve is a minus sine, so the figure is showing frequency deviation only.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3, Figure caption and figure aria: Section 3 correctly says Carson sits above 2Δf by 2f_m, and the caption correctly says the gap is 2f_m = 30 kHz. But the same paragraph then says the two estimates 'meet only as β grows', the caption says 'the two estimates meet only for large β', and the aria says 'the two curves merge once β is large'. The two curves never meet: Carson = 2Δf + 2f_m, so the separation stays exactly 30 kHz at every β. Only the relative gap (30 kHz divided by 2Δf) shrinks. A student will read 'meet' as the curves converging to one value, which contradicts both the formula and the plotted points.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, Bessel sidebands: 'The total power stays A_c²/2 because the Bessel squares sum to 1' gives a power value in V² with no resistance stated. A_c²/2 is the power only when the wave is delivered to a 1 Ω resistance; students often quote A_c²/2 as watts without knowing why.
- ch 3 [low] Beyond: The sentence says FM chips 'use a fractional-N phase-locked loop as the VCO'. A PLL is a feedback loop that controls and stabilises a VCO; it is not itself the VCO.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, FM/PM conversion paragraph: 'The same hardware can convert FM and PM' reads as if one signal is converted into the other. The paragraph is about generating FM or PM from the same modulator.
- ch 3 [low] Beyond, software-defined radio sentence: 'use the same multiplier-and-mixer arithmetic for upconversion' can mislead a student into thinking SDRs use Armstrong multiplier chains. Digital radios normally upconvert with an I-Q (quadrature) mixer driven by the numerically controlled oscillator.
- ch 4 [low] Lead: The claim 'Every FM broadcast receiver and every 10.7 MHz IF strip ends with one of these circuits' is too strong and contradicts the chapter's own Beyond paragraph: most modern receiver ICs use a quadrature detector, and many designs are DSP-based.
- ch 4 [low] Lead: 'yet every practical detector works on amplitude' is contradicted later by the polar discriminator, which works on phase samples and never forms an envelope.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 (balanced slope detector): The alignment cost is described as 'two primaries must be trimmed against each other'. In the usual balanced slope detector there is one primary tuned to f_c and two secondary (tank) circuits, one tuned above and one below f_c; those are what must be trimmed.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2 (linearity paragraph): 'Two linear laws multiplied give a linear detector' describes the wrong operation. The output is one linear law applied after the other (phase against frequency, then magnitude difference against phase), not a product of two linear functions.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 versus Exam traps (limiting claim): Section 3 says AM is 'squeezed out inside the detector, without a separate limiter stage', but the trap later says the ratio detector gives 'only modest limiting' and that one must not claim it needs no limiter. The two statements pull in opposite directions.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, last sentence: 'For NBFM the Foster-Seeley is the usual choice, since narrow deviation already gives a small output' reads as if a small output were a reason to choose that circuit; a student can misread the logic.
- ch 4 [low] Figure spec (node f): 'De-emphasis 75 µs' is only the American value. De-emphasis is region-dependent: 75 µs in the Americas, 50 µs in India and most of Europe and Asia.
- ch 5 [high] Section 1 (definition of PC), used in Section 2 and the worked example: PC is defined as the received carrier power, and the input CNR as PC/(N0fm). With that definition the AM derivation in Section 2 does not give the stated result. Dividing the AM output SNR (Ac²m²/2)/(2N0fm) by (Ac²/2)/(N0fm) gives FOM = m²/2, whose maximum at m = 1 is 0.5, not 1/3. The stated m²/(2+m²) only comes out if the denominator uses the total received power PT = (Ac²/2)(1 + m²/2), i.e. carrier plus sidebands. So the definition contradicts the derivation and the standard textbook value 1/3 (−4.77 dB).
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, sentence giving Sno(f): The text reads 'Sno(f) = N0f²/Ac² for |f| m to fm', which is garbled and will confuse a student about the band of integration.
- ch 5 [low] Lead, worked example 1, quiz Q6 ('same carrier power'): Saying the two schemes are compared at the same carrier power contradicts the corrected definition, in which the reference is the total received power. At equal carrier power, an AM signal at m = 1 actually carries 1.5 times the FM signal power, so the stated AM-versus-FM comparison would not hold.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2 and the figure data (actual series) versus the threshold definition in Section 1: The chapter defines the threshold as the input CNR where the output SNR is 1 dB below the above-threshold law. But the plotted 'actual' curve puts the output at 20 dB for 10 dB input, against an ideal 25.7 dB — a 5.7 dB shortfall, not 1 dB. So in the chapter's own numbers the knee is not at 10 dB; the curve has already broken away several dB above it. A student who applies the definition to the figure will get a threshold of roughly 11–12 dB, and the sentence 'Below roughly 10 dB they break away' contradicts the data point at 10 dB.
- ch 6 [low] Lead paragraph: 'the stronger one suppresses the weaker by a few dB' is vague: it mixes the size of the suppression with the capture ratio and can be read as saying the weaker signal is merely reduced a few dB, when in fact it is largely rejected once the capture ratio is exceeded.
- ch 7 [high] lead and section 2 table: The lead says 'Broadcast FM in India and Europe uses a 75 µs time constant' and the table puts 75 µs as the India/Europe broadcast value and 50 µs as 'some services and older systems'. This is backwards: 75 µs is the American (FCC) standard, while Europe and India follow the ITU/CCIR 50 µs value. A student will carry the wrong constant into exams and into any receiver alignment work.
- ch 7 [low] exam trap 1: Swapping the networks does not 'double the treble cut'. If de-emphasis sits in the transmitter and pre-emphasis in the receiver, the receiver boosts the treble and therefore boosts the discriminator noise; the signal response still multiplies to one, so the sound is not dull, it is bright and hissy with much worse SNR.
- ch 7 [low] quiz Q4: The estimate uses fm/f1 = 4.7, which is not 'much greater than f1'. The exact improvement factor (fm/f1)³ / [3((fm/f1) − arctan(fm/f1))] gives about 10.4 (≈10.2 dB) here, against 7.40 (8.69 dB) from the approximation — an error of roughly 1.5 dB, larger than the chapter's own caveat suggests.
- ch 7 [low] section 3, last sentence: After the stereo decoder the two signals present are left and right, not sum and difference. The sentence as written muddles where the sum (L+R) and difference (L−R) subcarrier exist.
- ch 8 [high] Section 3, receiver chain, sentence after the bullets: 'Because de-emphasis is a linear filter, putting it on each output after the matrix gives the same result as putting it once on the composite before decoding.': The claim is wrong. De-emphasis is a real filter with a cut above roughly 2 kHz. Put on the composite, it would act on the difference channel at 38 kHz ± f (23–53 kHz), where its response is a flat, heavy attenuation, not a treble cut. So L − R would come out flat and 20 dB or more weaker, while L + R is properly de-emphasised; the matrix would then give the wrong treble balance. It would also attenuate the 19 kHz pilot before the decoder can lock to it.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, receiver chain bullet: 'De-emphasis cuts the treble that pre-emphasis boosted, restoring the audio and the noise with it.': Read literally, this says de-emphasis restores the noise as well as the audio, which is the opposite of what it does. De-emphasis restores flat audio response and at the same time cuts much of the high-frequency noise, which is the main reason pre-emphasis is used.
- ch 8 [low] Quiz Q2, answer: 'the receiver would have to filter it out of the audio' is inaccurate. A transmitted 38 kHz carrier lies far above the 15 kHz audio band, so it would never be heard in the audio; a mono receiver's low-pass already removes it. The real penalties are wasted power in the carrier and the need to separate the carrier cleanly before detection.
