## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 3, masking paragraph: The rule 'an ion stops inside resist or oxide when Rp is less than the mask thickness' is too optimistic. Ions straggle around Rp, so a mask only a little thicker than Rp still lets a noticeable fraction through, and the tail shows up in the silicon.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, 'self-aligned' sentence: 'The same oxide window that defines a pattern therefore defines the doping, so the implant is self-aligned and needs no extra mask' misuses the term self-aligned. In CMOS, a self-aligned implant is one masked by the gate electrode itself; the oxide window is still a patterned mask. A student may conclude that implants never need masks.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q5: The problem calls 0.03 μm (30 nm) a gate oxide, but Section 1 states gate oxides are 'often only 2–10 nm'. The example contradicts the chapter's own number.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q5, constant B/A: B/A = 0.107 μm/h is not the standard Deal-Grove dry-oxygen value at 1000 °C; textbook tables give about 0.07 μm/h (A ≈ 0.165 μm, B ≈ 0.0117 μm²/h). The arithmetic in the answer is consistent with the number supplied, but the number itself looks wrong.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q6: 'How much longer must the oxidation run?' invites two readings. The answer gives the factor 6.25 for the total time, not the extra time, so a student who answers 5.25 times longer is not obviously wrong.
- ch 1 [low] Figure: The block diagram puts a diffusion drive-in and an ion implant in series inside one doping step. Real flows use them as alternatives (predeposition + drive-in, or implant + anneal); the caption's line that 'the same oxide window serves both the diffusion and the implant' reinforces a sequence no CMOS flow uses.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, Arrhenius formula key: D0 is described only as 'a material constant' with no units, while every other symbol in the chapter carries units. Students cannot check the exponential's dimensions.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, resolution formula vs formula box and worked example: R is defined twice with different meanings. The text calls R 'the smallest printable half-pitch in nm', but the formula box and the worked example call it 'the smallest printable feature'. A student cannot tell whether R is the width of one line (the feature) or half the line-plus-space pitch.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, contact and proximity printing: 'Both damage or blur the mask' is wrong for proximity printing. Contact printing damages the mask (and the resist). Proximity printing leaves a small gap, which blurs the printed image; the mask itself is not damaged or blurred.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q7 (and the matching line in Section 3): The answer credits epitaxy with the 'low-resistance buried collector path' and says the epi layer grows 'on the heavily doped substrate'. In BiCMOS the low-resistance path is the n+ buried layer formed in the p-substrate before growth; the epi layer itself is lightly doped and forms the collector. The substrate is lightly doped p-type, not heavily doped.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, value of k: 'About 0.5 to 0.8 for production' leaves out that production k falls well below 0.5 once resolution enhancement and immersion are used, so a student meeting advanced-node numbers will think they are wrong.
- ch 3 [low] Figure (CMOS mask sequence): The block diagram shows only 7 blocks (well, active, poly, n+ S/D, p+ S/D, contact, metal), while the worked example and quiz count 10 masks. The via and pad masks are hidden inside the note 'plus via, pad' on the metal block, so a student counting blocks gets a different number from the text.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: 'The gate oxide is thin and must not see those later high-temperature steps' is backwards: the well drive-in happens before the gate oxide, so those steps are earlier, not later. A student can read it as if some step after the gate oxide is being discussed.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, nMOS flow (n+ implant step and following paragraph): 'The poly gate is the mask, so source and drain align to it' is stated after the spacer has been formed. At that point the n+ edge is set by the poly gate plus the spacer width, not by the gate edge alone; the next paragraph says so, which makes the two sentences look inconsistent.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2 (back-end masks): A via is described as 'a hole between metal layers'. It is a hole cut in the oxide between two metal layers and then filled with metal; a bare hole would not connect anything.
- ch 3 [low] Sections 2 and 3 (cross-references): Chapter 2.4 and Unit 4 are named in plain text, so on a phone the reader cannot tap through to them.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3 (last lines) and Section 1 (last lines): The latch-up parasitic bipolar path is pointed to two different chapters. Section 1 says the parasitic devices are covered in Chapter 2.8, but Section 3 says 'The bipolar path itself is left to Chapter 2.5.' The parasitic npn/pnp path is exactly the latch-up mechanism of Chapter 2.8, so the student is sent to the wrong chapter.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3: 'the substrate tie to VSS is thick and low resistance' mixes up the tie contact with the substrate. A tie is a small p+ contact; it is the substrate that is thick and gives a low resistance path.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, table, twin-tub row: The table gives only 'p-substrate to VSS' for twin-tub. With a p-type starting wafer the p-well is part of the same p region and is also tied to VSS; without saying so, a student may think the p-well body is left floating or needs its own pad.
- ch 5 [high] Section 1, last paragraph (epitaxial trade-off): The trade-off is stated backwards for capacitance. Thin epitaxy lowers the collector series resistance Rc, but it does not lower the collector capacitance — it raises it. With a thin epi the base-collector depletion region reaches the n+ buried layer, so the base-collector capacitance Cjc rises, and the n-epi/p-substrate junction capacitance also rises. Thin epi also lowers breakdown, not only light doping.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q4: The answer repeats the wrong claim that thin epi lowers collector capacitance. A student who writes this loses marks in a process-technology paper.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, flow bullets and mask table (sinker position): The sinker is listed after the emitter implant. A deep n+ sinker must be diffused down several micrometres to touch the buried layer, and an implant alone cannot reach it. Placing it after the emitter leaves no high-temperature drive-in, and the text's own rule says every step after the base must stay cool.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q7: The thermal-budget point contradicts the flow order given in Section 2. There, the n+ and p+ source/drain anneal happens before the base implant, so the shallow base cannot restrict the source/drain anneal.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, formula block: The same symbol VT is used for the thermal voltage (26 mV) and for the MOSFET threshold voltage. The note that says so helps, but a student copying the formula can still read VT as 26 mV in the MOSFET expression.
- ch 5 [low] Figure (BiCMOS flow block diagram): The diagram has no sinker block, although the text and the mask table treat the sinker as one of the three or four extra masks.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, third bullet (higher beta): The sentence lumps two base changes together. A thinner base does not raise the base resistance; it raises β and lowers the punch-through/breakdown voltage. It is the lighter base doping that raises the base resistance.
- ch 6 [high] Section 1 (and the same-layer spacing sentence): The derivation of the same-layer spacing rule is technically wrong. It says two shapes on the same layer must be separated by 'more than twice the sideways etch plus Δ', where Δ is mask misalignment. Shapes on the same layer come from the same mask, so they shift together; misalignment between masks changes overlap and enclosure between different layers, not the gap between two shapes on one mask. The section also states that etch bias always makes gaps wider, which is only one sign of the bias; in the worst case each shape prints larger and the gap shrinks.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q3: The answer says 'Mask misalignment and sideways etch can close a narrow gap'. Misalignment cannot close a gap between two shapes on the same layer, because both shapes are printed by the same mask and shift together.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q7: 'A metal wire is 20 squares long' treats squares as a length unit; the number of squares is a count, not a length.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, and worked example steps 4 and 5: The stated rule cannot be followed on this gate. The rule says to find an Euler path on the nMOS graph from VSS to y and on the pMOS graph from VDD to y, but neither path exists for y = ¬(a·b + c). The nMOS graph (nodes VSS, y, n1, edges c:VSS–y, a:y–n1, b:n1–VSS) has every node of even degree, so any Euler trail is closed; the trail given in step 4 starts and ends at VSS. The pMOS graph (c:y–n2, a:n2–VDD, b:n2–VDD) has odd-degree nodes y and n2, so its Euler path must run from y to n2, which is why the step 5 walk ends at n2, not at VDD. A student who tries the stated rule will find no path and think the method is wrong.
- ch 7 [high] Section 1, paragraph 2: "There is no wire crossing in a stick diagram" is false as written. Metal lines routinely cross poly and diffusion with no contact, and that crossing is pure wiring, not a device. Only a poly-diffusion crossing always forms a transistor. A student will read this sentence as a ban on crossing metal over poly or diffusion, which would make routing impossible.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, first bullet: "Every line thickness becomes a drawn width" is misleading. A stick line has no meaningful thickness; the drawn width comes from the design rules, not from the sketch.
- ch 7 [low] Lead and Section 1 (layer list): The lead lists contact among the layers drawn as a line, then Section 1 says a contact is a dot. The two statements do not match.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2 and worked example steps 4 and 5: The same rail is called GND in the walks and VSS everywhere else, with no statement that they are the same node. A reader may think two different rails are involved.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, Section 3, and Quiz Q2: Chapter 2.6 is referred to in plain text, so on a phone the reader cannot jump to the design-rule chapter.
- ch 8 [high] Section 3, process choices paragraph: 'Retrograde wells have a low-resistance profile near the surface' gets the retrograde profile backwards. A retrograde well is made by a high-energy implant whose doping peak sits below the silicon surface, not at it; the concentration is comparatively low right at the surface.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, formula block: V_well = I_inj × R_well defines the well drop as coming from the injected current, but the current through R_well is the npn collector current (set by the npn once it turns on), not the trigger current itself. The text also never says which way the well potential moves: the well is pulled below VDD, and that is what forward-biases the pnp emitter-base junction.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, part (c) and answer: Part (c) asks whether the loop is regenerative 'once an emitter is on', but Section 2 correctly says both emitters must be forward biased before the loop closes. As worded, the question and answer suggest one junction is enough.
- ch 8 [low] Figure caption: The caption says the resistor values are 'the ones used in the worked example', but the worked example also uses R_sub = 500 Ω in part (b), while the figure shows only 200 Ω.
