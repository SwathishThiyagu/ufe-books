## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1, second paragraph (and the figure): The stated order for making the mould skips the cope completely. It goes: drag flask on board, pattern, ram, draw pattern, cut gates, then 'the cope is closed on the drag'. Nothing ever says the cope was rammed, so a student reads the cope as a ready-made cover. The sprue is also never formed, although the text says metal enters through a vertical sprue in the cope. The figure repeats the same gap: it jumps from 'Gating cut' to 'Cope closed'.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, first sentence: 'A sand mould is a refractory sand shape: it stays strong when hot' defines refractoriness wrongly. Refractoriness is resistance to fusing or softening at the temperature of the molten metal; it does not mean the mould keeps its strength when hot. The chapter's own table gives the correct meaning, so the two statements clash.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, green sand paragraph: 'some syllabi call it foundry sand' is misleading. Foundry sand is the general name for any sand a foundry uses; green sand is one kind of it (moist, clay-bonded sand). The sentence reads as if the two names mean the same thing.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3 formula and Quiz Q4: The formula L_pattern = L_casting × (1 + s) + m applies shrinkage only to the finished casting dimension and adds machining stock afterwards, so the stock itself is not shrunk. Physically the whole block of metal, casting plus machining stock, contracts, so the strict textbook result is a slightly larger pattern. In Q4 the difference is small (512.07 mm against 512 mm) but a student who writes the chapter's answer where the examiner expects shrinkage on the stock-included dimension loses marks, and the chapter itself insists the order must be stated.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, step 4 of the bench/floor sequence: The sprue pin is mentioned while the drag is being rammed, but the sprue is formed in the cope, which is rammed two steps later. A student following the list in order will look for a sprue pin in the drag that is not there.
- ch 3 [low] Figure, jolt-squeeze cycle: Node a is already labelled 'Mould box, sand filled', but the arrow from a to b is labelled 'fill'. Filling is shown twice and the arrow text does not match the step it leads to (jolt).
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q2: It asks for 'the two ramming actions used in moulding machines', but the same section also describes the sand slinger, which packs sand by the impact of thrown sand. A student can reasonably answer 'jolt and slinger' and lose marks.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, loam moulding paragraph and table row: 'there is no flask, so bells and very large gear moulds are made this way' gives the wrong reason. Loam moulds are used for bells and big gears because the mould is swept from loam on brickwork without a pattern, which suits one-off very large shapes; the absence of a flask is a detail, not the cause.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, the formula and definition line: The definition of k writes the exponent n as plain text: 'time per unit (V/A)n'. A student can read 'n' as a multiplier, and the unit of k is only min/cm² when n = 2, which the sentence does not tie back clearly.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, blind riser: The vent of a blind riser is described only as avoiding gas pressure. Its main jobs are to let mould gases out during filling and to keep the riser open to atmosphere, so that atmospheric pressure pushes metal down into the casting as it feeds.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, modulus definition: A is called the cooling surface area without saying that, for the riser, the area joined to the casting through the neck is not a cooling surface. Students then compute a riser modulus that is too small and under-size the riser.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, uses of Chvorinov's rule: 'places chills' is loose: the rule does not place anything. It gives freezing times that tell you where a chill or riser is needed.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, the ratio form of Chvorinov's rule: The exponent is written flat: t₂/t₁ = [(V/A)₂/(V/A)₁]n. A student can read the trailing 'n' as multiplication by n instead of a power, and this form is the one used all through the worked example and Q3 and Q7.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, second bullet: The text uses a caret, '(length ratio)^n'. Caret notation is not an inline superscript and looks like a typo on a phone screen.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, meaning of A: A is defined only as 'the heat-losing surface area'. Students often take the whole outer surface even for a riser or a casting joined to a gating system, where the contacting face does not lose heat to the mould.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, coke formula and Worked example 1: Coke mass = coke ratio × metal charge gives only the coke fed with the charge. The initial coke bed, burnt to white heat before charging starts, is a separate quantity and is not counted in the coke ratio, so 'the coke needed is 1.8 t' reads as the whole coke consumption of the shift.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, induction furnace: 'The frequency sets the heating depth and the amount of stirring' does not say in which direction, so a student cannot use the statement.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, electric arc furnace: 'The arc is adjusted by the electrical power' is circular and vague; it does not say what the operator actually changes.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, ladle treatment: Calling an inoculant an additive that 'controls graphite shape' is loose. In ductile iron the spheroidal shape comes mainly from magnesium or cerium treatment; inoculation mainly promotes nucleation of fine graphite and prevents chill and carbides.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3 and Exam traps, high superheat defects: 'Sand penetration' is not the standard name; the defect is metal entering the gaps between sand grains.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3 (pressure die casting), the sentence 'The die has two halves: one carries the cavity, the other the cover.': This mislabels the standard die-casting terms and suggests the cover half has no cavity. In standard texts the two halves are the fixed cover die half, which carries the sprue and runner, and the moving ejector die half, which carries the ejector pins; the cavity is split between the two halves. A student can read 'cover' as a plain lid.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, step 'Wait a few seconds for a 5-10 mm shell to cure.': A 5-10 mm shell is at or beyond the top of the usual range; standard texts quote roughly 3-6 mm, and a shell of that thickness needs the sand in contact for around 10-30 s, not 'a few seconds'.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2 versus the table row for Investment: The text says investment casting is 'usually small to medium size', but the table lists its size as 'Small'. The two statements disagree.
- ch 8 [high] Section 1: The sentence lists dye penetrant together with radiography and ultrasonic testing as methods that 'find internal defects'. Dye penetrant testing only reveals defects that reach the surface; it cannot see inside the casting. A student would learn the wrong capability for PT.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, table row for Porosity, remedy column: 'lower pouring gas' is not a foundry variable and reads as a typo. It is unclear what the student should change.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3: Gas porosity is placed in the freezing family, but the table gives its main cause as 'gas dissolved in metal or steam from sand'. Steam from sand is a sand cause, so the table and the grouping pull in two directions, and the figure shows only hot tear under freezing, not porosity.
