## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: The sentence 'Some syllabi call it a voltage-variable capacitor' repeats the definition already given in the previous sentence ('A varactor is a voltage-variable capacitor'), so it reads like a printing mistake and gives no new name.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: 'A small ratio gives fine control' can mislead: a small Cmax/Cmin means a narrow tuning band (low sensitivity, small Kvco), not better tuning resolution of the tank itself. Also the text does not say which bias limit gives Cmax.
- ch 1 [low] Figure caption and tree's capacitor branch: The caption says the figure puts the worked-example values on the capacitor branch, and the branch shows 'C = 1 pF'. But the worked example never computes a capacitor Q; the 1 pF there is the inductor's shunt capacitance Cp. A student may take the 1 pF as a separate MIM capacitor example.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, last line: 'MMIC passives are covered in Unit 4' points the reader elsewhere but gives no link, so on a phone the reader cannot jump to it.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1, first paragraph (noise density at 290 K): The noise density at T₀ = 290 K is stated as 4.14×10⁻²¹ W/Hz. That is the value of kT at 300 K, not 290 K. With k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K and T₀ = 290 K, kT₀ = 4.00×10⁻²¹ W/Hz, which is exactly the −174 dBm/Hz quoted in the same sentence and later in the −174 dBm = 4×10⁻²¹ W bullet. The 4.14 figure contradicts those numbers, and students who plug 4.14×10⁻²¹ into the sensitivity formula will not get −174 dBm.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q5 answer: The LNA-first case is numerically loose. The filter term is (F_filter − 1)/G_LNA = (2 − 1)/31.62 = 0.0316 in noise factor, not 'roughly 0.06', so it adds about 0.085 dB, not 0.06 dB. With those two stages F = 1.585 + 0.0316 = 1.617, giving NF = 2.09 dB, not 'near 2.2 dB'. The conclusion (amplifier first, gain ≈ 3 dB) is right, but the intermediate numbers are wrong.
- ch 3 [high] Section 1, formula, worked example 1 (steps 5 and 6), exam trap 4: P1dB,out is redefined as the straight-line (extrapolated) output at the compression input. In datasheets and standard texts the output 1 dB compression point is the measured output, which is already 1 dB below the straight-line value. So the formula P1dB,in = P1dB,out − G and the trap 'P1dB,in and P1dB,out differ by G' are off by 1 dB against every real datasheet a student will read.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, paragraph 1: 'The a3 term makes the gain fall as the signal amplitude rises' is not true for any a3. Compression needs a3 negative; a positive a3 gives gain expansion at low drive.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, SFDR derivation: The text says the IM3 output is set equal to the output-referred noise floor N + G, but the written equation is 3Pin − 2IIP3 = N, which drops G without showing it cancels.
- ch 3 [low] Figure (aria text): The aria text says input and output intercept values are marked, but the plot has only the ideal line and the measured points; no IIP3 or OIP3 marker exists.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: 'A lower IM3 number in dBc means cleaner performance' is ambiguous, because IM3 in dBc is normally a negative number (−40 dBc is cleaner than −30 dBc, yet −40 is the lower number).
- ch 4 [low] Section 2 (direct conversion), sentence 'There is no image at a non-zero IF, because the wanted signal is at DC': The wording is confusing and can be read as 'a zero-IF receiver has no image problem at all'. In a zero-IF receiver the image frequency is fRF itself (the mirror at negative frequency), so the image coincides with the wanted channel; this is exactly why quadrature (I and Q) downconversion and calibration are needed. A student may not connect this sentence to the later residual-image and I/Q mismatch discussion.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 (image-reject mixing), IRR definition: IRR is defined as 'ratio of wanted-signal power to image-signal power at the output'. That ratio equals IRR only when the wanted and image inputs arrive with equal power. The standard definition is the ratio of the conversion gain for the wanted frequency to the conversion gain for the image frequency.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 (superheterodyne), low-side LO formula: fimage = fRF − 2fIF is quoted without the condition that it must stay positive. A low-side LO needs fRF > 2fIF; otherwise the image falls at a negative frequency (there is no physical image at fLO − fIF) and the design must use a high-side LO instead.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 (Hartley architecture): The text says one path gets a 90° phase shift but does not give the sign. With the wrong sign the wanted signal cancels and the image adds — the opposite of what is wanted. Students frequently lose marks on this.
- ch 4 [low] Lead: 'It cannot filter sharply at 900 MHz or 2.4 GHz' is too absolute. Fixed SAW and BAW filters at 2 GHz are sharp and are used in phones. The real difficulty is a sharp filter that can be tuned from channel to channel across the band.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q8 answer: The answer says only that 'there is no non-zero IF where an image sits'. The sharper and more standard point is that with fIF = 0 the image frequency equals fRF, so the image cannot be separated from the wanted channel by any filter.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, EIRP formula and its term list (also Quiz Q1 answer): The formula subtracts L_feed but the term list defines P_TX as the power delivered at the antenna port. If P_TX is already at the antenna port, the feed loss has already been taken out, so subtracting L_feed again double-counts it. Worked example 1 does it the correct way (loss removed first, then add G_ant), so the formula and the example disagree. Q1 repeats the wrong definition.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: 'ACLR means adjacent-channel leakage ratio; some syllabi call it adjacent-channel leakage ratio' says the same thing twice. The alternative name students will meet is ACPR. As printed, the sentence teaches nothing and looks like a typo.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: '0 dBm + 25 dB = 25 dBm, not 0 dBm' gives a wrong answer that no student would produce from multiplying, so the warning is unclear. The mistake students actually make is writing 0 dBm × 25 dB, or treating the gain as dBm.
- ch 5 [low] Figure (Direct-conversion transmitter), compared with Section 1: The text puts a reconstruction filter after the DAC, a driver amplifier before the PA and a harmonic filter after the PA, but the block diagram shows DAC straight into the I/Q modulator and the PA straight into the duplexer. A student revising from the diagram alone will drop the harmonic filter, which is exactly the block that suppresses 2f_LO and 3f_LO.
- ch 6 [high] Section 1, noise-figure formula: The formula NF = NFmin + (4Rn/Z0)·|Γs − Γopt|²/((1 − |Γs|²)|1 + Γopt|²) is the standard expression for the linear noise <i>factor</i> F, not for the noise figure in dB. The chapter uses NF in dB everywhere else (1.5 dB in the example, 12 dB gain, etc.), so a student who substitutes a dB value into this equation gets a wrong answer.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, source degeneration: The text calls ωT = gm/Cgs 'the transit frequency' without saying it is an angular frequency in rad/s. Students will confuse it with fT = gm/(2πCgs) quoted in datasheets, and then get Rin off by 2π.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, design step: 'Pick Ls for 50 Ω and Lg for the channel' is unclear — 'the channel' has no defined meaning here.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, cascode noise: 'The cascode device adds little noise because the same bias current flows through both transistors' is loose. Equal current alone does not explain the small contribution; the cascode transistor's noise is small when referred to the input because it is divided by the gain of the common-source stage.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, device table, MOSFET row: 'Cheap, integrates with CMOS' is circular: a MOSFET is CMOS.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: 'Input-referred noise floor with NF = −104 dBm + 1.5 dB = −102.5 dBm' reads as if NF were an addition to a floor; the wording hides that the noise floor is being raised by the noise figure.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2: Saying passive diode mixers 'are linear' is too absolute and can stick in a student's mind. Passive mixers are <i>more</i> linear than active ones (higher IIP3, higher P1dB), but they still compress and generate spurs at high RF or LO levels — which the same paragraph itself admits by asking for linear mixer design in Section 3.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, formula box: The box defines f_IF as 'the selected difference in Hz for down-conversion' and then, in the same box, writes up-conversion as f_LO ± f_IF. A student can read this as f_IF being defined only for down-conversion, or as the RF being the difference of LO and IF in up-conversion.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, Figure, Quiz Q7: Class B drain efficiency is treated as falling as the power ratio 10^(-BO/10). For ideal Class B, DC current and efficiency fall with signal amplitude, so efficiency falls as 10^(-BO/20). At 6 dB back-off, Class B efficiency is about 78.5% x 0.501 = 39.3%, not 19.6%. The figure's Class B expression and points, and the Q7 answer, are wrong.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2: The Class A optimum-load formula Ropt is about VDD / Imax is wrong by about a factor of two if Imax is the peak current, because the text says current swings from near zero to Imax. For a choke-fed Class A stage with supply VDD, drain voltage swings from Vknee to about 2VDD - Vknee, so Ropt is about 2(VDD - Vknee) / Imax, or VDD / IDC using quiescent current IDC.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, Envelope tracking: The phrase 'keeps a small VDD - Vknee at all times' is confusing. ET makes VDD track the RF envelope plus headroom; the quantity kept small is the instantaneous voltage across the transistor, VDD(t) - Vout(t), not VDD - Vknee.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1, ring oscillator sentence: The sentence 'the loop oscillates when the total phase is 180° plus inversion' is muddled: it never states the total needed (360°) and 'plus inversion' is unclear to a first-time reader, who may think 180° alone is the condition.
- ch 9 [low] Figure, plotted phase-noise points: The plotted slopes do not match the Leeson slopes taught in the same section. VCO falls 20 dB from 1 kHz to 10 kHz but only 10 dB per decade from 10 kHz to 1 MHz, and the reference path also has 10 dB/decade segments. The text states the regions are 30 dB/decade (1/f³) and 20 dB/decade (1/f²), so a student sees a 10 dB/decade slope that the model does not predict.
- ch 9 [low] Exam traps, second bullet (20 log₁₀N): 'The 20 comes from voltage; 10 would be a power ratio without squaring' does not explain why a power-like quantity gives 20 log N, and can leave the student confused about why phase noise, measured in dBc, scales by 20.
