## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 2, paragraph 3: The text says one node can read another node's memory over the LAN. In a cluster each node is an independent computer with its own private memory; nodes exchange data by message passing, not by reading each other's RAM. Direct remote memory access only exists with special interconnects (RDMA/SCI) and is not the general cluster model.
- ch 1 [low] Section 4, paragraph 4 and the figure: The chain mainframe → cluster → grid → utility → cloud is presented as the order of events. As a logical build-up it is fine, but as history it is loose: selling computing by the meter was already discussed in the mainframe time-sharing era, well before grids appeared, so a student may read the arrows as dates.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1: Step 8 adds power, cooling and staff to the bought option, but step 4 charges only ₹30 per server-hour for renting. The comparison silently assumes the rent already covers power, cooling and staff, and also that the bought servers need no extra staff during the peak days. The arithmetic is correct; the assumption is unstated.
- ch 2 [low] Lead: The cause-and-effect sentence 'One processor cannot get much faster, so since the 1960s we buy more of them' muddles the timeline. Single-processor clock speeds kept rising for decades; parallel machines existed in the 1960s for other reasons, and buying many cores became the normal response only when single-core gains stalled in the 2000s.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 vs Section 2: Section 1 defines parallel computing as processors inside one machine that share one memory, but Section 2 lists 'a cluster' as a MIMD parallel machine and says 'the servers inside one rack are message passing'. A cluster is several machines with private memory, so the student sees the definition contradicted two paragraphs later.
- ch 2 [low] Exam trap 5: 'p = parallel time ÷ total serial time' is ambiguous, because 'serial' is used elsewhere in the chapter for the non-parallelisable fraction 1 − p. A student can read it as dividing by the serial part and get a nonsense value above 1.
- ch 2 [low] Figure caption: The caption says the curves flatten at ceilings of 20×, 10× and 4×, but the y-axis stops at 16, so for p = 0.95 the curve only reaches about 15.4× at n = 64 and the 20× ceiling is never visible in the plot.
- ch 3 [high] Exam traps, trap 1: The trap says a VM on your laptop 'has pooling of hardware'. It does not. Resource pooling means one provider's pool serves many separate customers at the same time; a single user's VM on a laptop is one tenant on one machine. The sentence teaches the exact confusion (virtualisation = pooling) that the trap is supposed to warn against.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, step 6: The step says the ₹500-per-student-per-year fee is 'not metered per hour or per GB' and then awards c₅ = 0.5. Under the chapter's own rubric (1 fully holds, 0.5 partly, 0 not at all) a flat per-head fee with no usage metering is a full miss, so the credit contradicts the reason given and makes the total look generous.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: 'a class of 60 cannot become 600 machines in 6 minutes' mixes students with machines and is hard to read; a student may think the 60 refers to servers.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, end paragraph: 'Pooling needs a way to slice one physical machine into many isolated pieces' describes virtualisation, but slicing one machine into many pieces is not yet pooling; pooling is when those pieces go to different customers. Read alone, the sentence supports the wrong idea that any VM is pooled.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q6: The answer says the meter supplies the signal for automatic scaling and that 'without metering, scaling is blind or manual'. Autoscaling is normally driven by monitoring telemetry (CPU load, queue length, requests per second), which is not the same thing as the metering used for billing, so the answer blurs two ideas.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3, benefit-to-risk table, row 'Elastic scale': The row pairs elasticity with 'noisy neighbour'. Elastic scale does not create a noisy neighbour: a noisy neighbour is another tenant's heavy load degrading your virtual machine, and it comes from shared hardware, which is a different row in the same table. A student copying this ledger into an answer will state a wrong cause and lose the mark.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, benefit-to-risk table, row 'Provider patching': The stated risk, 'lock-in to provider tools', does not follow from provider patching. Patching is routine maintenance; lock-in comes from proprietary APIs and data formats (already given in Section 2) or from managed services you stop being able to run yourself.
- ch 5 [high] Worked example 1, step 7 and the answer line: The 500 GB object store is placed at the physical layer, but the same step calls it 'a shared, virtualised slice' of hardware. The physical layer was defined as fixed, owned assets (servers, disks, switches); a pooled, software-presented bucket is not that. The answer line repeats 'object storage at the physical layer', so a student learns the wrong placement from both the step and the summary.
- ch 5 [high] Exam traps, last trap: 'Treating object storage as an application feature. It is a physical-layer resource that has been pooled and virtualised.' A resource that has been pooled and virtualised belongs to the virtualisation layer by the chapter's own definition, so the trap teaches a contradiction.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q7: The question says only that the provider patches the guest operating system, but the answer adds that the provider 'now manages the OS and runtime too' and concludes PaaS. The runtime was never handed over. Guest-OS patching alone moves the boundary to the top of the operating system; the platform layer above it is still the college's.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, Security: Security is listed as 'confidentiality, integrity, authentication and isolation', which drops availability, even though the chapter later measures uptime under SLA management. A student may conclude uptime is only a management concern, not a security one.
- ch 5 [low] Figure caption: 'The highlighted top layer is the only one the consumer builds alone' contradicts Section 1, which says the consumer also builds the platform when buying bare infrastructure, and is wrong for SaaS, where the consumer builds nothing at all.
- ch 5 [low] Beyond: The Kubernetes scheduling layer is said to sit 'between virtualisation and platform', but containers run on an operating system, and the OS is at the bottom of the platform layer. The scheduling layer therefore sits above the OS, inside the platform, not below it.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: The 4 VMs and the load balancer are grouped together, then the step says the college 'installs, patches and secures the operating system and everything above it'. That is true of the VMs only; the load balancer is a provider-managed virtual networking service, so the 'who manages it' half of the answer is wrong for one of the two items.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q7: The reason given for IaaS is not accurate. IaaS providers do expose load balancers and metric-based autoscaling rules (for example, scale on requests per target or on CPU). So the sentence 'the provider sees only virtual machines and not your request rate' is false, and a student who repeats it in an exam can be marked down.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, formula explanation: The subscript tags are dropped in the 'where' list, so the symbols appear as run-together words: 'ratehour', 'rateGB', 'GBout', 'rateout'. A student matching symbols to the formula cannot tell them apart from a single variable name.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, repeated in Exam trap 1 and Quiz Q3: The chapter says a private cloud must have 'the essential characteristics from chapter 1.3' and then lists only four: pooling, self-service, elasticity and metering. The standard list has five; broad network access is dropped, so a student who counts on the exam will write four and lose marks.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, step 1: The normal load of 200 requests per second is given but never used, so a student cannot tell whether the shortfall should be measured from 200 or from 300. The step silently switches from load to capacity.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: Section 3 defines multi-cloud as 'using two or more public providers', but the Q6 answer calls Team A's private cloud plus a public SaaS mailbox 'coexistence or multi-cloud'. That contradicts the definition given two pages earlier.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3 table, row 'Load has sharp, short spikes': The row sends every spiky workload to hybrid with bursting. Bursting needs a private base to burst out of; a new project with spiky load and no data centre should simply use a public cloud with auto-scaling. The row as written teaches the wrong reflex.
- ch 7 [low] Figure and its spec: The Venn diagram puts hybrid in the overlap of private and public only. Section 2 itself says hybrid joins two or more distinct clouds, which can also be two public clouds or a community plus a public cloud. The single overlap reinforces the common misconception the chapter is trying to kill.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, sentence before the formula: It says 99.9% availability in a 30-day month allows 'at most 43 minutes' of downtime, but the formula right below gives 43.2 minutes. The two numbers do not match, and a student copying 43 into an answer loses marks.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, the four bullet points after the formula: The list appears with no lead-in sentence, and it mixes billing meters (compute, storage, managed database) with a software stack (OpenStack). A reader can take OpenStack as a fourth thing that is billed.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1 and the figure: The text says the carrier links the consumer's network to the provider's edge, but the figure has only one carrier edge, carrier to provider, so the consumer side of the link is not drawn. The text also says the broker aggregates provider catalogues, yet no edge runs from provider to broker.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3 (pricing, TCO and metering paragraphs): The terms 'GB-month' and 'egress' are used without definition. A first-time reader may not know that GB-month means one GB stored for one month, or that egress means data leaving the provider.
- ch 9 [high] Figure caption: The caption says the edge node at 5 ms is the only tier that fits a 50 ms budget with 20 ms of compute. The regional fog node at 25 ms round trip also fits: 25 + 20 = 45 ms, which is under 50 ms. Only the cloud (60 + 20 = 80 ms) and the camera itself (90 ms) fail. A student reading the caption and then applying T_total = T_net + T_comp will get a contradiction.
- ch 9 [low] Lead: 'Both trade the cloud's unlimited capacity for milliseconds and saved bandwidth' is compressed to the point of confusion; 'milliseconds' is not a thing you get in exchange.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q5: 'An offload saves the phone 40% of its battery' reads as 40% of the whole battery charge, which is not what a single offload can do.
- ch 9 [low] Section 4, last sentence: 'The cloud does not disappear; it moves further from the sensor and closer to the archive' is unclear — the cloud is not moving anywhere; its role shifts to later stages.
