## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 2, area comparison: A gram of activated carbon with 1000 m² is compared to a football field, but a football pitch is roughly 7000 m², so the comparison overstates the area about seven times and a student may quote the wrong picture in an exam.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, first line of the pressure/temperature paragraph: 'Pressure and temperature change the equilibrium qualitatively' is the wrong word. Pressure and temperature change the equilibrium loading in magnitude and direction; they do not change what kind of equilibrium it is.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, ion-exchange resins: 'The surface holds the ions' contradicts the standard picture. In a resin the ions diffuse into the bead and exchange at charged groups distributed through the whole bead volume; the take-up is stoichiometric (one charge for one charge), not surface attachment, which is why ion exchange is normally classed apart from adsorption.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q6, answer: The phrase 'held more strongly by polar sites' is loose. Nitrogen is held more strongly because its quadrupole interacts with the cations in the zeolite, while a carbon molecular sieve separates the pair by the different rates of diffusion into the pores.
- ch 2 [low] Lead: The lead says the isotherm answers 'for a given feed concentration, how much solute will a gram hold'. Loading is set by the concentration left in the liquid at equilibrium, not by the feed concentration. A student may plug the feed value straight into q = qmax K_L C/(1 + K_L C) and oversize or undersize the carbon dose.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, text after the C/q formula: 'Divide intercept into 1/q_max to get K_L' can be read two ways; a student may divide the intercept by 1/q_max, which gives 1/K_L instead of K_L.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 and Section 2 formula annotations: The 'where:' clause of each displayed formula ends with plotting instructions ('Plot C/q against C: slope = ...'), which repeat the sentence just below and read like part of the symbol list.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2: n is called the heterogeneity index and then said to describe 'how steeply loading rises with concentration'. The steepness of the log-log line is 1/n, not n; calling n the steepness measure invites a student to read the slope as n.
- ch 3 [high] Section 2, paragraph on curve shape: It says an early breakthrough means a short unused bed. This is backwards: early breakthrough means little capacity has been used, so a large part of the bed is still fresh and the length of unused bed (LUB) is long, not short.
- ch 3 [high] Section 2, paragraph on curve shape: A favourable isotherm is given as a cause of a long tail. A favourable (concave-down, Langmuir-type) isotherm sharpens the front and gives a short MTZ and a steep curve; a diffuse, long-tailed front comes from an unfavourable or near-linear isotherm, plus slow kinetics and axial dispersion.
- ch 3 [high] Quiz Q5 answer: Repeats the same error: a strongly favourable isotherm is listed as a cause of a long tail. A favourable isotherm compresses the mass-transfer zone; it does not lengthen the tail.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, final paragraph: It says stagewise and moving-bed adsorbers are 'alternative names' some syllabi use. They are not alternative names; they are different contacting arrangements (stagewise/moving-bed versus fixed-bed). The earlier sentence in Section 2 states this correctly, so the two statements also contradict each other.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, first paragraph and formula: The text says 'W grams of dry adsorbent', but the symbol list then says W is in g or kg. Mixing the two is exactly the error the exam trap warns about.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, step 5 and answer: q_e = 9.6 mg/g comes from the zero-exit shortcut, which is an overestimate of the true capacity at exhaustion. Because q_e is too large, the unused fraction 0.667 is also an overestimate (the exact value is smaller). The answer gives 0.667 with no such caveat, and the reader cannot tell which way the error goes.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: The wording 'It ignores solute leaving before tb and assumes C = 0' is confused: the shortcut assumes no solute leaves before tb, so nothing is 'ignored' there. The real omission is the capacity still unused at breakthrough. Also, integrating over the whole curve to te improves q_e, not q_b, while the question asks about q_b.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q3: The answer says 'the number of dissolved ions stays about the same'. That is wrong: one Ca²⁺ is replaced by two Na⁺, so the particle count doubles (4 meq/L of Ca²⁺ becomes 4 meq/L of Na⁺, i.e. twice as many ions). What stays the same is the number of equivalents, and hence roughly the mass of dissolved solids (Ca, 40 g/mol, becomes 2 Na, 46 g/mol).
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, selectivity: The rule 'among ions of the same charge, the smaller hydrated ion is often preferred' holds for cation resins (Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺) but reverses for strong-base anion resins, where the smaller hydrated ions (OH⁻, F⁻) are least preferred (SO₄²⁻ > NO₃⁻ > Cl⁻ > OH⁻ > F⁻). A student will over-generalise it.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: 'Operating capacity is the smaller useful value between regenerations' is unclear. It mixes the size of the working capacity with the number of regenerations.
- ch 4 [low] Figure, edge regenerate → rinse: The label 'fresh resin' says regeneration produces fresh resin, which contradicts the exam trap 'thinking regeneration creates new resin'.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, formula box: Feed flow is written C and ionic concentration c; the two symbols differ only by case and are easy to confuse in handwritten work.
- ch 5 [high] Figure: The 'feed and final' series joins (60 °C, 40 g/100 g water) straight to (20 °C, 20 g/100 g water). Both end points already lie on the solubility line, so this drawn 'cooling path' sits exactly on top of the curve and tells the student nothing. More seriously, it is the wrong path: while the solution cools with no crystals forming, its concentration stays at 40 g/100 g water, so the composition moves horizontally at C = 40 from 60 °C to 20 °C. The point (20 °C, 40 g/100 g water) is the supersaturated state, well above the curve at 20 g/100 g water, and only then does the mother liquor settle back to (20, 20) as crystals form.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, cooling bullet: The physics is right (C* falls while C stays), but the pointer 'This is the straight line in the figure' is wrong: a line at constant C is horizontal, while the figure shows a slanted line falling from 40 to 20.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, formula box: The formula crystals = W(C₁ − C₂)/100 and the words 'C₁ is the solubility at the feed temperature' hold only when the feed is saturated. If the feed is unsaturated, C₁ must be the actual solute concentration in the feed per 100 g water, otherwise the crystals are overstated.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, first paragraph: The sentence 'Many correlations write S for σ' can mislead students. In many standard textbooks S denotes the supersaturation ratio C/C* (dimensionless ratio), not the relative supersaturation σ = (C − C*)/C*. A student may substitute the wrong definition into B = k_n S^n or G = k_g S^g and get a wrong numerical value.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: The answer defines 'S is supersaturation' without saying whether S is relative or absolute supersaturation. This is the exact confusion the chapter warns about, so the answer is incomplete for a 2-mark definition question.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, heat balance formula Qtotal = mf cp,f (Tf - T) + P ΔHc + mv ΔHvap and the next line 'Cooling water flow is mw = Qtotal/(cp,w ΔTw)': One additive total is used for three different units, so the balance is wrong for two of them. In a vacuum crystallizer the flash evaporation is the cooling: the latent heat carried off by the vapour equals the sensible heat released by the cooling liquor plus the heat of crystallization, so mv ΔHvap ≈ Qsens + Qcryst. Adding Qevap again to the same side double counts and gives a false, too large cooling-water flow. In a steam-heated evaporative crystallizer heat is supplied, not removed, so steam duty is not divided by cp,w ΔTw of cooling water. Only the plain cooling case matches the worked example.
- ch 7 [low] Figure tree and lead paragraph, Section 1: The lead names four classes (cooling, evaporative, vacuum, reaction), but the tree has only three branches, and the note under 'Reaction' says 'salting-out'. Salting-out is not reaction crystallization; Section 1 itself introduces it as a separate idea (adding a foreign substance that lowers solubility). A student reading the tree will conclude salting-out is a reaction.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The clause 'vacuum supplies part of the cooling' is misleading. The vacuum does not cool anything; it only lowers the boiling point. All the cooling comes from the latent heat carried away by the evaporating solvent.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q1 answer: The chapter treats 'vacuum' as its own class in the lead and in Section 1, then the quiz answer files vacuum and draft-tube-baffle under 'evaporative'. The answer contradicts the classification given earlier in the same chapter, so a student cannot tell which list to use.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, 'Q = U A ΔTlm' and 'pumps magma': ΔTlm and magma are used without definition, in a chapter written for a student meeting these terms for the first time.
- ch 8 [high] Quiz Q7: The answer says that reducing supersaturation makes the dominant size 3Gτ move upward. In the chapter's own MSMPR relation the dominant size is 3Gτ, and lowering supersaturation lowers the growth rate G, so at fixed τ the dominant size 3Gτ goes down, not up. Only the increase in τ raises 3Gτ. The answer mixes up 'fewer fines because B° falls' with 'coarser product' and a student will learn the wrong causal link.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1, osmotic pressure formula: The text says i is 'the number of ions per formula unit, 2 for NaCl' without saying this is the ideal value. Real NaCl solutions give i slightly below 2, so students who use 2 for strong seawater or brine get a high π.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, reverse osmosis: The section defines flux as J = Qp/A but never gives the driving-force relation, so the student cannot connect applied pressure, osmotic pressure and flux, which is the core of RO.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, electrodialysis energy sentence: 'Energy use scales with current and salt removed' is muddled: energy is V × I × t, while salt removed also depends on current, so the two are not independent factors. A student may think energy depends on salt removed separately from current.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, cross-reference: The reference to '6.4 Ion exchange' is plain text. In this series other-chapter references are links, and a plain figure number cannot be tapped on a phone.
