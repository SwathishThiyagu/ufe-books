## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Figure and Section 3: The text says a typical nerve spike lasts 1–2 ms, but the plotted trace crosses threshold at 1 ms and only returns to rest at 4 ms, so the spike shown is about 3 ms long. A student comparing text and figure sees a mismatch.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3 and Worked example 1: 'Peak-to-peak amplitude' is used for peak minus rest (100 mV), but the usual meaning of peak-to-peak is maximum minus minimum of the whole waveform. The plotted trace goes down to −80 mV, so its true peak-to-peak value is 110 mV, and a careful student will be confused.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3: The sentence '100 mV, not 85 mV' refers to 85 mV without saying where that number comes from, so it reads as an unexplained warning.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1 code block: The code prints the literal values 15 and 100 instead of performing the two subtractions. The output is right, but a student copying the code does not see the sign handling that step 3 stresses.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1: The gradient sentence lists only Na+ and K+, though Cl− and negatively charged proteins were named as main ions. The resting distribution of Cl− (high outside) and protein (high inside) is left unstated.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, impedance matching: The sentence says unequal electrode impedance turns mains pick-up into a differential voltage 'that the amplifier cannot reject'. A differential amplifier always rejects some of it; the true effect is that the rejection gets worse.
- ch 2 [low] Figure, Electrode–electrolyte path: The diagram shows only one double layer, at the active metal, and runs an edge straight from skin to the reference block, although the caption and the reference label say the reference is a second half-cell with its own double layer.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q6, answer: The ratio is written as bare numbers, 100 ÷ (100 + 2), with no units. The chapter's own rule is that numbers carry units, and a student may copy this without the MΩ.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, unipolar or bipolar: It calls the unipolar reference 'a distant reference'. For chest leads that reference is the Wilson central terminal, which is a weighted combination of three limb electrodes, not one distant electrode.
- ch 3 [low] Lead: The lead says the chapter "compares five transducer families", but Section 2 actually describes six: resistive, capacitive, inductive, piezoelectric, photoelectric and electrochemical (temperature sensors are added separately as a short comparison). The count contradicts the text.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, Selection criteria: "Range is the smallest to largest input it can measure without damage" mixes the measuring range with the damage/overload limit. A transducer's range is the span over which it meets its stated specifications; it can often survive inputs well beyond that before damage.
- ch 4 [high] Exam traps, trap 1: The claim that using 80 directly instead of 10⁴ gives an answer 'wrong by four orders of magnitude' is wrong. Using CMRR = 80 gives Acm = 1000/80 = 12.5 and an interference output of 12.5 × 50 mV = 625 mV, against the correct 5 mV. That is 125 times too large, about two orders of magnitude, not four.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, sentence before the CMRR formula: With the chapter's own definitions, V1 = Vcm + Vd/2 and V2 = Vcm − Vd/2, so a single-ended amplifier of gain G gives G(Vcm + Vd/2), not G(Vd + Vcm). A careful student will find the two statements do not agree.
- ch 4 [low] Section 4, sampling-rate paragraph: The chapter says 4 to 10 times Nyquist is used in practice, but its own numbers do not match: ECG at 500 samples/s with a 150 Hz band is about 3.3 times the 150 Hz top frequency (1.7 times Nyquist), and EEG at 250 to 500 samples/s with a 70 Hz band is about 2 to 3.5 times.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, resistor-matched difference amplifier: Saying 0.1 % resistors give 'about 60 dB' of CMRR is optimistic. The usual result is that 1 % resistors limit CMRR to roughly 34 dB, so 0.1 % parts give roughly 50 dB in the worst case.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, vectorcardiography paragraph: The Frank lead system is described as using eight electrodes. Frank's system uses seven: five around the chest at the fourth intercostal level, one on the back of the neck and one on the left leg. A ground electrode may be added, but that is not counted as a signal electrode.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, vectorcardiography paragraph: "Z back to front" points the Z axis towards the front, but the standard Frank convention makes Z positive posteriorly (front to back). It also clashes with the parallel phrase "Y head to foot", which does match the standard Y polarity (positive downwards).
- ch 5 [low] Section 3 table, Section 3 bullets, and Quiz Q7: The table says QRS "0.06–0.10 s (up to 0.12 s)", the bullet calls a QRS "wider than 0.12 s" abnormal, but Q7 calls a QRS of exactly 0.12 s "a wide QRS". A student cannot tell whether 0.12 s is normal or wide.
- ch 5 [low] Figure caption and figure edges: The caption says the ECG and VCG paths "share the same electrodes", and the diagram branches from the ECG electrodes straight into the Frank lead network. The Frank network needs its own electrodes at different body positions, so the diagram teaches a wrong connection.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, question line: "the QRS complex measures 2.0 mm across" is ambiguous, because amplitude is also measured in mm on the strip; a student can read it as a height of 2.0 mm.
- ch 6 [high] Lead, Section 1 lead-in and Quiz Q2 answer: The text says the EEG and EMG sources differ by 'about a thousand times', and Q2 repeats that surface EMG is 'about a thousand times larger' than EEG. This contradicts the chapter's own ranges: EEG is 10–100 µV, surface EMG is 0.1–5 mV (100–5000 µV). The real ratio is roughly 10 to 500, typically about 20–50 for a 50 µV EEG against a 1–2 mV EMG, which is why the gains quoted are 10⁴ against 10²–10³ (a factor of 10–100, not 1000). A student will memorise the wrong ratio and then wonder why the gains differ only tenfold.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, processing chain paragraph: The chain 'band-pass filter, notch filter, rectifier, low-pass smoothing, then root mean square (RMS)' mixes two alternative amplitude measures. RMS already includes the averaging that the low-pass smoothing does, so applying RMS after smoothing adds nothing and confuses the student.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, first bullet of the checks paragraph: 'EEG at 50 µV through EMG gain 10³ gives 0.05 V, close to noise' is loose. 50 mV output is well above the amplifier noise floor; the real trouble is that it is only a small part of the 0.5–1 V ADC range, so the digitised trace has poor resolution.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, sampling paragraph and Exam trap 4: The text concludes that a 125 Hz Nyquist frequency makes sampling 'safe', but noise and muscle activity above 125 Hz will still fold back into the 0–70 Hz band unless an anti-aliasing low-pass filter sits before the ADC.
- ch 7 [high] Quiz Q6: Q6 assumes cooling adds a fixed 1 ms to both latencies, so the answer says the calculated velocity is unchanged. That is physically wrong and directly contradicts Section 3, which correctly says cooling slows conduction in the measured segment. Cooling lengthens the distal latency AND slows conduction over the measured segment, so the proximal latency grows by more than the distal one and Δt increases. A fixed common delay is a different thing (e.g. an extra delay at the neuromuscular junction or in the recording system), and only that cancels.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, amplitude paragraph: The first sentence ('A small amplitude with slow velocity suggests loss of myelin or axons') is vague and partly clashes with the next sentence. A student may conclude that slow velocity points to myelin loss while small amplitude with slow velocity points to both, which is muddled.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q3: The answer explains the subtracted part only as the neuromuscular junction delay and muscle activation. That is true for a motor study, but the chapter also covers sensory studies, where there is no neuromuscular junction; a student may not see what is subtracted in a sensory study.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, formula block and first line: The formula line uses subscripts for t but the 'where' line writes 'tdistal and tproximal' as plain text, so the symbols do not match. The opening 'Stimulate the nerve far from the muscle' is also unclear about which site is meant.
- ch 8 [high] Section 3, troubleshooting table, row 'No calibration pulse': The stated cause is wrong. A gain or time-calibration error still produces a pulse, only at the wrong height or width. A missing pulse means the calibration circuit is faulty or the pulse is not being applied/injected, so the row sends the student to the wrong check.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, routine checks paragraph: 'Check paper speed' sits in a paragraph that also covers Holter checks, but a Holter has no paper and no pen. A student may look for a paper-speed setting on a Holter recorder.
- ch 8 [low] Exam traps, first trap: '108,000 beats in 24 h is 75 bpm, not 4,500 bpm' mixes units. Dividing by hours gives 4,500 beats per hour, which is not a bpm value at all, so the wrong answer shown is not the wrong answer a student would actually write.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, filtered QRS detection bullet and formula: The band-pass filter is described only qualitatively, and the symbol tmin in HRavg = NQRS ÷ tmin is not typographically clear (it reads as one word).
- ch 9 [low] exam traps, first bullet: The phrase "stays and can even grow if it is time-locked" is misleading. In the average a time-locked artefact keeps exactly its original amplitude; it does not grow. Only its size relative to the shrinking random noise increases. A student can misread this as "averaging amplifies artefacts".
- ch 10 [low] Section 1 and Figure (block diagram): The text chain is rectifier then smoother then comparator, but the figure jumps straight from the Rectifier block to the Threshold block. A student copying the diagram for the exam will leave out the smoothing stage, which is exactly what stops the comparator chattering.
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, third paragraph (threshold choice): The rule 'Too high a threshold gives no reward. Too low a threshold gives reward all the time' assumes the tone itself is the reward. Two paragraphs earlier the EMG design sets the threshold below resting level, so the tone is on at rest and silence is the reward. With silence as reward the rule reverses, and a student can carry the wrong one into the exam.
- ch 10 [low] Section 1, formula box: Units are given for Vin, Vout and T but not for Venv, and 'average' hides that the smoother is a low-pass filter with a time constant. T is said to be 'in the same unit as Venv', which leaves the student guessing.
- ch 10 [low] Lead and Section 3 (last sentence): The remark that full loop design is taught in only a few syllabi appears three times (lead, Section 3, and again in the closing line of Section 3). Repetition wastes space on a phone screen.
- ch 11 [high] Section 3 and Quiz Q6: The chapter says velocity comes from integrating accelerometer output, with no mention that an accelerometer measures gravity as well as motion. On a limb, the gravity term (about 9.81 m/s²) is much larger than the movement signal. Integrating the raw reading gives a completely wrong velocity and position, so a student would apply the method incorrectly.
- ch 11 [low] Section 1, calibration paragraph: A known mass gives a force in newton, not a pressure in kPa. Saying 'put a known weight on the platform ... the slope is sensitivity in mV/kPa' skips the area step and mixes the two units.
- ch 11 [low] Quiz Q5 answer (and Q6): 'A gyroscope helps angle but not translational drift' is too strong. Orientation from the gyroscope is exactly what lets you subtract gravity from the accelerometer, and that greatly reduces the drift in integrated position.
- ch 11 [low] Lead paragraph: 'Limb position sensors record joint angle, velocity and acceleration' suggests all three are measured directly. A goniometer measures angle only; velocity and acceleration are obtained by differentiating marker positions or from an IMU.
