## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3, bridge rectifier: The explanation of bridge PIV is technically wrong. It says each diode sees only Vm 'because two diodes share the reverse voltage around the loop'. In a bridge the two reverse-biased diodes do not share or divide the voltage. Each one independently blocks the full peak supply voltage Vm, because each is connected across the supply through a forward-conducting diode. A student will wrongly conclude that each diode blocks only Vm/2.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2 and Quiz Q3: The phrase 'the whole secondary voltage, which is 2Vm peak-to-centre' (and 'the full peak-to-centre voltage of both halves' in Q3) is confused wording. 'Peak-to-centre' is not a standard term and reads as if 2Vm were measured from a peak to the centre tap, which would be Vm.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, bridge description: 'The supply connects to opposite bridge corners, and the load connects across the other pair' is vague; 'corners' can be read as the four diode positions rather than the four bridge nodes.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1, final sentence: "The peak inverse voltage is the same as without the capacitor" is false for a half-wave rectifier, which this section explicitly covers (the half-wave ripple formula is given right above). Without C the half-wave diode blocks Vm; with C it blocks Vm (held by the capacitor) plus Vm (source swinging to the opposite peak) = 2Vm. Only full-wave circuits keep the same PIV.
- ch 2 [high] Exam traps, trap 2: The trap repeats the wrong general claim: "The diodes still block the same reverse voltage." A student who answers this way for a half-wave capacitor filter loses marks, and the chapter has already told them half-wave is in scope.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, series L filter: "Ripple current is cut roughly in proportion to the reactance 2πf_r L" reads as if ripple current rises with reactance. The ripple current is the ripple voltage divided by the reactance, so it falls as the reactance grows.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, L-C filter: "ripple falls by roughly 1/((2πf_r)²LC)" mixes a ratio with a voltage. 1/((2πf_r)²LC) is a plain number (the attenuation factor), not an amount of ripple.
- ch 2 [low] Figure and its caption: The curve is drawn from C = 100 µF, where the formula gives 200 V of ripple, far outside the small-ripple assumption the formula rests on; and the caption does not say the curve is for a full-wave (100 Hz) case, so a student cannot tell why 1000 µF gives 20 V and not 40 V.
- ch 2 [low] Figure caption: "doubling the capacitor only halves the ripple" uses "only" as if halving were a poor return, while the curve and the formula show exact proportionality, ripple ∝ 1/C. A student can read the caption as contradicting the formula.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q5, answer: "The load charge per cycle, Idc/f" is ambiguous in a rectifier chapter: per supply cycle the charge is Idc/f, but per ripple cycle (the cycle that sets the pulse width) it is Idc/(2f) in a full-wave circuit.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, table row 'Half-wave | R, or discontinuous RL': The table gives the pure-resistance value (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α) for a half-wave circuit with a discontinuous RL load. With any inductance the current does not stop at π; it reaches zero at an extinction angle β > π, and Vdc = (Vm/2π)(cos α − cos β), which is smaller than (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α). Section 1 of the same chapter says exactly this ('the output has a negative part and Vdc falls'), so the table contradicts the text.
- ch 3 [high] Section 2, last sentence of the R-load paragraph: 'otherwise it is discontinuous and the cosine formula over-predicts the output' has the sign of the error reversed. When the current is discontinuous, the actual average is higher than (2Vm/π)cos α, not lower; the chapter's own numbers show it (at α = 60°, R load gives 0.477 Vm against 0.318 Vm from the cosine formula), and the general rule for a phase-controlled converter is that discontinuous conduction raises Vdc above the continuous-conduction value.
- ch 3 [high] Quiz Q6, answer: 'Below the boundary the continuous-current formula over-predicts Vdc' repeats the reversed claim. Discontinuous operation gives a higher average output, so the continuous-current formula under-predicts Vdc for α < 90° and gives too large a negative value for α > 90°.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, second paragraph: 'an RLE load whose back EMF is smaller than the negative output' reads as a magnitude comparison. The condition is a numerical one: E must satisfy E = Vdc − IdcR, so the back EMF must be more negative than the converter output, that is of larger magnitude. As written, most students will take the opposite condition.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q1, definition of natural commutation point: The term is defined here as the instant the device would start conducting if it were a diode. Many textbooks reserve 'natural commutation' for the instant a conducting device turns off at current zero (for a half-wave RL circuit that is β, not the start of conduction), so a student may be marked down for this wording.
- ch 3 [low] Lead paragraph: The lead says the chapter covers R, RL and RLE loads, but RLE appears only in one clause and one exam trap: no formula, no example and no table row for a back EMF.
- ch 4 [high] Section 2 (firing sequence) and the figure caption/gantt: The chapter defines va = Vm sin θ, but then says 'Firing T1 at θ = α starts conduction with T6', and the gantt starts T1 at 0°. With these voltage equations, T1's anode voltage only becomes the most positive (and T1 forward biased) at θ = 30°, where va = vc. At, say, α = 10° T1 is still reverse biased (va = 0.17 Vm while vc = 0.77 Vm), so the stated firing instant is wrong, and the formula Vdc = 1.35 VL cos α is only valid when α is counted from that natural commutation point, not from the θ = 0 crossing of va.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2 (RLE load sentence): 'Conduction begins when the supply voltage exceeds E' is vague and slightly misleading: in a B6 bridge the thyristor pair sees the line-to-line voltage, not a phase voltage, and the current only starts once that voltage exceeds E plus the drop across the conducting devices.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q7 answer: It says the output 'follows part of the phase voltage'. In a B6 bridge the discontinuous output follows segments of the line-to-line voltages, not a phase voltage, so the answer is imprecise for the bridge case.
- ch 5 [low] Lead: The sentence says a capacitor-input bridge draws spikes "even when voltage and current cross zero together". The current spikes occur near the voltage peak, not at the zero crossings; the intended point is that the fundamental current is nearly in phase with the voltage. A student may take the phrase literally and be confused.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, crest factor: V_peak appears in CF = V_peak/V_rms without being defined, although the chapter defines every symbol where it first appears.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, 1/h rule: "I_h = I₁/h" calls I_h a harmonic amplitude, but Section 2 defined I₁ as fundamental RMS current. Mixing peak amplitude and RMS can confuse students checking the figure table.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: "see 2.6" is plain text, while the book's cross-references use chapter links; on a phone the reader cannot jump to the overlap chapter.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, third bullet ('Some syllabi call Ls the commutating reactance'): Reactance is ωLs, measured in ohms; Ls is an inductance in henries. The worked example (step 2) correctly calls ωLs = 0.3142 Ω the commutating reactance, so this bullet contradicts the example and can make a student put henries into a formula that expects ohms.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, last paragraph (commutation failure): 'The bridge shorts the supply' is loose and misleading. In commutation failure the current fails to transfer, the outgoing device keeps conducting, two devices of the same leg conduct together, and it is the DC link that is shorted (giving a large overcurrent). The AC supply itself is not shorted.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1 definition of Ls and the single-phase drop formula in Section 2 (also used in Q3 and Q4): Ls is defined as the source inductance per line, but the single-phase formula 2ωLs Idc/π comes from taking Ls as the whole commutating inductance of the loop. In a single-phase bridge the source current path runs out through one line and back through the other, so a per-line Ls gives a loop inductance of 2Ls and a drop of 4ωLs Idc/π. As printed, a student who reads 'per line' literally will be out by a factor of 2 (with 2 mH per line and 10 A, the derivation gives about 23°, not the quoted 16°). The three-phase formula is consistent with per-phase Ls.
- ch 7 [high] Worked example 1 (and the figure note 'Load R: 5 Ω, 35.9 A'): The step uses V1 = (2Vm/π) cos α1, which Section 1 itself states holds only for continuous current. The load is described as a plain 5 Ω resistor with no inductance, so the bridge current is discontinuous for any α > 0 and the formula does not apply. For an R load the average of a single-phase fully controlled bridge is (Vm/π)(1 + cos α) = (325.3/π)(1 + 0.866) = 193.2 V, giving 38.6 A, and the neat symmetry V2 = −V1 no longer holds (V2 would be about +13.9 V).
- ch 7 [high] Exam traps, fourth bullet: The sentence is broken: 'Watch the sign of V2. For α1  90°, so cos α2 is negative and V2 is negative.' The inequality has dropped out, so the student cannot see which case gives a negative V2, and the bullet no longer states a correct condition.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, crane hoist example: The claim that the hoist needs 'one current direction to lift and the other to lower or hold a load' is wrong for holding. Holding a load at standstill needs torque in the lifting direction, so the armature current is in the same direction as when lifting, not reversed. Only power lowering (a light load driven down) needs reversed torque and hence reversed current; regenerative lowering keeps the lifting-direction current.
- ch 8 [high] Lead: "Phone chargers, LED drivers, computer supplies and EV chargers use PFC" is an over-generalisation. Ordinary phone chargers (roughly 5 W to 25 W) are below the power and current thresholds of the harmonic standards and almost never have a PFC front end; a student would wrongly conclude his own charger contains a boost PFC stage.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2: The effective input resistance is written "Re = Vrms2/P". With the missing superscript this reads as Vrms times 2, and a student may copy the wrong formula. Elsewhere the chapter uses Unicode superscripts (√2, ²) correctly.
- ch 8 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: "Below the mains peak the bridge would feed the bus directly" is ambiguous: it can be read as "during the part of the cycle below the peak", which is the opposite of the intended meaning (bus voltage lower than the mains peak).
- ch 8 [low] Section 3: "many low-power equipments" is not idiomatic; "equipment" is uncountable in this technical sense and the phrase reads poorly for second-language readers.
