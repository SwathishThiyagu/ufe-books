## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: The step calls 200 J the energy 'delivered to the patient' and then says the remainder is 'lost in paddles, cables and the chest wall'. Energy dissipated in the chest wall is already inside the energy delivered to the patient; it is not a separate loss. A student will be confused about which energy is stored, which is delivered, and which reaches the heart.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, bullets on pulse width: The output stage is described only as delivering a pulse. The standard pacemaker output circuit has a coupling capacitor in series with the lead, so the pulse has zero net DC charge. Without it the electrode polarises and the heart does not respond; the point is missing.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q3: The arithmetic is right (2.0 Ah ÷ 0.0876 Ah per year = 22.8 years), but a student may take 22.8 years as a realistic implant life. Real pacemakers last roughly 5–12 years because of self-discharge, telemetry and higher current during pacing.
- ch 1 [low] Figure, node 'batt': The text spells the cell as lithium-iodine but the figure writes only 'Li-I₂'. Readers with English as a second language may not connect the two names.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3 (Safety and the team): The low-pressure alarm is said to warn of a 'blocked or disconnected line'. A block, kink, clamp or clot raises the pressure between the pump and the patient; it cannot lower the line pressure. Low pressure means a disconnected or leaking line, an empty reservoir or falling flow.
- ch 2 [high] Exam traps, trap 4: It gives the normal mean arterial pressure as 90-120 mmHg. 90-120 mmHg is the usual range for systolic pressure. Normal MAP is about 70-100 mmHg, so the comparison number is wrong.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2 (Pumps, flows and pressures), last sentence: 'A ventilator (see 5.3) takes over breathing after the lungs start again' is confusing. During bypass the lungs are not ventilated; ventilation is restarted when bypass ends. The lungs do not start again on their own.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q2: 'Why is heparin given before the venous cannula is opened?' is unclear. Heparin is given before any cannulation, not at the moment the venous cannula is opened.
- ch 3 [high] Lead: The lead says the variable that stops inspiration 'gives the mode its name'. The chapter itself then shows this is false: pressure control is time-cycled but is named after its pressure limit. A student who memorises the lead sentence will get pressure control wrong in the exam.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1: The chapter counts only three events (trigger, limit, cycle). Standard descriptions list four variables that describe a breath; the fourth is the baseline variable, which sets the pressure or flow the patient exhales to. Some syllabi ask for four variables by name.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q3 answer: 'Ending on pressure would use the same variable again' is not the reason and is confusing. The real reason is that airway pressure rises quickly to the set limit, so a pressure-cycled breath would end very early, giving short and variable inspiratory time, variable tidal volume, and no control of I:E ratio or mean airway pressure.
- ch 3 [low] Figure: The branch 'Pressure set → pressure control' reads as if pressure control were pressure-cycled, which contradicts Section 1; the correction appears only in the figure note.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1: The PBW formula is given without saying it is the male version. For a female patient the constant is 45.5 kg, not 50 kg; a student who applies 50 kg to a woman gets a tidal volume about 4.5 mL/kg too high.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q5 answer: The answer lists resistance and compliance causes but never says how to tell them apart, which is the examine-worthy step.
- ch 4 [high] Section 2 (HME paragraph): The chapter says an HME 'suits short transport or a patient with a natural upper airway'. A patient with an intact natural upper airway does not need an HME, because the nose and pharynx already heat and humidify the gas. An HME replaces that function only when the upper airway is bypassed, that is, with an endotracheal tube, tracheostomy, or during transport/anaesthesia. As written, the indication is the opposite of standard practice.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, after the formula block: 'For dry gas, AH is the final saturated value if we ask how much water must be added' is muddled. Students may read it as saying AH changes for dry gas, when AH is fixed at 44 mg/L and only the starting humidity (zero, for dry gas) changes the amount to be added.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, first paragraph: The formula M_w = AH × Q is stated as a sentence just before the formula block that repeats it, and the symbol M_w is later written as M_w in plain text. Minor duplication/symbol presentation.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, final paragraph (dose): "The dose is set by output power in watts and time in minutes" is fine for short-wave and microwave, but for ultrasound the dose depends on intensity in W/cm² and the size of the treatment head, not total watts, and also on continuous versus pulsed mode. A student could carry the wrong dose model into an ultrasound question.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, paragraph 1 (output power): "Commonly up to about 200 W" understates many short-wave physiotherapy units, which are rated at several hundred watts. The fixed number may be marked wrong if a student quotes 200 W as the machine maximum.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, step 4 and Section 1: The long wavelength (11 m against a body of about 1.7 m) explains why the body does not block the field, but it is not the whole reason for deep heating. Deep heating also needs low absorption per centimetre, which is true at 27.12 MHz and false at 2450 MHz. As written, a student may think wavelength alone decides penetration depth.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q8, answer: "The implant may also be near the return path" is loose wording. A return plate belongs to surgical diathermy; in therapeutic short-wave the risk is the implant sitting in the field between the applicator plates and concentrating current density.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q6: The answer has no burst duration to work from. It divides the whole 20 ms burst period by the 0.4 ms carrier period and gets 50 cycles, which only holds if the carrier is on for the entire period. That is continuous output, not 'switched on and off in bursts'. The standard Russian protocol switches on for 10 ms and off for 10 ms, so one burst holds 25 carrier cycles, not 50.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, Russian current: The burst is described only as 'on and off in bursts at 50 Hz'. The on-time is never given, so the student cannot tell a 10 ms burst from a full-period burst, and this is exactly the gap that breaks Quiz Q6.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q4 answer: 'Motor fibres have a higher threshold or are not in the current path' is a hedge with two different explanations joined by 'or'. The accepted explanation is that TENS intensity is kept below the motor threshold and the electrodes sit over the painful area or its nerve, not over the muscle's motor point.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, charge density: Charge density is defined but given no unit, so the student cannot write it in an exam answer or compare it with a safety limit.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: It says the stone ends as 'fragments of a few millimetres, small enough to travel down the ureter'. The ureter's narrowest points are only about 2–3 mm wide, so a student may think 4–5 mm pieces pass easily. Fragments of a few millimetres often obstruct and need a stent or a second session.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, piezoelectric source: 'Its focal zone is small, so each shock hurts less and carries less energy' is confusing. A small focal zone concentrates the same electrical energy into a smaller volume; the point is that less total energy and a smaller skin area are needed, so there is less pain and bruising, not that the shock itself is weaker.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, electromagnetic source: 'The moving membrane makes the pulse' does not say what kind of pulse. The membrane makes an acoustic (pressure) pulse in the water; the electric pulse comes from the coil.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, second bullet: 'Swapping the labels gives a correct-looking diagram with the wrong physics' misleads. The ellipsoid property PF₁ + PF₂ = constant is symmetric, so the path lengths still work with the labels swapped. What breaks is the machine geometry: F₁ is the near focus inside the water bath where the spark can sit, and F₂ is the far focus at the fixed focal distance where the stone can be placed.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q6, answer: 'a long skin-to-stone distance may keep the stone outside the focal zone where energy density is high' is garbled and can be read two ways.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, steps 5–7 and output: The latent-heat figure is rounded sloppily: 0.0008 kg × 334 000 J/kg = 267.2 J, not 267.0 J, so the total is 466.7 J and the nitrogen mass is 466.7 ÷ 199 000 = 2.345 g. The printed 267.0, 466.5 and 2.34 do not follow from the numbers given.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, Joule–Thomson paragraph: Saying the gas simply 'expands and cools' is incomplete: a real gas cools on throttling only below its inversion temperature and warms above it. As written, a student could think every compressed gas cools on expansion.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, Storage paragraph and the bullet list after it: Four safety bullets (cold burns, asphyxiation, venting, pressure) appear straight after the storage paragraph with no heading or lead-in, so they read as part of the storage topic rather than as a separate safety section.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1 and Quiz Q4: Both exercises cool the tissue only to −20 °C, which is above the −40 °C lethal line the chapter insists on in Section 1. A student may think −20 °C is an adequate clinical endpoint.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, step 4: The calculation assumes all 80 % of the tissue water freezes by −20 °C. In tissue a fraction of the water stays unfrozen at that temperature, so the latent heat is an upper estimate.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, Storage: Listing blood with semen and embryos 'kept for years' can mislead: routine blood-bank storage is about 4 °C for a few weeks. Years-long blood storage needs glycerol freezing of rare or autologous units.
- ch 8 [low] Figure spec: The block order puts 'Cell death' before the 'Thaw cycle', but the chapter itself says thaw is the second half of the kill. The diagram implies the thaw is a separate post-death step.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2, bullet on neon lamp; repeated in Exam traps, bullet 2: The firing voltage of the neon lamp is given as 'a few hundred volts'. Neon lamps used across ECG inputs typically ionise at roughly 60–100 V. A few hundred volts is the range for a spark gap or gas-discharge tube, not a neon lamp. A student will carry the wrong number into the exam.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2, bullet on defibrillator-proof leads: It says the resistors and clamps are built inside the lead connector, 'so any monitor used with them survives the shock'. The clamp diodes sit at the amplifier input inside the monitor, not in the lead. A defibrillator-proof lead only gives protection when it is plugged into a monitor designed for it; it cannot protect an unprotected monitor. As written, the sentence contradicts standard practice and misleads about where the components live.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, bullet on Delivery: The chapter never mentions the return (dispersive/indifferent) electrode pad on the patient's back or thigh. Without it, a student may think the RF current returns through the catheter or the table, and the circuit is incomplete.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, first bullet, compared with Quiz Q5: The bullet says the series resistors limit the lead current to 'a few tens of milliamperes' for a 5000 V shock. Q5 uses the same kind of lead (20 kΩ per lead) and gets 100 mA. The two statements do not match.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: The step says the 70.7 V ablation voltage 'can reach the electrode leads, so the protection network must survive it as well as the 5000 V shock'. That 70 V is far below the shunt's firing voltage, so the lamp does nothing here; the clamp diodes and the series resistors handle it. As worded, a student may think the neon lamp fires during ablation.
