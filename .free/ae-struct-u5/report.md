## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1: The text says q at a point depends on "the direct stress on the section". Bending shear flow depends on how fast the direct stress changes along the beam (the gradient), not on the direct stress itself. Where the bending moment is largest, the direct stress is largest but the shear force and shear flow are zero. A student reading this will wrongly size the shear flow from the bending stress value.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2: "The point where this line cuts the section is the shear centre" is loose. The line of action of the resultant lies in the plane of the cross-section, so it cuts the section along a line, not at a point. The shear centre is one particular point on that line.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3: "The sign is opposite the flanges" is too terse to follow. It is not clear whether it refers to the sign of e, of the torque, or of the moment.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: The forward references to 5.2, 5.3 and 5.6 are plain text, so a student on a phone cannot jump to the worked distribution or the closed-cell method.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps, last bullet (mixing units): The claim 'Using metres gives a factor-of-1000 error' is wrong. Working entirely in metres is perfectly consistent and gives q correctly in N/m, which converts exactly to N/mm by dividing by 1000. The real danger is mixing systems, for example taking I in mm⁴ while t, y and s are in metres, which is out by 10⁹, or S in N with lengths in m and I in mm⁴.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q1: The question asks for the SI unit, but the answer gives N/mm. The coherent SI unit of force per unit length is N/m; N/mm is a commonly used submultiple, not the SI unit.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q3, answer: The written expression '−(5000/2×10⁶)×2×30×20' can be read as (5000/2)×10⁶ because of operator precedence, which is not the intended calculation (the numerical answer −3 N/mm is correct for 5000/(2×10⁶)).
- ch 2 [low] Lead: The lead promises the chapter turns the idea 'into numbers for I, C, Z and lipped sections', but only one worked example is given and it is a Z-section; no I, C or lipped section is worked numerically.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, step 3 (and the qb formula in Section 1): It says 'y is distance from the centroidal axis'. A distance is never negative, but the formula qb = −(S/Ix)∫t y ds only works when y is a signed coordinate. In the worked example y runs from +50 to −50, so a student reading 'distance' will make every contribution positive and get the wrong qb.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example, step 6: 'top and bottom flange moments cancel' suggests each flange carries a non-zero moment and the two happen to cancel. In fact the net flow along each flange is zero (q runs from +42.86 to −42.86 N/mm), so each flange contributes zero moment about O on its own.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example, statement of the problem: The load is said to act at the mid-point of the left wall, yet q = qb + q0 is built from the zero-twist condition, so it is the flow for the same shear acting through the shear centre, which turns out 100 mm to the right. The real box loaded at the left wall also carries a torsional flow. Nothing in parts (a)–(c) is wrong, but the step is easy to misread.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: 'q values equal and opposite on the two sides' mixes two conventions: the physical flow in the two vertical walls of a symmetric box points the same way (both up), while the signed values round a single loop are equal and opposite. As written a student may think the two walls carry flows in opposite physical directions.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q5: The question says 'A wing box' without specifying single-cell. Real aircraft wing boxes are often multi-cell, where shear flow is not the same in every wall, so the answer 'q is the same all round' can mislead.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3: 'The theory fails if the wall is not thin' is too strong. Bredt-Batho becomes inaccurate for thicker walls rather than failing outright.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, definition of Sᵢ: Sᵢ = ∮ ds/t is stated to be 'in mm, with t in mm'. ds/t is a length divided by a length, so Sᵢ is dimensionless. The worked example uses S₁ = 500 with no unit, which is right, so the stated unit contradicts the example.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, shear centre paragraph: The procedure is self-contradictory and the closing sentence is wrong. You cannot apply the load through a trial point and at the same time force every cell to zero twist; zero twist rate is the assumption used to find the q₀ values for the (unknown) shear-centre load line. Also, the moment of the shear flows about a reference point does not vanish at the shear centre. That moment equals V·e, where e is the distance from the reference point to the shear-centre line, and e is what you solve for.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q6: 'The point where the load must act for zero moment is the shear centre' repeats the muddled moment statement of Section 3; a student can read it as saying the shear-flow moment is zero at the shear centre.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, 'Walls that take no direct stress': 'Only booms are dropped from the shear integral' is confusing, because booms are lumped areas and never appear as a wall of length ds in ∮ds/t at all.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, formula definition and the straight-run rule: b is defined as the stringer pitch, and a straight-run boom with skin on both sides is told to use b = the full pitch, giving B = A_stringer + t·b/2 when stresses are equal. That is only half the skin. A stringer at pitch p owns half of each adjacent panel, so the skin working with it is a full pitch wide and B must become A_stringer + t·p. The corner rule in the same section, (t/6)(b₁+b₂)(2+σskin/σboom) with b₁ and b₂ the full panel widths, is correct and contradicts this, because there b is the sum of the two panel widths, not the pitch. A student following the straight-run rule gets half the correct boom area.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q3: The answer uses b = 80 mm, the pitch, and gets B = 180 mm². A straight stringer at 80 mm pitch has skin on both sides, so b = b₁ + b₂ = 160 mm and the skin contribution is twice as large. The stated answer is half the correct boom area.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q5: The answer contradicts itself ('...equal and opposite contributions? No: ...') and says the two webs carry 'equal and opposite flows'. The flows are equal in magnitude and act in the same physical direction; they only look opposite in sign because the loop is traced in opposite senses on the two sides. A student reading this cannot tell which statement is meant.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q6: The answer says 'q in the webs is proportional to B/Ixx', which is wrong: q ∝ B·y/Ixx, where y is the boom height. It also assumes the booms scale equally and the boom positions do not move, which the question does not state. If doubling every panel width doubles the box, the webs get deeper and the shear flow for the same S halves, it does not stay the same.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1, steps 6 and 7: The signs −37.5 N/mm on the left web and +37.5 N/mm on the right web can be read as the two webs carrying shear in opposite physical directions. The sign change comes only from tracing the loop in opposite senses, not from the flow direction.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, definition line after the dP/dz formula: The text defines dM/dz = S as the shear force in N, but then writes qb = −(1/h)(dM/dz) = S/h. With that definition qb would be −S/h, not +S/h. The worked example uses dM/dz = −20 000 N with S = +20 000 N, so the stated definition contradicts both the formula and step 9. A student following the definition gets the opposite sign for the bending flow.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, sweep paragraph: 'Both leanings put the boom axis at a small angle to the span and both feed the same term' and 'A swept boom's transverse component acts both through the depth and chordwise' are wrong for sweep alone. The boom force Pi = M yi Ai/Ixx depends only on the vertical distance yi from the neutral axis. A boom that leans only in plan (sweep) has the same yi and the same dPi/dz, so sweep does not feed the q = −ΣdPi/dz term at all. Its chordwise component P dx/dz is balanced by a chordwise circulation of shear flow (a constant q0 round the box), not by the vertical web flow.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: 'The taper flow is largest near the root, where M is largest and the depth changes fastest per unit length' is misleading. For a linear taper dh/dz is constant along the span, so the depth does not change fastest at the root. Also qtaper = M h'/h² has h² in the denominator, so qtaper is not guaranteed to be largest at the root for a strong taper.
- ch 7 [low] Figure caption: The caption says 'The bending moment gradient sets the boom force', but the boom force is set by the bending moment M itself (P = M/h). The gradient dM/dz sets dP/dz and the ordinary bending flow, not the boom force.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, paragraph 2: The text says 'dP/dz picks up a second term'. In general, differentiating Pi = M yi Ai/Ixx gives two extra terms, one from dyi/dz and one from dIxx/dz. Only in the two-boom spar do these combine into a single geometry term.
- ch 8 [high] Worked example 1, step 2: Ixx is written as 1.30×106 mm4. Read literally this is 1.30×106 = 137.8 mm4, not the second moment used in the calculation. The exponent is missing and the unit is not formatted.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, two-cell compatibility sentence: The displayed equation (1/(2Ai))∮qi/t ds = (1/(2Aj))∮qj/t ds is incomplete for two cells sharing a wall. If qi and qj are the cell redundant flows q0, the shared wall carries qb + qi − qj, and the twist-rate integral for cell i must subtract the qj term on the shared wall and include qb. As written, it contradicts the next sentence that the shared wall carries qi−qj.
- ch 8 [high] Exam traps, fourth trap, and Quiz Q4 answer: The text says an open flange has no twist rate. An open section does have a twist rate; it simply has no enclosed area, no Bredt circulation q0, and no separate closed-cell compatibility equation. A student can take away the false idea that open members do not twist.
- ch 9 [high] Exam traps, fourth trap (sin α cos α): The direction of the error is stated backwards. In σ_t = ΔS/(t d sin α cos α) the factor sin α cos α = 0.5 sits in the denominator, so dropping it makes σ_t larger, not smaller: the wrong value is 35 000/750 = 46.7 N/mm², exactly half of the correct 93.3 N/mm². The trap claims forgetting it 'doubles σ_t', which is the opposite of what happens, and it hides that this mistake is an unsafe underestimate of the band stress.
- ch 9 [low] Figure caption and node labels: The caption says the band 'passes 35 kN into the booms', but the boom node and the aria text give 52.5 kN per boom over the bay. Both numbers are right for different quantities (35 kN is the band shear ΔS; 52.5 kN is the boom axial force P = ΔS L/d with L/d = 750/500 = 1.5), but shown side by side in one figure without saying so, a student reads them as contradictory.
- ch 10 [high] Section 3, worked example step 6, table, and exam trap 1 (Bredt on the angle): The perimeter is wrong. With a centre-line side of 58 mm the perimeter is 4 × 58 = 232 mm, not 240 mm. Every closed-box number built on s = 240 (J = 377 217 mm⁴, θ = 0.0393 rad = 2.25°, the 1180 stiffness ratio, 14.86 MPa curve) inherits the error.
- ch 10 [high] Exam traps, last bullet (unit slips): The statement 'Two silent factors of 1000 turn 46 rad into 46 mrad' is wrong. The two factors cancel: skipping both conversions gives 200 × 2000 / (27 × 320) = 46.3, the right number by accident. One factor gives 46 296 rad (G left in GPa) or 46.3 mrad (T left in N·m), not two.
- ch 10 [high] Section 3 opening sentence and worked example parts (a) and (b): 'The same 240 mm of 2 mm strip that a 60 mm × 60 mm angle uses' is wrong. The angle has two legs of 60 mm along the centre line, so it uses about 120 mm of strip (about 118 mm developed), and step 1 itself adds only 60 + 60 = 120 mm. The closed box therefore uses roughly twice the material of the angle, so the claim that the comparison is 'from identical material' is false.
- ch 10 [low] Quiz Q6: 'The same 200 mm of 3 mm strip' is not the same strip as in Q4, where the strip is 100 mm wide and 3 mm thick. A student may think the 200 mm is the 100 mm width of Q4.
