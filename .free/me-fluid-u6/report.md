## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3, last paragraph: The sentence 'The series arrangement gives more work than one vane because the jet is used again and again' is misleading. In a series of vanes the same water is not reused by successive vanes; each vane is struck by fresh water from the jet. The series gives more work because the full jet mass flow ρaV strikes the vanes, whereas a single vane is struck only by ρa(V − u). A student could wrongly conclude that one parcel of water hits several vanes in turn.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, vane efficiency sentence: The efficiency of a single vane is defined as η = P/(½ρaV³), using the kinetic energy of the whole jet. Only the part ρa(V − u) actually strikes the single vane, so this denominator is not the energy supplied to that vane. The definition is a convention, but the text does not say so, and a student may be confused when comparing single-vane and series efficiency.
- ch 1 [low] Figure description: The curve labelled 'flat plate' is the work rate for a single moving flat plate, x(1 − x)². The description does not say 'single moving', so a reader may think it is a series of flat plates, which would be x(1 − x) and would peak at u/V = 0.5, not near 0.33.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3, degree of reaction paragraph: The sentence 'For a reaction turbine the pressure falls across the runner, so 0 6.5.' is broken text. The range of R has been lost and the trailing '6.5' is meaningless. A student cannot read the value or the limit, and it sits where the impulse/reaction contrast is being made.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q3, answer: The clause 'the runner extracts the largest head from the inlet whirl' is loose and clashes with Q4 and Q7, which correctly say that an opposite (negative) outlet whirl gives a larger Euler head. A student may write that V_w2 = 0 gives the maximum possible head, which is false.
- ch 2 [low] Beyond paragraph: 'CFD codes solve Euler's equation on every blade passage' confuses Euler's turbomachine equation (an integral angular momentum balance across the runner) with the CFD Euler equations for inviscid flow. CFD actually solves the flow equations over the whole passage.
- ch 3 [high] Quiz Q3 (answer): The answer says a flooded casing 'would pressurise the runner, turning it into a reaction machine'. That is wrong. Even inside a pressurised casing, the pressure at bucket inlet and bucket outlet would still be equal, so the degree of reaction would still be zero and the machine would still be an impulse machine. This also contradicts the chapter's own exam trap, which says a casing proves nothing and only the pressure drop across the runner decides the type. The real reason is that the free jet must travel through air; if the wheel is submerged, the surrounding water breaks up the jet and the buckets lose most of the kinetic energy to churning and friction.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 (degree of reaction formula): The denominator is defined as 'H is the total head at runner inlet', but a degree of reaction compares the pressure drop inside the runner with the head that the runner itself converts (inlet total head minus outlet total head). Using the head at inlet alone makes R read low for every machine and is not consistent with the quoted values (about 0.5 for a Francis, higher for a Kaplan).
- ch 4 [low] Lead: The lead says the jet is 'turned through almost 180°', but Section 1 correctly says a bucket turns it through about 165°. A student skimming the lead will carry the wrong deflection angle into Vw2 = u + kVr1 cos δ and get a sign or magnitude error.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, parts and working: The head range given (roughly 100 m to over 1000 m) starts lower and ends lower than the usual textbook range; Pelton units are normally quoted from about 150–300 m up to nearly 2000 m for the largest units.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, speed ratio: The chapter defines speed ratio as φ = u/V1 and gives 0.46. Some textbooks define speed ratio as u/√(2gH), so a student meeting the other convention in a question paper will use the wrong number.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps, first trap: The trap warns against Vw2 = u − kVr1 cos δ using δ = 165° but never says how that other form arises, so a student who has seen Vw2 = u − Vr2 cos θ in another book cannot tell which angle is meant.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q2: The reason given (outgoing water would hit the following bucket) is the textbook reason, but it is not true at the design point: at exactly u = V1/2 a full 180° turn would leave the water with zero absolute velocity. The interference argument only holds away from u = V1/2.
- ch 4 [low] Figure, node 'Penstock': The note 'net head H' is attached to the penstock, but the net head is the head available at the nozzle after penstock friction, so the label can mislead a student into using the gross head.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3: The blade-speed formula is written u = πDN ÷ 60, but D is then defined as 'the radius of that section in m'. The formula only gives the correct speed when D is the diameter. With D as the radius the answer is half the true blade speed, and the same section later says 'the radius changes from hub to tip'.
- ch 5 [high] Worked example 1 (step 5 and step 6): The 45 m is called 'the net head available at the runner, after casing, guide vane and draft tube losses are removed'. The draft tube is downstream of the runner, so its loss cannot be removed from the head at the runner inlet. Step 6 then says the 18.5% is lost as 'friction and eddy losses inside the runner and guide vanes', but guide vane loss was already taken out of the 45 m. The definition and the loss accounting contradict each other.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps (trap 2): Two problems. (a) The same wrong head is asked for here ('after casing, guide vane and draft tube losses'). (b) 'An efficiency above 100% means a wrong head was used' does not follow: putting the gross head in the denominator gives an efficiency lower than the true one, not above 100%.
- ch 5 [low] Lead: 'The runner runs full' and 'recovers the speed the water leaves with' are ambiguous. Students may read the second as recovering velocity, which is the opposite of what the draft tube does.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 (list of parts): 'Stay vanes: fixed vanes that remove the swirl left by the casing' is not the standard description. The swirl is set by the guide vanes; the stay vanes mainly pass the water from the casing to the guide vanes evenly and carry the top cover.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3 (cavitation paragraph): 'A Kaplan ... needs the deepest draft tube' is misleading. What protects a Kaplan is that its runner is set lower below tailwater (more submergence), because it has the highest specific speed and passes the most water. A long draft tube actually raises the runner above tailwater, which makes cavitation worse.
- ch 6 [high] Figure (plot spec for section 2): The figure's x-axis is labelled 'area ratio A₂/A₃' and runs from 1 to 4, but the plotted expression is 1−1/x². With the chapter's own definition x = A₂/A₃ (inlet over outlet, always less than 1 for a diverging tube), the correct curve is 1−x². The plotted curve 1−1/x² only matches Δh/(V₂²/2g) if x means A₃/A₂ (outlet over inlet). As printed, a student reading the axis literally (A₂/A₃ = 2 or 3) would conclude the tube is converging, and the graph contradicts the formula in the text.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, note under H_s,max formula: 'the second term is the velocity head that must be recovered' is loosely worded and can be misread. The term (V₂² − V₃²)/2g is the velocity head the tube converts; it is subtracted because converting it lowers p₂, which eats into the allowable suction head.
- ch 7 [high] Exam traps, trap 3: The unit check is wrong. It says "m^{1.25} in the denominator cancels against √P and leaves a plain number", but P is a power in kW, so √P has units kW^0.5, not metres. Nothing cancels, and N√P/H^{5/4} is not dimensionless (its units are rpm·kW^0.5·m^-1.25). A student who follows this check will believe specific speed is dimensionless, which contradicts the later statement that the dimensionless form is ω√Q/(gH)^{3/4}.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, note under the N_s formula: Calling N_s "a plain number" sits badly next to "quoted in rpm" and with the later sentence that only ω√Q/(gH)^{3/4} has the units divided out. Readers may conclude the rpm form is dimensionless.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, trap 4: The wording is muddled. It sets up "not √(N P)" but then finishes with "not 5000", mixing two different mistakes: forgetting the square root on P (70.71 instead of 5000) and taking the root of the whole product (√(300 × 5000) = 1224.7).
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, closing paragraph: The worked example is summarised as giving "unit quantities of 33.54 rpm and 0.805 m³/s", but three unit quantities were computed; unit power 6.988 kW is dropped.
- ch 8 [high] Figure (efficiency vs Q/Q design): The plot contradicts the chapter's own definition of the design point. The text says the hill's peak is the design point, so each curve should be highest at Q/Q design = 1.0. Instead the Pelton series peaks at 0.7 (0.88) and drops to 0.82 at 1.0, and the caption/aria calls this 'peaks sharply near design flow'. A student would read the Pelton design flow as 0.7 Q design, and that Pelton is worse than Francis and Kaplan at full design flow.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, paragraph 1: 'Muschel curves and the iso-efficiency hill collect many such tests' introduces a named curve without saying what it plots, so the reader cannot tell it apart from a main curve or an operating curve.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, paragraph 2: 'keeps the speed ratio near its best value' is the wrong variable for a Kaplan. A grid-connected Kaplan runs at fixed N and (for a given site) fixed H, so u and the speed ratio u/V are fixed; what the double adjustment preserves is the flow angle into the runner (the velocity triangles).
