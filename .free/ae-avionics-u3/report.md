## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1 (DC chain) and Quiz Q1: The text gives the DC chain as generator → voltage regulator → reverse-current cut-out → busbar → loads. This puts the voltage regulator in series with the generator output. The regulator is a control block: it senses voltage and controls the field current, and it does not carry load current. The quiz Q1 answer repeats the same wrong series order.
- ch 1 [high] Section 2 (AC chain): The text gives the AC chain as generator → GCU → GCB → AC bus → loads. This puts the GCU in series with the generator output. The GCU is a control unit: it senses voltage, current and frequency, controls the field and commands the GCB. It does not carry generator power. The figure on the same page correctly shows the GCU as a side control block, so the text contradicts the figure.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1 (28 V DC source): "In a 28 V DC system the source is a shunt or compound DC generator" is too absolute. Many aircraft feed the 28 V DC bus from transformer rectifier units, as Section 2 itself says. A student may think every 28 V DC bus is fed by a DC generator.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2 (GCU senses field): "The GCU senses voltage, current, frequency and field" names field as a sensed quantity. The GCU senses field current, not the magnetic field itself.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3 protection table (under-voltage row): "Under-voltage | Loads from dropout or overload" is unclear. Overload is primarily an over-current condition; under-voltage protection protects loads from sustained low voltage, which may have several causes.
- ch 1 [low] Figure (GCB note): The text defines GCB as generator circuit breaker, but the figure note calls it "generator contactor." A student may think GCB stands for generator contactor or that the device is only a contactor, not a breaker.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1 formula and Worked example 1, step 3: The example says the TRU is 'fed from a 115 V three-phase AC bus' and then puts 115 V into the formula as V_LL. On aircraft, a 115 V three-phase system means 115 V line-to-neutral; the line-to-line voltage is about 200 V (115 × √3 = 199.2 V). Using 115 V as V_LL gives a line current of 30.6 A, but a real TRU on that bus would draw about 6087 / (√3 × 200) = 17.6 A.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 3: The intermediate value is wrong: 6086.96 / 199.19 = 30.56 A, not 30.58 A. The final answer 'about 30.6 A' is correct, so the slip is only in the digit shown.
- ch 2 [low] Figure: The text says the battery drives the inverter through the DC bus, but the figure draws a dashed edge straight from the battery to the inverter, which suggests a direct battery-to-inverter connection.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, charging paragraph: The chapter says charging 'starts at constant current, then switches to constant voltage to avoid overcharge' without saying which chemistry. That is the lead-acid (CC then CV/float) method. Nickel-cadmium aircraft batteries are charged at constant current only, with voltage and temperature cut-offs. A constant-voltage finish on NiCd is exactly what the chapter itself says drives thermal runaway in Section 2, so a student gets two contradicting rules.
- ch 3 [low] Lead paragraph: 'The battery is the only source with the engines off' reads as an absolute rule. With the engines off an APU generator or a ground power unit can be feeding the DC buses, and the battery is then a standby source, not the only one.
- ch 3 [low] Figure specification: The lead-acid curve starts at 25.5 V (2.13 V per cell) and is still at 24.6 V at 40% while discharging at 1C. Those values are close to open-circuit or surface-charge values; under a steady 1C load a 12-cell battery settles near 24 V (about 2.0 V per cell) within the first minutes, then falls to the 21.4 V end point.
- ch 4 [high] Figure (Emergency supply chain after generator loss): The dashed edge from PMG to the essential DC bus, labelled 'engine control', contradicts Section 2. The PMG does not feed the aircraft DC bus; it feeds the engine (or APU) control unit only, and its output is rectified inside that unit. The figure and its aria text say the PMG 'holds up the essential DC bus', which is a technical error a student will reproduce in an exam.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, PMG paragraph: 'supplies ignition and control power' is loose. On an engine the ignition exciter is normally fed from the aircraft AC bus, not from the PMG; the PMG's job is control (and speed) power for the FADEC/ECU, and APU control power. As written, a student may write 'PMG powers the igniters' in an exam.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, last lines: 'as 3.3 shows' points at another chapter but gives no usable link, so the reader cannot jump to the battery capacity discussion.
- ch 5 [high] Worked example 1, step 7 (and Quiz Q4 wording): Step 7 labels the 1.1 Ah result 'Energy taken'. An ampere-hour is a unit of electric charge, not energy. The question asks for 'the energy taken from the pack', so a student will write energy in Ah and lose marks. Energy is charge × voltage: 1.1 Ah × 24 V = 26.4 Wh.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, first line: 'each one sits on its own battery pack' contradicts the next paragraph, which says a set of packs is spread through the cabin, each pack feeding several lamps. A student may think every single lamp has a dedicated pack.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, end of first paragraph: The claim that some syllabi demonstrate the cabin lighting control panel on an A300 simulator is a specific, unverifiable detail. If it cannot be sourced, it should not be stated.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, dome light bullet: Fixing the dome light 'on the aft bulkhead' is too specific; on many types it is in the ceiling above the crew, and on others on the aft bulkhead.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, step 9: The cross-check 4 Ah ÷ 5.5 A assumes the full 4 Ah is available at any current. Discharging a 4 Ah pack at 5.5 A is about 1.4 times the one-hour rate, and usable capacity falls at high rates, so 43.6 min is optimistic.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 formula and Quiz Q5: I = P ÷ V is exact for DC and resistive loads only. On a 115 V AC 400 Hz bus the current is P ÷ (V × power factor), so a real landing light draws somewhat more than 5.2 A.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q3: The cross-references 'as described in 3.2' and 'the limits of 3.6' are plain text, so the phone reader cannot jump to those chapters.
- ch 5 [low] Figure: The aria text mentions anti-collision lights, but the External branch of the tree shows only 'Navigation' and 'Beacon and landing'; strobes and logo lights appear nowhere.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 (Cooling): The statement 'Halving airflow raises Rth, often doubling it' is presented as a general rule. For turbulent ducted flow the heat-transfer coefficient varies roughly with the 0.8 power of mass flow, so the rise is close to doubling, but in other regimes the rise is much smaller. A student may apply the doubling as an exact law in every case.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 (Cooling), junction formula: Tj = Tcase + P × Rjc uses the symbol P without saying it is the dissipation of that particular device, while P in the previous formula is the whole LRU dissipation. A student may use the 120 W box total to find one chip's junction temperature.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3 (EMI/EMC), first paragraph: 'EMI is unwanted electrical energy' is imprecise: radiated interference is an electromagnetic field, not current in a wire, and the definition sits awkwardly with the very next sentence that splits conducted from radiated.
- ch 7 [high] Section 1, sentence after the ρ0 definition: The sentence "At low speed, q = ½ρV², so the same q gives a lower true speed in thin air" has the physics backwards. From V = √(2q/ρ), a fixed q with a smaller ρ gives a LARGER true speed, not a smaller one. The exam traps section itself says the opposite ("the same IAS corresponds to a higher TAS because the air density is lower"), so a student sees two contradicting statements.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, definitions of CAS and TAS: "True airspeed (TAS) corrects IAS for density" skips CAS and ignores compressibility, so the chain IAS → CAS → TAS is loose and the two corrections look like one.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, formula line: q = P0 − P = ½ρV² is written as if it always holds. It is the incompressible form, valid only below roughly Mach 0.3; above that the relation between impact pressure and speed changes and the ASI needs a compressibility correction.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, cross-reference sentence: Fuel quantity indication is usually capacitive or float based, not a pressure-to-reading chain; only some systems (for example pressure or density sensors in a tank) work from pressure, so the flat comparison can mislead.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, mechanical tachometer paragraph: The sentence 'The indicator is a small generator or a drag-cup mechanism' mixes two different instruments and reads as if both are parts of the indicator. A drag-cup (eddy-current) tachometer has a shaft-driven rotating magnet that drags a spring-restrained metal cup; no generator sits in the indicator.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, thermocouple sensitivity: S is presented as a fixed 41 µV/°C for type K, but the Seebeck coefficient of type K changes with temperature (about 39 µV/°C near 0 °C and over 42 µV/°C in the middle of the range), so the linear formula is an approximation.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, cold-junction compensation: The compensation term 'S × T_cold' is only correct when the reference is 0 °C; a student may think any cold-junction temperature simply gets added as an absolute figure.
- ch 8 [low] Figure, node 'mv': The node note 'about 41 µV/°C' is the sensitivity of the sensor, not the size of the millivolt signal, which is about 41 µV per degree of temperature difference (a few millivolts for hundreds of degrees). A student can misread the signal level.
- ch 8 [low] Figure caption versus figure: The caption says 'Both signals are conditioned', but the upper (speed) chain goes straight from 'Voltage or frequency' to the cockpit indicator with no conditioning block, while only the EGT chain shows 'Amplifier and ADC'.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, N1 and N2 definitions: Only two spools are defined; on three-spool engines there is also N3, and a student meeting N3 in another text has no anchor.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, cruise drift sentence: 'A slow EGT drift at the same N1 can show a dirty compressor or a drifting sensor' omits that EGT also moves with ambient temperature, altitude and bleed configuration, so drift must be judged against a comparable baseline.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1 and Section 3 (PWM average): The formula average field current = D·I_closed + (1 − D)·I_open treats the current as jumping instantly between the two steady-state values. The field winding is highly inductive, so in each interval the current rises or decays exponentially and may not reach the steady value. The duty cycle found (30.4 %) is therefore an approximation, and the chapter does not say so, so a student may take it as exact.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2 and comparison table (carbon-pile, 'Wear: None'): Saying a carbon-pile regulator has 'no wear' is too strong. The discs are mechanically loaded, erode slowly, shed carbon dust and drift with age and heat, which is why such regulators need periodic adjustment. The real advantage is no contacts and no arcing, not zero wear.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q7: The question's premise contradicts itself: if the regulator holds 28 V, the bus should not be in over-voltage. The answer then says 'not necessarily' the regulator is at fault, but immediately adds that the regulator may have lost sensing or have a stuck field drive, which are regulator faults. A student cannot tell what conclusion is being taught.
- ch 10 [high] Worked example 1, step 2 (and the data given in the example): The resistance quoted for 8 AWG copper, 0.63 mΩ/m, is not the resistance of 8 AWG. Standard annealed copper of 8 AWG (about 8.4 mm²) has a resistance near 2.1 mΩ/m at 20 °C. A value of 0.63 mΩ/m belongs to roughly 3 AWG. A student who memorises this pair will carry a wrong constant into the exam, and the answers to (a) and (b) are built on it.
- ch 10 [low] Quiz Q7: The numbers given (breaker 10 s, wire 8 s at 60 A) contradict the chapter's own figure, where at 60 A the 20 A breaker clears in about 2.2 s and the wire survives about 14 s. A student who checks the figure will think the curves were drawn wrong or that the quiz is wrong.
- ch 10 [low] Section 3, paragraph 1: 'For every current the device curve must lie below the wire damage curve, and above the normal load current' mixes a time comparison with a current comparison in one sentence, and 'above the normal load current' can be read as a second curve requirement.
- ch 11 [high] Section 1 (also exam traps): It calls the generator contactor "GCB". In standard aircraft electrical terminology GCB means generator circuit breaker, not generator contactor. A contactor and a circuit breaker are different devices, and the exam trap repeats the wrong expansion.
- ch 11 [high] Section 4: The paragraph says "A switch is operated by hand" and then lists the microswitch and the proximity switch as cockpit types. A microswitch is actuated mechanically by a lever or strut, and a proximity switch is a non-contact sensor, not a hand-operated cockpit switch.
- ch 11 [low] Section 2: It says the generator with the smaller k takes the larger current. This is true only if both generators have the same no-load voltage V₀; with unequal V₀ the sharing depends on both V₀ and k.
- ch 11 [low] Section 1: The TRU sentence is contradictory: a TRU feeding bus 1 from the other side is replacing the lost generator for DC loads, but the next clause says it cannot replace a generator. It also mixes DC battery/TRU language with an AC generator bus.
- ch 11 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q3: "No two sources are ever connected" is too broad. A battery or a TRU can be connected to a bus that also has a generator; the point is that no two generators are paralleled.
- ch 11 [low] Figure: The figure shows solid feeders from BUS 1 and BUS 2 to the same ESS BUS. Without diode symbols or labels, this looks like it ties the two buses and defeats the open BTB. The text says the essential bus is fed through diodes.
- ch 11 [low] Beyond: Variable-frequency AC is produced by generators, not by paralleled inverters in general; the phrase "paralleled inverters must share without a mechanical drive" muddles the point.
- ch 12 [high] section 2: The text says each engine carries one large starter-generator, but the B787 has two variable-frequency starter-generators per engine (four total).
- ch 12 [high] section 3 table: The table lists the B787 main generators as 'Two engine starter-generators', but the B787 has four main generators, two per engine.
- ch 12 [low] lead: 'The reason is cable weight and load growth' is vague; the antecedent of 'the reason' is unclear.
- ch 12 [low] section 3 table: The emergency source row is inconsistent: 'RAT generator' for A320 but 'RAT' for B787.
- ch 13 [low] Section 1, table row for Torque and the paragraph after the table: The table says a torque sensor shows 'Shaft power, turboprop' and the text says it shows 'percentage or shaft power'. Torque and power are different quantities: shaft power = torque × angular velocity. A torque indicator alone cannot show power.
- ch 13 [low] Section 1, table row for Fuel flow and the sentence after the table: Turbine and paddlewheel meters sense volume flow. Turning pulses into kilograms per hour needs the fuel density, and the chapter does not say this, so a student may think the meter measures mass directly.
