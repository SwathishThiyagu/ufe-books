## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 1, derivation bullets: The letter A is used twice: first for the cross-section area in m², then again as the amplitude in the solution v = A sin kx + B cos kx, and the bullet 'The straight column is A = 0' refers to the amplitude, not the area. A student can easily read A = 0 as 'the area is zero'.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, sentence on fixed-fixed ends: 'the ends force a full sine wave into half the length' is muddled and, read literally, implies the buckled wave fits a full wave into L/2, which would give P_cr = 16π²EI/L², not 4π²EI/L². The correct picture is a full wave over the real length L, so the equivalent pinned half-wave has length L/2.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, validity sentence: 'for a mild-steel strut this needs λ roughly above 100; for aluminium alloy the limit is lower' leaves 'the limit' undefined; it can be read as the proportional limit rather than the limiting slenderness (aluminium has both a lower proportional limit and a lower limiting λ, so the sentence does not settle which is meant).
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q3: The chapter presents the reciprocal form as if it came from physics: 'A reciprocal is a flexibility, so the column's two ways of failing add like compliances in series', and Q3 says 'the two failure modes share the load, so neither is ever fully reached'. Rankine's reciprocal sum is an empirical fit chosen so the curve passes through the squash load at λ = 0 and the Euler load at large λ; it is not a spring-in-series model, and the two modes do not share load. The chapter itself contradicts this later by saying a comes from tests.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q6: One of the two suggested causes is in the wrong direction. Putting σ_c in MPa and E in GPa makes a a thousand times too large, so P_R comes out too small, not larger than P_E. That slip cannot produce the reported 60 kN against a 50 kN Euler load.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps, third bullet: 'the two differ by a square root of the area' is imprecise. I and r are not related by a factor √A; they are related by I = A r², so I/r = A r, which depends on r as well as on A.
- ch 3 [high] Section 1, paragraph 1: It claims an initial bow a and an end eccentricity e give 'the same governing equation and the same peak stress'. Only the first-order moment is the same. The bow problem obeys EI v″ + P v = −P v₀(x) with v₀(x) = 0 at the ends; the eccentric-load problem obeys EI v″ + P v = −P e, a constant right-hand side. The magnification depends on the bow shape: a sinusoidal bow gives mid-span moment P a/(1 − P/P_E) = 2.38 P a at P/P_E = 0.58 in the worked example, while the secant gives 2.73 P a. The peak stresses are close, not identical.
- ch 3 [high] Exam trap 3 (Mixing e and c): The trap says 'swapping their roles changes the answer'. The term ec/r² is symmetric: 0.002 × 0.010 = 0.010 × 0.002 = 0.20 either way, so the number does not change. The stated consequence is wrong.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, Perry-Robertson paragraph: 'the secant formula would overpredict because the stress has passed the proportional limit' is ambiguous and reads as a contradiction with the next paragraph and with trap 5, which say the true stress is larger than the secant prediction once yielding starts.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 and Section 3: The symbol λ_t is defined twice with two different formulas: in Section 1 it is √(π²E/σ_prop), the slenderness where Euler stress reaches the proportional limit; in Section 3 it is √(2π²E/σ_y), the point where Johnson's parabola meets Euler. Quiz Q5 and Q6 then ask for 'the transition slenderness' with two different answers. A student can easily substitute σ_y into the Euler cutoff (or σ_prop into the Johnson cutoff).
- ch 4 [low] Figure: The Johnson parabola is plotted from λ = 0 up to λ = 120, passing zero near λ ≈ 93 and reaching about −214 MPa at λ = 120. This contradicts the text, which says the parabola is valid only up to λ ≈ 66 and that Euler must be used above that.
- ch 4 [low] Worked example, step 8: The step rejects Euler because 431.8 MPa is 'above yield'. The correct cut-off for Euler validity is the proportional limit, which is what Section 1 and the fourth exam trap say. The number is right and the conclusion is right, but the stated reason contradicts the rule the chapter teaches.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2: Saying early tests on 'perfect specimens' gave strengths above Engesser's prediction is misleading. Real test columns always have initial crookedness and load eccentricity; the classical tests were on ordinary imperfect specimens, and that is exactly why Shanley's load-path explanation was needed.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1: The slenderness λ appears in σ_cr = π²E/λ² and in the formula for λ_t before it is defined. Only Section 3 states λ = L_e/r.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 (also Section 3): The example 'a lower wing spar panel can carry compression on the ground and bending from lift' mixes two opposite load cases. Lift bends the wing upward, which puts the lower skin and lower spar panel in tension. The panel is in compression in the ground case, when the wing bends down under its own weight. As written, a student may think lift bending and ground compression act together, which is wrong.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: The paragraph starts with a central transverse load W and M0 = WL/4, then says 'For a sine-shaped deflection, v = vm sin(πx/L). Substitution ... gives vm = M0/(PE − P).' That substitution is only valid if the first-order moment itself is M0 sin(πx/L). A central point load does not give a sine moment, so the step does not follow from the load just stated.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, second paragraph ('A central point load is close ...'): Calling the sine formula 'close' for a central point load understates the gap. The exact elastic result for a central load is Mmax = (W/2k) tan(kL/2), that is A = tan(u)/u with u = (π/2)√(P/PE). At P/PE ≈ 0.5 this gives about 1.84, against 2.03 from 1/(1 − P/PE), roughly 10% higher.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, fifth bullet (unit slip): The direction of the unit mistake is inverted. E = 70 GPa written as the number 70 while stresses are in MPa is a modulus 1000 times too small, so ε = σ/E comes out 1000 times too LARGE, and the factor of safety σ_y/(E·ε_max) comes out 1000 times too SMALL. The trap as printed tells the student the opposite.
- ch 6 [low] Figure, node d: The node is labelled 'Max principal strain' but its note gives 120.0 in MPa, which is the strain multiplied by E, not a strain. A student can read it as a strain with stress units.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, first paragraph (Rankine against σ_yt): The limit is called 'the yield strength in tension'. Cast irons and other brittle castings, which the same paragraph says Rankine suits, show no clear yield point; the usual Rankine limit for them is the ultimate tensile (and compressive) strength.
- ch 7 [high] Worked example 1 (also Section 1 formula notes and Quiz Q5): The symbol σy is used for two different quantities in the same sentence: the normal stress in the y direction (σy = −40 MPa) and the tensile yield strength (σy = 250 MPa). The formula notes also define 'σy is the tensile yield strength' while σx, σy are defined as normal stresses. A student cannot tell which σy the numbers belong to, and the factor of safety 250/152.32 is meaningless if σy is read as the −40 MPa stress.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, comparison paragraph: It says the von Mises stress is the root-mean-square of the three principal differences. It is not. The RMS of the differences (σ1−σ2), (σ2−σ3), (σ3−σ1) is √{[(σ1−σ2)²+(σ2−σ3)²+(σ3−σ1)²]/3}, while σeq,M is √{[(σ1−σ2)²+(σ2−σ3)²+(σ3−σ1)²]/2}. The von Mises value is √(3/2) ≈ 1.225 times that RMS, not equal to it. In the worked example the RMS of 152.32, 46.16 and 106.16 MPa is 110.5 MPa, but σeq,M is 135.3 MPa.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, last sentence of the octahedral paragraph: 'the mean stress (σ1+σ2+σ3)/3 only moves the plane along its normal' is unclear and misleading. The mean stress is the normal stress acting on the octahedral plane; it does not move the plane and does not change the octahedral shear.
- ch 7 [low] Lead: 'Stress offices use these' is unexplained jargon. A reader meeting the phrase for the first time will not know it means the stress-analysis group in a design organisation.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q7, answer: 'The von Mises factor 1.25 is acceptable only when the code is validated for the material' is ambiguous and loosely argued. 'Code' can be read as finite element software or as a design code, and 'validated for the material' is not the usual reason. The standard practice is: von Mises is the normal criterion for ductile metals; a Tresca check is added when a conservative lower bound is wanted.
- ch 8 [low] Example 1, step 8: Step 8 says 'The endurance limit is 400 MPa, half of 800 MPa.' Stating the fraction invites students to use Se = Su/2 as a rule, which the exam traps then tell them not to do. The sentence pulls against the trap.
- ch 8 [low] Lead: The lead says 500 MPa repeated 'ten thousand times' can grow a crack until the part breaks. The worked example gives the life at 500 MPa as about 22,300 cycles, so 10,000 cycles would not break this part. The two numbers contradict each other.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1: The S-N curve is only valid for a fully reversed cycle (mean stress zero, R = −1), which is what rotating bending gives. The chapter never states this baseline condition, though it later says mean stress changes the result.
- ch 8 [low] Figure: The flat 400 MPa endurance-limit line is drawn from 10³ cycles onwards, so it overlaps the whole life range even though the data only become horizontal at 10⁶ cycles. A student may read it as 'endurance limit applies from 10³'.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1: The sentence 'Two ratios fix the shape of the cycle' is misleading: R alone fixes the shape, and A = sigma_a/sigma_m follows from R as A = (1 - R)/(1 + R). A is also undefined at R = -1, where sigma_m = 0.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2: The chapter says a constant-life curve for 'a chosen life' meets the vertical axis at the endurance limit S_e. The endurance limit is the fatigue strength at infinite life. For a finite chosen life the intercept is the fatigue strength at that life, not S_e.
- ch 9 [low] Figure: The Soderberg series is evaluated up to sigma_m = 800 MPa, producing negative alternating stresses below sigma_m = 600 MPa. A constant-life line is only meaningful for sigma_a >= 0.
- ch 9 [low] Exam traps: The yield check is stated only as testing sigma_a + sigma_m against S_y (tensile peak). A compressive mean stress can cause first-cycle compressive yielding, which this check misses.
