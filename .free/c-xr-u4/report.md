## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 2, point clouds: The text says point clouds have no normals and that a meshing step is needed before ordinary rendering. Many laser scanners and depth pipelines output per-point normals, and point clouds are commonly rendered directly as point or splat primitives without any meshing.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, UV mapping: "Both numbers lie between 0 and 1" is only true for the base tile. UV values outside [0, 1] are normal and are used to repeat or tile a texture across a surface; the statement as written would make tiling look impossible.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, tessellation: Tessellation by itself only subdivides a polygon; it does not round a curved silhouette. The rounding comes from the curved surface definition used with the subdivision (for example PN triangles or Phong tessellation) or from a displacement map.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, Rz(θ) formula: The rotation matrix is given as a 3 × 3, but every other formula in the chapter works with 4 × 4 homogeneous matrices, and Section 2 multiplies T · R · S as 4 × 4. A student can be stuck on how a 3 × 3 multiplies the 4-number column (x, y, z, 1). The remark 'θ is measured in degrees here, radians inside the maths' also states two conventions without saying which one to use on a calculator.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps, fourth bullet: The bullet gives the matrix product T · R in the first sentence and then switches to the order of operations ('rotating after a translation'). Row order of writing and order of application are exactly what students confuse, so the bullet can be read the wrong way round.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q1: The question says 'the last column of a 4 × 4 transform matrix', but the answer (translation) is true only for an affine transform. In a projection matrix the last column carries the perspective terms, so the wording is not strictly correct.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q6 and Section 3: 'Every distance measured inside the parent doubles' (Q6) and 'every distance measured in the parent's frame' stays fixed (Section 3) mix two frames in one phrase. Lengths measured in the parent's own frame never change; it is lengths in world space that double under a parent scale of 2.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q4: The answer credits the low-pass filter with keeping output within the device's force range. A filter only reduces jumps and adds lag; it cannot guarantee a limit. The chapter itself says the smoothed value is then scaled and clamped to the device range, so the quiz answer mixes two different stages.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: "Using only one radius gives 0.5 m instead of 1.0 m" does not say which quantity the 0.5 m is. A student may read it as a penetration value, and with d = 0.9 m the single-radius sum would in fact give no contact at all (0.5 m − 0.9 m < 0), so the trap is easy to misread.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, last sentence: 'A VR toolkit is only the container for the database' overstates the role. A toolkit also renders frames, reads input and tracks the headset and hands, so a student may think a toolkit is nothing but storage.
- ch 4 [low] Figure stack: The stack runs world → rooms → objects → geometry → scripts → 2D control panel and labels the bottom as 'finer', which suggests the panel sits inside the scripts. A control panel is a separate world record (or hangs off a panel object), not a subdivision of a script.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, projection matrix sentence: The two scale factors are attached to the wrong axes. With θ as the vertical field of view and a = width/height, the vertical scale is the larger one, 1/tan(θ/2), and the horizontal scale is 1/(a·tan(θ/2)). The chapter gives x the 1/tan(θ/2) factor and y the 1/(a·tan(θ/2)) factor, which is backwards: a student who transposes this matrix gets a wrongly stretched image on any non-square viewport.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2 (look-at) versus Section 3 (camera at origin looking along +z): The chapter switches camera convention without saying so. Section 2 ends with 'a point straight ahead of the camera now has x = y = 0 and negative z', i.e. the standard camera looks along −z. Section 3 opens with 'Put the camera at the origin looking along +z' and writes x′ = f·x/z, which only works with positive z in front. The exam trap itself warns against mixing conventions, so the chapter contradicts its own advice and a student cannot tell which sign to use in the exam.
- ch 5 [low] Section 4, first paragraph: 'After the divide, every visible point lies in NDC ... Hardware clips triangles against these planes' places clipping after the perspective divide, which contradicts Section 1 ('Clipping needs depth in front of the camera, so it comes before the divide'). Hardware clips in clip space against the near plane before dividing.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: 'Clipping is likely missing or applied after projection' is misleading: clipping is supposed to happen after projection, in clip space. The real failure is a missing near-plane / w > 0 test before the divide.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: W is called 'its world matrix' but the formula p_world = W·M·p applies M as well, so W is really the parent chain's accumulated matrix and W·M is the object's world matrix. The two statements cannot both be right.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, perspective divide paragraph: The fourth clip coordinate is written w_k in one sentence and w_c everywhere else, including the formulas just below.
- ch 5 [low] Section 4, viewport formula: The text allows '(X, Y) is the viewport's lower-left or top-left origin', but the y formula y_px = Y + (1 − y_ndc)·H/2 only holds for a top-left origin with rows counted downward.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, fourth trap (screen size vs distance): The trap states the rule correctly (use distance to the bounding-sphere centre) but then draws the wrong conclusion from it. With the centre rule the projected size is under-estimated, so a long object loses detail too early, not too late. Staying at high detail far too long is what happens when you use the distance to the nearest vertex. As written, a student will attach the effect to the wrong method and learn the opposite lesson.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: "The view frustum is the six-sided pyramid of space the camera can see" is loose: a frustum is a pyramid with its top cut off, bounded by six planes. A pyramid has five faces, so "six-sided pyramid" reads as a hexagonal-based pyramid.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, terrain paragraph: "This is chunked LOD, also called geomipmapping" treats two separately published terrain schemes as one name. Chunked LOD is the quadtree of chunks with per-chunk meshes; geomipmapping is a fixed grid of square patches, each with a few discrete levels and stitched edges. The two are related, not synonyms.
- ch 6 [low] Figure, LOD node: The node reads 'LOD 40%' with the note '1,000 tris each', but after LOD each object has 400 triangles. 1,500 objects at 1,000 triangles each would be 1,500,000 triangles, not the 600,000 shown at the end of the flow.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, backface culling: "A triangle whose vertices wind clockwise on screen is a back face" is only true under a stated convention. If front faces are counter-clockwise (the usual OpenGL default) the statement holds; Direct3D defaults to clockwise front faces, and then it is wrong.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, occlusion queries: "how many pixels a bounding box actually wrote" is imprecise: during an occlusion query the box writes no colour. The query counts fragments or samples that pass the depth test.
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, third trap (occlusion culling outdoors): "An outdoor scene gets no gain from it" is overstated. Terrains have real occluders: hills, buildings, walls, trees. Also a wire fence does occlude partially; the point is that it rarely hides a whole object.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: Listing "backface during rasterisation, then frustum per object" as the cheap-first order confuses cost order with pipeline order: per-object frustum culling has to happen before the object is rasterised at all, since backface culling needs the triangles already in front of the rasteriser.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3 and Worked example 1, steps 4–5 (also quiz Q4): Section 3 says that with pipelining the stages overlap and the rate is set by the slowest stage instead of the sum, and the exam traps repeat that pipelining raises the frame rate without shortening latency. But step 4 then computes the highest sustained rate as 1000 ÷ T, where T is the whole 12 ms chain, and step 5 says the system can only hold 83.3 frames per second. The two rules contradict each other, and Q4's answer (ten ms chain, 100 frames per second) repeats the contradiction. A student cannot tell which rule to apply.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, formula note: The note defines f twice: once as 'the display refresh rate in hertz' and again as 'f is frames per second'. A student may think these are two different quantities and look for two symbols.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: 'the GPU shades thousands of pixels per pass' clashes with Section 1, which says the GPU works over millions of pixels. A frame shades millions of pixels; only one pass works on thousands at a time.
- ch 8 [high] Worked example 1, output block: The output block does not match the stated answer and cannot be read as either the quaternion or the rotated point. It shows six numbers (0.7071, 0.7071, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0, 0.0), while the answer is q = 0.7071 + 0i + 0j + 0.7071k and p′ = (0, 1, 0). A student checking their work against this block will think either the quaternion or the point has wrong components.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, bullet 'To a matrix': 'Read yaw, pitch and roll from that matrix by the outline above' points to an outline that the chapter never gives. Nothing above explains how to extract Euler angles from a rotation matrix, so the sentence sends the student looking for missing text.
- ch 8 [low] Figure caption: The caption says yaw, pitch and roll are 'each named after its axis'. The names come from the motion (head shake, nod, head tilt), not from the axis names; the axes are up, side and forward. A student may take this to mean the angles are called 'up', 'side' and 'forward'.
