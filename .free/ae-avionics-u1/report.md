## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3, closing paragraph ("The chain is sized by gearing"): The sentence "Too little gearing gives a twitchy aircraft; too much gives a heavy one" contradicts the gearing definition used in this chapter (mm of column travel per degree of surface). With gearing r = stick travel ÷ surface angle, the stick force for a given hinge moment H is F = H ÷ r. So a large r (many mm per degree) gives light stick forces, not heavy ones: the pilot moves the stick a long way and pushes lightly, so the response is sluggish but the controls feel light. Small r gives both a twitchy response and high stick forces, because a tiny stick movement gives a large deflection.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2 heading and first line: The heading says secondary controls "change the wing, not the attitude directly", but the list includes trim tabs and throttle, which act on the surface hinge moment and on thrust, not on the wing. The paragraph at the end of the section says this correctly, so the heading misleads a quick reader.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, steps 3 to 4, and Quiz Q7: The hinge moment is taken as force × horn arm, which is only true when the push-rod force acts perpendicular to the horn arm. The assumption is not stated, so a student may apply the formula to a rod pulling at an angle.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: "Must beat the opposing aerodynamic hinge moment before the elevator moves" suggests the rod moment only has to be larger. In a steady hold the two moments balance; the rod moment must exceed the aerodynamic moment only to accelerate the surface.
- ch 1 [low] Beyond paragraph: "Even an Airbus or a Boeing with sidesticks keeps a physical path for the rudder and for the trim in some cases" is vague and can be read as true of every fly-by-wire type. It is not; the mechanical backup differs from type to type.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, steps 1 to 4: Gearing (mm of stick travel per degree) and mechanical advantage are treated as unrelated numbers. For a rod driving the horn they are the same kinematic ratio: gearing = mechanical advantage × horn arm (in consistent units). Taken together, 4.8 mm/deg with an 80 mm arm implies a mechanical advantage of about 3.4, not 4, so the numbers look inconsistent to a careful student.
- ch 2 [low] Figure (Rod path and cable path): The blocks run control column → bell crank → both a rod and a cable loop → horn. This shows the rod and the cable as two branches hanging off the same bell crank, so a student may read it as one aircraft using both paths in series. A bell crank also belongs to the rod run, not to the start of a cable loop.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, rod end description: 'It removes side loads' is misleading. A spherical rod end cannot remove a side load; it simply carries axial load only, so any side load must be reacted by the guides or idler levers, and no bending moment is passed into the tube.
- ch 2 [low] Beyond: 'Modern transports keep cables only as a backup or for secondary controls; the main path is fly-by-wire' overgeneralises. Many airliners in service today still use conventional cable and rod primary controls with hydraulic boost, and fly-by-wire is common mainly on newer designs.
- ch 3 [high] Section 1, first paragraph: The sentence says the air load 'acts mostly near the hinge line, so it resists deflection with a moment about the hinge'. A load acting near the hinge would give almost no moment, so the 'so' does not follow and a student will picture the wrong physics. The load is spread over the whole surface, and its resultant centre of pressure lies well aft of the hinge.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, paragraph on manual reversion: The text says the pilot can fly on the same linkage after hydraulic failure, but does not say that the actuator must be bypassed. As written, a reader can conclude that the trapped fluid which 'locks the actuator' a few lines earlier would also block manual reversion.
- ch 3 [low] Lead and Section 3, opening paragraph: 'Every airliner and fighter flies this way' is too strong. Many large transports and most fighters are fully powered, but several smaller transports, turboprops, trainers and business aircraft use boosted (power-assisted) controls.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2 and Worked example 1, step 4: The boost ratio is used as a defined quantity only inside the worked example, where it appears as M divided by the pilot's moment. A student meeting 'boost ratios of about 20 to 40' in Section 2 has not been told what is being compared with what.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3, tab table, row 'Geared, balance': The table says a geared balance tab moves 'with the surface' and still cuts hinge moment. A balance tab geared to the control surface deflects in the opposite direction to the surface; that opposite lift at the trailing edge is what produces the assisting moment and cuts hinge moment. Moving with the surface would increase hinge moment, which is the anti-servo job listed two rows below.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3, tab table, row 'Spring': The row gives the movement as 'with it, only under load' and the job as 'assists, adds feel'. A spring tab is a servo tab with a spring in its linkage: while the load is below the spring setting the tab does not deflect relative to the surface, and once the load is large enough the spring yields and the tab deflects opposite to the surface and assists. It reduces pilot effort; it does not add feel (adding feel is the anti-servo tab's job, as the memory line itself says).
- ch 4 [high] Worked example 1, steps 2 and 5 and the answer: The example says the aircraft is trimmed at 75 m/s, but trim means zero stick force there, yet step 2 calls 10.34 N 'the trim reference'. With the gradient given as 3 N per kPa counted from zero q, the force at 150 m/s is 41.34 N absolute; measured from a 75 m/s trim, the pilot must apply only 41.34 − 10.34 = 31.0 N. The stated answer of 41.34 N contradicts the trim condition.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps, bullet 2: 'Using indicated airspeed where the law needs true airspeed' is misleading. The Q-feel unit senses impact pressure (pitot minus static), which is exactly ½ρV² with true airspeed and local density, and also equals ½ρ₀V_E², so an indicated/equivalent airspeed with sea-level density gives the same q. The trap as written contradicts the sensing chain described in Section 2.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, anti-servo paragraph: 'All-moving tailplanes, which have no fixed fin behind them' uses the wrong word: a fin is vertical, and the reason for the anti-servo tab is that there is no fixed horizontal stabilizer behind the moving surface to supply a natural hinge-moment rise.
- ch 4 [low] Lead paragraph: 'every transport aircraft uses both ideas' overstates it. Artificial feel is universal on irreversible powered controls, but many fly-by-wire transports have no servo tabs at all and generate feel in software.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, step 4: The sentence says 'voting and isolation reduce it further' after the all-lanes-fail probability. Once every lane has failed there is nothing left to vote on or isolate, so this teaches the wrong mechanism. Voting and isolation act before the all-lanes-fail event, not after it.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, third bullet: 'Digital FBW is not just analog FBW with a computer' is confusing because analog FBW also used a computer. The real difference is sampling and software.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q6: The question asks for two reasons FBL suits fuel tanks, but the answer gives 'no spark hazard' plus 'immunity to EMI and ground loops'. EMI immunity is not a fuel-tank reason; it is a general FBL advantage.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q4: The answer writes 'pk' as plain text, which reads as p times k instead of p to the power k.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, signal list: 'dynamic pressure qbar' uses a spelled-out symbol and gives no unit, unlike the other quantities.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q2: The answer says a 'sidestick gradient must create stick force that rises with speed and deflection'. A plain spring gradient in a sidestick does not rise with speed; only Q-feel does.
- ch 6 [high] Worked example 1: The sign convention is wrong. If positive aileron is right and p is roll rate positive right, an aircraft 4° left of the commanded bank has e = +4° (needs right aileron), but rolling left has p = −2°/s. The formula gives δ = 3×4 − 0.8×(−2) = 13.6° right. Rate feedback adds 1.6°, because it opposes the left roll. The example treats left roll rate as positive and subtracts, which is anti-damping.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, formula: p is defined as body rate, but p conventionally means roll rate. For the elevator channel the rate is pitch rate q. The same formula cannot use p for both aileron and elevator without saying which rate.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3: The sentence 'if speed is low, the autopilot may command a descent' is misleading. In the normal split, the autopilot holds the selected path with pitch, and the auto-throttle adds thrust for low speed. Low speed alone does not make the autopilot command a descent.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 and Quiz Q6: A turn coordinator is primarily a rate-of-turn instrument. Its canted gyro also responds to roll rate, but calling it simply a roll-rate sensor is incomplete and may make students think it measures bank angle.
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, third bullet: The bullet says 'Rate feedback is subtracted' without stating the sign convention. If the aircraft is rolling away from the target, the rate term adds to the correction. This is the confusion in Worked example 1.
- ch 7 [high] Exam traps, bullet 2: The claim that 'a high-pass would react to a steady climb and to sensor noise' is wrong. A high-pass filter rejects the steady (DC) part, so it would <i>not</i> react to a steady climb; it would pass sensor noise. It is a low-pass that would follow the steady climb. As written the trap contradicts the Q4 answer and teaches the filter behaviour backwards.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, load alleviation paragraph: 'the short-period gust response, roughly 1–3 Hz' mixes two different things. 'Short-period' is the standard name for the pitch mode, which for transports sits well below 1 Hz (order 0.2–0.5 Hz); 1–3 Hz is the gust and flexible-wing response band. A student can carry away the wrong frequency for the short-period mode.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, flutter suppression sentence: 'drives the surface out of phase at the flutter frequency' is too vague — out of phase with what? Damping comes from a force in phase with velocity, that is 90° out of phase with displacement. Unqualified, the sentence could be read as simply opposing the motion.
- ch 8 [high] Section 1, sentence 'The outputs are the fuel metering valve, variable stator vanes, bleed valves, ignition and the thrust reverser': A hydro-mechanical fuel control unit (FCU) is a fuel-metering device. It does not switch the ignition system and does not command the thrust reverser; on these engines ignition and reversal are separate systems. Students will wrongly learn that the FCU drives ignition and the reverser.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, sentence on EGT and TGT: EGT and TGT are not 'the same limit'. They are the same kind of temperature measured at different stations involving the turbine, so the two limits have different numerical values and are not interchangeable.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, first paragraph: Stating flatly that the thrust parameter is N1 is only true for most turbofans. Other engines use N2 or engine pressure ratio (EPR) as the thrust-setting parameter, and a student may think N1 is universal.
- ch 8 [low] Figure, sensors node note: The sensors node note reads 'P1, T1, N2, EGT' and leaves out N1, but the caption and the aria text say the sensors return N1, N2 and EGT. The feedback path therefore contradicts the figure's own description.
