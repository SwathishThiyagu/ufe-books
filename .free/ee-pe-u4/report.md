## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 1: The chapter never says where the load is connected. It only says S1 connects the load to +Vdc/2 and S2 to −Vdc/2, so a student cannot tell that the other load terminal is the midpoint of C1 and C2.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q1: The question asks for the 'peak amplitude', but the chapter also uses 'peak' for the peak of the fundamental (2Vdc/π). The answer '±Vdc/2' mixes polarity with magnitude and does not separate the square-wave amplitude from the fundamental peak.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, Fourier formula block: The 'where' list defines n as the harmonic order, but n does not appear in the written series, which shows only the 1/3 and 1/5 terms. The general term is never shown.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, harmonic rms formula: The harmonic rms formula is given only for the full bridge. A student may apply 4Vdc/(nπ√2) to the half-bridge, where the fundamental and harmonic amplitudes are half as large.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3 (120° conduction mode): The chapter says each leg has one 60° interval with both switches off. In 120° conduction each device conducts 120°, so the two devices of a leg together use only 240° and the leg is inactive for 120° per cycle, split into two separate 60° intervals (for example, top device 0°–120°, bottom device 180°–300°, leg off 120°–180° and 300°–360°). A single 60° gap would leave 60° of the cycle unaccounted for.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3 (120° conduction mode): The reason given for the lower fundamental is wrong: 'the line-to-line waveform has wider zero-voltage intervals'. For the standard resistive star-load analysis, the 120° line-to-line voltage has no zero intervals at all. It steps through four levels, +Vdc, +Vdc/2, −Vdc/2 and −Vdc, because in each 60° block one line is at ±Vdc/2 and the other line is floating at the neutral potential.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q2: The answer repeats the wrong gap count: 'Each leg has a 60° gap with both switches off'. Exam trap 5 ('a 60° gap in each leg') repeats it as well. The conclusion that a leg cannot short the bus is still right; only the number is wrong.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3 (120° conduction mode): 'a leg can never short the DC bus, even if a gate signal is wrong' is too strong. A gate signal that turns on both devices of a leg at the same time will still short the bus; the protection comes from the gate pattern itself, not from the circuit.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3 and Quiz Q7: 'the phase terminal is not actively connected to either rail' is misleading. When the current freewheels through a bridge diode, that diode does tie the terminal to one rail, and the terminal voltage is fixed by it; only the switches are off.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 (three legs and the 180° switch table): 'this gives one valid path from the positive rail through the load to the negative rail' is not accurate. With S1, S5 and S6 on, current enters the load on phases A and C and returns on phase B, so there are two parallel paths, not one. A student may conclude that only two phases carry current.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3 (paragraph on the trade-off) and Exam traps, third bullet: The switching count is wrong by a factor of two. The text says "Each pulse edge is a commutation, so p pulses per half cycle give 2p commutations per cycle", and the trap repeats "a full cycle has 2p pulses and 2p commutations". A full cycle has 2p pulses and every pulse has two edges (turn-on and turn-off), so the output changes level 4p times per cycle. The number 2p is the pulse count, not the edge count.
- ch 3 [low] Lead and Quiz Q1: The lead says "One notch gives single-pulse modulation" and Q1 says "One zero-voltage notch per half cycle", but Section 1 describes the positive half cycle as +Vdc for 2d centred at 90° and zero for the rest - that is two zero intervals inside the half cycle, or equivalently one zero notch of width π−2d straddling each zero crossing. Students will not be able to reconcile the two statements.
- ch 3 [low] Lead, first sentence: "the fundamental is fixed at 4Vdc/π" states a peak value without saying so; the rest of the chapter and the figure use "fundamental peak". A student may take 4Vdc/π as an rms figure.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 (also the T1/T2 formula block and exam trap 3): The symbols V1 and V2 are reused as generic names for the two active vectors that bound any sector, but in Section 1 they are fixed labels (V1 = 100, V2 = 110). A student working in sector 4 may read the formula as asking for the states 100 and 110 instead of V4 and V5.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, paragraph 2: 'Draw these three voltages as one vector in the αβ plane' uses the αβ plane and the vector definition without saying what they are. The length 2Vdc/3 follows only from the 2/3 scaling convention, which is never stated.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, first sentence: 'T0 must not be one frozen block with all switches held' is unclear. T0 is a time, not a state, and 'all switches held' is ambiguous.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, carrier-based equivalence: 'matches sine-triangle PWM with a common-mode third-harmonic component added to the references' is only an approximation. The exact equivalence is with the min–max zero-sequence -(max+min)/2, which contains a third harmonic plus higher triplen terms.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The answer states the two numbers but not why the limit is lower, and the closing sentence 'the vector length and the phase peak are different quantities' misleads: for the reference vector, |Vref| is the phase peak Vm.
- ch 6 [high] Section 1 and Worked example 1: The waveform described does not produce the formula given. If the output really "starts at +Vdc each quarter cycle" and the first switching instant at α1 takes it to zero, the coefficient is b_n = (4Vdc/(nπ))(1 − cos nα1). At α1 = 30° that gives b1 = 280.1 × (1 − 0.8660) = 37.5 V and b3 = 93.37 × (1 − 0) = 93.4 V, not the 242.6 V and 0 V of the example. The formula b_n = (4Vdc/(nπ))Σ(−1)^(k−1) cos(nα_k) belongs to a different waveform: output zero at the start of each quarter, rising to +Vdc at α1, falling back to zero at α2, and so on, so the notch is centred on the zero crossing.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q7: The answer says "Only n = 3 gives cos 90° = 0 at this angle." At α1 = 30° the cosine also vanishes for n = 9 (cos 270°) and n = 15 (cos 450°), so a student who checks b9 finds zero and thinks the statement is wrong.
- ch 6 [low] Figure: The plotted expression is evaluated at every x from 1 to 15, so the curve is nonzero at even orders (for example about 0.32 at n = 2), while the caption and the text state that all even harmonics are zero. The picture contradicts its own caption.
- ch 6 [low] Beyond: "SHE appears in medium-voltage drives and grid-connected transformers" names the wrong equipment; the switching-angle method is applied in the converter, not the transformer.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, paragraph on load voltage: The sentence 'leakage inductance and any parallel capacitor smooth the current pulses into a nearly sinusoidal voltage' is muddled: inductance and capacitance do not smooth current pulses into a voltage; they filter the quasi-square current so that the terminal voltage comes out nearly sinusoidal. A student can read it as if the current were the thing being made sinusoidal.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, last sentence before Section 3: 'The load voltage is not square' is stated without a condition. It is true for an inductive or motor load, but not for the purely resistive load used in the worked example, where the phase voltage is just a scaled copy of the current and is also a quasi-square.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: The displayed arithmetic does not give the displayed result: √(1666.3 − 1519.8) = √146.5 = 12.10 A, but the step writes 12.12 A. The final answer 12.12 A matches the more precise squares (1666.27 − 1519.83 = 146.44, √ = 12.10), so the intermediate numbers and the stated result disagree slightly.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, induction heating paragraph: The cross-reference is plain text, '(see Unit 6, Induction heating)', so it is not a working link like the other chapter references in the book.
- ch 8 [high] Exam traps, item 4: The trap says the DC-bus capacitors are counted per leg ('two capacitors and two clamping diodes per leg'), and that the counts are 'not per whole inverter'. Only the clamping diodes are per leg. The m−1 DC-bus capacitors form the shared DC bus for the whole inverter, not one set per leg; a three-phase three-level NPC has two DC-bus capacitors in total, not two per leg.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: 'A diode-clamped leg, also called neutral-point-clamped (NPC)' treats the two names as fully interchangeable. NPC is really the three-level diode-clamped circuit; four- and five-level diode-clamped legs are usually just called diode-clamped.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1, the sentence that sets I*: "A DC-link or MPPT loop compares available DC power with delivered AC power and sets I*" blends two different functions. MPPT hunts the PV operating point; it does not compare DC power with AC power. The block that actually produces the active current reference is the DC-link voltage controller, which reacts to the power imbalance.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, reactive power paragraph: The text says the amplitude "follows the power demand and the reactive limit" without stating the catch: at a fixed I*, changing φ* also changes real power, because P = V_rms I_rms cos φ. A student can conclude that φ* shifts reactive power alone.
- ch 9 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: Two different faults (commanding a voltage amplitude, and omitting the PLL) are joined to one consequence (wrong phase and reactive power). Commanding voltage amplitude gives an uncontrolled current set by the impedance difference, not necessarily a wrong phase.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, opening lines: "A current loop often runs at 1–2 kHz" is ambiguous: 1–2 kHz is the achievable closed-loop bandwidth, while the switching and sampling frequencies are much higher. A student may read it as the PWM switching frequency.
