## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] figure (aria description): The screen-reader description says the steeper warm line means warm solvent needs a much higher liquid concentration for the same gas concentration. That is backwards: a steeper line means that at the same y the warmer solvent needs a LOWER x, because less gas dissolves. It directly contradicts the figure caption and the quiz Q6 answer.
- ch 1 [low] figure: The caption labels the lines as SO2 in water, but the plotted slopes m = 2 (10 °C) and m = 5 (30 °C) mean H = 2 and 5 atm per mole fraction at 1 atm, far below the real Henry's constant for SO2 in water (roughly 20 to 50 atm per mole fraction in this temperature range). The chapter's own worked example uses H = 40 atm per mole fraction for SO2 in water, so the two numbers clash.
- ch 1 [low] section 1: The chapter never states when Henry's law holds. Students then apply pA = H xA to concentrated solutions and to gases such as SO2, NH3 or CO2 that react with water, where the law describes only the physically dissolved species.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, step-by-step bullets, first bullet: G = total gas flow × (1 − y) does not say that y must be the mole fraction at the same terminal where the total gas flow is known. A student may pair the bottom total flow with y_in and the top total flow with y_out.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps, second bullet: L = L_total(1 − x) leaves x undefined; the correction applies only to the entering solvent composition. Using x at the outlet would understate L.
- ch 2 [low] Figure caption and aria text (also Section 3): 'Smallest at the lean-gas top' is true only for the numbers plotted (L/G = 2.1053 is well above the minimum 1.809, so the gap grows downwards). With solvent near the minimum rate the pinch is at the rich-gas bottom, and there the smallest gap is at the bottom. Stated without the condition, it teaches a false general rule.
- ch 3 [high] Beyond: The solvent argument is backwards. A steep equilibrium line means a large slope m, and the chapter's own result (L/G)_min = (Y_in − Y_out)/(Y_in/m − X_in) shows that a large m makes X*_in small and (L/G)_min large. So a steep equilibrium line raises the minimum liquid rate; it does not lower it. The amine example also contradicts the sentence, since amines are good precisely because they hold CO2 with a shallow (low-m) equilibrium line, giving a low liquid demand.
- ch 3 [high] Quiz Q6: The gap expression has the wrong sign, so the stated reasoning contradicts the stated result. In absorption the driving force is Y_operating − Y*, that is Y_out + (L/G)(X − X_in) − mX. The expression written, mX − Y_out − (L/G)(X − X_in), is Y* − Y_operating, and it rises with X (slope m − L/G > 0), so it is smallest at the top, not the bottom. A student copying this gets a wrong line of algebra even though the conclusion (pinch at the bottom) is right.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, paragraph on the pinch: "The driving force is zero at the crossing, and the column needs infinite stages" is loose. If L/G is below the minimum the operating line crosses the equilibrium line, the driving force turns negative beyond the crossing, and the specified Y_in cannot be reached at any height. Infinite height is the situation at exactly (L/G)_min, not below it.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 4: Rounding is inconsistent. Step 3 rounds the design ratio to 2.71, but step 4 reports 2.71 × 95 = 257.7 kmol/h, while 2.71 × 95 = 257.45 kmol/h (about 257.5). The value 257.7 comes from the unrounded 2.713, which is fine, but the two steps should use the same number.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, definition of m: The slope m is described as 'kmol gas per kmol liquid'. For Y* in kmol solute/kmol carrier gas and X in kmol solute/kmol solvent, m = Y*/X has units kmol solvent/kmol carrier gas, so the stated unit is the reciprocal of the correct one.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, cross-current description: 'A stage receives gas of composition Yin' is true only for the first stage. Later stages receive gas at the composition leaving the previous stage, so the generic 'a stage' is misleading.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, 'each stage sees a larger driving force': Unqualified, this can be read as saying cross-current has a larger average driving force than counter-current, which contradicts Section 2. The larger driving force occurs at the start of each stage because the liquid is fresh; over the whole cascade the average is smaller than counter-current's.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, 'keeps a positive driving force over the whole column': This is true only when L/G is above the minimum (pinch) value. At a lower solvent rate the operating line crosses the equilibrium line somewhere in the column and the driving force becomes negative.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, 'Cross-current lies between them in stage count': For the same duty and L/G, co-current may not reach the duty at all, so there is no co-current stage count for cross-current to lie between. The comparison is unclear.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q5: The explanation is wrong for the plotted 90 % removal case. At A = 1 the operating and equilibrium lines are parallel, but the driving force is not zero; for fixed removal the stage count approaches the finite limit N = R/(1−R) = 9 stages, not infinity. The stage count rises without bound only near the pinch A = R for fresh solvent (A = 0.9 here), not at A = 1.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, problem statement: It says L = 2.72 kmol per kmol of inert gas, but L is a total molar flow in kmol/h and the ratio is L/G. This is a unit/notation slip.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q6 answer: The answer says a '50–70 % margin' and that operating at 50–70 % of the flooding rate 'keeps the bed below loading'. The margin below flooding is 30–50 %, not 50–70 %; and the loading point typically sits around 65–85 % of the flooding gas rate, so a design point at 70 % of flooding can be at or above loading. A student will carry both errors into the exam.
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, first bullet: The sentence 'A 25 mm ring has a near 200 m²/m³, not 25 m²/m³' reads badly: the symbol a is used as an English word and the sentence can be misread.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, definition of a (and Quiz Q1 answer): a is defined as the 'wetted area' of packing per unit volume of bed, then used as a fixed geometric property of the packing. The standard definition is the total surface area of the packing per unit volume of bed; the wetted area equals it only when the liquid covers the whole surface.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, bullet on size and void fraction: The statement that ε usually lies between 0.6 and 0.95 is too tight: gauze packings and the more open structured sheet packings reach about 0.95–0.98, and the chapter's own later discussion of structured packings expects high voidage.
- ch 7 [high] Worked example 1, step 8: The design height is 2.62 m. Rounding up to the next half metre gives 3.0 m, not 2.7 m. The chapter's own rule says 'round the built height upward to the next half metre'. Quiz Q5 applies that rule correctly (4.29 m becomes 4.5 m), so the example contradicts the rule and the quiz.
- ch 7 [high] Exam traps, first bullet: The explanation says the bottom driving force is understated and so the bed comes out short. That causality is wrong: a smaller driving force in the denominator would make NOG larger and the bed taller. The net effect is shorter only because dy < dY, not because the driving force is smaller.
- ch 8 [high] Section 3, stripping paragraph: It says the liquid cools as solute leaves and 'the falling m helps the stripping'. This is backwards. Cooling lowers m, so the equilibrium gas composition Y* = mX at each liquid concentration falls towards the stripping operating line, and the driving force (Y* − Y) shrinks. That is exactly why stripping must be heated: warming the liquid raises m, raises Y*, and opens the driving force.
- ch 8 [high] Figure and its caption / aria: The plotted numbers do not show what the caption claims. With operating line Y = 0.005025 + 2.72X and the 50 °C line Y = 6X, the crossing is at X = 0.005025/(6 − 2.72) ≈ 0.0015, i.e. at the lean end of a 0 to 0.02 axis, not the rich end. Over the rest of the chart the hot equilibrium line is above the operating line, and at X = 0.02 the hot equilibrium Y* = 0.12 is far above Y = 0.0594, so the drawing shows the largest driving force exactly where the caption says it vanishes. A student reading the graph sees a pinch at the wrong end of the tower.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, 'The operating line is not fixed either': On the solute-free Y–X coordinates this chapter itself uses, the operating line is exactly straight at any concentration: L_s and G_s are the solute-free flows and neither changes as solute transfers, so the slope L_s/G_s is constant. The claim that L rises and G falls, and that a concentrated tower needs the line recomputed point by point, is wrong for Y–X and misleads students about why concentrated towers are harder.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, answer line: 'the driving force is smaller everywhere' overstates it. Both equilibrium lines pass through the origin, so at the lean end (X → 0) the driving force is essentially unchanged; only where X is appreciable does the 28 % steeper line cut it.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2, Section 3, Quiz Q6: The text writes the equilibrium line and back pressure as y = 0 (and Q6 says y ≈ 0). Here y is the actual gas mole fraction; if y were zero there would be no solute and no driving force. The intended quantity is the equilibrium gas mole fraction y* (or y_e).
- ch 9 [high] Section 2 fast-reaction bullet, Exam traps bullet 3: The rule E ≈ Ha is stated for all Ha above about 3. It is valid only when Ha is also much smaller than the instantaneous enhancement factor E_i. If Ha approaches or exceeds E_i, the reaction is instantaneous and the enhancement is limited by E_i, not Ha.
- ch 9 [low] Figure and Section 2 figure description: The fast-reaction curve exp(-3x) is a semi-infinite-medium approximation. For a finite film with zero bulk solute, the concentration at the film edge x/δ = 1 should be zero, not 0.05 c_Ai; the exact first-order film profile is c/c_Ai = sinh[Ha(1 − x/δ)]/sinh(Ha) with c = 0 at the film edge.
