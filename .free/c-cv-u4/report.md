## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 1, threshold definition: "T is a threshold in grey levels" suggests T is on the same 0–255 grey scale as the pixels. But R = Σ wᵢzᵢ is a weighted sum, so with the point mask R can reach about ±8×255. A student may pick T = 128 as a "grey level" and think it is a sensible threshold, when it is far too high for some masks and too low for others.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, edge models, roof edge: The sentence "the second derivative has a strong spike at the peak" leaves out that the second derivative of a roof edge is also non-zero where each ramp starts and ends. Standard treatments describe the roof second derivative as non-zero at the onset and end of the ramps, with the largest spike at the sharp apex. As written, a student would expect a single spike only.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3, double threshold bullet: The weak band is written as "a pixel with Tlow ≤ |G| high is weak". The text is garbled and the upper limit is missing, so a student cannot tell when a pixel is weak and when it is strong.
- ch 2 [high] Worked example 1, step 2: The Gy arithmetic uses 10 10 10 for the top and bottom rows, but every row of the given patch is 10 10 50. The stated numbers do not match the patch (the final answer 0 happens to be right).
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 and Section 3, direction formula: θ = tan⁻¹(Gy/Gx) loses the quadrant (the ratio is the same for opposite directions). The quadrant matters when NMS uses θ.
- ch 2 [low] Figure aria text: The figure caption says five main operations, but the aria description says "a six-step downward pipeline", so the two counts disagree.
- ch 3 [high] Exam traps, third trap (origin shift): It says moving the origin from the top-left corner to the image centre changes every ρ by a constant. This is false. If the new origin is at (x₀, y₀), then ρ' = (x−x₀)cos θ + (y−y₀)sin θ = ρ − (x₀ cos θ + y₀ sin θ). The shift −(x₀ cos θ + y₀ sin θ) depends on θ, so different θ columns move by different amounts and the accumulator cannot be reused after a change of origin.
- ch 3 [low] Worked example 1 and the accumulator figure: Section 2 says to quantise ρ in 1-pixel steps, but the example and figure use ρ bins at 1.41 and 2.83, i.e. about 0.71-pixel spacing. A student following the stated rule would round 1.41 to 1 and 2.83 to 3 and get different row labels.
- ch 4 [high] Exam traps, second trap: The trap says the equal values at k = 0 and k = 2 mean 'only the rule decides which pixels become 1'. That is false. Both 0.5625 values are below the maximum 0.81 at k = 1, so k = 1 wins outright and the split rule decides nothing in this example. A student will think there is a tie that must be broken.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q4 answer: The answer says Otsu rewards 'balanced class weights, not compact classes'. This contradicts Section 3, which shows σT² = σW² + σB² with σT² fixed, so maximising σB² is exactly minimising the within-class spread. Compact classes are precisely what Otsu rewards; unbalanced class sizes only weaken how sharply the criterion peaks.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q3 answer: The question asks for the Otsu threshold, but the answer tests only k = 0 and k = 1, so the choice of T = 1 is not actually justified over k = 2.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q4 answer, last sentence: 'Many background pixels are labelled foreground' does not follow from k = 120. With a peak at 40, class 0 covers levels 0 to 120, so nearly the whole peak stays background; what is wrongly labelled foreground is the tail above 120, which may be few pixels.
- ch 5 [high] Section 1 (also Exam trap 2 and Quiz Q2): The chapter says growing depends on the order of visiting neighbours and that 'two runs with different orders can give slightly different borders'. With the predicate actually defined, P(x,y): |f(x,y) − f(x_s,y_s)| ≤ T, this is false. P depends only on the pixel's own value and the fixed seed value, so every pixel connected to the seed by a path of pixels satisfying P is accepted, in any order. The grown set is exactly the connected component of the seed in {pixels with value in [f(seed)−T, f(seed)+T]}. A rejected pixel can never become acceptable later, so queue order cannot open or block a path. Order matters only when P uses running region statistics (for example the mean of the region grown so far) or a neighbour-difference rule such as |f(x,y) − f(accepted neighbour)| ≤ T. Quiz Q2's answer is therefore wrong as stated.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, final sentence (and Quiz Q7): It says 'the final regions are the leaves of the quadtree after allowed merges'. After merging, a region can join leaves that sit under different parents, so the merged region is a union of quadtree leaves, not a leaf, and its boundary does not follow quadtree lines. Q7's 'leaves are the regions left after splitting and allowed merges' has the same slip.
- ch 5 [low] Beyond box: 'Region growing is the base of superpixel methods such as SLIC' is inaccurate. SLIC forms superpixels by clustering pixels on colour plus (x,y) position, in the spirit of k-means, not by seeded region growing. Seeded region growing underlies other superpixel and interactive segmentation methods.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, marker-controlled watershed (morphological opening sentence): The role of opening is stated wrongly. Grayscale opening removes small BRIGHT bumps; it does not remove 'small dark details' — that is what closing does. Also, opening (which only lowers values) does not delete dark dips, so a shallow minimum is not removed by opening itself; the bright ridge separating two minima vanishes and the shallow basin gets swallowed by its deeper neighbour.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q4 answer: The answer says morphological opening on the gradient 'smooths small dips'. Opening cannot fill a dark dip. It removes small bright ridges between basins, which reduces the number of minima only indirectly.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, end of distance-transform paragraph: 'Some syllabi call this the watershed transformation and describe the Meyer flooding algorithm' has no clear subject: 'this' could be the distance transform, the flooding of -D, or watershed in general, and Meyer flooding is a general flooding algorithm, not a distance-transform method. A student may think Meyer flooding is part of the distance transform recipe.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1 and Section 3, chapter cross-references: Chapter 4.2 and Chapter 4.5 are named in plain text, so a reader on a phone cannot jump to them, although the book format allows chapter links.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, bullet on large β: "Large β: the snake refuses to bend, so it flattens and can shrink away from a weak edge." The shrinking under a weak edge is caused mainly by α (the length/stretch term acting like a taut rubber band). Large β makes the closed curve stiff and smooth, so it cannot bend into corners, dents or narrow concavities and cuts across them; it does not itself make the snake shrink.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, second bullet: "A large β with a weak edge makes the snake shrink on its own" repeats the same mixing of roles: shrinkage when the edge is weak is an α effect. With large β and a weak edge the curve instead becomes too rigid to enter concave parts of the boundary.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, discrete E_int formula note: "the second difference vᵢ₊₁ − 2vᵢ + vᵢ₋₁ is large exactly where the curve bends sharply" is only true when the sample points are evenly spaced. Uneven spacing also makes the second difference non-zero on a straight part of the contour.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, last bullet: "square every side length, do not take square roots at the last step" is confusing, and it contradicts the worked example, which does write √5 = 2.236 and then squares it. A student may read it as "never take a square root" or as "square the side length twice".
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, opening paragraph: Section 2 opens by saying the raw code depends on 'the start pixel and on the tracing direction', and then claims 'Normalisation removes these accidental differences.' The first difference and the shape number only remove the start point (and rotation by multiples of 45 degrees). They do not remove the tracing direction: the anticlockwise code of the 3x3 ring is 6 6 0 0 2 2 4 4, whose first difference is 2 0 2 0 2 0 2 0 and whose shape number is 02020202, not 06060606. A student will wrongly write that the shape number is independent of clockwise versus anticlockwise tracing.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, tracing steps (bullet 'From the previous direction, scan the 8 neighbours clockwise; take the first unvisited boundary pixel'): The rule is underspecified and the test 'unvisited' is not the standard one. Standard boundary following (Moore/square tracing) takes the first neighbour that belongs to the region, starting the clockwise scan from the neighbour the trace came from; skipping already visited pixels can jump over the correct next pixel where the boundary touches itself, and 'set the previous direction to point into the start' does not say what value to use at the very first step.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, curvature bullet: The claim 'a sharp bend gives a large first-difference change' is not true for differences taken mod 8: a 45 degree turn gives 7 (or 1), a 90 degree turn gives 6 (or 2), and a 180 degree reversal gives 4. The largest number therefore does not mean the sharpest bend, and a student can misread curvature from the code.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, resampling paragraph: The paragraph says only 'resample the boundary on a fixed grid spacing' and omits the usual convention that the number of codes (the order of the shape number) is chosen as a power of 2, such as 8, 16 or 32, so that the resampled grid is square and a 45 degree rotation still maps the grid onto itself.
- ch 9 [high] Quiz Q6: The answer is wrong for the figure actually shown. The hole at (1,1) is a single 0 whose eight neighbours are all 1s, so it has no background neighbour at all and stays a hole even with 8-connectivity for the background. H stays 1 and E stays 2, so nothing goes wrong. The leakage the answer describes needs a ring with a diagonal step, which this figure does not have.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, third moment bullet: 'negative means the histogram leans dark' can be read two ways and the common reading is wrong. A negative third moment means the tail points toward the dark levels, so most pixels are bright; the plain reading 'leans dark' says the opposite.
- ch 9 [low] Exam traps, compactness trap: A circle's P/A is 2/r, which depends on r and is not about 3.5. The number 3.5 only appears if the square is dropped from P but the square root is kept on A, giving P/√A = 2√π ≈ 3.54.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2 and Section 3: The letter H stands for the number of holes in Section 2 and the worked example, and for entropy in Section 3 and the same worked example. In the worked example the two meanings appear a few lines apart.
