## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 3 formula box and Worked example 1 (steps 1–3), Exam trap 2, Quiz Q5: The circuit as described contradicts the formula and every number built on it. The text says the thermistor R_s is connected from +5 V to the output node and the 1 kΩ fixed resistor R_f from the output node to ground. For that circuit the node voltage is V_out = V_s × R_f/(R_s + R_f), not V_s × R_s/(R_s + R_f); the formula given is the voltage across the thermistor, not the output. With the circuit as described the outputs would be 1.667 V at 25 °C and 3.333 V at 80 °C (counts 341 and 682), the output would rise with temperature, and Exam trap 2 ('the output falls with temperature, many answers show the voltage rising and lose all the marks') would itself be wrong. Quiz Q5 has the same fault: with 1.5 kΩ from +5 V and 1 kΩ to ground the node sits at 5 × 1000/2500 = 2.0 V (409 counts), not 3.0 V (614 counts).
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, first bullet (active vs passive): The chapter uses the automotive convention (active = needs an excitation supply, passive = self-generating). Many measurement and instrumentation textbooks use the opposite convention (active/self-generating transducer = thermocouple or piezoelectric; passive = thermistor, potentiometer, strain gauge). A student writing the instrumentation answer in an exam will be marked wrong without warning.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q7, answer: The proposed test for self-heating, 'compare readings with the supply briefly removed', cannot be done: with the excitation removed a thermistor divider produces no output to compare. Only the second half of that sentence (lower excitation current) is a usable test.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q1, answer: The question asks for one automotive difference in use, but the answer first says the words are interchangeable and then gives a general energy-conversion meaning. The promised 'difference' is never actually stated, so the 2 marks are not clearly earned.
- ch 2 [high] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 6: The labels are physically wrong for a multi-cylinder engine. Step 3 calls N ÷ 2 = 25 s⁻¹ the number of 'intake strokes per second'; at 3000 rpm each cylinder of a four-cylinder engine draws air 25 times per second, so the engine makes about 100 intake strokes per second. N ÷ 2 is the number of engine cycles (two-revolution cycles) per second. Step 6 then calls ρ × V_d (with V_d the total 1.6 L displacement) the 'ideal air mass per intake stroke', but one intake stroke of one cylinder takes in only V_d divided by the number of cylinders. The two errors cancel so the final number is right, but a student builds a wrong mental model.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, first paragraph (and quiz Q8): Volumetric efficiency is defined here at manifold conditions, and with that definition it stays below about 1 even on a boosted engine; the 'higher for turbocharged engines' claim holds only when VE is referenced to ambient pressure, which contradicts the definition given in the same paragraph.
- ch 2 [low] Lead paragraph: 'An air flow sensor measures this mass directly' is not true of every air flow meter described later; a vane meter measures volume flow, and only the hot-wire/hot-film meters give mass directly.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, signal shape paragraph: 'VR output is an AC waveform that crosses zero at each tooth edge' is wrong. The induced voltage is proportional to the rate of change of flux, so it is zero when the flux is maximum — that is, when the tooth is centred under the pole piece, not when a tooth edge passes. A student who takes the zero crossing as a tooth edge will put the reference point one half-tooth pitch off, and will not understand why a VR sensor and a Hall sensor on the same wheel need different calibration offsets.
- ch 3 [low] Figure caption and spec, compared with worked example 1: The figure says the gap lasts 999 µs, while the worked example gives 3 × 0.333333 ms = 1.000 ms for the same 3000 rpm and 60-2 wheel. The two numbers should agree.
- ch 3 [low] Quiz Q5: 'High coil resistance' is not a sound cause of a weak VR signal, and 'damaged tooth' does not explain a fault that disappears above 500 rpm. A shorted or open winding, or high resistance in the wiring and connector, lowers the signal; more turns of wire raise both the resistance and the voltage.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, toothed wheels sentence: 12+1 is listed with 60-2 and 36-1 but never explained, and the chapter's gap logic (a longer interval, gap = 3T or 2T, Δθ = 360°/z) does not apply to it. The 12+1 wheel has no missing tooth; its reference comes from one tooth spacing that is shorter than the other twelve, so the edge count per revolution is 13 and the spacing is uneven.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, Hall formula: The chapter defines K_H = 1/(n q t) and calls it 'the Hall coefficient in m²/C'. Standard textbooks define the Hall coefficient as R_H = 1/(n q), with units m³/C. The quantity 1/(n q t) is a sensitivity (it depends on plate thickness), not the Hall coefficient, so a student writing the textbook definition in the exam will lose marks.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, sentence on output: 'Hall sensors are used for cam and crank position, wheel speed, pedal position and gear selection. They need a supply and give a digital signal' — pedal position normally uses a linear (analog output) Hall sensor, so 'digital signal' contradicts the pedal example given in the same sentence.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, comparison table: The column header 'Dirt' with values High / Medium / Low can be read either as dirt tolerance or as dirt sensitivity. A student could read 'VR: High' as 'VR is badly affected by dirt', the opposite of what the text says.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, reference-error sentence: The text says 'A small reference error moves every reading, so the ECU supply is regulated and checked.' In most ECUs the sensor supply is also the ADC reference, so a drift in it cancels in the ratiometric measurement and does not move the reading. As written, a student may think every 5 V drift is a direct error.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q1, answer: The answer adds ignition timing and says the TPS measures 'throttle plate angle or accelerator position'. The section lists uses without ignition timing, and it keeps the throttle-plate TPS separate from the accelerator-pedal sensors. The mismatch can confuse a student reading both.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: 'The actuators are covered in 3.8' is a plain-text cross-reference. The house style asks for other chapters to be linked with the anchor tag.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q5, answer: The question states a constant 20 °C error at all temperatures, but the causes given (ground resistance, corroded connector, wrong pull-up) add a resistance or voltage offset. In an NTC divider that gives a temperature error that grows as the thermistor gets hot, not a fixed 20 °C everywhere.
- ch 6 [high] Section 1, paragraph on lambda definition: The comparison sign is missing: the text reads "λ  1 is lean (excess air)", so the sentence states nothing, and a student can read it as λ less than 1 being lean, which is the opposite of the truth.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, first bullet: Same missing sign: "The narrowband output is about 0.9 V at λ  1." The high-voltage case is rich, i.e. λ less than 1, so as printed the bullet contradicts itself (high voltage stated for the lean side).
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: "roughly 0.7 to air" is unclear. Air is not a λ value on the same scale; the upper end is free air, λ tending to infinity.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, first sentence: 'Knock is uncontrolled detonation after the spark' is loose. Standard texts define knock as autoignition of the end gas ahead of the flame front, which gives the sharp pressure rise; 'detonation' is a related but distinct term and some examiners mark this as a misuse.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, window description: The knock window is said to open 'around top dead centre'. Knock normally appears after the spark, roughly 10–40° after TDC in the combustion period, so 'around TDC' can make a student expect a symmetric window on both sides of TDC.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1 and Figure, rectifier stage: A rectifier on its own does not produce an envelope; the envelope comes only when the rectified signal is smoothed by a low-pass filter. The block diagram shows 'Rectifier → envelope' with no smoothing element.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3 and Quiz Q7: LIDAR and flex-fuel composition sensing sit outside the chapter title (knock, acceleration and torque sensors). The chapter itself points to Unit 5 for detail, so carrying a full LIDAR formula, a worked quiz question and exam traps on it risks duplication and confusion about which unit to revise.
- ch 8 [high] Section 1, sentence listing applications: "ignition coil drivers use this pattern" is wrong. The pattern just described is a driver with a freewheeling diode across the coil. An ignition coil primary is switched without a freewheeling diode on purpose: the diode would clamp the inductive kick that produces the spark. It also contradicts the chapter's own exam trap, which says the spike can destroy the driver.
- ch 8 [low] Figure caption: "Notice that the freewheeling diode sits across the coil even though it is not drawn here" tells the student to notice something that is absent. The caption contradicts itself and confuses the reader.
- ch 8 [low] Exam traps, last bullet: "Torque per microstep is lower" can be misread as the motor producing less torque in microstepping. What is true is that the incremental torque gained per microstep (or per unit of current change) is smaller; the maximum holding torque is unchanged.
- ch 9 [low] Lead: 'an isolated divider measures voltage' can read as if the resistor divider itself isolates the high-voltage pack; a resistive divider alone does not provide isolation, and a student may take away a wrong safety architecture.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3: The discrete form 'ΔSOC = I × Δt / C_n' has no minus sign, while discharge is defined as positive and SOC must fall. Read as the change in SOC, the formula gives the wrong sign; only the later 'SOC drop' wording makes it correct.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3: 'The estimate drifts because current offset and capacity fade accumulate' mixes two different effects: current offset and gain error cause accumulating drift, while capacity fade is a scale error from a stale C_n, not drift.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q4: The question says the counter reads 0 A at rest, which rules out a static offset at rest, yet the answer gives 'current offset' as a cause; the premise and the answer do not fit together cleanly.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3 and Quiz Q7: SOF is defined using only present SOC and temperature. Power capability also falls as the pack ages, so SOH belongs in the definition; as written, a student can miss that dependence.
