## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 2, energy-source table, first row: Gas cutting is listed as a 'Fusion' process in a table of joining processes. Oxy-fuel gas cutting separates metal by burning it away; it joins nothing, so putting it under a welding family contradicts the chapter's own definition of coalescence.
- ch 1 [low] Worked example 1, step 7 and answer: It says 'a brazing alloy melting between 300 °C and 1085 °C is safe' and 'a filler melting above 300 °C'. A filler melting at, say, 350 °C is below the 450 °C line, so by this chapter's own rule it is a solder, and at a 300 °C service temperature it has almost no margin. The range given is looser than the definition.
- ch 1 [low] Lead, Section 1 and the figure caption: The text says there are three families and treats brazing plus soldering as one filler-melting family, but the tree shows five separate branches (Fusion, Solid state, Brazing, Soldering, Adhesive) under 'Joining', and the caption still says 'three families'. A student cannot tell whether brazing/soldering are one family or two, or where adhesive fits.
- ch 2 [high] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 4 and the answer: The example applies pV = constant to the acetylene cylinder: 15 bar × 40 L = 600 L, then 6 m³ ÷ 0.6 m³ = 10 cylinders. This is wrong. Acetylene in the cylinder is not a free gas filling the 40 L shell; it is dissolved in acetone held in the porous mass. The 15 bar is only the pressure over the acetone. A 40 L acetylene cylinder delivers roughly 6 m³ (about 6000 L) of gas, not 600 L, so one oxygen cylinder serves about one acetylene cylinder, not ten. The stated answer also contradicts step 5: if acetylene were only 600 L, the torch at 300 L/h would run out in 2 hours, not weld for 20 hours.
- ch 2 [low] Figure, node notes for the cylinders: The notes read 'O₂ cylinder: 150 bar, 40 L' and 'C₂H₂ cylinder: 15 bar, 40 L'. Seen side by side, this suggests the acetylene cylinder holds one-tenth as much gas, which is false and pushes students toward the error in the worked example.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, gas cutting bullet list: The four cutting steps appear with no sentence saying what gas cutting is, so the first bullet ('preheat the steel to its ignition temperature') arrives before the student is told that the oxygen jet burns the iron. The explanation comes only after the list.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, bullet list: 'Open the acetylene valve, then the oxygen valve, slowly' does not say which valves. A student may read this as the cylinder valves instead of the torch needle valves.
- ch 3 [low] Lead: The lead says the arc physics 'fixes the duty cycle a machine can safely deliver'. Duty cycle is set by the machine's thermal design (heating from I² times time, and cooling), not by the arc physics itself. A student may think duty cycle is an arc property.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 formula, against Section 2: Section 1 states V_c and V_a are nearly fixed, but Section 2 says at high current the cathode and anode drops increase. The two statements pull in opposite directions without explanation.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, operating point: The text says the arc runs where the source curve and the arc curve cross, but never states the condition for that crossing to be stable. A student can pick any crossing, including an unstable one.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, cathode spot: The sentence 'the cathode spot is small and does not sit still; it wanders over the surface' is true for cold cathodes with oxide films, but on a hot thermionic electrode such as thoriated tungsten the arc root stays at the tip. Stated without qualification it misleads TIG readers.
- ch 3 [low] Figure spec, 'Drooping source': The line 80 − 0.22x falls below 0 V above about 364 A; at 600 A it is −52 V. A voltage plot that runs the source curve below the axis looks wrong and hides where the useful part of the line is.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps, first trap: 'Not the arc-on time alone' is confusing. Duty cycle is in fact the arc-on time as a fraction of the 10-minute period, so this wording suggests the opposite of the definition given in Section 3.
- ch 4 [high] Lead: The lead says the arc itself is at 1500–2500 °C. That range is the melting range of the metal (steel melts near 1500 °C), not the arc temperature. An arc column is several thousand degrees hot, so a student learns a badly wrong number in the first sentence.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q1 (fourth digit of E6013): Saying E6013 runs on 'AC or DCEP' only is incomplete. The high-titania potassium (rutile) coating of E6013 also runs on DCEN, and a student may mark DCEN as wrong in an exam.
- ch 4 [low] Worked example 1, step 5 and answer: The 4800 kJ is heat input after multiplying by arc efficiency 0.8, so it is the net heat delivered to the joint. Total arc energy from the arc is V×I/v × length = 6000 kJ. Labelling 4800 kJ 'arc energy' mixes the two terms.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: The sentence says spot welding 'makes one nugget per cycle'. Earlier the chapter fixed 'cycle' as one electrical cycle of 20 ms at 50 Hz, and a real spot weld takes several cycles of current. A student can read this as one nugget every 20 ms.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: 'Electrode wear opens the contact face' is unclear. Wear makes the tip flatten and mushroom, so the contact area grows, not 'opens'.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, first paragraph under the heading: The list of four resistances is a grouped list, but it is written as if there is only one electrode contact and one sheet bulk. In the real circuit there are two electrode-to-sheet contacts and two sheet bulks, which is why textbooks often count seven resistance elements.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q5 answer: The question asks for two reasons seam welding is not a different process, but the first reason given ('it uses roller electrodes') is a difference in equipment, not a reason the process is the same.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1 and Worked example 2 (and Section 2 formula v = πDN/60): The power step multiplies the whole friction force by the rim speed, v = πDN/60. In a rotating interface the rubbing speed falls linearly from that value at the rim to zero at the centre, so the true friction power is P = (2/3)πμpωR³, which is two-thirds of the number shown. Example 1 therefore gives 19.3 kW where the integrated result is about 12.9 kW; Example 2 gives 4.74 kW where the integrated result is about 3.16 kW. The arithmetic is right, but the model is never stated as an approximation, so a student who uses the correct integral in the exam will think the book is wrong.
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, third bullet: The trap says 'In friction welding one of the parts spins', but Section 2 and the bullet just above it describe linear friction welding, where nothing spins and one flat part oscillates. A student reading the trap list alone will contradict the earlier statement.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, bullet 'Access from one side only; the beam can even pass through a glass window': Stated as a general property of laser welding, but it holds only for the 1 µm beams (Nd:YAG, fibre). A CO₂ beam at 10.6 µm is absorbed by ordinary glass and cannot pass through a window, so a student may wrongly generalise it.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, opening of the CMT explanation: 'In ordinary MIG the wire is fed forward at a steady speed and the arc runs at high current. When the wire touches the pool, a short circuit forms...': This describes only short-circuiting (dip) transfer MIG. Ordinary MIG can also run in spray or globular transfer with no short circuits at all, so the sentence as written is not true of 'ordinary MIG' in general.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, steps 5–7: Heat input H = P/v is used without being defined anywhere, and no efficiency factor is mentioned, so the student cannot tell why the number is 'heat input' rather than just 'energy per unit length of travel'.
- ch 8 [high] Lead paragraph: The lead says the process 'keeps the parent metal's strength and heat treatment intact' for the whole family. That is not true for brazing: brazing temperatures are typically several hundred degrees (roughly 580–900 °C), high enough to temper quenched steel, overage aluminium alloys and anneal cold-worked metal, so a brazed part can lose parent-metal strength.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, step 5 and answer: The explanation of why a real joint fills only a few millimetres leaves out the main limit. In a normal horizontal lap joint there is no gravitational rise to stop the filler; the formula really gives the equivalent capillary pressure head. Flow stops because of viscous resistance of the melt in a narrow gap, trapped gas, oxide blocking wetting, and freezing against cold metal.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1 and Quiz Q3 data: The surface tension given for a molten brazing filler, γ = 0.4 N/m and 0.35 N/m, is low. Molten brazing alloys are typically around 0.8–1.0 N/m (molten solders are nearer 0.5 N/m), so a student may carry away a wrong feel for the property.
- ch 9 [high] Section 3 formula, Worked example 1 and Quiz Q5: The chapter uses c = 5900 m/s as the shear wave speed in steel. 5900 m/s is the longitudinal (compression) wave speed. Shear waves in steel travel at about 3200 m/s. Every depth calculated in the example and in Q5 therefore has the wrong material constant behind it.
- ch 9 [high] Exam traps, fourth bullet: It says that forgetting the factor 2 gives half the depth. Forgetting to divide by 2 gives double the depth: 5900 × 4.07 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.024 m = 24 mm instead of 12 mm. The trap teaches the error backwards.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, step 6 and answer: 12.0 mm in a 20 mm weld lies just below mid-thickness, not at the root. Lack of penetration sits at the root, close to 20 mm depth, so calling a 12 mm indication a possible lack of penetration is a stretch and can teach wrong defect sizing.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3 formula and Worked example 1: The relation d = c × t / 2 gives the sound path length, which equals the depth only for a straight beam hitting the surface at 90°. Weld testing uses angle probes, where true depth = (c × t / 2) × cos θ, with θ the refracted angle in the metal. The chapter never defines θ or mentions this.
- ch 9 [low] Figure and its caption: The node is labelled 'No fusion', while the standard term used in the text and caption is lack of fusion or incomplete fusion. The caption also says each group points to a different inspection method, but the tree shows only defect names, no inspection methods, so the reader looks for something that is not there.
