## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 2, potentiometric sentence: 'No current is drawn' is stated as an absolute. A real potentiometric cell draws a very small current; the ideal is zero current, and the reading is only valid while the current stays negligible.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q3 answer: The answer says 'the electrode therefore sees a current from glucose only'. The electrode does not react with glucose; it oxidises the hydrogen peroxide that glucose oxidase produced, so the sentence can plant the wrong picture of the sensing chain.
- ch 1 [low] Lead: The lead says a chemical sensor turns concentration into an electrical signal, but Section 1 and the optical row of the table say the transducer may also give a light level. The two statements sit slightly apart.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 (absolute humidity); repeated in exam trap 2: The text says absolute humidity (g/m³) 'stays the same when air is heated or cooled, provided no water enters or leaves'. That is only true for a sealed, fixed volume. Air heated at constant pressure expands, so the same mass of vapour fills a larger volume and the g/m³ value falls slightly. Only dew point (and vapour partial pressure) is truly unchanged by heating.
- ch 3 [low] Beyond section, last line: The cross-reference 'see 5.7' is plain text, so on the phone it is not tappable like the other chapter links in the book.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, temperature warning: 'Carrier density grows sharply with T, roughly doubling every 10 K near room temperature for silicon. An unmodified silicon resistor drifts badly' — the doubling-every-10-K figure is for the intrinsic carrier density n_i of undoped silicon. A piezoresistor is heavily doped, so at room temperature its carrier density is fixed by the doping and does not double every 10 K. The drift of a doped piezoresistor is only about +0.1 to +0.3 %/K and comes mainly from the fall of mobility with temperature. As written, a student would conclude the bridge drifts by ~7 %/K, which is wrong and contradicts the usual need for a thermistor correction of a fraction of a percent per kelvin.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, second paragraph, last sentence: 'A gas pulls electrons out of the surface and pins the depletion layer wider' states only the oxidising-gas case. Read next to Section 2 and Quiz Q2, where CO (a reducing gas) gives electrons back and the resistance falls, the student sees two opposite directions for 'a gas' and cannot tell which is which.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, second paragraph: The electron density of a semiconductor is given as 'near 10¹⁶ per m³ at room temperature and is set by doping'. 10¹⁶ m⁻³ is the intrinsic value of undoped silicon; once doped, n or p is typically 10²¹ to 10²³ m⁻³, still far below a metal.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 last paragraph vs Section 2 (ZrO₂) : Solid electrolyte devices are said to be 'covered separately in 5.9', but the ZrO₂ oxygen sensor is later cross-referenced to 5.2. Two different chapter numbers are given for the same topic.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, last paragraph and table row 'PZT, quartz': Quartz is called a piezoelectric ceramic and its limit is given as 'depends on Curie point'. Quartz is a single crystal, not a ceramic, and it has no ferroelectric Curie point; its limit comes from the α–β transition near 573 °C and, in practice, from twinning and mounting losses at a few hundred degrees.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, silicon paragraph (also Exam traps and Quiz Q6): The 150 °C limit for silicon is correct, but the reason given — 'the intrinsic carrier density swamps the doping' — is not true at 150 °C. For typical doping the intrinsic density only becomes comparable at several hundred degrees Celsius; at 150 °C the practical failure is junction leakage and drift of the piezoresistors.
- ch 5 [high] Figure (blocks diagram) and its caption: The figure labels four implanted piezoresistors with ΔR/R = 0.024 and says the bridge hands 0.03 V to the amplifier and ADC. With all four gauges active the bridge output is V_s·(ΔR/R) = 5 V × 0.024 = 0.12 V. 0.03 V is the one-active-gauge value from worked example 1, so the figure contradicts the example and the text, and would push a student into the exact error listed in exam trap 1.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, thermal anemometer: 'the difference voltage is proportional to mass flow' is wrong. The differential-thermopile (calorimetric) output rises roughly linearly only at very small flow and then follows a strongly nonlinear, saturating curve (hot-wire anemometers follow King's law, output ≈ a + b·(mass flow)^0.5). A student writing 'proportional' loses marks and cannot explain the calibration curve.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, sensitivity sentence: 'commercial parts run from about 5 to 20 mV/V' drops the 'per bar' basis, so it cannot be compared with the 6 mV/V/bar just quoted or with data sheets.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, trap 4: The unit statement is backwards. A rate written in µm/s has a numerical value 60 times smaller than the same rate in µm/min, so the etching time comes out 60 times larger when expressed in seconds (not 60 times smaller). As written, a student who follows the trap will move the decimal the wrong way.
- ch 6 [high] Quiz Q7: The answer says "the pit widens as it goes down", which contradicts Section 2 ("A pit is wider at the top than at the bottom"). The conclusion is right but the stated reason is the opposite of the geometry, so a student revising from the answer learns the wrong shape.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q5 (and the deposition table in Section 3): The question asks for two deposition methods, but the answer gives thermal oxidation, which grows oxide by consuming silicon rather than depositing a film. The chapter itself says this in Section 3, so the answer and the table row quietly label growth as deposition. Only one true deposition method (sputtering) is offered.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, opening line: It says the processor runs a fixed sequence "at every sample", then lists autoranging and self-calibration. Those do not run on every sample: self-calibration is done periodically or on command, and autoranging only when the signal moves to a new range. A student may write that all seven functions execute each sample.
- ch 7 [low] Figure spec, digital bus node note: The bus node note "I²C, HART" puts HART beside a true digital bus. HART is a low-level FSK digital signal riding on the 4–20 mA analog current loop, not a bus like I²C, and the figure can mislead on that point.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, substrate list, and Quiz Q1: The PET sentence contradicts itself: PET 'softens near 80 °C, so keep processing and use below about 120 °C'. A student reads two different limits for the same plastic and cannot tell which one to quote. The same muddle appears in the Q1 answer ('usable below about 120 °C because it softens near 80 °C').
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, inverse-variance weighted average formula: The general formula x̂ = Σwᵢ xᵢ/Σwᵢ and σ_fused = 1/√(Σwᵢ) is stated without saying it holds only when the sensor errors are independent (uncorrelated). The assumption is only mentioned later, inside the worked example.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, majority voting, and Quiz Q5: 'Take the value that two agree on' suggests exact agreement, which never happens with analogue readings. Q5 works only because the student guesses a tolerance (30.1 and 30.2 'agree').
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, printed RTDs: Listing printed silver as an RTD metal without a caution is loose: platinum is the standard RTD material, while silver drifts as it oxidises and sulfidises, so it is used only where that drift is acceptable.
- ch 9 [high] Section 2 and Worked example 1, steps 1–3: The formula is written Δf/f = −Δv/v, but Δv is defined as the actual change in velocity. With a 0.10 % slowdown, Δv/v = −0.0010, so the written formula gives Δf/f = +0.0010, an upward shift. Step 2 then says the shift has 'the same size and sign', while step 3 computes Δf = −0.0010 × 100 MHz = −100 kHz, which uses Δf/f = +Δv/v. The formula, step 2 and step 3 cannot all be right, and the earlier line 'a 0.10 % velocity drop becomes a 0.10 % frequency drop' also needs the same sign.
- ch 9 [low] Lead and Section 1: A solid electrolyte is defined as 'a crystal', but Nafion is then listed as an example. Nafion is a hydrated polymer membrane, not a crystal, so the definition contradicts its own example.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, surface plasmon resonance: Saying light 'is absorbed at one angle' is loose. At the resonance angle the energy is coupled into a surface plasmon and the reflected beam drops sharply; the effect is seen as a dip in reflection, not as bulk absorption in the metal.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q4: The question uses T = 700 K, which is about 427 °C. The chapter itself says YSZ needs roughly 700 °C and that a cell below about 400 °C reads near zero, so students can confuse kelvin with degrees Celsius and get a cell that is barely at the edge of working temperature.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, step 5: Agreeing with 50 kHz/ng does not show that the film is thin enough for the mass-loading approximation; that sensitivity figure is itself a linear mass-loading value. The check tests arithmetic consistency, not the validity of the approximation.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2 and Quiz Q2: 'A pair of comb-shaped metal electrodes whose fingers sit a half wavelength apart' and 'two comb-shaped metal electrodes a half wavelength apart' can be misread as the two combs being half a wavelength apart. The combs interleave; the pitch between adjacent fingers (one from each comb) is λ/2, and fingers of the same comb are λ apart.
- ch 9 [low] Figure caption and figure spec: The mixer is shown with a single input, but a mixer needs a second signal (a reference at the unperturbed frequency or a local oscillator) to produce the 100 kHz difference; as drawn, a student cannot see where the comparison frequency comes from.
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, first paragraph of the ISFET part: The sentence 'a liquid cannot act as a gate on its own' is loose. The electrolyte does form the gate; what it lacks is a defined potential, which is why the reference electrode is needed. As written, a student may think the solution is electrically inactive, which then sits awkwardly with the quiz Q3 answer ('A liquid has no fixed potential of its own').
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, the IMFET and EnFET paragraphs: The abbreviations IMFET and EnFET are used but never expanded, unlike ISFET which is spelled out. A student meeting them for the first time has nothing to hang the letters on.
- ch 10 [low] Section 3, first paragraph: FAD is introduced without expansion ('It carries an FAD cofactor') and is used again in the second-generation paragraph. The reader cannot tell what FAD is or why it is the electron source.
- ch 10 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: The fasting range 'about 4 to 7 mmol/L' reaches 126 mg/dL, which is the diabetes cut-off, not a normal value. Normal fasting plasma glucose is usually quoted as 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L (70 to 100 mg/dL); 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L is impaired fasting glucose.
- ch 10 [low] Worked example 1, answer: The arithmetic is right, but 1.06 mA is the current only if every glucose molecule in the 10 µL reacts within the 10 s. Real strips read a small fraction of that charge in the first seconds, so a student may carry away a wrong idea of the current magnitude in a commercial strip.
