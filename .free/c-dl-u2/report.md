## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1, the update-rule formula note: The note says η has "the same units as θ" and then says η is "in volts per unit of slope". These two statements contradict each other, and the first is dimensionally wrong. In θ ← θ − η∇L the gradient ∇L = ∂L/∂θ has units of loss per unit of θ, so η must carry units of θ² per unit of loss, not units of θ. A student who believes η shares θ's units will misjudge why rescaling inputs forces a new η.
- ch 1 [high] Exam traps, fifth trap: The trap repeats the same unit error: "η has the units of θ". The second half of the trap (changing the input scale changes the effective step, so η must be re-tuned) is correct, but its stated reason is wrong.
- ch 1 [high] Quiz Q4, answer: "At η = 1 the parameter oscillates with constant size 4" is wrong. In this quiz the run starts at θ₀ = 2 (Q3), so at η = 1 the parameter flips sign with magnitude 2; the number 4 is the loss L = θ², not the parameter. The sentence also never states θ₀.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q6, answer: The answer explains the sum-versus-average bug but never uses the doubling that the question asks about, so the link between "doubles N" and "unstable" is left implicit. As written, "the effective step is then Nη" does not show what the doubling changed.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, mini-batch update formula: The update divides the sum by B, but the same section says the last batch may be smaller. For that batch the average must divide by the actual number of samples in the batch, not by B. Using 1/B scales the last update of every epoch down by |Bt|/B.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2, linear scaling heuristic; Quiz Q6: The variance argument alone does not justify multiplying η by k. A batch k times larger makes the gradient's standard deviation √k times smaller, which by itself suggests a √k scaling, not k. The standard justification for the k scaling is that k steps of size η on batches of size B approximate one step of size kη on a batch of size kB. Also, Q6's 'a step k times larger keeps a similar scale of parameter movement' can be read as per-update movement, which is actually k times larger; the similar movement is per epoch, because there are k times fewer updates.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 4 and answer; Quiz Q5: The variance values are reported without units. If the gradient is in gradient units, variance is in (gradient units)², while standard deviation is in gradient units. The answers attach 'gradient units' only to the standard deviations.
- ch 3 [high] Exam traps, first bullet: The bullet names a pair of updates that is in fact consistent and correct: v ← βv + ∇L together with θ ← θ − ηv is just the same method with η absorbed into the velocity (define u = ηv and it becomes u ← βu + η∇L, θ ← θ − u). The text then says this pairing 'applies η twice or not at all', which is false and will make students think a valid convention used by real libraries is a mistake.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: 'Training memory roughly doubles' overstates the cost. Momentum adds one number per parameter, so only the parameter/gradient-side memory roughly doubles; activation memory, which usually dominates for deep networks, is unchanged.
- ch 4 [high] Exam traps, first trap (skipping bias correction): The direction of the error is stated backwards. Without bias correction the early Adam steps are too large, not too small. With the defaults β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999 and a constant gradient g, the uncorrected ratio is m/√v = 0.1g/√(0.001g²) = 3.16 at t = 1, so the step is about 3.16η. v is pulled toward 0 more strongly than m because β₂ is closer to 1, so √v is underestimated more than m and the ratio is inflated. Bias correction removes this inflation and brings the first step back to about η.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, sentence after the bias-correction formulas: "The corrected values estimate the true mean and variance of recent gradients" is imprecise: v̂ estimates E[g²], the mean square (uncentered second moment) of the gradients, not their variance, which would be E[g²] − (E[g])².
- ch 4 [low] Worked example 1, answer line: The answer lists m and v twice with no time labels: "m = 0.1, v = 0.001, m = 0.19, v = 0.001999". A student can read this as a contradiction rather than values at t = 1 and t = 2.
- ch 4 [low] Worked example 1, lines labelled step 1 to step 6: The word "step" is used both for these calculation lines and for the optimizer steps t = 1 and t = 2. Readers may think there are six Adam steps.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q3: Setting ε = 0 contradicts the chapter's own rule that ε is a tiny positive constant, and it teaches a habit that divides by zero if v̂ comes out 0.
- ch 5 [high] Section 1, displayed formula for ∂E/∂h(1): The backward product is written as (∏ from ℓ=2 to L of Jℓ) · ∂E/∂h(L). With Jℓ = ∂h(ℓ)/∂h(ℓ−1) of size nℓ × nℓ−1, the factors in that order are not even dimensionally conformable (J2J3 multiplies n2×n1 by n3×n2), and the transpose needed by the chain rule is missing. As printed, a student cannot compute it.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, Section 2 and worked example step 2 (typesetting of powers): Several powers have lost their superscripts and read as plain multiplication: '0.25L', '0.2510 ≈ 9.5 × 10−7', '1.510 ≈ 57.7', and the table header '0.25L | 1.0L | 1.5L'. A student can read 0.2510 as a decimal number, which destroys the point of the section.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 and Section 2 (factor count): The chapter says a deep network 'multiplies L such factors in a row' and that 'the full gradient at layer 1 is a product of L Jacobians', but its own formula runs ℓ = 2 to L, which is L − 1 Jacobians between the output side and layer 1. The table and the sigmoid sentence '0.25^L' silently treat L as the number of factors, not the number of layers.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, third bullet: 'The factor is 0.5' and 'after 8 layers' give different numbers' does not say what the numbers are or what the mistake is; it reads as a fragment.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q1: The fan-out gloss is ambiguous and slightly self-contradictory. Section 1 calls fan-out "the number of units that unit feeds", while Q1 calls it "the number of units it feeds, equal to the current layer width". Under the first phrase a unit feeds the next layer (whose width is not known here); under the second it is the current layer width. A student can take the two halves as different quantities.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 (after the Xavier formulas): "It averages fan-in and fan-out" is inaccurate: 2/(n_in + n_out) is the harmonic mean of 1/n_in and 1/n_out, not an arithmetic average of the two fan counts. A student may plug (n_in + n_out)/2 into the wrong place later.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, closing sentence: "The rules below keep variance near one through the stack" is misleading. Xavier and He keep the variance the same from layer to layer; that common value need not be one, because it starts from the scale of the input data.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, paragraph after the uniform Xavier formula: The symbols are defined twice: Var(W), σ(W), n_in and n_out were already explained under the formula line and are repeated immediately after it, which slows a phone reader.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, the sentence after the running-average formula: The sentence 'If code uses running averages during training, the training signal no longer matches the forward pass' is muddled: if running averages are used in the forward pass, the forward pass and the gradient signal are consistent with each other. The real trouble is different, so a student may take away the wrong reason.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, the sentence about the cost of early stopping: The sentence says a run 'takes longer than a fixed schedule'. This is backwards in the normal case: training stops at the best epoch plus the patience, which is usually shorter than running a fixed schedule to convergence. The real costs are that you cannot plan the total compute in advance, you must hold out a validation split, and you must keep a checkpoint of the best weights.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, 'The prize is a free hyperparameter: no λ to choose': Patience is still a knob, and it sets the effective penalty strength (roughly 1/(ηt), as the same section says). Calling it 'free' can make a student think no hyperparameter is involved at all.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, 'the full network at test time behaves like an average of all of them': 'behaves like an average' is too strong as written. The scaled full network matches the average of the thinned networks exactly only for a linear model; for a real nonlinear network it is an approximation (an exact average would need many masked passes at test time, as in Monte-Carlo dropout).
- ch 9 [high] Section 1, augmentation paragraph: The sentence says a horizontal flip 'changes 6 into 9'. A horizontal (left-right) flip of the digit 6 gives a reversed 6, not a 9. The operation that turns 6 into 9 is a 180° rotation, or roughly a vertical (top-bottom) flip. The same chapter later calls 'vertical flip of digits' the label-changing trap, so the two statements contradict each other and a student will memorise the wrong axis.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q7: The answer says validation augmentation 'made the validation score optimistic'. Random crops on validation usually make the task harder and lower the score, not raise it; the real point is that the 0.90 is not comparable to a clean-input score. The second 'cause' just restates the first.
