## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 2 (also Exam traps item 1 and Quiz Q5 answer): The chapter says tie lines 'come out horizontal only on a right-angled plot with A on the vertical axis'. They do not. On a right-triangular plot with A as ordinate and S as abscissa, the two equilibrium layers generally have different A contents, so the tie line joining them slopes. What is horizontal on such a plot is a line of constant A content, which is not a tie line. Teaching the reverse rule makes a student accept a wrong extract composition on a right-angled diagram.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, end of the systems classification: Type 2 is described as 'the two-phase band hugs one corner', implying a single band. Two partly miscible pairs give two separate two-phase regions, one along each of the two sides that meet at the shared corner. The type 3 sentence ('a two-phase band crosses the diagram') is also loose.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The second sentence, 'A second layer would have to have a composition on the curve, which is not available at that overall composition', is vague: 'not available' does not say what fails. A student may read it as a matter of solubility rather than of the mass-balance lever rule on a tie line.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3, stage efficiency paragraph: The claim 'the usual estimate replaces K by η·K, so η = 0.7 makes three real stages behave like 2.1 ideal ones' is quantitatively wrong. If K is replaced by ηK, then X_n = X₀/(1 + ηE)ⁿ, which equals X₀/(1 + E)^m only when m = n·ln(1 + ηE)/ln(1 + E). For η = 0.7 and the chapter's own E = 1.0667 this gives m ≈ 2.3 ideal stages, not 2.1; the value 2.1 is only the limit as E → 0.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, stage efficiency paragraph: The definition given and the correction suggested do not describe the same model. If η is the fraction of the ideal composition change, then X_n = X_{n−1}·(1 + E − ηE)/(1 + E), which is not the same as X_{n−1}/(1 + ηE). For η = 0.7, E = 1.0667 the two give 0.639 and 0.573 respectively, so a student cannot tell which convention the book wants.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: 'Each fresh portion meets raffinate that is already lean' is not true for the first stage, where clean solvent meets the richest liquid. A student may take the wrong reason for the gain.
- ch 3 [high] Lead and Section 1: The lead says 'Every stage therefore sees a larger concentration difference than with fresh solvent added everywhere', and Section 1 says 'Each stage meets solvent richer in solute than fresh solvent. The driving force stays larger.' These two sentences contradict each other: solvent carrying more solute gives a smaller driving force, not a larger one. The claim is also false in fact. At the feed end, a cross-current first stage meets pure solvent, so its driving force is K·X0; the counter-current first stage meets solvent at Y2 > 0, so its driving force is smaller (0.18 against 0.64 in the worked example). A student will carry away a wrong reason for why counter-current is better.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, cross-current comparison: The claim 'About five cross-current stages are needed for 77.5%' is numerically impossible with the stated solvent. With 500 kg/h split equally over n stages, each stage keeps the ratio 700/(700 + 750/n), so X_n/X_0 = [700/(700 + 750/n)]^n. This falls to the limit e^(−1.0714) = 0.343, i.e. about 66% recovery, however many stages are used. Five stages give only 62%, and the figure 77.5% can never be reached with 500 kg/h in cross-current.
- ch 3 [high] Section 3, formula block: XN = X0(E−1)/(E^(N+1)−1) holds only when the entering solvent is pure (YN+1 = 0). The chapter presents it as a general constant-K result and in the very next line writes Y1 = (R/S)(X0−XN) + YN+1, which does allow a loaded solvent. A student who applies the XN formula to a recycled solvent stream, as Exam trap 3 tells them to, gets a wrong answer.
- ch 3 [high] Quiz Q2: The answer 'Each stage meets solvent richer in solute than fresh solvent, so the average driving force is larger' gives the wrong reason. Richer solvent means a smaller driving force, and with equal solvent and stages the counter-current driving forces are in fact smaller at both ends than the cross-current ones.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, first line: It says 'A mixer-settler gives one equilibrium stage per mixer plus settler', which clashes with Section 1: a real stage reaches only a fraction of equilibrium (E is typically 0.8-0.95), so it does not give a full equilibrium stage.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, last line: 'Pulsed units are in 5.6' is an unclear cross-reference. A student reading on a phone cannot tell whether 5.6 is a section number or a chapter number, and it is not a link.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 table, mixer-settler row, Capacity column: 'High per unit' is undefined: per unit of what? The text later defines capacity as throughput per unit area or volume, so the entry should name the basis.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, Z formula definition: 'Z is the packed height (m)' is incorrect for the spray and mechanically agitated columns discussed in the same section, where there is no packing; the formula is presented for columns in general.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, formula M = m_solution,in + S: The text defines m_solution,in as kg of feed liquid, but in leaching the feed often contains dry soluble solid. The total solution after contact must include the mass of that dissolved solute. Worked example 1 uses the 200 kg oil as part of M even though the oil is the solute in the solid, so the definition and the formula do not match.
- ch 5 [high] Section 2, recovery formula with impure solvent: The chapter says that if the solvent is not pure, add the solute it brings to the numerator and its mass to M. If that solute is included in 'solute entering', then Recovery = VX/(solute entering) × 100% counts solute that came in with the fresh solvent as recovered from the solid, and it changes the denominator. That is not recovery of the feed solute.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, X = (solute entering)/M: This equation assumes that all soluble solute has dissolved and the solution is below saturation. If the solvent is insufficient or the solute has limited solubility, some solute remains undissolved and X is the saturation composition, not total solute entering divided by M.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 and Quiz Q7, stage efficiency: The stage-efficiency formula uses Xin and Xout without defining them. A student may not know whether these are compositions of the incoming solution, the outgoing overflow, or the retained underflow solution.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, pulse velocity formula: The justification sentence is wrong about the motion: with A measured from the mean to one extreme, the net displacement over one full cycle is zero, and the liquid actually travels 4A per cycle for a sinusoidal pulse (A up, A back, A down, A back). Saying 'one full cycle moves the liquid 2A, so the average speed is 2Af' mixes up stroke and speed, and a student may then think 2Af is the instantaneous or true average speed.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, supercritical CO2 paragraph: 'Supercritical CO2 is non-polar, so it dissolves caffeine' implies caffeine is a non-polar compound. Caffeine is a polar molecule (amide and imidazole groups); its solubility in dense CO2 comes from the high density and favourable interactions, not from being non-polar. Students can carry away a wrong idea about polarity and solubility.
- ch 6 [low] Worked example 1, steps 2-3 and answer: The word 'minimum' is not explained. With the exit loading fixed at 1.5 mass%, the CO2 quantity is simply fixed; it is the minimum only if 1.5 mass% is the saturation (equilibrium) loading of the exit solution at 40 °C and 200 bar. A student can miss that assumption.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2 (wash ratio) versus Section 3 (E) and the worked example: Section 2 defines the wash ratio as the fresh wash water fed per mass of solution retained in the spent solid, but Section 3 calls E = V/L the wash ratio, where V is the overflow solution rate. These are different numbers whenever the feed brings its own solute: in the worked example fresh water/retained = 600/450 = 1.333 while E = 400/450 = 0.889. A student who follows Section 2 will use the wrong number and get a wrong answer, especially since the trap list then warns that V is not the fresh-water rate.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, first trap: Solids and liquor travelling the same way is co-current (parallel) flow, not 'cross-current in disguise'. Co-current flow through several tanks gives essentially one equilibrium stage, because liquor and solids keep the same composition; cross-current means fresh solvent is added separately to each stage. The label confuses two different arrangements.
- ch 7 [low] Figure (Shanks battery spec): The caption and aria text say fresh water enters at tank 4 and moves back toward tank 1, but the drawing has only solids edges (1→2→3→4) and one dashed edge from wash water to tank 4. Nothing shows liquor flowing from tank 4 back through tanks 3, 2 and 1, so the figure does not show the counter-current path it describes.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, formula for x_N/x_0: The formula is stated without its two assumptions: each stage is ideal (the retained solution and the overflow leaving a stage have the same solute fraction) and the retained solution rate L (and so V) is the same in every stage. Without these, students apply the equation to stages that are not well mixed or to beds where r changes.
