## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Lead: The lead says inference, 'deciding whether one sentence follows from another', 'becomes a finite computation on those forms'. In full first-order logic entailment is only semidecidable: a proof search may run forever, and no terminating procedure decides it in general. As written, a student will believe FOL inference always halts.
- ch 1 [low] Section 4, restricted quantifier display: The display line begins with the bare token '[formula]', a leftover placeholder that is not part of the formula and will confuse a reader about what the expression is.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, canonical form: 'Gita sold the car to Ravi' and 'Ravi bought the car from Gita' are not logically equivalent as formulas: Sell(Gita, car, Ravi) and Buy(Ravi, car, Gita) are different predicates, and making them equivalent needs meaning postulates. The sentence as written implies logic alone delivers the equivalence.
- ch 1 [low] Section 4, Davidson's events: The representation splits the verb's own arguments into separate predicates (Reader(e, Meera), Object(e, book1)). That is the neo-Davidsonian style; Davidson's original proposal keeps the verb's arguments inside the main predicate, Read(e, Meera, book1), and adds only modifiers like Place(e, library) as conjunctions.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2 and Exam traps, free variables: Saying a formula with a free variable 'has no truth value' is loose. Truth is defined relative to a model plus an assignment of values to the free variables; the formula lacks a truth value only when no assignment is given.
- ch 2 [low] Section 2 and Quiz Q3 versus Worked example 1 lexicon: The chapter uses two incompatible treatments of proper names. In the worked example lexicon Ravi is a generalized quantifier, Ravi: λP.P(Ravi) of type (e→t)→t, but Section 2 and Q3 apply the predicate λx.Student(x) of type e→t directly to Ravi. Under the worked example's entry that application is a type error, so a student who follows the lexicon cannot reproduce Q3's answer Student(Ravi).
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 and Worked example 1, step 1: The chapter's own etymology test says common origin points to polysemy, yet the worked example justifies homonymy by saying the financial and river senses of bank have unrelated histories. That etymological claim is not safe: both senses are commonly traced back to the same Germanic root for a bench/ridge, so a student who applies the chapter's etymology test to bank gets the opposite answer from the one the worked example gives.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, zeugma example for polysemy: The sentence 'She has a good head and a good head for numbers' pairs two uses of head that carry the same sense (intelligence, aptitude), so it tests nothing and does not show the sense relation. Students will also wonder why it is linked to the earlier head of a person / head of a department example.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1 (WordNet: synsets and pointers): The chapter says lexicographer files group synsets by subject and gives the example 'noun.vehicle'. WordNet's noun lexicographer files do not include one named noun.vehicle; car synsets sit under noun.artifact (with noun.Tops at the top). A student who looks for noun.vehicle will not find it.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 (WordNet: synsets and pointers): The text opens with 'Pointers are labelled links between synsets' and then lists antonym and derivation. In WordNet these two hold between lemmas (word forms), not between synsets; a synset has no antonym, a lemma does. The wording makes students think 'car' and 'auto' style synsets can have antonyms.
- ch 4 [low] Figure: The caption says upward edges point to hypernyms and downward edges to hyponyms, but the spec draws the hypernyms motor vehicle and vehicle at the same height as car (y = 2), and the hyponym used-car above car (y = 4), with only compact car below. The picture therefore contradicts its own caption and the exam trap about reading direction.
- ch 5 [high] Section 4, standard test sets sentence: The text names CoNLL shared tasks as a source of WSD test sets. The CoNLL shared tasks cover chunking, NER, dependency parsing, SRL and similar tasks; they did not run a word sense disambiguation task. Sense-tagged WSD evaluation data comes from the Senseval and SemEval campaigns.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, context window sentence: Section 1 says the window k is 'usually between 5 and 50' words to each side, but Section 3 says supervised WSD uses a window of 'usually ±5 to ±10'. The two statements contradict each other and a student will not know which figure to quote.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, 'Context is the only evidence': The bare claim contradicts Section 3, which lists part-of-speech tags, collocations and dependency features as separate evidence. A student may read it as 'no other features exist'.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, last bullet: 'a pronoun-free word three sentences later can still settle the sense' is unclear: 'pronoun-free word' has no defined meaning here, so the point about document-level evidence is lost.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, inventories table, FrameNet row: The table gives FrameNet's labels for 'Ravi broke the window with a stone' as Agent, Patient, Instrument, which are the generic names. The paragraph directly below says FrameNet is frame-specific and does not use generic Agent and Patient. A student reading the table will conclude the opposite of the paragraph. (Those three names happen to be the frame element names of this particular frame, but the table does not say so.)
- ch 6 [low] Figure (broke with Agent, Patient, Instrument, Time, Location): The caption says Time and Location are both optional adjuncts, but in the drawing yesterday is styled 'muted' while Chennai is styled 'normal'. The two adjuncts are shown with different visual importance for no reason.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, locative alternation: 'In the locative pair the Patient and Location shift' is too vague to check. It does not say which phrase carries which role in each variant, so a student cannot map the pair.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q4, answer: The answer uses 'Recipient', a role name that never appears in the chapter; only Goal and Benefactive are defined. A student meeting it in a model answer has no definition to fall back on.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q5: Q5 asks exactly the question already answered in Worked example 1, steps 10 to 12, with the same two sentences. The marked answer is printed twice in the same chapter.
- ch 7 [high] Quiz Q4: The model answer treats missing the bus and arriving late as something a reader could 'accept as a contrast'. They are not in contrast: missing the bus explains the lateness, so 'However' misfires. 'still reaching late' is also confusing, because reaching late is the expected outcome, not a surprising one. A student would learn a wrong notion of what Contrast means.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, coherence versus cohesion paragraph: 'Ravi missed the bus. He reached late.' is said to be 'coherent with little cohesion', but the example does contain a cohesive device, the pronoun 'he'. Students who just read that pronouns are cohesion may be confused.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, relation list: The chapter says most RST relations are asymmetric and then lists Contrast as a relation, giving a two-span Contrast example without noting that Contrast (like List and Sequence) is multinuclear: it has no nucleus and satellite.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, TextTiling: 'A low score is a likely boundary' is only half the algorithm. TextTiling turns the similarity sequence into depth scores and applies a threshold, so an area of uniformly low similarity is not automatically cut.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, Hobbs' algorithm bullets: The search step is wrong. Hobbs searches only the part of the subtree below X that lies to the left of the path from the pronoun up to X; if X is an S node it then searches the branches to the right of that path, without entering any NP or S it meets. As written ('Search that subtree left-to-right, breadth-first'), the search also covers the pronoun's own NP and noun phrases that come after the pronoun, so the algorithm can return a wrong antecedent. The standard algorithm also repeats the whole search in earlier sentences, most recent first, when X is the topmost S of the sentence; that step is missing entirely.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, Centering Theory transitions: The Retain definition is not the standard one. Retain means Cb(Un) = Cb(Un−1) and Cb(Un) is not the top-ranked Cf (Cp); the top Cf may well stay the same as in the previous utterance. Saying 'top Cf changes' makes Retain and Continue look like a partition of the Cb-stays cases, which they are not.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, evaluation: CoNLL-style scoring is not whole-entity scoring. Entity-based scoring is CEAF; the CoNLL shared-task metric averages link-based and mention-based scores (MUC, B³, CEAF). A student can lose marks repeating this.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, steps 2 to 5: The chains found in steps 2 to 4 contain 5 links (2 + 2 + 1), yet step 5 says the gold standard has 2 links. The arithmetic is right for the numbers given, but the numbers do not come from the chains the student has just built, which is confusing.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1, steps 2 to 4 and the answer: Set braces are used for chains that contain the same string twice, as in {Ravi, He, Ravi}. A set cannot hold duplicates, so this notation is wrong for two distinct mentions of Ravi.
- ch 8 [low] Quiz Q5: The statement of Centering Rule 1 does not say which utterance's Cf and which utterance's Cb are meant, so it can be read as applying within one utterance, where it is false.
