## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Lead, Section 2 heading/content, and Figure caption: The chapter calls all four responses 'first-order'. A band-pass or band-stop built from a high-pass plus a low-pass (or a twin-T) has two reactive sections, so it is second-order, not first-order. A single first-order section cannot produce a peak or a notch. The plotted band-pass/band-stop curves (peak 1 and zero at x = 1) are second-order shapes, so the label contradicts the figure itself.
- ch 1 [low] Figure (band-pass and band-stop curves): The plotted band-pass |H| = 1/√(1+(x−1/x)²) and band-stop have Q = 1. The cascade described in the text, when the two sections are set to the same cut-off (f_L = f_H), gives Q = 1/3 and a peak of only 0.5 at f0 before normalising. A student who computes the cascade and compares it with the figure will think the calculation is wrong.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1 (Butterworth and Chebyshev approximations): Two exponents are missing their superscripts, so the formulas read wrongly. |H(jω)|² = 1/(1 + (ω/ωc)2n) reads as (ω/ωc)×2n instead of (ω/ωc) raised to 2n, and ε = √(10Ap/10 − 1) reads as √(Ap − 1) instead of √(10^(Ap/10) − 1). A student will substitute the wrong value.
- ch 2 [high] Section 3 (order formula) and Quiz Q8: The order formula is printed as n ≥ log10(10As/10 − 1)/(2 log10(fs/fc)) and the quiz as log10(1040/10 − 1). Taken literally these are 10×As/10 = As and 1040/10 = 104, so both computations come out wrong even though the numeric answers (n = 7) assume the correct expression.
- ch 2 [high] Section 2 (Frequency scaling sentence): 'Frequency scaling multiplies every resistor by m and divides every capacitor by m; fc and Q stay the same' is not frequency scaling: multiplying R by m and dividing C by m leaves RC and hence fc unchanged; that is impedance scaling. Labelling it frequency scaling, and then repeating it as 'impedance scaling does the same', leaves the student with no working rule for moving fc.
- ch 2 [high] Section 2 (last sentence of the scaling block): The resistor rule is inverted: 'to move from fc to fc′, scale all capacitors by fc/fc′ or all resistors by fc′/fc'. Raising the cut-off requires smaller resistors, so multiplying R by fc′/fc lowers fc instead of raising it; the capacitor and resistor rules must use the same factor.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 1 and Quiz Q3: The powers of ten are printed without superscripts: 'C = 10 × 10−9 F', '1/(2π × 104 × 10−8)'. As typed these read as 10 × 10 − 9 and 104 × 10 − 8 and the arithmetic cannot be checked.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1 (damping factor sentences): The same name is used for two different numbers: earlier 'damping factor ζ = 1/√2' (0.707) and later 'd = 1/Q the damping factor; d = 1.414'. A student will think the text contradicts itself.
- ch 2 [low] Exam traps (resistor tolerance): 'A 5% error in R shifts fc by about 5% and also changes Q' is misleading. In the equal-component Sallen-Key, Q = 1/(3 − K) is set only by K = 1 + Rf/R1; an equal shift in both filter resistors changes fc and leaves Q unchanged. Only mismatch between the two resistors or capacitors, or tolerance in Rf and R1, moves Q.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 4 / Figure: The example rounds R to 8.2 kΩ but never states that the cut-off then moves away from the specified 2 kHz, so a student may believe the final circuit still cuts off at exactly 2 kHz.
- ch 3 [low] Lead and Section 2 (universal filter, notch output): The text says a fourth op-amp sums VHP, VBP and VLP and that the high-pass and low-pass parts can be made to cancel. If the band-pass term is added with any non-zero weight there is no true null at f0, so a student may expect a complete notch while keeping VBP in the sum.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2 (Tow-Thomas biquad): 'An input summer, two integrators and a feedback inverter' describes four op-amps. The standard Tow-Thomas biquad is a three-op-amp circuit: a summing integrator, a second integrator, and a unity-gain inverting amplifier that closes the loop; the low-pass and band-pass outputs are taken at the two integrator outputs, and a fourth op-amp (an extra input) is needed only for high-pass or notch.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1 and Section 2 (with the Figure note and Q1): The loop polarity as wired cannot oscillate; it latches. With R1 from the non-inverting input to ground and R2 from the output to the non-inverting input, the Schmitt trigger trips when the voltage on its inverting input rises past +βVsat, so it is an inverting stage from the triangle to the square. The integrator is also inverting (vout = −(1/RC)∫vin dt). Two inversions make the loop positive feedback: with the square at +Vsat the ramp falls, so the ramp can never climb to +βVsat, and the circuit sits at a rail. The text even says the ramp rises while the square is high ('When the ramp crosses +βVsat the comparator flips'), which an inverting integrator cannot do.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3: The diode names and the resistor names do not match. 'Diode A conducts while the square is at +Vsat' means the resistance on that path is the falling-path resistance, because the integrator inverts. The formula then defines RA as the resistance on the rising path. A student will pair diode A with RA and get the duty cycle upside down.
- ch 4 [low] Figure: The note gives thresholds ±4 V and β = 1/3 but never states Vsat, so a reader cannot check that βVsat = 4 V; the 12 V supply appears only in the worked example.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 ('Both are stable'): The generic cross-coupled loop is said to settle in Q1 ON/Q2 OFF or the mirror, and 'Both are stable'. That is true only for the bistable; in the astable and monostable the coupling capacitors make these states temporary, so a student meets a contradiction with the chapter's own definition of the astable.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 and Section 3 ('when its base reaches about 0 V, the transistor turns ON'): Section 1 correctly says a base must be driven past about 0.7 V to turn a BJT on, but the astable and monostable timing steps switch at 'about 0 V'. The 0 V figure is only the approximation behind 0.69RC; stated flatly it contradicts Section 1.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2 formula note ('0.69 is ln 2 from the capacitor charging from about −VCC to 0 V'): The note names the start and end voltages but not the supply the capacitor charges toward, so a student cannot see where the factor 2 (and hence ln 2) comes from.
- ch 5 [low] Beyond: The sentence lumps the 74HC123 and the CD4047 together as IC monostables that 'add Schmitt-trigger inputs'. The 74HC123 has Schmitt-trigger inputs; the CD4047 is an astable/monostable multivibrator IC whose trigger input is not specified as a Schmitt trigger, and it is not only a monostable.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, the diode sentence: The diode is described with the wrong polarity and the stated result is wrong. With the cathode at pin 7, the diode conducts during discharge (pin 7 is pulled to ground while the capacitor node sits at 2/3 V_CC), so it bypasses R_B during discharge and pushes the duty cycle towards 100 %, not 50 %. The claim 'T_L ≈ 0.693 R_A C' is also wrong: during discharge R_A is connected between V_CC and the grounded pin 7 and carries no capacitor current.
- ch 6 [high] Section 3, missing-pulse detector bullet: The output sense is inverted. A trigger sets the latch and drives the output HIGH, so a monostable that is kept triggered by arriving pulses sits HIGH, and it falls LOW when a pulse is missed. The chapter says the output stays low and rises on a missing pulse, which contradicts the monostable behaviour described two paragraphs earlier.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, note under T_H formula: '0.693 is ln 2, the time constant for charging between 1/3 and 2/3 V_CC' misuses the term time constant. The time constant is (R_A + R_B)C; ln 2 is the dimensionless factor that multiplies it.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, tone generator bullet: A 10 µF coupling capacitor into an 8 Ω speaker forms a high-pass corner at about 1/(2π × 8 Ω × 10 µF) ≈ 2 kHz, so most of the audio tone is attenuated.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q5 answer: 'The output is loaded so the thresholds shift' is not a valid cause. Loading pin 3 does not move the 2/3 V_CC and 1/3 V_CC thresholds, which come from the internal divider and V_CC.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, monostable, trigger condition: 'The circuit retriggers on the next edge' is vague and the effect is stronger than that: while the trigger is held below 1/3 V_CC the lower comparator keeps setting the latch, so after each timeout the output goes high again and the one-shot free-runs.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3 last paragraph, Exam traps, Quiz Q7: Calling a floating pin 4 'the classic build fault' with 'erratic output' overstates it for the bipolar NE555, whose reset input is a transistor base that normally stays off when left open; the timer usually runs. The erratic behaviour is typical of the CMOS 7555/TLC555, where reset is a high-impedance CMOS input.
- ch 7 [high] Section 1, paragraph on lock: It says a steady phase error is needed to hold a non-zero V_c, after saying the phase error is zero in an ideal integrator loop. This is contradictory: an integrator (type-II) loop can hold a non-zero V_c with zero steady phase error.
- ch 7 [high] Figure spec: The block diagram has no input/reference signal entering the phase detector. It shows only the feedback from the VCO, so the figure does not match the text and is incomplete.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, capture range sentence: Saying the capture range is roughly set by the loop-filter cut-off alone is imprecise; capture range also depends on loop gain.
- ch 8 [low] lead: Saying the ladder sits 'inside every successive-approximation ADC' is an absolute that is not true. Many CMOS SAR ADCs use a switched-capacitor (charge-scaling) DAC array, not a resistor ladder. A student will carry away a wrong generalization.
- ch 8 [low] lead and section 1 (also quiz Q2): The number 'about 4 bits' is not supported by the reason given. At n = 4 the resistor spread is only 2^(n-1) = 8 to 1 and the MSB tolerance needed is 1 in 16 (6%), both easy; the chain only becomes hard several bits higher. The claim 'uses trimmed thin-film resistors and is impractical beyond about 4 bits' also pulls against itself, because trimming is what pushes a weighted network past a few bits. Textbooks usually put the practical limit nearer 8 bits with trimming, so the chapter's figure is on the low side and its justification is weak.
- ch 8 [low] section 3 table: The table gives LSB = V_ref/2^n and full scale = V_ref(2^n − 1)/2^n without the R_f/R factor that the chapter's own transfer formula V_out = −V_ref(R_f/R)D/2^n carries. These values hold only when R_f = R, which the table never says.
- ch 8 [low] section 3, bipolar paragraph: The circuit described and the code named do not match. Adding a fixed half-scale offset (a second input resistor from −V_ref/2) shifts the whole curve by +V_ref/2, so 000…0 becomes the most negative output and 100…0 lands on 0 V. That is offset-binary coding. In two's complement 100…0 is the most negative code, which this offset arrangement does not produce. A student will map two's-complement codes onto a circuit that cannot generate them.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1 and Quiz Q8 (aperture time): The error criterion in Q8 is set at a change of one full step Q, but a sampled value is already off by one code when the input moves by half a step, Q/2. The chapter also uses 'aperture time' both for the sampling window and for the error it causes, without separating aperture jitter, so a student can lose marks on the standard half-LSB statement.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2 formula T_conv = n T_clk: The formula is written as exact, yet the sentence just above says 'n clocks plus a little overhead' and Quiz Q4 itself adds one overhead clock. A student who uses T_conv = n T_clk in Q4 gets 6.0 µs instead of 6.5 µs. The formula also ignores the acquisition (track) time, which the chapter has already introduced in Section 1.
- ch 9 [low] Figure (ADC families compared): The table has no column headers, so a reader on a phone sees '2ⁿ / fast / 8b' without knowing which cell is comparator count, speed or resolution. The resolution column also mixes units: 8b, 16b and 24b are bits, while the dual-slope entry 4½ is a digit count (about 15 bits).
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, last paragraph of the dual-slope text: 'weigh scales' is not standard English and reads oddly for an Indian reader.
