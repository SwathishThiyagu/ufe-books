## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 3, first paragraph, and quiz Q2 answer: The sentence 'Inductance does not respond to a steady current' is loose and can make a student think the inductance value itself depends on AC. L exists and still changes with gap under DC; what fails under steady DC is the read-out, because the bridge output and the induced emf need a changing current.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2 and quiz Q7: 'k is the fraction of flux from one coil that links the other' is only true when the two coils have similar geometry and turns. The general definition the formula uses is k = M/√(L₁L₂).
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, paragraph on the mutual-inductance transducer: The symbols e₂ and i₁ appear in e₂ = M di₁/dt without being defined or given units, unlike every other symbol in the chapter.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1 formula, Section 3 table (Sensitivity), worked example 1 and exam traps: The same symbol K carries two different units in one chapter. The formula V_out = K·x defines K in volts per millimetre, and the worked example uses K = 0.50 V/mm at 5 V rms excitation, so the arithmetic 0.40/0.50 = 0.80 mm only works in V/mm. The table instead defines sensitivity as 'output per millimetre per volt of excitation, in mV/(mm·V)', and the last exam trap says sensitivity is quoted per volt of excitation. A student who takes the table definition and plugs a datasheet figure of, say, 100 mV/(mm·V) straight into x = V_out/K will be wrong by the excitation voltage.
- ch 2 [low] Lead paragraph, first line: LVDT is never expanded at first use. The full name appears only in the last line of the 'Beyond' note, so a student meeting the abbreviation in the title and lead has to guess it.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3 table, Resolution row: The reason given does not match the property. Resolution is set by the readout electronics because there is no sliding contact to cause stiction or wear; 'because the core never rubs on the former' explains the absence of friction, not the link to the electronics.
- ch 3 [high] Lead paragraph: The lead says these devices 'measure or compare shaft positions without sliding contacts'. That is false for two of the three: the induction potentiometer feeds its rotor winding through slip rings (Section 2 says so), and the synchro transmitter excites its rotor through slip rings. Only the microsyn is brushless. A student who writes the lead sentence in the exam will lose marks and contradict the same chapter.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, first paragraph: 'Each device here is a small AC machine with two windings: one on the stator, one on the rotor' is a generalisation that is contradicted later. The synchro transmitter has a three-phase stator, and the microsyn rotor has no winding at all.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1 and Section 2: Section 1 defines the angle θ from the position of maximum coupling and writes M = M_max·cos θ, but Section 2 then writes e_o = k·E_x·sin θ with its null at θ = 0. The two formulas use different zero references, and the change is not pointed out.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps, trap 5: 'an ambiguous sign over part of its range' is the wrong description. The phase of the output fixes the sign; what the single sine output cannot fix is the angle itself, because sin θ = sin(180° − θ), so two angles give the same amplitude and the same phase.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps, trap 1: The wording 'Using degrees inside sin Δθ for the small-angle form' is muddled. Degrees inside sin Δθ are perfectly acceptable; the mistake is using degrees inside the linear approximation E_max·Δθ.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps, fourth trap (lift-off vs target thickness): The line 'a coating changes the effective gap but is not the same quantity' is ambiguous. Read quickly, it suggests a nonconductive coating is not part of lift-off, which is wrong: in eddy current work a nonconductive coating simply adds to the probe-to-surface gap, so it acts as extra lift-off. What the trap really wants to say is that coating thickness is not the same as the metal target's own thickness.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q5: The question asks for two applications but the answer lists four items. In an exam the student cannot tell which two are expected, and a marker may cut marks for an answer that does not match the demand of the question.
- ch 5 [high] Exam traps, bullet 3 (flush vs non-flush): The bullet says a shielded (flush) sensor loses range if metal is too close beside its face. That is backwards, and it contradicts Section 1 of the same chapter. Shielding exists so the field leaves only from the front face, which is exactly why a flush sensor can be sunk into a steel bracket without loss. It is the unshielded (non-flush) sensor that needs clear space, because metal brought alongside its face alters the field and the switching distance.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q6: The answer does not match its own question. (a) A PNP sensor on a sinking PLC input is the correct pairing, as the chapter's own exam trap states, so calling it 'mismatched' confuses the student; a true mismatch gives no signal, the opposite of the reported symptom. (b) The answer says a broken wire 'looks identical to an open gate', i.e. the machine stops, but the reported fault is that the machine keeps running with the gate open. (c) It then says an NC sensor is needed 'so a fault stops the machine', although the NO case it just described also stops the machine on a wire break.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1, steps 3 and 6 (and the answer line): The example calls 7.0 mm against S_brass = 6.4 mm and 6.0 mm against S_ss = 5.6 mm flatly 'not detected'. That is too strong, given the chapter's own ±10 % band: the operate point can sit up to 1.1 × 6.4 = 7.04 mm (brass) and 1.1 × 5.6 = 6.16 mm (stainless), so both targets may well switch, but not reliably. Step 4 already uses the 0.9 factor for guaranteed operation, so the reasoning is inconsistent within the same example.
- ch 5 [low] Section 4, 'Speed' bullet: Counting teeth does not give revolutions per second. A gear with N teeth turned at f revolutions per second produces N × f pulses per second, so teeth-per-second alone is not revolutions per second unless the gear has one tooth.
- ch 5 [low] Worked example 1 data (brass correction factor), with Section 1 and the exam traps: K = 0.80 for brass is far above the values in most datasheets, where brass is usually about 0.4 to 0.5 and aluminium about 0.4. A student who checks a real datasheet table will think the chapter's factor is wrong, or will use 0.8 for brass in the exam.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, paragraph 3: The sentence 'The change is small: a plate of 100 mm² at a 0.5 mm gap gives about 1.8 pF' gives the total capacitance but labels it as the change, so a student may read 1.8 pF as the signal change.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, third bullet: 'Amplify with a high input impedance amplifier; at balance the bridge output is close to zero' joins two unrelated ideas, so the reader cannot tell whether zero output at balance is a good thing or a problem.
- ch 6 [low] Exam traps, trap 3: The trap says 'gap for pressure and sound, area for displacement', but Section 2 also lists variable gap for small displacements. A student can take the two statements as contradicting each other.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q6 answer: 'DC charges the capacitor and causes polarisation and drift' is vague and partly wrong for an air gap, where there is nothing to polarise. The main reason DC fails is left out: a capacitor passes no steady current, so a DC circuit gives no continuous signal and only a decaying transient.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, Quiz Q4, and the cable-capacitance exam trap: This section mixes two different preamplifier modes. It first describes a charge amplifier whose input is a virtual ground and whose output is VpΔC/Cf, then says the microphone needs very high input impedance and that cable capacitance adds in parallel and steals charge/lowers sensitivity. For an ideal charge amplifier, the virtual-ground input means cable capacitance has almost no signal voltage across it and does not change that output. The high-input-impedance and cable-capacitance-divider effects apply to a high-impedance voltage preamplifier, not the charge-amplifier circuit described. Quiz Q4 is therefore wrong for the stated charge-amplifier mode.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, bullet 'High sensitivity for a small diaphragm': This is technically wrong. Sensitivity generally increases with diaphragm area and compliance. Small-diaphragm capacitor microphones have lower sensitivity than large-diaphragm ones; their advantage is usually better high-frequency and transient response.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, sound-pressure bullet list: The description only covers the positive-pressure half-cycle: diaphragm moves towards the back plate, gap decreases, capacitance increases. Sound pressure is alternating, so the diaphragm also moves away, the gap increases, and capacitance falls. A student may wrongly think capacitance only rises.
