## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Worked example 1, step 5: The subtraction shown is diag(60 − 60, 60 − 60, 0 − 60), which gives diag(0, 0, −60) MPa, not the diag(60, 0, −60) MPa stated as the answer. The first entry should be the original σx = 120 MPa minus the spherical 60 MPa, not 60 − 60.
- ch 1 [high] Lead paragraph: It says the labels uniaxial/biaxial/triaxial decide whether a problem is plane stress or plane strain. They do not: the geometry and the restraints decide that, and a biaxial state can be plane stress or plane strain. This contradicts the exam trap that warns against mixing biaxial with plane stress, so a student will carry the wrong idea into 2.3.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, formula where-list: The tensor is written with τxz but the list of six components below it names τzx. Both are equal, but switching symbols between the matrix and the list is confusing for a first reading.
- ch 2 [high] Section 2, last paragraph: The text says the shear sign flips when θ increases by 180°. With t defined as n rotated 90° counterclockwise, the formula gives τnt(θ+180°)=τnt(θ). The opposite face has an opposite traction vector, but t also reverses, so the τnt component is unchanged. A sign flip occurs for θ+90°, not θ+180°.
- ch 2 [high] Quiz Q6 answer: The answer names only σn. If the student uses 30° in cos2θ and 60° in sin2θ, the cos2θ term is wrong in both σn and τnt (unless that coefficient is zero), so both stresses are wrong in general. The sin2θ term with 60° is correct for θ=30°.
- ch 2 [low] Figure spec: The y-axis minimum is −30 MPa, but τnt reaches about −36.1 MPa near θ=72°. The shear curve is clipped, so the plotted shear variation is incomplete.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q5 answer: The phrase 'no plane carries shear' and 'on every plane' is too broad. In plane stress with σx=σy=100 MPa and σz=0, planes whose normal has an out-of-plane component can carry shear. The zero-shear result applies to planes with normals in the xy plane.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, τxy paragraph: The sentence 'Students often keep this term only in τnt' is misleading: τxy sin2θ appears in σn, while τnt has τxy cos2θ. A student could think τxy sin2θ belongs in τnt.
- ch 2 [low] Beyond: Strain rosettes do not directly invert the stress transformation to recover σx, σy and τxy from measured strains. They invert the strain transformation to get εx, εy and γxy; Hooke's law then gives stresses.
- ch 3 [low] Figure (table spec and caption): The column header says the columns are σx, σy, τxy, but the θ=45° row actually holds σx′, σy′ and τx′y′, and the invariant row holds the sums σx+σy and σx′+σy′, not components. A student can read 120 MPa as a normal stress on the x-face.
- ch 3 [low] Figure caption: "At θ = 45° the normal stresses stay 100 MPa and 20 MPa" is true only for these particular numbers (τxy = 40 MPa exactly offsets the (σx−σy)/2 cos 2θ term, which is zero at 45°). Read alone, it suggests normal stresses never change at 45°, which contradicts the general equations and Q3.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q4: The angle given for the maximum in-plane shear is wrong. Here σx = σy = 100 MPa, so tan2θp = 2τxy/(σx−σy) = 80/0, giving 2θp = 90° and θp = 45° (and 135°). Then θs = θp − 45° = 0°, i.e. the maximum shear acts on the x face itself (and the y face). The stated θs = −45° is 135°, which is a principal plane where the shear is zero, not maximum. Substituting θ = −45° in τ = −((σx−σy)/2)sin2θ + τxy cos2θ gives 0, not 40 MPa.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 and Worked example step 10: The chapter defines the three principal stresses with the ordering σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3 and then says 'Plane stress is the case σ3 = 0'. That holds only when both in-plane stresses are tensile. In the worked example the stresses are 98.10, 0 and −58.10 MPa, so under the stated ordering the zero stress is σ2 and σ3 is −58.10 MPa; step 10 labels the zero stress as σ3, contradicting the ordering given two paragraphs earlier.
- ch 5 [high] Section 1, drawing step 'To read a plane at angle θ from the x face, rotate CA by 2θ in the same sense', and Section 2, 'the plot fixes clockwise or counter-clockwise': The rotation-sense rule is reversed. With positive τ plotted upward and A at (σx, τxy), the plotted point is (σ, τx'y') written with the standard sign of the shear on the plane. Then for a plane rotated counter-clockwise by θ from the x face the point moves 2θ clockwise on the circle. Example: for σx = σy = 0 and τxy = 50 MPa, A = (0, 50) sits at the top of the circle; the major principal point σ1 is at the right edge, reached by a 90° clockwise turn on the plot, while the real rotation is 45° counter-clockwise. A student who follows 'same sense' reads the wrong plane and the wrong direction for θp.
- ch 5 [high] Worked example 1, step 8 and answer: The direction of θp is given as clockwise. On the plot the turn from CA (at +39.81°) to the σ1 axis is clockwise, so the physical rotation is counter-clockwise. Check with the transformation equations: at θ = +19.90° (counter-clockwise from the x face), σn = 20 + 60cos39.81° + 50sin39.81° = 98.10 MPa, so the major principal direction is 19.90° counter-clockwise, not clockwise.
- ch 5 [high] Quiz Q5: Same direction error. For σx = 60 MPa, σy = 20 MPa, τxy = 30 MPa the major principal direction is 28.16° counter-clockwise from the x face, not clockwise. Check: at 2θ = 56.31°, σn = 40 + 20cos56.31° + 30sin56.31° = 40 + 11.09 + 24.97 = 76.06 MPa = σ1.
- ch 5 [low] Exam trap 4 (mixing shear sign conventions): The claim 'the stated direction of θp reverses' is misleading. The physical direction of the principal plane is a fact about the stress state and does not change with the plotting convention. Mirroring the plot only changes the sense in which you rotate inside the drawn circle.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, sign convention: The positive direction of τxy and the sense in which θ is measured are never defined, yet steps 7–8 and Q5 give clockwise/counter-clockwise answers that depend on both.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, first sentence: 'A shaft under bending and torsion is a common user of this circle' is odd wording; a shaft does not use a circle.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1: The sentence says the factor 1/2 'makes the tensor symmetric, εxy = εyx'. That is not the reason. The strain tensor is symmetric because εxy = εyx by the definition εxy = (1/2)(∂u/∂y + ∂v/∂x). The 1/2 is there so that the tensor shear component equals half the engineering shear strain, which is what makes the components transform like stress components. As written, a student may believe that omitting the 1/2 would break symmetry.
- ch 6 [low] Quiz Q5: The answer says θp is negative, but tan 2θp = −1/3 has two roots in θp: −9.2° (which gives ε1) and +80.8° (which gives ε2). The two roots differ by 90°, so calling θp simply 'negative' without naming the ε1 root conflicts with the chapter's own exam trap. A student could think the second root is also negative.
- ch 7 [high] Section 2, delta rosette reduction formula: The delta reduction for shear strain is missing the factor 2. With gauges at 0°, 60° and 120° and the transformation equation given in the same section, ε60 − ε120 = (√3/2)γxy, so γxy = 2(ε60 − ε120)/√3. The printed γxy = (ε60 − ε120)/√3 gives half the correct shear strain.
- ch 7 [high] Quiz Q4: The answer uses the incorrect delta formula. For ε0 = 400×10⁻⁶, ε60 = 300×10⁻⁶, ε120 = 100×10⁻⁶ the correct value is γxy = 2(300 − 100)×10⁻⁶/1.732 = 231×10⁻⁶, not 115.5×10⁻⁶. The printed answer is exactly half.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, rectangular rosette paragraph: "The first two readings give εx and εy directly" is misleading: the 0° and 45° readings are the first two in the list, and the 45° reading does not give εy. Students may pair the wrong gauges.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, principal direction formula: tan 2θp = γxy/(εx − εy) has two solutions 90° apart, and the chapter never says how to decide which one belongs to ε1. Some syllabi ask for the principal direction of ε1 specifically.
- ch 8 [high] Section 3, von Mises formulas (also the plane-stress forms in step 7 and quiz Q4): The exponents are typed as plain digits, so the formulas read 'σvm = √(0.5[(σ1 − σ2)2 + (σ2 − σ3)2 + (σ3 − σ1)2])' and 'σvm = √(σ12 − σ1σ2 + σ22)' and '√(σx2 − σxσy + σy2 + 3τxy2)'. A reader sees σ12 or (σ1 − σ2)2, not a square, and the formula becomes wrong as printed.
- ch 8 [high] Exam traps, first trap: 'Using σx and σy directly in Tresca or von Mises. Both theories need the principal stresses' contradicts section 3, which gives a von Mises formula straight from σx, σy and τxy. von Mises can be computed from the given components; the real mistake is leaving out τxy, or using σx − σy directly in place of σ1 − σ3 for Tresca.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, Tresca paragraph: The theory is defined but never given its second standard name, so a student meeting 'maximum shear stress theory' in an exam question may not connect it to Tresca.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, Rankine paragraph (and Section 1 definition of σY): Rankine is applied with the yield strength σY, but cast iron and concrete do not yield; standard treatments compare the largest principal stress with the ultimate tensile strength (or the ultimate compressive strength when the largest stress is compressive). Using σY for a brittle material is inconsistent with the material behaviour the paragraph itself describes.
- ch 9 [low] figure (Displacement to strain): The caption says "The figure follows the worked example", but the figure is placed before Worked Example 1. Also the "Derivatives" node shows only ∂u/∂x = 3e-3 and ∂v/∂y = 2e-3, yet the next node produces γxy = 3e-3, which needs ∂u/∂y and ∂v/∂x. Those two mixed derivatives appear nowhere in the diagram, so a student cannot see where the shear number comes from, even though the caption tells them to notice that γxy uses both mixed derivatives.
- ch 9 [low] section 1 (body force paragraph): The sign convention for X, Y, Z is not stated. The text says only "add X to the x equation", but quiz Q5 needs X = -6 N/mm³ to restore balance. Without knowing that X is positive along +x, a student cannot tell whether the needed body force is +6 or -6.
- ch 9 [low] exam traps (unit mixing): "the strain is wrong by 1000" does not say whether the answer comes out too large or too small, which is exactly the point a student needs in the exam.
