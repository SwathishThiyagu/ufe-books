## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 2, Worked example 1, Quiz Q5 and Q6: The value v_g = 1.694 m³/kg is the saturated vapour volume near 100 kPa (1 bar), not at 101.325 kPa (1 atm). At 101.325 kPa and about 100 °C, v_g is approximately 1.673 m³/kg. The worked example and quiz answers therefore use a saturated vapour volume that is too large for the stated pressure.
- ch 1 [high] Section 3, paragraph on flattening to the T-v plane: The whole wet region on a T-v diagram is not a horizontal band at one T_sat. It is the area bounded by the saturated liquid and saturated vapour lines. Only a constant-pressure heating at one fixed pressure appears as a horizontal segment at that pressure's T_sat.
- ch 1 [high] Section 3, refrigeration bullet: A refrigeration evaporator normally leaves saturated vapour or slightly superheated vapour, not a wet mixture. Liquid droplets at the evaporator exit would enter the compressor. The wet mixture is produced after throttling and exists inside the evaporator, not normally at its exit.
- ch 1 [low] Lead: 'Closed vessel' can suggest a rigid closed vessel. In a rigid closed vessel the pressure rises during heating and water does not boil at a fixed temperature. The fixed-temperature boiling described happens only at constant pressure.
- ch 1 [high] Section 3, LNG storage bullet: LNG storage is near its normal boiling point, not near the triple line. For methane, the triple point is about 90.7 K and 11.7 kPa, while LNG is stored near 111 K at near-atmospheric pressure. A small pressure drop causes flash boiling, not freezing.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1: The text says the water triple point 'is used to define the kelvin'. Since the 2019 SI redefinition the kelvin is fixed by the Boltzmann constant, not by the triple point of water; the triple point is now a fixed calibration reference at 273.16 K.
- ch 2 [high] Worked example 1, step 5 and part (b): At exactly 0 °C (273.15 K) the minimum pressure for liquid water is not the triple-point pressure. The melting curve of water slopes backwards, so ice melts at 0 °C only at about 1 atm (101 kPa); 0.6117 kPa is the melting pressure at 0.01 °C. At 0 °C and any pressure between 0.6117 kPa and about 101 kPa the stable phase is ice, not liquid.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, setup and part (c): A closed rigid vessel cannot be heated at constant pressure: in a fixed volume the pressure rises as ice sublimes. Also, at 0 °C ice and vapour sit in equilibrium at about 0.61 kPa, not at 0.5 kPa.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, step 4: At 0 °C and 0.5 kPa, any liquid water present would flash to vapour. It would not freeze.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1: The sentence 'At pressures above the triple point, heating ice first melts it to liquid, then boils the liquid to vapour' fails above the critical pressure (22.064 MPa for water), where there is no distinct boiling.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3: Most vacuum drying runs at a few kPa, above the triple-point pressure, and water there evaporates from the liquid. Only freeze drying and low-pressure vacuum drying go below 0.6117 kPa.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, table row vg: The column is headed 1 atm (100 °C), and the hf, hfg and hg values (419, 2257, 2676 kJ/kg) are indeed the 100 °C / 1 atm values. But vg = 1.694 m³/kg is the value at 1 bar (99.63 °C), not at 1 atm. At 100 °C the correct value is about 1.673 m³/kg, so the table mixes two pressures in one column.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, T-v sketch sentence: "v jumps from vf to vg as x rises" suggests a sudden change. During boiling the specific volume changes continuously from vf to vg as x goes from 0 to 1; the point sits at a distance x(vg − vf) along the flat line.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, enthalpy definition: The relation h = u + pv is given without saying what u, p and v are, and v is later reused for specific volume of wet steam. A second-language reader has to guess.
- ch 4 [high] Section 3, bullet on constant-temperature lines: The bullet says superheated isotherms are 'nearly horizontal and droop at low pressure, where steam behaves like an ideal gas and h depends on T alone'. The reason given contradicts the claim: if h depends only on T, the isotherm is flat, not drooping. In fact an isotherm on the h-s chart runs from the saturation curve to the right and rises; it rises steeply near the saturation line at high pressure (at 400 °C, h goes from about 3097 kJ/kg at 10 MPa to about 3264 kJ/kg at 1 MPa) and flattens out at low pressure. So the sentence is wrong both in direction and in where the bend occurs.
- ch 4 [high] Quiz Q7: The arithmetic in the answer does not support the conclusion. The gap is 2939.8 − 2890 = about 50 kJ/kg, which is about 1.7 per cent, but the answer says a 1 per cent error is about 29 kJ/kg 'which covers the gap'. 29 kJ/kg is smaller than 50 kJ/kg, so it does not cover it. The answer also contradicts the chapter's own statement that the chart reads to 'about 1 or 2 per cent'.
- ch 4 [low] Worked example 1 / Section 2 interpolation: The example assumes 250 °C is missing from the 10 bar superheated table, yet the section says tables step in 50 K, and real 10 bar tables do carry a 250 °C column. Also, the interpolated h = 2939.8 kJ/kg is about 3 kJ/kg below the tabulated value at 10 bar, 250 °C (about 2943 kJ/kg; v about 0.2327 m³/kg, s about 6.926 kJ/kg·K). A student who checks the table will think the worked answer is wrong.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, mixture formula block: 'Because v_f is about a thousandth of v_g' is loose. At 10 bar, v_f = 0.001127 m³/kg and v_g = 0.1944 m³/kg, a ratio of about 1/170, not 1/1000. The shortcut v ≈ x·v_g also fails when x is very small, because then x·v_g becomes smaller than v_f itself.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3, chart accuracy sentence and exam traps: 'The printed scale limits the chart to about 1 or 2 per cent' is unclear: a chart is not limited to a percentage; its reading accuracy is. The phrase '1 per cent' later in Q7 adds to the confusion.
- ch 4 [low] Lead: The lead says the tables are 'arranged by pressure, by temperature or by dryness fraction'. Steam tables are laid out by pressure or by temperature; dryness fraction is not a layout, it is used through the mixture formulas.
- ch 5 [low] Lead paragraph: The lead says steam tables turn p₂ and T₂ into h₂ 'and then into dryness x₁', which suggests only the outlet measurements are needed. x₁ also needs the inlet pressure p₁ to get h_f1 and h_fg1. A student skimming the lead may miss this.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 and Section 2 limits: The chapter says the throttling calorimeter fails when the outlet stays wet, but never states the practical working range. The needed superheat shrinks as the inlet gets wetter and as p₁ rises, so the instrument only works for fairly dry steam (in practice x₁ roughly above 0.95, and less at high pressure). Without this, students may think any wet steam can be tested as long as the outlet happens to be superheated.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2 (Why real gases deviate): The claim that at 5 MPa carbon dioxide is 'off by several percent' badly understates the error. At 300 K and 5 MPa, CO2 sits just below its saturation pressure (about 6.7 MPa at that temperature) and its compressibility factor is roughly 0.7, so the ideal law is wrong by about 30 percent, not a few percent. The sentence also contradicts the next paragraph, which correctly says tens of percent near saturation.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2 (Why real gases deviate): 'or heated to high pressure well above the critical point' is garbled: pressure is not 'heated'. A student cannot tell whether the case meant is high pressure, high temperature, or supercritical state.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, formula boxes: The symbol R is used for two different constants: in pv = RT it is the universal constant 8.314 J/(mol·K), and in pV = mRT it is the specific constant in J/(kg·K). The second box then renames the universal constant R_u. Students will carry the wrong R into numerical work.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, second formula box and the air/CO2 example: M is defined as molar mass 'in kg/mol', but the worked substitutions use M = 28.97 kg/kmol and 44 kg/kmol with R_u = 8314. Mixing the two unit conventions is exactly the mistake the traps warn about.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3 (The van der Waals equation): 'The term a/v² adds back the pressure lost to attraction, as if an extra internal pressure pushed outward' is misleading: intermolecular attraction pulls molecules inward, which is why the wall pressure falls. The term a/v² is the pressure deficit, added to p, not an outward push.
- ch 7 [high] Section 3, third paragraph: The comparison sign is missing: the sentence reads "If Z  1, it is less compressible." As printed a student cannot tell which side of 1 is meant, and if the intended sign is <, the statement is factually wrong and contradicts the Q4 answer (Z < 1 means the gas is more compressible than ideal, not less).
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, first paragraph: "Near TR = 1 and pR = 1, Z can dip sharply because the gas is close to saturation" is loose. At TR = 1 the substance is exactly at its critical temperature, where there is no saturation; the deep dip comes from strong intermolecular attraction near the critical point. Only for TR slightly below 1 does the curve run into the saturation line.
