## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 1, after the definition of Sᵢⱼ: The sentence repeats itself: 'The array S is the scattering matrix; some syllabi call it the scattering matrix.' A student stops and wonders whether two different names are meant.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, reference-plane paragraph: Read literally, 'the phase of every S-parameter touching that port changes by e^{−jβl}' gives the wrong answer for the diagonal term Sᵢᵢ, which touches the same port at both ends and so picks up e^{−j2βl}. Only the off-diagonal terms touching the port once get a single factor.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, table of quantities: The 'Meaning' column describes return loss as 'Power reflected back at the input' and insertion loss as 'Power lost in forward transmission'. Both are dB ratios, not powers; a student may think RL is the reflected power in watts.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1, the one-line proof that a lossless reciprocal three-port cannot be matched: The proof is wrong. The first row of S†S = I with S11 = 0 gives |S12|² + |S13|² = 1, which does not force |S12| = 1; it only forces |S12| ≤ 1. Nothing in that step makes |S13| zero, so the chain of reasoning that follows ('second row forces |S23| = 0, third row fails') does not hold. A student who reproduces this in the exam writes an invalid proof.
- ch 2 [high] Quiz Q4 answer: The answer repeats the same faulty proof as Section 1: 'the first row of S†S = I needs |S12| = 1, the second row then needs |S23| = 0'. The first row only gives |S12|² + |S13|² = 1, so the step is not valid and the answer earns no marks for reasoning.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, Hardware around the junction: Corners, E-plane bends, H-plane bends and twists are general waveguide hardware, not parts of a tee, and this section sits inside a chapter on waveguide tees without saying how these pieces are used with a tee. Read on a phone the week before the exam, it looks like material from a different chapter.
- ch 2 [low] Figure caption and figure spec: The caption says the E-arm receives 0 mW, but the figure shows two dashed edges from the E-arm to ports 1 and 2 labelled '−' and '+'. A student can read this as power flowing out of the E-arm in this experiment, which contradicts the caption.
- ch 3 [high] Section 2 (hybrid ring paragraph) and Quiz Q7: The port numbering used for the rat-race is impossible for a ring whose four ports are numbered in order around the ring with three λg/4 arcs and one 3λg/4 arc. Ports 1 and 4 are then neighbours (one arc apart of λg/4 or 3λg/4), and two neighbouring ports always couple: their two paths differ by 0 or by λg, so they add, they never cancel. In the standard figure, the isolated pair is ports 1 and 3 (paths differ by λg/2 one way and λg the other), the sum port is 1, the difference port is 3, and the outputs are 2 and 4. So the chapter's claims that feeding port 1 gives in-phase outputs at 2 and 3 with port 4 isolated, and feeding port 4 gives 180° at 2 and 3 with port 1 isolated, contradict the spacing rule the chapter itself states, and a student who draws the usual ring will get Q7 wrong.
- ch 3 [low] Lead: The sentence 'The fourth port, port 4, should receive almost nothing; how little is set by directivity' is wrong as stated. How much reaches port 4 is set by the isolation I, and I = C + D. Directivity only gives the ratio between the coupled wave and the leakage; two couplers with the same directivity but different coupling give very different port-4 levels.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, hybrid ring paragraph: 'The 3λg/4 arm supplies a 180° phase shift' is loose: a 3λg/4 arm is 270° of electrical length, and it only counts as 180° when compared with the λg/4 arms of the same ring.
- ch 4 [high] Worked example 1, steps 1–2; quiz Q3 and Q6; Section 2 formula note: The powers of ten have lost their superscripts, so the expressions read as ordinary arithmetic and give wrong numbers. Step 1 shows 'Pin = 1020/10 mW = 100 mW', which a student will read as 1020 ÷ 10 = 102 mW. Step 2 shows '100 × 10−6/10 mW', Q3 shows '104/10 mW', Q6 shows '10−16/10 mW', and the note after the Wilkinson formula says '|S|2 is a power ratio' instead of |S|². Only subscripts are marked up; no exponent is.
- ch 4 [low] Quiz Q2: The answer names the unitary condition but then gives a restatement ('matching all ports would leave no place for reflected or dissipated power') instead of the actual argument, so it does not really show why the condition fails.
- ch 4 [low] Lead paragraph: 'Power is a wave quantity' is loose and can mislead: power is not a wave quantity, and the point being made is simply that splits and losses are power ratios, so they are counted in dB of power.
- ch 5 [high] Figure and its aria description (Section 3 illustration): The block diagram draws the whole loop 1→2→3→1 as 'low loss', but the notes give port 2 as 9.5 dBm and port 3 as −15 dBm. A student following the arrows computes port 3 ≈ 9.0 dBm (10 dBm minus two low-loss hops), not −15 dBm. The caption adds to the confusion by saying power at port 2 'would continue on to port 3'. The diagram also contradicts its own caption, because the low-loss edge p3→p1 shows power getting back to port 1, while the caption says the load absorbs it and nothing comes back.
- ch 5 [low] Exam traps, fourth bullet: 'return loss is reflection at one port (S₁₁ in dB, negative)' invites the wrong sign habit: return loss is conventionally quoted as a positive dB number, equal to −20 log₁₀|S₁₁|, so a −20 dB S₁₁ is a 20 dB return loss.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, second paragraph: 'Reverse, port 2 → port 3, the load soaks up the power and nothing reaches port 1; that reverse attenuation is the isolation I' suggests the load's absorption creates the isolation. The isolation figure is set by the circulator's own reverse leakage (2→1); a perfect load only stops reflections from making it worse, as the next paragraph says.
- ch 6 [high] Section 1, velocity modulation paragraph: The bunching description is reversed. It says 'Fast electrons leave ahead of slow ones', but electrons speeded up by the gap cross later than the slowed ones, so they are behind and catch up. As written, fast electrons ahead of slow ones would move even further ahead and no bunching could ever form; the next sentence ('the fast ones catch the slow ones') contradicts it.
- ch 6 [high] Exam traps, third bullet: The claimed 'error of 10⁶' is wrong. kV multiplied by mA already gives watts (10³ × 10⁻³ = 1), so 2 kV × 50 mA = 100 W is correct with no conversion. Only converting one quantity, not both, produces an error.
- ch 6 [low] Comparison table, Reflex klystron row, Gain column: The gain cell reads 'oscillator', which is a device type, not a gain. A student filling in a table column may be confused about what value belongs there.
- ch 6 [low] Comparison table, Two-cavity klystron row, Typical use column: Two-cavity klystrons have only about 15–30 dB gain and are rarely the output tube of a TV or accelerator transmitter; those use multicavity klystrons with much higher gain. Listing 'UHF TV, particle accelerators' against the two-cavity row misleads.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1: The arithmetic is right, but the numbers give a DC-to-RF efficiency of 25%. Real Gunn oscillators run at roughly 2–10% (pulsed devices can reach about 15%), so a student may take 25% as a normal Gunn figure and carry it into the exam.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, TRAPATT paragraph: 'The waveform is rich in harmonics, so the cavity may operate at a lower frequency' gives the wrong reason. Harmonics lie above the fundamental, not below it. TRAPATT oscillates below the transit-time frequency because the trapped plasma is extracted slowly, so the operating period is much longer than the transit time; the sharp pulse waveform is a consequence, not the cause.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q4: The answer puts the whole phase delay in the drift region. The avalanche region itself makes the conduction current lag the voltage by about 90° (a quarter period), and the drift transit adds the rest; without the avalanche lag, a drift transit of half a period would over-delay the current and negative resistance would not follow.
- ch 7 [low] Exam traps, tunnel-diode bullet: 'Bias must sit between peak and valley current' describes the wrong quantity. The bias point is set by the DC voltage, which must lie between the peak-current voltage and the valley-current voltage, inside the negative-slope region.
- ch 7 [low] Figure: The block diagram shows all four sources (Gunn, IMPATT, TRAPATT, tunnel) driving one shared cavity at the same time, which is not how any real source works; each source is used with its own resonator.
- ch 7 [low] Quiz Q3: Powers of ten are typed with the caret (1×10^7 cm/s, 8.33×10^-6 m) while the rest of the chapter uses Unicode superscripts.
- ch 8 [low] Worked example 1 and the figure (10 GHz, λg = 30 mm): At 10 GHz the free-space wavelength is 30 mm, so a guide wavelength of exactly 30 mm is only possible on an air-filled coaxial slotted line. In a rectangular waveguide λg is always greater than λ0 (about 40 mm in X-band guide at 10 GHz), so a student who assumes the slotted line is a waveguide will get a contradiction.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, thermocouple bullet: 'A DC voltage appears at the cold junction' can be misread as if the cold junction alone generates the emf. The emf comes from the temperature difference between the two junctions.
- ch 8 [low] Figure caption: 'the detector is square law, so work in voltage or take square roots of power readings' is muddled. A square-law detector output follows RF power; it is the meter calibration (volts or power) that decides how the readings must be treated.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, square-law detector paragraph: Only the VSWR form of the square-root correction is given. A student with a power-calibrated meter also needs the corrected formula for |Γ|.
- ch 9 [high] Worked example 1, steps 3–5: The example sets the actual output equal to the 1 dB compression point (18 dBm) and then calls the difference from the linear value (20 dBm) a 2 dB compression. That contradicts the definition given in step 2: by definition the output is only 1 dB below the straight line when it equals P1dB(out) = 18 dBm. Driving 2 dB past P1dB means the real output is above 18 dBm and the compression is under 2 dB, not exactly 2 dB. Steps 3, 4 and 5 are therefore wrong, and so is the answer.
- ch 9 [high] Quiz Q4: Same error as the worked example. Linear output 7 dBm is 2 dB above P1dB(out) = 5 dBm, so the real output is not 'held near 5 dBm' and the compression is not 2 dB. 5 dBm is the 1 dB compression point, not the saturation output; the answer confuses P1dB with P_sat and breaks the definition of compression.
- ch 9 [low] Figure and section 2 process steps: The process list makes the airbridge the second (plated) metal, but the figure stack shows a separate 'Metal 2 interconnect' layer under the dielectric with the airbridge as a further layer on top. A student cannot tell whether metal 2 is the airbridge or the interconnect below it.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, transistor bullet: 'a device with usable gain at 2 GHz is small at 20 GHz' is unclear — 'small' does not say what is small (the device, the gain, the output power).
