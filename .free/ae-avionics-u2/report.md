## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Lead: "It stores fluid under pressure" is ambiguous: the reservoir stores fluid at low pressure; only the accumulator stores fluid under pressure. A student may take it to mean the reservoir is pressurised.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2: "Some syllabi call the accumulator an oil-gas accumulator" is not the usual textbook name and may confuse a reader looking it up.
- ch 1 [low] Quiz Q2: The answer says peak flows are supplied "without the pump changing speed immediately". A variable-displacement pump answers demand by changing its displacement, not its speed.
- ch 2 [high] Section 2 / Figure and its caption: The chapter shows APU bleed joining upstream of the precooler and passing through it. In a typical installation the precooler is on the engine bleed line and is cooled by that engine's fan air; APU bleed joins the manifold downstream of the precooler and pressure regulating valve, through its own shut-off valve and check valve. A student would learn the wrong routing, and would not be able to explain why APU-only ground operation works with no engine fan air available.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3 and Quiz Q6: Cabin pressurisation is listed among manifold users 'each through its own valve' and in the Q6 answer as a bleed user, but the outflow valve is a fuselage valve that is not fed by bleed air; cabin pressure is a downstream result of conditioned pack air.
- ch 3 [high] Worked example 1, step 5: Step 5 claims carbon discs 'survive about 22 MJ per brake'. This number is not a standard textbook value; it has been reverse-engineered from the answer and stated as a material specification. It also adds a choice of disc material that the question never asked about.
- ch 3 [high] Exam traps, trap 3: 'The downlock is the one that holds the aircraft up at touchdown' is wrong as written. The gear leg carries the aircraft; the downlock only keeps the leg locked straight. A student can read this as the lock supporting the aircraft's weight.
- ch 3 [low] Section 1, oleo strut paragraph: 'The static seal holds gas pressure when the strut is still, and the dynamic seal slides with the piston and keeps oil in' suggests the static seal stops working once the strut moves. A static seal separates two parts that never move relative to each other; the sliding joint is sealed by the dynamic seal at all times.
- ch 3 [low] Figure spec and caption: The tree puts 'Antiskid' as a sibling of 'Steel disc' and 'Carbon', so it reads as a type of brake disc, but antiskid is a control system that works on the brake. It also places 'Nose gear' only under 'Retractable', although nose gear can be fixed.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: The cross-references to Chapter 2.10 and Chapter 2.1 are plain text, so a student reading on a phone cannot jump to them, while the book's convention is a chapter link.
- ch 4 [high] Section 1, fuel path paragraph: It says 'The metering unit, or FADEC (full authority digital engine control), sets the flow'. The metering unit and FADEC are not the same thing. FADEC is the electronic control system (computers plus a hydromechanical unit); the metering unit is the hardware that actually meters fuel, and it is commanded by the FADEC. A student will write that FADEC is a fuel-metering valve in the exam.
- ch 4 [low] Figure (fuel path blocks): The figure leaves out the fuel filter that the Section 1 text puts in the path, so the diagram and the text show different fuel paths. A student revising from the picture alone will miss the filter and its bypass.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1 formula and Section 2 formula: Density in kg/L is not the usual unit; a student may substitute kg/m³ from a data table and get an answer 1000 times wrong. The oil formula is fine but the unit convention is not stated for the SI case.
- ch 5 [high] Exam traps, bullet 4: The trap says that 'ignition before sufficient N2, or fuel too early, gives a rich mixture and a hot start'. Ignition switched on early is harmless and is in fact required: the interlock in Section 3 says ignition must be on before fuel, and igniters are normally energised at the start of cranking. Only early or excess fuel causes the rich mixture. As written, a student will memorise that early ignition causes a hot start, which contradicts Section 3.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 and Section 3 (cut-out N2): Starter cut-out is given as 'often 25-30 % N2' as if it were general. The value is engine-specific and on many large turbofans cut-out is well above this, nearer 35-45 % N2, with idle around 60 % N2.
- ch 5 [low] Section 3, start sequence: Ignition is given as 'about 15-20 % N2' and fuel as 'about 17-20 % N2'. The two bands overlap, so the stated order (ignition before fuel) is not guaranteed and the numbers are ambiguous. The timeline (fuel at 17 s) also sits above the 20 % band if N2 rises linearly to 25 % at 20 s.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, first and last paragraphs: The exciter is first defined as converting 'low-voltage DC' into a high-voltage pulse, then the last paragraph says it 'receives 28 V DC or 115 V AC depending on type'. The two statements contradict each other for AC-supplied exciters.
- ch 6 [low] Lead: The lead says every airliner uses this bleed-fed pack, and names the 787 as an example, but the Beyond section correctly says the 787 carries no bleed air and drives its packs with electric compressors. A student meets the opposite claim twice.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, pack component list: The bullet list of pack components ends with a stray bullet reading only 'Cabin.' The cabin is the destination, not a component of the pack, and the bullet carries no information.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, secondary heat exchanger bullet: 'The air leaves cooler than it did after the primary' is not reliably true. Both heat exchangers cool towards ram-air temperature; the secondary one removes the heat the compressor has just added and brings the air back down to roughly the primary outlet temperature.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, compressor bullet: The chapter says the compressor makes the air hotter at higher pressure but never says why it is there, so the reader cannot see why the pack is not simply two heat exchangers and a turbine.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2, protections: 'A dump switch opens the valve fully on the ground, used for smoke removal' ties the dump function to the ground, but smoke and fumes are cleared in flight, and the ground use is to bring the cabin to field elevation.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, formula Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp: 'The condenser ... is always larger than the evaporator' mixes two ideas: the heat duty is larger, and the physical size is usually larger. A student can read it as a size rule and then wonder why a small condenser on a ground cart still works.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1, R-134a boiling point: The −26 °C figure is the boiling point at one atmosphere, but the sentence can make a student think the evaporator runs at −26 °C. In a real pack the evaporator saturation temperature is set by the suction pressure and is held a few degrees above 0 °C so that condensate on the fins does not freeze.
- ch 7 [low] Figure caption: 'both heat flows pass through the compressor work' is unclear and technically loose: the work does not pass through anything, it is added and appears again in the condenser duty.
- ch 7 [low] Worked example 1, part (c): The wording 'the same compressor is used in a pack that must reach COP = 3.5' is confusing. A given compressor at a given speed has roughly fixed mass flow and work, so a student may ask how the COP can be chosen independently of the compressor.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, regulators: The text says 'Regulators come in three forms' (demand, diluter-demand, pressure-demand) but then says passenger masks are usually continuous-flow. A student can read this as a contradiction, because a continuous-flow passenger system also has a regulator (a simple pressure reducer feeding a fixed orifice).
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: 'Stored or chemical oxygen raises FO2 in the mask to nearly 1.0' reads as if the mask always gets pure oxygen. A diluter-demand regulator deliberately keeps FO2 well below 1.0 at low cabin altitude, as Section 2 and Quiz Q2 say.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1, TUC sentence: 'many minutes at 25,000 ft' is loose. Standard tables give roughly 3-5 minutes of useful consciousness at 25,000 ft, which a student may read as ten minutes or more.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, mask drop-out: The sentence 'The passenger pulls the mask to start flow; this is automatic mask drop-out' attaches the term drop-out to the passenger's pull. Drop-out is the automatic release of the masks from the overhead panels; the pull is a separate manual action that starts the flow.
- ch 9 [high] Section 3, paragraph starting 'Engine and APU bottles are usually fixed': It says engine extinguishing bottles are usually fixed in the nacelle. The nacelle is the fire zone itself: it is hot, vibrating and inaccessible, and the agent has to be delivered into it from outside. On jet transports the engine bottles sit in the fuselage or wing/fairing structure near the engine, with pipes running to the nacelle discharge nozzles. Only the APU bottle is in the tail cone/aft bay.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, answer line: 'The bottle must hold 2.4 m³ of agent vapour' is misleading. The bottle stores the agent as a liquid under pressure; 2.4 m³ is the volume that vapour occupies after release and expansion in the zone.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, formula Va = C × Vz: This is an approximation that ignores the fact that the released agent displaces air in the zone. The standard total-flooding form divides by (1 − C), which matters when the concentration is not small.
- ch 9 [low] Quiz Q1 and its answer: The answer presents exactly four zones as the whole list. Many transport aircraft also have wheel-well fire detection and avionics/equipment-bay overheat or smoke detection, so a student may mark the four as universal.
- ch 9 [low] Section 2, control unit paragraph: The chapter never says that engine and APU detection loops are duplicated (an A loop and a B loop). This is why the control unit can show FIRE, FAULT or normal, and it is a standard exam point on how a single broken wire does not lose protection.
- ch 9 [low] Section 3, first paragraph after 'When the control unit confirms a fire': It implies an APU fire is always handled by the crew. On the ground, many types shut the APU down automatically and discharge the APU bottle without crew action; the crew handle is for in-flight use.
- ch 10 [high] Worked example 1 (statement and step 2): The stated law is wrong. For a fixed configuration, stall speed rises with mass as V_stall ∝ √m, not 1/√m (V_stall = √(2W ÷ (ρ·S·C_Lmax))). The step 2 arithmetic (√0.8 = 0.894, giving a lower speed at lower mass) follows V ∝ √m, so the text contradicts its own working.
- ch 10 [high] Quiz Q5: The question tells the student to use V ∝ 1/√m, which is the wrong relation; a heavier aircraft stalls at a higher speed. The numerical answer (116.2 kt) is the one that comes from V ∝ √m, so the question and the answer do not match the stated law.
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, skew sensor sentence: 'One flap surface twisting on its tracks' is loose. A flap does not twist; skew means one flap surface on a wing is not in the same position as the other surface on that same wing, for example after a drive-shaft or track failure. Asymmetry is the left-versus-right comparison the next sentence describes.
- ch 10 [low] Quiz Q2 answer: The second function is stated vaguely ('remove hydraulic pressure safely'), and the answer repeats the lamp function once only. Downlock switches are mainly there to confirm the lock, to light the green lamp, and to tell the control unit to shut off hydraulic pressure and interlock retraction.
- ch 10 [low] Quiz Q8 answer: It mixes two mechanisms: the limit switch 'opens the motor circuit' and also 'the control unit then stops the drive'. Say which one actually stops the motor.
- ch 11 [low] Section 2, table row 'Electro-thermal': The row says the heater mats are 'switched in cycles' as if this applies to every electro-thermal user. Pitot heads, TAT probes and windscreens are normally heated continuously (windscreens often with two power levels); cycling is used mainly for propeller mats and some leading-edge mats, where power is shared between zones.
- ch 11 [low] Section 2 (last paragraph), Exam traps, Quiz Q6: The thickness at which boots are first cycled is given loosely as 'a few millimetres' and then 'roughly 5 mm or more'. Standard training guidance is about 6 mm to 13 mm (1/4 to 1/2 inch) of ice before the first cycle. The chapter also gives two different figures for the same number.
- ch 11 [low] Section 1, bullet 'Engine and propeller': 'can damage them or cause a stall' is ambiguous: a student may read 'stall' as the aerodynamic stall of the aircraft, which is a different topic. The intended meaning is an engine compressor stall (surge).
- ch 11 [low] Section 3, list of protected areas: Angle-of-attack vanes (and, on some types, the ice detector probe itself) are electrically heated, but they are left out of the list and out of the Q2 answer. The list claims to cover the air-data probes and does not.
- ch 11 [low] Section 3, rain removal: 'the blade is held against it by air pressure' is unclear: the arm is spring-loaded against the glass, and the airflow adds to or relieves that load; 'air pressure' also suggests a pneumatic motor, which is a different part of the system.
- ch 11 [low] Quiz Q4 answer: 'The tail also works at small or negative angles of attack in cruise, where any roughness hurts' explains nothing: it repeats the claim instead of saying why. The marks are in the thin section, the short chord and the fact that the tail is trimmed close to its own critical angle in some flight conditions.
