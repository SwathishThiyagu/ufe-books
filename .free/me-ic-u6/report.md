## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [low] Section 2, third bullet (bullets list): The bullet is headed 'Crevasse volumes'. A crevasse is a deep crack in a glacier; the engine term is 'crevice'. The rest of the chapter (and the quiz) uses 'crevice', so the heading looks like a spelling error a student may copy.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, second paragraph: The water-gas shift is said to 'remove CO when water and oxygen are available'. The shift reaction CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2 needs steam, not oxygen; oxygen removes CO by direct oxidation (CO + ½O2 → CO2). As written, a student will think O2 is a reactant in the shift.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, Zeldovich formula block: The text says 'These three reactions are the Zeldovich mechanism'. Standard usage: the first two (O + N2 and N + O2) are the Zeldovich (thermal) mechanism; adding N + OH → NO + H makes it the extended Zeldovich mechanism.
- ch 1 [low] Section 3 and Exam trap 1 (peak NOx position): The peak is placed at 'φ ≈ 0.9' in the text and trap, but at 'air-fuel ratio 15' in the text and figure. With a stoichiometric ratio of 14.7:1, φ = 0.9 means an air-fuel ratio of about 16.3, while an air-fuel ratio of 15 means φ ≈ 0.98. The two numbers do not describe the same point.
- ch 1 [low] Section 2, wall-quenching bullet, and Quiz Q3 answer: The quench layer is given as 'roughly 1 mm'. The value usually quoted for flame quenching at a cold wall in an SI engine is of the order of 0.1 mm, so 1 mm overstates it by about ten times, and the quiz answer repeats it.
- ch 2 [high] Section 1, second paragraph: The text says dry soot 'is the only part you see as smoke'. Section 3 then says blue smoke is burning lube oil and white smoke is unburnt fuel or water vapour, which are visible but are not dry soot. The two statements contradict each other and a student will not know which is right.
- ch 2 [high] Section 4, bullet list: The list is introduced with 'These changes cut PM', but three of the six bullets (higher load, injection retard, EGR) say PM rises. The heading contradicts its own items.
- ch 2 [low] Section 1, first paragraph, and Section 2 formula: The text says PM 'has three parts', then describes four (soot, SOF, sulphate with water, ash). The formula PM = m_soot + m_SOF + m_sulphate + m_water also leaves out the ash fraction that the paragraph mentions. The parts and the formula do not match.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, steps 2–5 (and Quiz Q3): 0.8 m³ is the volume pulled through the filter, i.e. the sample flow, not the total exhaust flow of the engine. The example silently treats sample flow as engine exhaust flow. That is only valid when a full-flow dilution tunnel takes a proportional sample; the chapter never says so, and a student may apply the method to a non-proportional sample.
- ch 3 [high] Worked example 1, step 5 and answer (and the last exam trap, and quiz Q3): The arithmetic is right, but the closing sentence mislabels the quantity. With the formula used, the intake-charge exhaust fraction is x = (1.80 − 0.04)/(12.0 − 0.04) = 1.76/11.96 = 14.7% by mass. The chapter's formula, which divides by (CO₂ exhaust − CO₂ intake) instead of (CO₂ exhaust − CO₂ ambient), gives x/(1 − x) = 17.3%, i.e. the mass of recirculated exhaust per 100 parts of fresh air. Calling 17.3% 'the EGR percentage of the intake charge' is wrong, and a student who writes that in an exam is wrong.
- ch 3 [high] Exam traps, first trap: Unit slip. Section 2 gives the window as ±0.3 in air-fuel ratio, but the trap says the TWC needs 'λ within about ±0.3 of stoichiometric'. A λ tolerance of ±0.3 means AFR from about 10.3 to 19.1, which is outside the catalyst window completely; the two statements also contradict each other.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2, TWC paragraph: The window is given only as 'about ±0.3 air-fuel ratio'. The width differs between sources and is clearest as a λ band; stated alone as an AFR tolerance it is on the wide side and does not match the ±0.3 λ slip in the traps.
- ch 3 [low] Section 3, table, lean-burn petrol row: A lean-burn petrol engine is not normally given a DPF; its NOx route is an LNT (or SCR), and the particulate filter that appears on modern direct-injection petrol cars is a gasoline particulate filter, which the table does not mention. As written, students will think a diesel-style DPF is standard on lean petrol.
- ch 4 [high] Figure (block diagram), node f and edges b→f: The smoke meter is fed from the same branch that contains the soot filter. The filter exists to stop soot reaching the gas analysers, but soot is exactly what an opacimeter or filter-paper meter measures, so a smoke channel placed after the filter would read almost clean gas. In practice an opacimeter measures across the exhaust pipe or a partial-flow line, not downstream of a soot filter.
- ch 4 [high] Figure (block diagram), nodes b→c and b→d, and caption: NDIR and CLD are shown taking sample straight from the heated line at about 190 °C, with no cooler or dryer. Section 1 of the same chapter says water must be removed before NDIR and CLD because it absorbs infrared light and quenches the NO2* signal. The caption 'every gas channel sees the same conditioned sample' therefore contradicts the text.
- ch 4 [low] Section 4, methane sentence: The claim that the FID response to methane is 'poor' is stronger than the standard data support. On a carbon-atom basis methane responds close to 1 ppmC per ppm and is widely used as the methane span gas. The large under-responses are for oxygenated species such as methanol and formaldehyde, and for CO.
- ch 4 [low] Section 3 and Worked example 1, 'spiking test': The spiking test is named twice but never defined, so a student cannot say what is added to what or how the efficiency number is obtained.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, third paragraph: The sentence says 'A limit in g/km or g/kWh is a mass per unit work.' Only g/kWh is mass per unit work. A g/km limit is mass per unit distance, so the sentence contradicts the definitions given just above it in the formula box.
- ch 5 [low] Lead and Quiz Q1 answer: The cycle is said to 'fix the work done'. For a g/km limit the cycle fixes the distance covered and the speed-time history; work only enters when the limit is in g/kWh. A student may think work is always measured for light vehicles.
- ch 5 [low] Section 2, first sentence: The text jumps from 'up to BS-III, Indian cars were tested on the Indian Driving Cycle and its modified version' straight to BS-VI using WLTC. It leaves out BS-IV, which also used the Modified Indian Driving Cycle, so a reader cannot tell what was used between BS-III and BS-VI.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1 table, PM row for petrol car: The table says PM is 'mostly not limited' for petrol cars. Under BS-VI a particle mass limit of 0.0045 g/km applies to petrol cars as well, so the entry is misleading for the standard the chapter is about.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q3: The question tests a CO result against a 0.06 g/km limit. Real CO limits are of the order of 1 g/km; 0.06 g/km is a NOx-type number, so the numbers can plant a wrong memory of typical CO limits.
- ch 6 [high] Worked example 1, after step 7: A leftover authoring artefact is printed in the middle of the worked example: "dry run: {'head': [], 'rows': []}". Students will see an empty table skeleton and a raw object instead of a solution step.
- ch 6 [high] Section 2, producer gas sentence: Producer gas LHV is given as "about 5–6 MJ/kg". Air-blown producer gas is normally quoted per normal cubic metre, about 4–6 MJ/Nm³; because its density is roughly 1.1–1.2 kg/Nm³, the value per kg is only about 4–4.5 MJ/kg. The stated figure overstates energy per kg by about a third.
- ch 6 [low] Figure, root node note: The single root note "lower CO2 per MJ" is attached to all six fuels. On a per-megajoule basis biodiesel is about the same as, or slightly above, petroleum diesel (about 0.074 vs 0.072 kg CO2/MJ); its benefit is biogenic carbon and well-to-wheel accounting, not tailpipe CO2 per MJ. A student may carry away a wrong generalisation.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1, LPG sentence: "Its supply is tied to refinery output" is only partly true. A large share of LPG comes from natural gas processing and fractionation of natural gas liquids, not from crude oil refineries alone.
- ch 6 [low] Figure, 'Liquid' branch: DME is grouped as a liquid fuel, but at ambient temperature and pressure it is a gas; it is carried as a liquid only under about 5 bar pressure. On its own the tree implies DME is a liquid like ethanol.
- ch 7 [high] Exam traps, fifth trap: The trap says methane slip is 'not a product of incomplete flame travel'. That contradicts Section 3, which says lean light-load cycles burn incompletely and HC climbs, and it contradicts the standard picture: most slip is unburnt methane left by incomplete or missed combustion (lean misfire, quench at the walls and crevices), with some from charge short-circuiting during valve overlap. A student would write a wrong answer in the exam.
- ch 7 [high] Section 4, multi-fuel engines, compression ratio sentence: The sentence is self-contradictory: if the compression ratio were set for the least friendly fuel (the petrol-like, low-cetane fuel), that fuel would be the one protected from knock, not the one that knocks. In a compression-ignition multi-fuel engine the ratio is a compromise, held high for diesel ignition but capped because petrol-like fuels knock.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2, list of steps, second bullet: 'A diesel compression ratio is too cold to ignite methane' is loose and does not match the quiz answer in Q3. At a compression ratio near 17 the charge reaches roughly 500–550 °C, which is around methane's auto-ignition temperature; the real reason methane fails is the very long ignition delay, not simply a cold charge.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, first line: 'High substitution at high load is where dual fuel knocks' is awkward and ambiguous; a student may read it as saying high substitution prevents knock.
- ch 9 [high] Section 1 (paragraph 2, last sentence) and the figure caption and figure aria text: The text says the figure shows that NOx climbs above 1900 K 'while soot falls, so both are low only below that temperature', and the caption says 'NOx is low only below about 1900 K, where soot has already fallen'. The plotted data contradict this: soot is 0.9 at 1500 K, 0.85 at 1700 K and 0.6 at 1900 K, so in the whole 'below 1900 K' range soot is high, not low. A student reading the sentence and then the graph will be confused, and the sentence misstates the physics: a temperature cut alone cannot give a low-soot, low-NOx window; soot is low only at high temperature, so the lean φ condition is what closes the window.
- ch 9 [low] Section 1, paragraph 2: 'The high-soot region sits at φ > 1.' In the standard φ–T map the soot island is centred at much richer mixtures, roughly 2 < φ < 4 for 1500 K < T < 2500 K, with φ > 1 only the start of the rich, oxygen-poor side.
- ch 9 [low] Figure axis and caption: The φ–T map is drawn in terms of local, in-cylinder equivalence ratio and local temperature, but the figure x-axis is labelled 'peak cycle temperature'. The two are not the same quantity and the section switches between them without saying so.
- ch 9 [low] Lead: 'They are used in research engines and in a few production petrol engines as a lean-burn mode' covers both HCCI and RCCI. HCCI has appeared in a small number of production petrol engines, but RCCI has not gone into production.
- ch 10 [low] Section 1, Bowditch extended piston: Saying the camera 'looks along the cylinder axis' hides the folding: the light travels down the axis through the crown window, then the 45° mirror turns it out sideways, so the camera itself sits beside the engine looking horizontally.
- ch 10 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: The sentence 'it fires only a few cycles, so cycle-to-cycle variation is large' gives the wrong cause. Firing few cycles makes the variation hard to measure; the variation itself comes from the low compression ratio, non-production chamber and uneven wall temperatures.
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, lasers sentence: 532 nm is not the natural output of Nd:YAG; it is the frequency-doubled second harmonic. A student may quote 532 nm as the fundamental wavelength.
- ch 10 [low] Section 2, LDA sentence: LDA measures velocity at one point; swirl or tumble is obtained only by traversing the measurement volume across the cylinder, which the sentence does not say.
