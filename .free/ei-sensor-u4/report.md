## Reviewer notes (after any revision)

- ch 1 [high] Section 1, direct effect: "Release it and the charge reverses" contradicts the chapter's own law Q = dF. Charge is proportional to the applied stress, so removing the force brings the charge back to zero, not to an opposite value. A student will read this as: pressing gives +Q and releasing gives −Q.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, formula V = g t P: The formula writes g = d/ε but ε is never defined; εr appears in the material list and ε0 only appears later in Section 2.
- ch 1 [low] Section 1, last paragraph: The d33 / d31 labelling is stated as if it holds for every crystal. Those subscripts belong to poled materials such as PZT and PVDF; quartz is not poled and its datasheet quotes d11 (and d14).
- ch 1 [low] Section 3, pressure measurement: The text says a stack of crystal plates converts pressure to force and then uses q = dAP. In a stack of n plates the charges add, so the formula underestimates the output by a factor n.
- ch 2 [high] Quiz Q6 (question premise and answer): The direction of the error is wrong. On a warmer day the real speed of sound is higher, so for a fixed true distance the time of flight is shorter. A sensor that still uses the old, lower value of v computes d = v_fixed × t / 2, which comes out smaller than the true distance. The reading therefore becomes too short, not 'about 4 % too far'. The answer also contradicts itself: it says the time of flight is shorter and then concludes the reported distance is too long.
- ch 2 [low] Worked example 1, output block: The three printed numbers carry no units and are not in the same unit: 2.058 and 0.1715 are in metres, while 8.575 is the wavelength in millimetres (8.575 × 10⁻³ m). A student copying the output block cannot tell.
- ch 2 [low] Quiz Q5, answer line: The line '1.776 × 10⁶ / 2 × 10⁶ = 0.888' is ambiguous: without brackets it can be read as (1.776 × 10⁶ / 2) × 10⁶. The denominator is 2 × 2 × 10⁶ × 0.5 = 2 × 10⁶.
- ch 2 [low] Section 3, level measurement paragraph: 'The depth falls as t grows' uses 'depth' for the liquid level while the same sentence uses d = v t / 2 for the distance from the sensor down to the surface. A student can confuse d with the depth being measured.
- ch 3 [high] Section 2, table, Q2: The comparison sign is missing, so the key condition reads as nonsense: "used with f n" (Section 2), "Accelerometer | f n" (table), "Why is an accelerometer used with f n?" (Q2). A student cannot tell whether f must be far below or far above fn, which is the whole point of the section.
- ch 3 [low] Section 2 and Q6: Damping near ζ = 0.6–0.7 is said to "flatten H near the low-frequency end". At r ≪ 1 the ratio H ≈ 1 whatever ζ is; the working band of an accelerometer is set by ζ at the upper end, where damping removes the peak near r = 1 and pushes the flat region to higher r.
- ch 3 [low] Exam traps and Q4 answer: The symbol D appears in the trap ("H = 1/D", "Z/Y = r²/D") and in the Q4 answer, but D is never defined anywhere in the chapter.
- ch 4 [high] Section 2, compass paragraph (compared with Quiz Q6): The chapter says the earth's horizontal field (25–65 µT) is 'tens of times weaker than a small permanent magnet'. Quiz Q6 says it is 'roughly a thousandth' of the field near a gear tooth and puts gear-tooth field changes at tens of millitesla. Tens of times and a thousandth differ by about two orders of magnitude, so the same comparison is stated two different ways.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, driving paragraph, and Quiz Q2 answer: 'VH is proportional to B alone' and 'the output depends only on B' overstate the case. The Hall coefficient RH = 1/(n e) also changes with temperature because n changes, so current drive removes only the drift caused by plate resistance. The same paragraph later admits RH and n drift with heat, which contradicts the earlier sentence.
- ch 4 [low] Section 2, last paragraph: The sentence 'temperature drift comes from RH, n and plate resistance all changing with heat, so a ratiometric or chopper-stabilised part is chosen' gives the wrong remedy. Ratiometric output cancels supply-voltage variation, not temperature drift, and chopper stabilisation removes amplifier offset and its drift, not the drift of RH.
- ch 4 [low] Section 1, material paragraph (and Quiz Q6 answer): 'InSb with n around 2 × 10²² m⁻³ is far more sensitive than silicon with n around 10²³ m⁻³' is too strong: the quoted densities differ by about 5 times, and the chapter's own values (RH = 3.8 × 10⁻⁴ m³/C for InSb, 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³/C for silicon) differ by about 4 times. Quiz Q6 then attributes the compass design to 'a high-mobility material', which is a different property from high sensitivity.
- ch 4 [low] Exam traps, fourth trap: 'Picking the wrong pair of edges gives zero' misleads. Contacts taken along the current path do read a voltage: the ohmic IR drop of the plate, which is usually much larger than VH. Only the Hall component between them is zero.
- ch 5 [high] Section 3, first paragraph, and quiz Q2 answer: Both say bias moves the operating point 'away from the steep zero-field region'. This is backwards and it contradicts Section 1, which says the biased sensor works on a steeper part of the curve. At zero bias the λ-H curve has a flat minimum, so dλ/dH is close to zero there; bias is applied to reach the region of maximum slope, not to leave it.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, paragraph on the λ-H curve: Calling the unbiased λ-H curve 'S-shaped' can mislead: for a positive-magnetostriction material λ is the same sign for +H and -H and rises from a flat minimum at H = 0, so the curve does not cross zero with a steep slope at the origin.
- ch 5 [low] Section 1, the 'where' line under the formula: λ = ΔL/L returns a pure dimensionless fraction; the line 'λ is the magnetostriction in ppm or as a dimensionless fraction' mixes the fraction with the ppm value, which is λ × 10⁶.
- ch 5 [low] Quiz Q4 answer: The third cause, 'Temperature drift in the bias can also appear as direction-dependent output', is not right. Bias or temperature drift gives an offset or span error, not a difference between the rising and falling paths.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1: The threshold is stated only in terms of the work function Φ. That is right for photoemissive devices, but the receivers used later in the chapter (photodiodes) are semiconductors, where the threshold is the band gap energy Eg, not the work function. A student may carry the wrong threshold to photodiodes.
- ch 6 [low] Section 2: 'If the angle inside the core is greater than the critical angle' does not say how the angle is measured. Students often measure it from the boundary and then get the inequality the wrong way round.
- ch 6 [low] Section 3, last paragraph: 'The reading is a change in light, not the absolute light level' is unclear and sits oddly next to the drift warning; the receiver does see an absolute power level, and the measurand is encoded as a change in it.
- ch 6 [low] Beyond: Standard optical time-domain reflectometry (OTDR) is not a phase-sensitive method; it times Rayleigh backscatter and measures loss along the fibre. Only the phase-sensitive variant (φ-OTDR) works on phase.
- ch 6 [low] Section 1: The chapter names the photoelectric effect and the beam arrangements but never classifies photoelectric transducers themselves (photoemissive, photoconductive, photovoltaic), which many syllabi expect under this heading.
- ch 7 [high] Section 1 and Section 2 (A/B phase): The two sentences contradict each other and the table. 'B's windows sit a quarter of a slot pitch ahead of track A' means B's edge arrives first, i.e. B leads A; the text then says 'B lags A by 90°'. The table (00 → 01 → 11 → 10) also shows B changing before A, i.e. B leads, yet Section 2 says 'If A leads B the table is read downwards'. A student cannot tell which channel leads in the given direction.
- ch 7 [high] Quiz Q4 answer: 'Direction sensing is also lost' is wrong. Direction is not stored anywhere; it is derived live from the A/B phase order, so the decoder knows the direction as soon as the shaft moves after power-up. Only the position count is lost.
- ch 7 [low] Section 1 formula box: The formula mixes cases: p is called 'grating pitch or slot pitch in µm (millimetres on a linear scale)' and the answer is said to be 'in µm per count'. For a rotary encoder the slot pitch is in degrees and the answer is in degrees per count, not µm.
- ch 7 [low] Section 2 formula box (counts per millimetre): counts per millimetre = 4/p only holds if p is inserted in millimetres. With p in µm, as used everywhere else in the chapter, the expression gives counts per µm.
- ch 7 [low] Section 3, binary code example: 'the outer track's window is a little wider' is vague and often physically wrong: the outer track usually carries the finest (least significant) pattern, while the bit that causes the big jump is the most significant one.
- ch 7 [low] Figure caption and spec: The caption talks about 'the marked column', but the pointer marks a single cell in Track A (row 0, cell 2), and its label 'next' means nothing to a reader.
- ch 8 [high] Section 2, also the second Exam trap and the Quiz Q5 answer: The recommended procedure is backwards. It says to start at a low flash rate and stop at the first single still image. With one painted mark, a single still image also appears whenever the shaft makes a whole number of turns between flashes, that is at f = (n/60)/m for m = 2, 3, ... Sweeping up from below, the first single still image therefore appears at half (or a third) of the true speed, and the dial under-reads. The standard rule is to come down from a high rate, because the first single still image met from above is the true speed.
- ch 8 [high] Quiz Q5 (question and answer): The question says a 'single still mark' is seen at 1200 and again at 2400 flashes/min, but the answer justifies the 1200 rpm reading with k = 1 and 2, and the 600 rpm reading with k = 2 and 4. With one painted mark, k = 2 shows two marks 180° apart and k = 4 shows four marks, not a single mark. So the observation as worded rules out both readings the answer gives; a single still mark at both rates would actually fit n = 2400 rpm (k = 1/2 at 1200/min, k = 1 at 2400/min) and its multiples. Question and answer contradict each other.
- ch 8 [low] Section 2, third paragraph: 'Below the fundamental you see several marks and read a fraction' mixes up the two cases. At an exact sub-multiple of the fundamental (f = (n/60)/m) the mark looks like one single still mark and the dial reads a fraction of the true speed. It is above the fundamental, at k = 2 or 3, that you see k separate images of the one mark.
- ch 8 [low] Section 3, frequency-to-voltage formula and Worked example 2: Vout = Vref × T × fin holds only while the pulse width T is shorter than the input period, that is fin ≤ 1/T. If the input frequency rises above 1/T the pulses run into each other, the monostable output stays high, and Vout saturates at Vref instead of rising further. The text does not state this limit.
- ch 8 [low] Section 1 and Section 2, symbol p: The letter p means pole pairs in the AC tachogenerator formula and number of painted marks in the stroboscope formula, two sections apart. A student revising quickly can carry the wrong meaning across.
- ch 9 [low] Worked example 1, answer: The answer says the transmitter 'sends' the 0.16 A as its output signal, but Section 3 says the output is a current, often 4–20 mA. A student may think the loop current itself is the transmitted signal and that 160 mA is a normal output.
- ch 10 [high] Section 2: The chapter defines transit time as "the spread in electron arrival times at the anode". Transit time itself is the average time an electron takes to travel from the photocathode to the anode. The spread of those times is a separate quantity, transit-time spread (or jitter). Students will confuse the two and write the wrong definition in the exam.
- ch 10 [low] Section 3, first sentence: "Detects radiation that does not make enough light by itself" is vague: a student may read "by itself" as referring to the counter rather than the radiation.
- ch 11 [low] Section 2, comparison table, photoconductive cell row: The Bias column says "none" for the photoconductive cell. An LDR is a resistor: it needs an applied voltage (usually through a series resistor in a divider) before the resistance change becomes a measurable voltage or current. Only the junction bias is zero, not the circuit bias.
- ch 11 [low] Section 3, paragraph after the Beer-Lambert formulas: "Absorbance is logarithmic, so it adds when samples add" is too loose to be useful. A student can read it as "absorbance is not proportional to concentration" or as "adding more volume to the cuvette raises A".
- ch 11 [low] Lead paragraph: "These devices are cheap, small and fast" is applied to all four devices, but the LDR and the photovoltaic cell are the slow ones, as the table itself says later. The lead contradicts the table.
- ch 11 [low] Quiz Q7: The question "Name two types of photodiode used for light detection" is vague: every photodiode detects light, so the expected answer (PIN and avalanche) is a structural classification, not a use-based one, and a student can legitimately answer "pn and PIN".
- ch 11 [low] Section 2, comparison table, photodiode row: "light meter" is listed under a reverse-biased photodiode, but light meters and exposure meters normally run the photodiode at zero bias (photovoltaic mode) to get good linearity and low dark current. As written it clashes with the photovoltaic row, which also claims the exposure meter.
